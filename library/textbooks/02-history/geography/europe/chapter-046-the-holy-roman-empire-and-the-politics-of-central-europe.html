The Holy Roman Empire and the Politics of Central Europe

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

In November 1158, on a plain near Piacenza in northern Italy, the ruler of the German kingdom held an assembly at Roncaglia and asserted rights over Italian cities, roads, rivers, mints, and tolls. Within a generation, those same cities had formed a league, defeated his army in the field, and negotiated a settlement that recognized much of what they had claimed for themselves. The episode contains the central problem of this chapter: how a political order could be at once ambitious in its claims and decentralized in its operation.

The assembly at Roncaglia, convened by Frederick I, called Barbarossa, belongs to a long tradition of medieval royal assemblies, but it was unusual in its setting and its aims. Frederick was king of the Germans, king of Italy, and, since 1155, crowned emperor. He governed no single territory in the way that a contemporary king of England or a king of France governed his realm. His authority rested on the cooperation of dukes, margraves, counts, archbishops, bishops, abbots, and, increasingly, urban communities, and he exercised it largely by traveling among them. The assembly at Roncaglia shows an emperor attempting to convert a bundle of customary claims in Italy into reliable revenue and jurisdiction. The resistance that followed shows why that conversion proved so difficult.

This chapter examines the political structure usually called, in English, the Holy Roman Empire, as it developed from the tenth century to the middle of the thirteenth century, with a closing look at the decades after 1250. It follows the formation of a medieval empire under the Saxon Ottonian kings, its expansion into Italy and Burgundy, its entanglement with the reforming papacy, and its long engagement with northern Italian communes and the Norman kingdom of Sicily. It also follows a quieter transformation that proceeded alongside the dramatic confrontations between emperors and popes: the growth of coherent territorial lordships under princes, bishops, and abbots, and the emergence of cities as political actors. These developments together produced a political order of multiple centers of authority that was neither anarchy nor a failed centralized monarchy.

The chapter deliberately differs from Chapter 45, which examined Norman England and the growth of stronger monarchies. England and France are important points of comparison, and the chapter returns to them in its final sections, but the Empire should not be judged simply by how closely it resembled them. Historians have long debated whether the medieval Empire was a source of German weakness or an effective system suited to its own circumstances. This chapter treats that question as a genuine problem of interpretation, and it asks students to consider what imperial rulers, princes, bishops, and townspeople themselves expected from the political order they inhabited.

The subject also requires careful use of language. The Empire was not Germany, and it was not a nation-state in embryo. It included the kingdom of Germany, the kingdom of Italy, and, after 1032, the kingdom of Burgundy, and its inhabitants spoke Germanic, Romance, and Slavic languages, among others. The title of its ruler, the name of the polity, and the boundaries of its authority all changed over time. Students should resist the habit, encouraged by later national histories, of reading the Empire as an incomplete Germany or as a collection of regions waiting for unification. Those later outcomes, examined in Chapter 78 and Chapter 100, were contingent and not predetermined.

The evidence for this subject is rich but unevenly distributed. Imperial charters, the products of the royal writing office, survive in large numbers and record grants, privileges, and the movements of the court. Chronicles by Widukind of Corvey, Thietmar of Merseburg, and Otto of Freising, and the continuation of Otto's work by his secretary Rahewin, provide narrative accounts, though each writer was a cleric with particular loyalties. Legal compilations such as the Saxon law book known as the Sachsenspiegel, papal registers, communal records from Italian cities, coinage, castles, imperial palaces, and cathedrals such as Speyer supply additional evidence. Most of this material reflects the viewpoint of rulers, churchmen, and urban elites, and the experience of peasants and of women outside the ruling families is usually visible only indirectly.

Within Part VII, this chapter occupies a pivotal place. Chapter 44 examined the papal reform movement and the Investiture Conflict, which reshaped relations between spiritual and secular authority. Chapter 45 traced the consolidation of monarchies in England and France. Chapter 46 asks what happened in the political system where the Investiture Conflict was most disruptive and where monarchy took a different course. Chapter 47 will then follow the crusading movement, in which emperors and German princes played prominent roles. For the larger argument of Volume II, the Empire illustrates that medieval Europe developed several viable political models, and that the distinctive European pattern of the High Middle Ages was variety rather than convergence.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the Ottonian kings created a medieval empire from the East Frankish kingdom and why the name "Holy Roman Empire" developed gradually.
  2. Describe the Empire as a composite political order made up of the kingdoms of Germany, Italy, and Burgundy rather than as a centralized territorial state.
  3. Analyze how royal election, itinerant kingship, assemblies, and dynastic lands shaped the exercise of imperial authority.
  4. Assess why bishops and abbots served as major political and territorial rulers and why this system survived the Investiture Conflict.
  5. Trace the growth of territorial lordship under dukes, counts, margraves, and ecclesiastical princes, and explain why decentralization did not mean the absence of government.
  6. Evaluate Frederick Barbarossa's strategy for reinforcing imperial authority in Germany and Italy and its limits.
  7. Explain the conflict between the emperors and the northern Italian communes, including the Lombard League and the Peace of Constance.
  8. Compare the more centralized kingdom of Sicily with the political structure of Germany under Frederick II.
  9. Interpret the Great Interregnum without describing it as total anarchy, and identify the institutions that continued to function.
  10. Distinguish the political roles of princes, imperial cities, ministeriales, and ecclesiastical territories in central Europe.
  11. Contrast the Empire with England and France and evaluate the claim that centralization signified success and decentralization signified failure.
  12. Interpret primary source evidence from imperial and communal perspectives, noting what each source can and cannot establish.

Historical Context

The political world examined in this chapter emerged from the fragmentation of the Carolingian order, traced in Chapter 31 and Chapter 32. After the division of Charlemagne's empire in the ninth century, the eastern portion, usually called East Francia, developed under its own Carolingian kings and then under rulers drawn from regional aristocracies. It was a realm of powerful stem duchies, regional units that historians associate with Saxony, Bavaria, Swabia, Franconia, and, in some interpretations, Lotharingia. These units had distinct aristocracies and customs, and the extent to which they represented ancient peoples rather than recent political groupings remains a subject of scholarly discussion. Chapter 33 examined the formation of the German kingdom alongside the consolidation of England and France, and this chapter takes up the story from the tenth century onward.

Three features of the inherited context shaped everything that followed. First, kingship in East Francia was personal and relational. Kings ruled through landholding, family connections, oaths, gifts, and assemblies, and they relied on a literate clergy trained in royal and episcopal chapels. Second, the Church held large estates and rights. Bishoprics and monasteries received land, immunities, and jurisdiction from earlier kings and had become major centers of wealth, learning, and administration. Third, the memory of Rome and of Charlemagne gave the imperial title a prestige that no other ruler's title could match. Chapter 34 described the lordship and dependency that structured rural society, and those arrangements formed the social base for the territorial lordships examined below.

External pressures also mattered. Raiding by Magyars, examined in Chapter 32, tested the East Frankish kings from the early tenth century, and the victory over them in 955 enhanced royal prestige. To the north and east lay Slavic and Scandinavian societies, whose relations with the Empire included warfare, tribute, missionary activity, and, from the twelfth century, settlement and political expansion. To the south lay Italy, a land of old cities, papal authority around Rome, Lombard and Byzantine inheritances, and growing commercial communities. Chapter 35 treated the conversion of Poland, Bohemia, and Hungary, and Chapter 43 described the commercial revival of Italian and northern towns. These developments explain why imperial rulers found Italy so attractive and so difficult to govern.

Finally, the Empire's story is bound up with the papacy. Emperors from Otto I onward presented themselves as protectors of the Roman Church, and for much of the tenth and eleventh centuries they intervened in papal affairs. The reform movement and the Investiture Conflict, examined in Chapter 44, transformed that relationship. This chapter does not retell the confrontation between Gregory VII and Henry IV or the settlement at Worms in 1122. It focuses instead on the institutional and political consequences: what the Empire was after the conflict, who held power within it, and how Italy and the papacy continued to shape its history.

Historians approach this subject through the products of royal and episcopal chanceries, narrative histories, legal texts, and archaeology. The Diplomata, as the royal charters are called, allow scholars to reconstruct the itineraries of kings by recording where grants were issued. Studies of these itineraries show that rulers concentrated their time in particular regions, notably Saxony under the Ottonians and the Rhine and Main valleys under the Salians and Hohenstaufen, and that the regions they visited least were often those where their authority was most dependent on local magnates. The itinerary is therefore both a source and an argument about how power worked.

Section 46.1

From East Francia to the Medieval Empire

The medieval Empire grew from the East Frankish kingdom, which emerged from the division of the Carolingian realm in the ninth century. When the last East Frankish Carolingian died in 911, the magnates chose Conrad I of Franconia, whose reign was troubled by conflict with the dukes. In 919 the Saxon duke Henry I, called the Fowler, was elected king, and he ruled until 936. Henry's kingship rested on agreements with the other dukes, whom he treated as partners rather than subordinates, and on a defensive system of fortified places against Magyar raiders. His reign set a pattern that persisted for centuries: the king's strength depended on his ability to reach understandings with powerful regional lords.

His son Otto I, who reigned from 936 to 973, built on this foundation. Otto faced revolts by relatives and dukes in the early years of his reign, and he gradually reduced the independence of the stem duchies by placing family members and trusted allies in them and, more importantly, by drawing bishops and abbots into the service of the crown. In 951 he intervened in Italy and took the Lombard royal title. In 955 he defeated the Magyars at the Lechfeld near Augsburg, a victory that his contemporaries and later chroniclers celebrated and that enhanced his prestige as a protector of Christian people. In 962 Pope John XII crowned him emperor in Rome, and in the following years the relationship between the new emperor and the papacy proved far from harmonious.

The imperial coronation of 962 should not be read as the founding of a modern German state. Otto and his successors understood themselves as kings first, and the imperial title added a Roman dimension to a power that was rooted in the lands north of the Alps. The title brought obligations in Italy and the expectation of protecting the Roman Church, and it gave rulers a claim to precedence among Christian kings that other monarchs did not always accept. Otto's son Otto II married the Byzantine princess Theophanu, and Otto III, whose reign ended in 1002, pursued a vision of Roman renewal centered on the city of Rome itself. These rulers operated within a Mediterranean and European network of courts that included Byzantium, a connection discussed in Chapter 36 and Chapter 37.

The name that later became standard, Holy Roman Empire, emerged slowly. Ottonian and Salian writers spoke of the Roman Empire or of the emperor of the Romans. The phrase sacrum imperium, holy empire, appears in the chancery of Frederick Barbarossa in 1157, the fuller sacrum Romanum imperium is attested in 1184, and that formula became standard in chancery usage from about 1254. Students should therefore use the later name with awareness that it names a political order whose self-description evolved over about three centuries. The chapter uses the terms Empire and Reich, and where precision is needed it distinguishes the kingdoms that composed it.

Section 46.2

What Was the Holy Roman Empire?

The Empire is best understood as a composite political order. Its core was the kingdom of Germany, sometimes called the East Frankish or Teutonic kingdom in contemporary usage, whose ruler was elected by the magnates. Its second component was the kingdom of Italy, covering northern and central Italy as far as the borders of the papal territories, which the German king claimed by virtue of the Lombard crown. Its third component was the kingdom of Burgundy, which Conrad II claimed after 1032 and secured by 1034, and which covered territory in the Rhône and Saône valleys and the western Alps. The ruler of these three kingdoms was entitled to seek coronation as emperor, a ceremony that traditionally took place in Rome and that required papal participation.

Understanding this structure requires distinguishing titles. The man elected by the princes of Germany was called king, and from the eleventh century he was often styled king of the Romans, signaling his claim to the imperial crown. Only after coronation by the pope did he become emperor. Some rulers, notably Otto IV and Frederick II, were crowned emperor only after years as king, and some elected kings were never crowned. This distinction matters because it shows that imperial authority was not a single office but a layered claim, and that rulers' recognition and practical authority varied among the kingdoms.

The Empire's boundaries were fluid. Its northern and eastern frontiers shifted as the kings and princes extended their influence over Slavic territories, and its relationships with the kings of Denmark, Poland, Bohemia, and Hungary varied from alliance to overlordship claims to open conflict. The population was linguistically diverse. Germanic dialects predominated north of the Alps, Romance languages in Italy and Burgundy, and Slavic languages in the eastern marches, while Latin served as the language of the Church and chancery and Hebrew was used by the Jewish communities of the Rhineland and other towns. Chapter 49 examines Jewish life and other aspects of social experience in more detail, but it should be noted here that Jewish communities in cities such as Mainz, Worms, and Speyer were under the protection of kings and bishops, a relationship that sometimes failed them, as it did during the violence accompanying the First Crusade in 1096.

The result was a polity without a capital. Rulers had favorite residences, including royal palaces known as Pfalzen at places such as Aachen, Ingelheim, Goslar, Gelnhausen, and Nuremberg, but there was no permanent administrative center comparable to Westminster or Paris. The Empire was held together by a shared ruler, shared legitimating ideas, a body of customary law, a network of ecclesiastical institutions, and the loyalties and interests of those who benefited from it. Historians describe it using terms such as composite monarchy or polycentric polity, concepts that emphasize that authority was distributed among multiple levels.

Historical Vocabulary
Holy Roman Empire

Holy Roman Empire is the conventional English name for the medieval and early modern political order whose ruler held the western imperial title from 962 until 1806. In the period of this chapter its inhabitants spoke of the Roman Empire, the Roman kingdom, or the Reich, and the full formula became common only in the thirteenth century. The name does not mean that the polity was a German national state; it combined several kingdoms and many languages. It also should not be read as claiming direct continuity with the ancient Roman Empire in a literal administrative sense, although contemporaries sincerely understood the imperial office as a revival and continuation of Roman rulership. Later writers sometimes mocked the label, but such judgments come from later political perspectives and do not describe how medieval people understood their own world.

Section 46.3

Kingship, Election, and Dynastic Power

Kingship in the Empire was neither strictly hereditary nor freely elective. The great princes took part in choosing the king, and in many cases they selected a member of the previous dynasty, particularly a son designated in the ruler's lifetime. Ottonian rulers regularly had their sons elected or acclaimed as co-kings, a technique that smoothed succession. The Salian dynasty continued the pattern from 1024 to 1125, as did the Hohenstaufen after 1138. But dynastic continuity was never guaranteed. When Henry II, the last Ottonian, died childless in 1024, the magnates chose Conrad II, and when the Salians failed in 1125 the princes selected Lothar III of Saxony over the Hohenstaufen claimants, and in 1138 they chose Conrad III, the first Hohenstaufen king. Election mattered because it meant that every king had to build consent among the most powerful lords.

The practical business of rule was carried out through itinerant kingship. Kings traveled with a household that included clergy of the royal chapel, officials, and warriors, and they stayed at palaces, episcopal residences, and monasteries, whose inhabitants were obliged to provide hospitality. Wherever the court halted, the king heard disputes, issued charters, received petitioners, and held assemblies. This was an efficient method in societies without a developed system of written administration and fixed taxation, because it brought the ruler to the people whose cooperation he needed. It also meant that the king's presence mattered a great deal, and that regions he did not visit were governed by those who held power locally.

The material basis of royal power consisted of royal estates and fiscal rights, rights over churches, and the revenues of the imperial church, as explored in the next section. Rulers also drew on their dynastic lands, which became more important as imperial lands were alienated through grants. Henry III, whose reign from 1039 to 1056 is often seen as a high point of Salian power, relied heavily on a coordinated system of royal estates and the support of the episcopate. But royal resources never matched those of a territorial monarchy with a systematic tax regime. Consequently, successful kings used patronage, marriage alliances, and the redistribution of offices, and they sought to make their own dynastic lands the foundation of a stable power base. This is a major reason why dynastic territory became politically decisive later in the thirteenth century.

Assemblies, called Hoftage or court days, were the principal forum for consultation. At these gatherings the king consulted lay and ecclesiastical magnates, announced decisions, adjudicated disputes, and sometimes secured their consent to wars and legislation. Historians have emphasized that this was government by consensus, a style in which rulers who ignored the advice of the great risked rebellion, but also one in which consensus was shaped by ritual, favor, and pressure. By the later twelfth century, the participation of princes in major decisions was increasingly regarded as a right, not a courtesy. Frederick Barbarossa's reign shows both sides of this development: he issued important peace legislation with princely consent, and he also deposed a leading prince through a legal process dominated by the other princes.

Section 46.4

Bishops, Abbots, and the Imperial Church

The imperial Church, or Reichskirche, was the most distinctive feature of Ottonian and Salian rule. Ottonian kings endowed bishoprics and monasteries with lands, rights to tolls and markets, and immunities that placed church property under royal protection and excluded the interference of local counts. In return, bishops and abbots served as royal counselors, hosted the itinerant court, provided contingents of armed men for imperial campaigns, and administered portions of the realm. The system had a political logic. Because episcopal and abbatial offices were not hereditary, rulers could support trusted candidates for vacant sees without creating an office that automatically descended from father to son. The lands themselves remained attached to the church institution, although rulers could exercise important rights during vacancies and over the temporal regalia associated with the office. Historians use the term Reichskirche for this close relationship between monarchy and major churches, while debating how deliberate and systematic it actually was.

This arrangement made bishops into territorial rulers. The archbishops of Mainz, Cologne, and Trier, along with bishops of Würzburg, Bamberg, Worms, Speyer, Magdeburg, and many others, held extensive lands and exercised jurisdiction over them, often through lay officials called advocates (Vögte) who protected church property and handled bloodshed and military duties that clergy were not supposed to perform. Cathedral cities became centers of both religious and political life. Mainz, the seat of the archbishop who traditionally held a leading role in royal elections, was among the most important political centers of the realm. These episcopal territories later formed the core of the ecclesiastical principalities that remained part of the Empire's political map until the early nineteenth century.

Students should avoid the assumption that this system simply made the Church an arm of the state. Bishops were also members of powerful families, scholars, pastors, builders, and participants in a wider Christian community that included the papacy. Their loyalties could be divided, and the reform movement of the eleventh century, with its insistence that lay rulers should not control spiritual offices, made the situation acute. Yet even critics of royal investiture often recognized that bishops held lands and jurisdictions that came from the crown. The key question is why bishops remained major territorial rulers after the settlement at Worms. The answer is that the territories, the political roles, and the practical needs of both rulers and bishops remained in place; what changed was the process of selecting and investing bishops, not the underlying fact that episcopal lordship was part of the political order.

Monasteries and abbeys also held imperial status. Abbeys such as Fulda, Lorsch, Corvey, and Reichenau were centers of learning, manuscript production, and landholding, and some abbots exercised the rights of princes. Women's communities, including the Ottonian foundations of Quedlinburg and Gandersheim, were ruled by abbesses who belonged to royal and noble families and who participated in the political and cultural life of the realm. The writings of Hrotsvit of Gandersheim, a canoness of the tenth century, show a high level of learning in such communities. The imperial Church thus offers a rare view of elite women as holders of political office, though one confined to a small and aristocratic group.

Historical Vocabulary
Regalia (iura regalia)

Regalia were the rights regarded as belonging to the crown, including the right to mint coins, to collect tolls and certain fines, to hold markets, to exploit mines and salt works, to control roads and rivers, and to exercise high justice. In the twelfth century, jurists and imperial officials increasingly tried to define these rights precisely, and in Italy Frederick I used the concept to claim revenues and jurisdiction long exercised by local communities. The term is easily confused with the physical regalia, the crown and other symbols of rule. In this chapter it refers to royal rights, which could be exercised directly, delegated to princes and bishops, or usurped by local powers. The disputes over who legitimately held them were among the central conflicts of the period.

Section 46.5

After Worms: Empire and Church after the Investiture Conflict

Chapter 44 examined the Investiture Conflict and the settlement of 1122, and this section addresses its consequences for the Empire's political order. The Concordat of Worms distinguished between the spiritual investiture of a bishop, with ring and staff, and the grant of the regalia, the temporal rights and lands attached to the office. In Germany, the king or his representative was to be present at the election of bishops and abbots and to confer the regalia by the scepter, with the investiture preceding consecration. In Italy and Burgundy the sequence was reversed and royal influence was weaker. The arrangement preserved a royal voice in the choice of leading churchmen in Germany while acknowledging the Church's claim to spiritual autonomy.

In practice, the effect was to reduce direct royal control and to increase the role of cathedral chapters and local nobility in selecting bishops. The period after Worms saw kings still influence episcopal elections through persuasion and pressure, but also saw chapters assert independence and princes seek to place their relatives in bishoprics. Lothar III, king from 1125 and emperor from 1133, ruled with the support of Saxon magnates and the Church and relied on episcopal backing, and Conrad III, the first Hohenstaufen king, faced rival Welf claimants and made limited headway in imposing royal authority. Both rulers worked in a political environment in which the princes were stronger relative to the crown than they had been under the Ottonians.

It would be wrong, however, to conclude that the settlement ended the imperial Church. On the contrary, the ecclesiastical princes remained among the Empire's most important political actors and, because they were celibate, they tended to be less likely than secular princes to build dynastic territories that threatened the crown. Rulers continued to value them as counselors and allies. The change was subtler: the Church was recognized as an independent institution within the Empire, and the political bonds between ruler and bishops became more negotiated and less hierarchical. This was a shift of balance, and it explains why bishops continued to be important rulers, both as holders of territorial lordships and as advisers at the imperial court.

Section 46.6

Princes and the Territorialization of Power

During the twelfth and thirteenth centuries a group of leading lords, historically described as princes, consolidated their authority over defined territories. The category included dukes, margraves, counts palatine, landgraves, and the leading bishops and abbots. Their power rested on inherited lands, rights granted by the crown, the control of castles, the exercise of justice, and the loyalty of vassals and ministeriales. Historians use the German term Landesherrschaft, territorial lordship, to describe this process, in which regional rulers shifted from holding scattered rights to governing a more coherent territory in which they exercised jurisdiction over most of the inhabitants.

The tools of territorialization were varied. Princes built or acquired castles at strategic points and used them as administrative centers. They claimed jurisdiction over high justice, including cases of serious crime, and they set up courts. They founded or favored towns, whose markets and tolls produced revenue, and they minted coin. They acquired rights over forests, mines, and roads, and they gained the position of advocate over monasteries and bishoprics, which provided access to church lands. They employed ministeriales as officials and knights, and they gradually developed a more regular form of administration. Chapter 34 described the lordship, dependency, and castles of rural society, and Chapter 42 and Chapter 43 described the agricultural expansion and urban growth that provided the resources for these ambitions.

Students should note that decentralization at the imperial level did not signify an absence of government. On the contrary, effective government often developed at the level of the principality. A duke of Bavaria, a landgrave of Thuringia, or an archbishop of Cologne could exercise real authority, hold courts, issue rulings, collect revenues, and wage war. In some principalities, administrative sophistication rivaled that of kingdoms. The Empire distributed political authority among numerous levels, and each level could function with considerable effectiveness. The imperial framework provided legitimacy, arbitration, and, at times, protection, while daily government was usually local or regional.

The relationship between the crown and the princes shifted over time. Under the Ottonians the king could install dukes and bishops with considerable freedom. After the twelfth century, the greater princes had a firmer hold on hereditary titles, and the king's capacity to dispose of their lands was limited by custom and by the political need to secure the consent of other princes. The expansion of Welf power under Henry the Lion, who held both Saxony and Bavaria and who founded Munich around 1158 and refounded Lübeck in 1159, illustrates what a prince could accomplish. His eventual fall also illustrates the limits of a prince's independence, as explained below. The princely order itself became more formalized, with increasing distinction between those who held lands directly from the crown and others.

Everyday Life and Social History: Everyday Government

Living amid Overlapping Authorities

Students sometimes imagine that decentralization meant people lived without government. For most inhabitants of the Empire, the reverse was true: they encountered several authorities at once. A peasant on a monastic estate in the Rhineland owed labor and dues to the abbey, appeared in the abbey's court for disputes over land, and might be subject to the jurisdiction of a count or the abbey's advocate for serious crimes. If he traveled to market, he paid tolls to a lord who held the right to collect them on a particular road or river crossing, and if he sold goods in a town, he came under the town's law and its officials.

A townsperson in an imperial city might be subject to the city's council, to royal officers who represented the crown, and to the jurisdiction of a bishop in ecclesiastical matters. A merchant carrying goods between Cologne and Italy encountered a series of local tolls, safe-conduct arrangements, and market privileges, each granted by a different holder of regalia. Jewish residents in a Rhineland town lived under special protection from the king or the bishop, which carried fiscal obligations, and their security depended on the willingness of these protectors to act. Local peace arrangements, known as Landfrieden, sought to limit feuding and violence in particular regions, and they show attempts to provide order through agreement among rulers rather than through a single state apparatus. Government was dense, local, and layered, and its effectiveness depended heavily on the particular jurisdiction involved.

Cause and Effect

Why Territorial Principalities Strengthened

Long-term causes

The Carolingian inheritance of regional aristocracies and church estates, the necessity for rulers to govern through local magnates, the growth of agricultural production and population, and the spread of castle building gave regional lords resources and bases of power.

Short-term causes

The Investiture Conflict weakened royal control over bishoprics and encouraged rival kings and princes, contested successions after 1125 and 1197 required rulers to buy support with grants, and the monarchy's absence in Italy left Germany in the hands of regional lords.

Immediate effects

Princes secured hereditary titles, rights of jurisdiction, castles, and the allegiance of ministeriales, while kings had fewer lands and offices to redistribute. The princes became an organized order with a collective voice in the choice of kings.

Long-term consequences

Principalities developed administrations, courts, and fiscal systems that became the political units of later German history, and the imperial constitution came to rest on negotiation between the ruler and the territorial estates.

Historical complexity: The strengthening of principalities did not follow from a single cause or run along a straight path. Kings sometimes promoted princes as counterweights to rivals, and princes sometimes defended the Empire's framework because it protected their own rights. The process also differed by region, as the later feature on regional variation shows.

Section 46.7

Frederick Barbarossa and the Revival of Imperial Authority

Frederick I, called Barbarossa for his red beard by Italian observers, was elected king in 1152 and crowned emperor in 1155. His mother belonged to the Welf family and his father to the Hohenstaufen, which gave him links to both of the great rival houses that had contested the throne. His accession was a skillful settlement of that rivalry, and he cultivated a close relationship with his cousin Henry the Lion, to whom he returned the duchy of Bavaria in 1156. In the same year, by an arrangement known as the Privilegium Minus, he made Austria a duchy in its own right for Henry Jasomirgott, an example of how a ruler could reorganize princely territories to settle disputes while also creating new hereditary titles.

Frederick's political aims have been interpreted in various ways, but most historians agree that he sought to strengthen the rights and prestige of the crown, which he called the honor of the Empire, while operating within the limits of princely consent. He relied on the counsel of the princes, used his dynastic lands in Swabia and Alsace as a power base, and expanded royal estates in the Rhine and Main regions. He promoted the use of the term holy for the Empire, and his chancery and his entourage drew on the language of Roman law and Roman imperial tradition to articulate claims about the emperor's rights. Chapter 48 examines the revival of Roman law and its teaching at Bologna, and here it is enough to observe that jurists supplied a vocabulary for defining imperial rights, not a program that governed practical politics.

Within Germany, Frederick's reign was marked by cooperation with the princes and by a consolidation of imperial strength after the instability that had followed Lothar III's death. The peace legislation of his reign, the construction of palaces such as Gelnhausen and the growth of royal towns, and the use of ministeriales as royal officials all contributed to a stronger royal position in the heartland of Staufer lands. He should not be remembered merely as an Italian campaigner. Most of his reign was spent in Germany, and he made it the basis for his Italian policies, which required money, soldiers, and princely support.

The fall of Henry the Lion in 1180 demonstrates the interplay between royal power and princely politics. After a dispute that included Henry's refusal to provide military support in Italy in 1176 and complaints from other princes about his conduct, Frederick summoned Henry to answer charges before princely courts. When Henry failed to appear, he was condemned and stripped of his duchies. Saxony was divided, with the western portion going to the archbishop of Cologne as the duchy of Westphalia, and Bavaria went to Otto of Wittelsbach, whose family would hold it for centuries. The case shows that a determined emperor, backed by other princes, could break the most powerful lord in Germany, but it also shows that the beneficiaries were chiefly other princes. The result strengthened the princely order as a whole while weakening a single dynasty.

Section 46.8

Italy, the Communes, and the Lombard League

The kingdom of Italy in the twelfth century was very different from Germany. Northern and central Italy contained a dense network of cities, many of which had been governed by bishops and imperial officials until the eleventh century and had since developed into self-governing communes. In cities such as Milan, Pavia, Cremona, Lodi, Bologna, and Verona, groups of citizens, including landowning nobles, merchants, and professionals, swore oaths to a communal association and elected consuls to govern. The communes controlled the surrounding countryside, collected tolls, raised armies, and conducted wars against each other. Chapter 43 described the commercial and financial growth that sustained them, and Chapter 56 will examine the later Italian city-states of the Renaissance.

Frederick's first Italian expedition in 1154 and 1155 brought his coronation in Rome, but it also revealed the problem of authority in Lombardy. At Roncaglia, in 1154 and again in 1158, he held assemblies at which representatives of the cities, with legal experts and princes present, were asked to identify the regalia. The result in 1158 was a declaration of imperial rights over a wide range of fiscal and jurisdictional matters, including tolls, mints, and the appointment of officials. Frederick appointed imperial officials, sometimes called podestàs, to govern cities, and he demanded revenues that the cities regarded as infringements of their long established liberties. Otto of Freising, the emperor's uncle and a historian, remarked with a mixture of admiration and unease that the Lombard cities had developed a form of government in which even men of modest rank participated, an observation that reveals how unusual these communities seemed to a German bishop.

Conflict followed. Milan resisted, and after a long siege the city surrendered in 1162, whereupon Frederick ordered its fortifications and many of its buildings destroyed. The harshness provoked a broader reaction. In 1167 several cities formed the Lombard League, an association that included former rivals of Milan and that received encouragement and support from Pope Alexander III, who had his own quarrel with the emperor. The league rebuilt Milan and founded a new city, named Alessandria in the pope's honor. Frederick's fifth expedition ended in defeat at Legnano on 29 May 1176, when the forces of the league, including the Milanese militia, defeated his army. This was not a total destruction of imperial power, but it was a decisive check.

The settlement came in stages. In 1177 Frederick reached a peace with Alexander III at Venice, ending the schism that had divided the Church during his reign. In 1183, by the Peace of Constance, the emperor recognized the cities' right to elect their own consuls and to exercise many rights and jurisdictions that they had assumed, while the cities acknowledged his overlordship, accepted the investiture of consuls by imperial representatives, and agreed to certain payments and services. The settlement did not eliminate imperial claims, and it did not make the cities independent states. It established a framework in which the communes operated with extensive autonomy inside a nominal imperial order. In this sense Italy, like Germany, became a region of overlapping jurisdictions.

The second is a compromise that records the reality of communal power while preserving imperial dignity. Neither is a neutral description of practice. The Roncaglia text speaks from the perspective of the imperial court and its learned advisers, and it was framed to justify royal revenues. The Peace of Constance preserves the cities' negotiated outcome but it was also drafted in terms acceptable to the emperor, who is shown as granting what he could no longer prevent. Section 46.9

Pope, Emperor, and the Politics of Italy

Italy repeatedly entangled imperial and papal power because it was the place where their claims overlapped. The emperor was crowned in Rome and regarded himself as protector of the Roman Church, while the popes were territorial rulers of Rome and of lands in central Italy, and they claimed the authority to crown and, in some views, to approve emperors. This was not simply a theoretical issue. The geography of Italy, with its imperial kingdom in the north, papal territories in the center, and the Norman kingdom of Sicily in the south, meant that an emperor marching to Rome had to pass through lands claimed by others, and that any shift of power in one region altered the balance among all three.

The conflict was not a simple contest between two unified sides. Within Italian cities, factions allied with the emperor or the pope for local reasons, and those allegiances were later labeled Ghibelline and Guelf. The names derive from German dynastic labels, associated with the Hohenstaufen and the Welfs, but their meaning in Italy was shaped by local rivalries rather than by a consistent ideology. Popes also had varied interests. Alexander III opposed Frederick I in part because the emperor supported rival popes, but papal policy equally reflected concern for the security of the Papal State and a desire to prevent a single ruler from dominating Italy. Chapter 40 examined the divergence of Latin and Greek Christianity, which provided another dimension to papal diplomacy, since both the Empire and the papacy dealt with Byzantium.

The competing concepts of universal authority gave the contest its intensity. Papal writers argued that spiritual authority was superior and that the pope played a role in the making of emperors, while imperial supporters emphasized that the emperor's authority came directly from God through election by the princes. These ideas were often used instrumentally, but they were also sincerely held and they shaped political outcomes. The practical result was a long pattern of negotiation and conflict in which neither emperors nor popes could eliminate the other, and in which the lesser powers of Italy, from communes to the Norman monarchy, used the rivalry to preserve their own freedom of action.

Section 46.10

Henry VI, Sicily, and the Hohenstaufen Mediterranean Monarchy

The kingdom of Sicily was founded by Norman rulers in the early twelfth century, and Roger II received a royal title in 1130 from the contested pope Anacletus II and was crowned that Christmas; Innocent II, who had opposed the new kingship, formally recognized Roger in 1139 after being defeated and captured. It combined territory on the Italian mainland with the island of Sicily and incorporated a population of Latin Christians, Greek Christians, and Muslims, with an administration that drew on Byzantine, Arab, and Norman practices. The kingdom, with its capital at Palermo, was wealthy and unusually well organized, and its rulers had an extensive Mediterranean reach. It was the product of a different history from that of Germany, and it offered a contrast with the Empire's decentralized structure.

In 1186 Frederick Barbarossa arranged the marriage of his son Henry, already crowned king of the Germans, to Constance, the aunt of King William II of Sicily and a daughter of the first Norman king, Roger II. When William died in 1189 without a direct heir, Sicilian nobles chose Tancred of Lecce, an illegitimate relative of the royal house, as king. Barbarossa meanwhile died in 1190 in Anatolia during the Third Crusade, and Henry VI succeeded him, was crowned emperor in 1191, and then pursued his wife's claim. After a first expedition failed, he conquered the kingdom in 1194, aided by the death of Tancred and by funds that included the ransom of Richard I of England, whom Henry had held prisoner. He was crowned king of Sicily at Palermo on Christmas Day 1194.

The union of Sicily and the Empire was a transformation in the geopolitics of Europe. It gave the Hohenstaufen a base in the south, rich revenues, and a Mediterranean presence, and it left the papacy with Hohenstaufen power on both north and south of its territory. For popes, who depended on political and territorial independence, this was a danger. Henry VI planned a series of ambitious projects, including an attempt to make the imperial crown hereditary in his family, which the German princes resisted, and he also organized a major crusading expedition, although he died before he could personally lead it. Chapter 47 treats that expedition in the wider crusading context. His premature death in 1197, at Messina, left a three-year-old heir and a divided Empire. The consequences, as shown in the following sections, were a double election in Germany and a prolonged struggle in which Pope Innocent III played a decisive role.

Section 46.11

Frederick II: Emperor, King of Sicily, and Political Experiment

Frederick II, born in 1194 in central Italy, was the son of Henry VI and Constance of Sicily. Orphaned at an early age, he was crowned king of Sicily as a small child, and Pope Innocent III acted as his guardian. He grew up in Palermo amid the competing factions of the Sicilian court, in a multilingual environment in which Latin, Greek, Arabic, and Italian vernaculars were all in use. Meanwhile the German kingdom was divided after 1198 between the Hohenstaufen candidate Philip of Swabia and the Welf Otto IV, a contest that Innocent III exploited. After Philip's murder in 1208, Otto was crowned emperor in 1209, but his attempt to claim Sicily brought a break with the pope, who supported Frederick. Anti-Welf princes first elected Frederick in absentia in 1211. After accepting the invitation and reaching Germany in 1212, he was elected again at Frankfurt in December and crowned at Mainz. After Otto's defeat in 1214 at Bouvines, an engagement closely tied to the rivalry of England and France examined in Chapter 45, he secured broader recognition, culminating in his coronation at Aachen in 1215. Pope Honorius III crowned him emperor in Rome in 1220.

Frederick's reign shows an unusual feature of Hohenstaufen rule: the same ruler governed two very different political systems. In Sicily, building on Norman foundations, he developed a relatively centralized monarchy. The Constitutions of Melfi, issued in 1231, drew together royal laws and asserted the crown's control of justice, with salaried officials, royal courts, and royal supervision of castles and fiscal rights. He founded a university at Naples in 1224 to train officials, and he imposed taxation on a scale unknown in Germany. In the kingdom of Sicily, the ruler exercised authority through officials appointed by and answerable to the crown, and the barons of the realm had comparatively little independent jurisdiction.

In Germany, by contrast, Frederick confirmed and extended the rights of the princes. To secure the election of his young son Henry (VII) as king in 1220, he issued a privilege to the ecclesiastical princes. In 1231 his son Henry (VII), governing in Germany, issued the Statutum in favorem principum for the secular princes, and Frederick confirmed the concessions in 1232. These measures confirmed their territorial rights, including their jurisdiction and their control of castles and minting. They have often been read as concessions that weakened the crown, and they certainly recognized the princes' position. They can also be seen as acknowledgments of reality, designed to ensure princely support for the dynasty while the emperor concentrated on Italy. When Henry (VII) rebelled in 1234, Frederick deposed him and later had his younger son Conrad IV elected king. In 1235 at Mainz he promulgated a general peace for the Empire, a landmark in imperial legislation that was issued in both Latin and German.

The court of Frederick II attracted scholars, translators, and poets, and the emperor himself wrote a treatise on falconry and corresponded about natural philosophy. Contemporaries and later writers presented him as a ruler of unusual intellectual curiosity, and some, such as the chronicler Matthew Paris, depicted him as a menace to Christendom. Students should be cautious with both portraits, since they were shaped by the political purposes of those who wrote them. Frederick's crusading career, including the Sixth Crusade of 1228 and 1229, during which he negotiated the return of Jerusalem and other places to Christian control while under papal excommunication, is examined in Chapter 47. For the present chapter, the essential point is that the emperor treated the kingdoms of the Empire pragmatically, using centralized methods where the inherited structures supported them and compromise where they did not.

Section 46.12

Frederick II, the Papacy, and the Final Hohenstaufen Struggle

Relations between Frederick II and the papacy had been strained from the start, and they deteriorated sharply in the 1230s. The papal territories were encircled by Hohenstaufen lands in the north and south, and Frederick's efforts to assert authority in Lombardy renewed the old conflict with the northern communes, which reconstituted the Lombard League in 1226. After the imperial victory at Cortenuova in 1237, Frederick's position in northern Italy appeared strong, but the resistance of cities such as Milan and Brescia continued. Pope Gregory IX excommunicated the emperor in 1239, and a war of propaganda, with letters and manifestos addressed to princes and kings across Europe, accompanied the fighting.

The pope's successor Innocent IV, who fled to Lyon in 1244, summoned a council there in 1245 and declared Frederick deposed, a bold claim that went beyond earlier excommunications. The deposition was not universally accepted, and many German princes and bishops continued to support Frederick, while others elected rival kings. Henry Raspe of Thuringia was elected in 1246 and William of Holland in 1247. The conflict thus spread to Germany, where it intersected with local rivalries and with the ambitions of princes who saw advantage in supporting or opposing the dynasty. Fighting in Italy continued, and the emperor suffered setbacks, including a defeat at Parma in 1248.

Frederick died on 13 December 1250 in southern Italy. His son Conrad IV, already king of Germany, struggled to assert control and died in 1254. In Sicily, Frederick's illegitimate son Manfred took power and was crowned king in 1258. The papacy, determined to prevent Hohenstaufen rule in the south, offered the crown to a French prince, Charles of Anjou, brother of Louis IX of France. Charles defeated Manfred at Benevento in 1266, and in 1268 Conrad IV's young son Conradin, who had come to claim his inheritance, was defeated at Tagliacozzo and executed at Naples. Hohenstaufen dynastic rule in southern Italy ended, and the kingdom passed to the Angevin dynasty, an outcome with long consequences for Mediterranean politics, including the later conflict over Sicily.

This was not merely a military struggle but a transformation of imperial politics. The papacy had demonstrated that it could depose an emperor in theory and help destroy Hohenstaufen rule in southern Italy, but it had done so by relying on French power and by spending its own moral capital. The Empire, for its part, lost its southern base and the rulers who might have reconstructed a stronger monarchy, leaving Germany to princes who had expanded their rights in the course of the conflict. Historians continue to debate whether the Hohenstaufen should be seen as having overreached by trying to combine imperial and Sicilian power or as having been defeated by circumstances that no ruler could have mastered.

Section 46.13

The Great Interregnum and the Problem of Imperial Election

The broader succession crisis began after Frederick II's death in 1250, while the period conventionally called the Great Interregnum is often dated from Conrad IV's death in 1254 or, more narrowly, William of Holland's death in 1256, to Rudolf's election in 1273. Following the deposition of Frederick II and the death of Conrad IV, no ruler obtained general recognition. William of Holland was widely accepted in the Rhineland until his death in 1256, but after that the electors divided. In 1257 one group chose Richard of Cornwall, brother of the king of England, and another selected Alfonso X of Castile. Neither established a durable authority in Germany. Richard visited the Empire several times and Alfonso never traveled there, but both were chosen because their wealth, prestige, or connections could assist electors who had their own interests.

The term interregnum can mislead. Germany did not fall into total anarchy, and many institutions continued to function. Princes governed their territories, courts met, towns maintained their privileges and negotiated their own security, and ecclesiastical lords administered their estates. Regional leagues, such as the Rhenish League of 1254, brought towns and lords together to maintain peace and trade, showing that political cooperation could develop without a king. Violence and usurpation of royal rights and lands certainly increased, and the crown lost property and revenues that would be difficult to recover. But the period also demonstrates the extent to which government had become embedded in local and regional structures.

The Interregnum also clarified the position of the electors. By the mid-thirteenth century a small group of princes, including the archbishops of Mainz, Cologne, and Trier and several secular princes, had emerged as the principal voters in royal elections. The Sachsenspiegel, a Saxon law book compiled in the 1220s and 1230s by Eike von Repgow, already attributes particular rights to a limited set of princes, and by the 1250s and 1260s the practice of a defined college was becoming established. The details of who counted as an elector, including the role of the king of Bohemia, were still being settled. The Golden Bull of 1356, which regularized the system, falls outside the period of this chapter and is examined in the context of later medieval developments, but the formation of an electoral group is one of the principal institutional outcomes of the thirteenth century.

The Interregnum ended in 1273 with the election of Rudolf, count of Habsburg, a count whose formal rank stood below the greatest princes but whose extensive lands in Swabia, Alsace, and what is now Switzerland made him a major power in southwestern Germany. The princes had reasons to avoid a ruler powerful enough to dominate them, while Pope Gregory X pressed for the election of a generally recognized king who could restore order and assist his crusading plans. Rudolf's reign, to 1291, was not marked by a reconstruction of Hohenstaufen power. He instead focused on recovering royal lands and on building a dynastic base. Rudolf's subsequent victory over Ottokar II in 1278 and acquisition of Austria for his dynasty would make the election of 1273 the beginning of the Habsburg territorial rise, a later development treated in Chapter 75 and Chapter 78.

Multiple Perspectives

Living in the Empire: Perspectives on the Imperial Order in the Twelfth and Thirteenth Centuries

They are not quotations from any single named historical actor. The groups did not have equal power, and their perspectives are presented to show different interests rather than equivalent influence.

The emperor: The imperial title brings prestige and legitimacy, but the ruler's practical power depends on travel, on the cooperation of princes, and on resources that are scattered across the Empire. Italy offers revenue and glory but also resistance, and each success there requires men, money, and support from Germany.
A prince-bishop: The bishop is a spiritual leader and a territorial lord, bound to the Church and to the crown. The right to hold regalia and to command a military force strengthens his independence, and the settlement at Worms confirms that his office is not simply a royal gift, though he still needs royal favor to protect his lands.
A secular prince: The prince seeks to consolidate scattered rights into a coherent territory, to secure hereditary succession, and to resist royal interference. He values the Empire as a source of legitimacy and an arena in which to advance his house, but he is wary of any king who might diminish his rights.
A ministerialis: Of unfree origin, he serves a prince or bishop as a castellan, steward, or mounted warrior. His service offers land and rising status, and his descendants may join the lesser nobility, but his position depends on the lord whom he serves.
A member of a northern Italian commune: The citizen sees the commune as a sworn association that protects liberties acquired through generations of effort. He may recognize the emperor's rank but resists the idea that distant officials can tax and judge the city, and local rivalries often matter more to him than the imperial question.
An urban merchant: The merchant requires secure roads, predictable tolls, and legal protection for contracts. He benefits from privileges granted by kings, bishops, and princes, and he supports whichever authority can guarantee peace in the region where he trades.
Section 46.14

Cities, Princes, and the Political Geography of Central Europe

The growth of towns transformed the political geography of the Empire. Imperial cities, later called free imperial cities, were towns that stood directly under the king and that acquired privileges, self-government, and, over time, the right to participate in imperial affairs. They included centers on the Rhine and Main such as Frankfurt, Worms, and Speyer, and southern German towns such as Augsburg and Nuremberg. Other towns lay within territorial principalities, and their lords granted charters and favored the growth of markets. Episcopal cities, such as Cologne, struggled with their bishops over rights of governance, and Cologne's citizens, led by merchant families, gradually won extensive autonomy. Chapter 43 described the commercial revival that supported these communities, and Chapter 54 examines the Hanseatic world that linked northern German towns in the later Middle Ages.

Territorial differences were pronounced. The Rhineland, with its wealthy archbishoprics and commercial towns, was a region of ecclesiastical princes and urban power. Saxony, after the fall of Henry the Lion, was fragmented, with numerous counts and bishops, and colonization eastward by Saxon lords created new territories such as Brandenburg, established by Albert the Bear and his successors. Bavaria, under the Wittelsbach after 1180, became a consolidated duchy. Swabia, the home of the Hohenstaufen, lost its ducal unity after the dynasty's fall and broke into numerous counts and cities. Austria, raised to a duchy in 1156, became a major territorial power under the Babenberg dukes, and later passed to the Habsburgs.

The eastern frontier was a zone of political and settlement expansion. German lords, with the support of bishops and monastic orders, expanded their power over Slavic regions of the Elbe and Oder, and settlers from the west founded villages and towns. This process, known to historians as Ostsiedlung or eastward settlement, was complex, with local Slavic elites, Polish dukes, and other actors participating, and it was not a single conquest. Chapter 35 examined the Christianization of east-central Europe, and the present chapter notes only that the political expansion of the Empire's princes belonged to the larger story of territorial lordship and settlement.

Bohemia held a special position. Its rulers, the Přemyslid dukes, had long been tied to the Empire, and in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries they obtained a royal title that Frederick II confirmed as hereditary in 1212. The king of Bohemia participated in imperial assemblies and, by the thirteenth century, held a place among those entitled to take part in royal elections, though his exact role remained disputed. Bohemia combined integration into the Empire with a high level of internal autonomy, and its development under Ottokar II, who briefly held Austria, Styria, and Carinthia, showed the potential of a powerful lord on the Empire's southeastern edge. His career, which ended in conflict with Rudolf of Habsburg, shows how the paths of Bohemian and Habsburg power would diverge.

Historical Vocabulary
Ministeriales

Ministeriales were administrators, castellans, and mounted warriors who served kings, princes, bishops, and abbeys, and who were originally unfree in legal status. Their service, often as stewards or military retainers, gave them land and trust, and in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries many of them rose into a recognized lower nobility, building castles and founding families. The term is sometimes translated as "serf knights," but this is misleading because they were not agricultural dependents and could wield considerable local power. Ministeriales mattered because they enabled kings and princes to build administrations staffed by dependents rather than by independent nobles, and they were an important element in the territorialization of power. They also illustrate how social categories changed during this period.

Regional Variation

Five Political Landscapes within One Empire

The Rhineland: Dominated by the archbishoprics of Mainz, Cologne, and Trier and by prosperous towns, it was a region of ecclesiastical lordship, river commerce, and urban autonomy, with royal palaces and a heavy royal presence in the twelfth century.
Saxony: Once the Ottonian heartland, it became in the twelfth century the base of the Welf duke Henry the Lion. After 1180 it fragmented into many lordships, while its princes expanded eastward into Slavic lands.
Bavaria and Austria: Bavaria under the Wittelsbach consolidated as a ducal territory, while Austria emerged as a distinct duchy whose rulers developed strong territorial authority and later passed to the Habsburgs.
Northern Italy: A region of self-governing communes, with consular government and podestà, where imperial authority was a framework to be negotiated, not a day-to-day administration.
The ecclesiastical territories: Bishoprics and abbeys formed political units across the Empire, particularly along the Rhine, the Main, and in southern Germany, ruled by prelates chosen by chapters and, to varying degrees, by royal influence.
Section 46.15

A Decentralized Empire: Weakness, Adaptation, or Different Political Order?

By the 1270s the Empire differed significantly from its Ottonian and Salian form and from the stronger monarchies of England and France. English kings had developed royal justice, a system of taxation and, through settlements such as Magna Carta, a negotiated relationship with their subjects, as Chapter 45 explained. French kings, after Philip II and Louis IX, extended direct royal rule over large parts of the realm. In the Empire, by contrast, the ruler's power depended on election by a group of princes, on his own dynastic territory, and on negotiated cooperation with princes, prelates, and cities. Political authority was distributed among the monarch, secular princes, ecclesiastical princes, cities, and other jurisdictions.

Historians once tended to describe this outcome as a failure. In this view, German kings sacrificed their authority to chase imperial glory in Italy and thereby left Germany fragmented and vulnerable, while England and France built states that later became powerful nations. This interpretation reflected the concerns of nineteenth-century nationalists, who judged the medieval past by its contribution to national unification, a perspective that the chapter has attempted to avoid. More recent scholarship stresses that medieval rulers and subjects had no reason to expect a national state, and that the Empire's flexible structure was workable and, in many respects, effective. It provided arbitration among princes, a framework of legitimacy, protection for privileges, and the capacity to coordinate large-scale action when interests converged.

The conclusion that centralization equals success and decentralization equals failure should be rejected. England and France centralized through specific circumstances, including the compactness of their territories, the strength of their ruling families, and, in England's case, a conquest that gave the crown extensive lands. The Empire's circumstances were different: it was larger, more linguistically diverse, dependent on election, and tied to Italy and the papacy. Its decentralization was, in some respects, a result of those conditions, and it also gave the Empire strengths, including resilience when kings were absent or weak. The principalities and cities of the Empire were often well governed, and their economic and cultural life was vigorous.

At the same time, the Empire's structure had costs. The absence of a regular fiscal system limited the crown's ability to wage war or enforce judgments, the competition between dynasties for the crown led to periods of conflict, and the entanglement with Italy and the papacy drew resources away from internal consolidation. The imperial order that emerged by 1273 was a system in which the ruler's influence depended on his dynastic territories and the consent of princes. That arrangement shaped the later development of the Empire, including the formal definition of election procedures in the fourteenth century, the rise of Habsburg and Luxembourg power, and, centuries later, the Reformation conflicts and the settlement of 1648 examined in Chapter 67. This chapter stops at 1273, but the questions it raises about the nature of imperial authority persisted for several centuries.

Historical Debate

Was the Empire Weak, or Simply Decentralized?

The question: Did the involvement of emperors in Italy and the growth of princely power in the High Middle Ages represent a decline in the effectiveness of German government, or did they produce a different and viable political order?

Interpretation A: The Empire as a weakened monarchy. Some historians, particularly in older national traditions, argued that the emperors' Italian ambitions and their concessions to princes, such as the statutes of Frederick II, undermined royal authority and prevented the formation of a unified German state. In this view, the pattern was damaging, and it left Germany politically fragmented compared with western neighbors.
Interpretation B: The Empire as a different political order. Many recent historians emphasize consensus, negotiation, and the effectiveness of regional government. They argue that the Empire's rulers retained substantial authority in practice, that princely consent was an accepted part of governance, and that the comparison with England and France assumes a model of statehood that was not the norm in the thirteenth century. They also point out that centralized outcomes in the west came with their own conflicts and limits.

Evidence: Both views rely on royal charters, chronicles, legislation, and the political outcomes of conflicts. Interpretation A tends to emphasize the grants and concessions made to princes and the loss of royal estates. Interpretation B emphasizes the successful functioning of assemblies, peace legislation, and regional administration, and it relates the Empire's structure to its multi-kingdom composition.

Why it matters: How historians resolve the question affects how the Empire is placed in European history, whether as an exception that failed or as one of several workable medieval models. It also shapes how historians understand the later German past and avoids reading nineteenth-century nationalism back into the Middle Ages. The scholarship is better described as overlapping than as two sharply opposed camps, since few historians now deny either the real costs of fragmentation or the real capacities of regional governments.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Imperial Order from Otto I to Rudolf of Habsburg

What Changed

  • The king's capacity to appoint bishops and dukes declined, while the princes acquired hereditary titles and organized collective influence
  • Territorial principalities developed administrations, courts, and fiscal rights
  • Italian communes and German cities emerged as political actors
  • The Hohenstaufen connection to Sicily was created and then destroyed, and the Habsburgs rose as a territorial dynasty

What Stayed the Same

  • The ruler was chosen with the consent of leading princes and ruled through assemblies and personal relationships
  • The imperial title remained linked to Rome and to papal coronation
  • Bishops and abbots continued to be territorial rulers and counselors
  • The Empire remained a composite of kingdoms without a single capital or uniform administration

Who experienced change most directly: The princes and the cities experienced the greatest gains in autonomy, while the crown experienced the greatest loss of control over appointments and lands. Who experienced greater continuity: Most rural inhabitants continued to live under local lords and church institutions, with changes affecting them mainly through the shifting boundaries of lordship.

Why the pattern matters: The combination of change and continuity shows that the Empire was a dynamic system that adjusted to circumstances, a conclusion that guards against both nostalgia for an imagined strong medieval Germany and the assumption that decentralization was a simple decline.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Holy Roman Empire from its formation under the Ottonians to the election of Rudolf of Habsburg in 1273, with the aim of explaining a political order that was polycentric, elective, and territorially fragmented, yet durable and meaningful. It began with the East Frankish kingdom, whose rulers, Henry I and Otto I, built a monarchy that depended on agreements with powerful dukes and on the service of bishops and abbots. Otto's victory at the Lechfeld in 955 and his imperial coronation in 962 linked the northern kingdom to Italy and the papacy and created the medieval Empire, a name that developed gradually over the following centuries.

The Empire was a composite of three kingdoms, Germany, Italy, and Burgundy, whose ruler's authority and practical recognition varied across the three kingdoms, with rule in each resting on different relationships with regional elites, and who became emperor only through papal coronation. It had no permanent capital, and its ruler governed largely by traveling among palaces, bishoprics, and abbeys, holding assemblies, and securing the consent of princes. Kingship was neither purely hereditary nor freely elective, and dynastic continuity under the Ottonians, Salians, and Hohenstaufen coexisted with the repeated assertion of the princes' role in choosing the king.

The imperial Church was central to this order. Bishops and abbots held large territories and served as counselors, hosts, and military contributors, and they remained major rulers after the Concordat of Worms in 1122, which changed the procedures of investiture but not the underlying political roles of ecclesiastical lords. The chapter emphasized that the Investiture Conflict itself belongs to Chapter 44, and it concentrated on the institutional consequences of the settlement: greater independence for cathedral chapters, a more negotiated relationship between rulers and prelates, and the persistence of ecclesiastical principalities.

A second major development was the territorialization of power. Dukes, counts, margraves, and ecclesiastical princes consolidated rights of jurisdiction, castles, tolls, and mints into coherent territories, using ministeriales as officials and founding towns as sources of revenue. The chapter stressed that this decentralization did not mean an absence of government, since principalities developed effective administration at the regional level, and since ordinary inhabitants experienced dense and overlapping authorities.

Frederick Barbarossa's reign marked the most sustained twelfth-century effort to reinforce imperial authority. He cooperated with the German princes, expanded royal lands, and deposed Henry the Lion in 1180 with the support of other princes, an outcome that strengthened the princely order as a whole. In Italy, his assertion of regalia at Roncaglia in 1158 provoked the resistance of northern communes, which formed the Lombard League, defeated him at Legnano in 1176, and secured recognition of their rights at Constance in 1183. The Italian conflict shows that imperial claims were negotiable and that Italy was a region of overlapping jurisdictions.

The Hohenstaufen union with the kingdom of Sicily, achieved by Henry VI in 1194, transformed the geopolitics of Europe. It gave the dynasty a rich Mediterranean base and alarmed the papacy. Frederick II, who governed both Sicily and Germany, used relatively centralized methods in Sicily and compromise in Germany, issuing the Constitutions of Melfi in 1231 and confirming princely rights in 1220 and 1232, building on a statute issued in 1231 by his son Henry (VII). His long struggle with the papacy, which included excommunication and a declaration of deposition in 1245, ended with his death in 1250 and the destruction of Hohenstaufen power in southern Italy by 1268.

The succession crisis after 1250 and the Great Interregnum that followed, to 1273, revealed both the Empire's vulnerabilities and its resilience. Contested elections of foreign candidates showed the absence of a generally recognized ruler, yet princes, cities, and ecclesiastical lords continued to govern, and regional leagues such as the Rhenish League maintained peace. The period clarified the role of a limited group of electors and led to the election of Rudolf of Habsburg, whose reign began the rise of a territorial dynasty. The chapter also surveyed the political geography of central Europe, including imperial cities, regional differences, the eastern frontier, and the special position of Bohemia.

The chapter concluded by comparing the Empire with England and France and rejecting the equation of centralization with success. The Empire distributed authority among the monarch, secular and ecclesiastical princes, cities, and other jurisdictions, and this structure provided arbitration, legitimacy, and regional government, while also imposing costs in fiscal capacity and in the absorption of resources by Italian and papal conflicts. Its history cannot be understood as an incomplete German state, and it should be treated as a distinctive medieval political form whose later course, examined in Chapter 67 and Chapter 78, was contingent and not predetermined.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that medieval Europe produced more than one successful way of organizing political life. Where Chapter 45 followed the consolidation of stronger monarchies, Chapter 46 has followed an empire whose rulers governed through election, travel, negotiation, and the cooperation of bishops, princes, and cities. Seen on its own terms, this order was not an unfinished version of a modern state. It was a working political system that arbitrated disputes, protected privileges, and coordinated action across a wide and varied territory.

The chapter also illuminates larger processes in the High Middle Ages. The growth of principalities, the rise of self-governing cities, the changing role of bishops, and the entanglement of rulers with Italy and the papacy were not unique to the Empire, but they interacted there in a distinctive way. The struggle with the papacy, culminating in the fall of the Hohenstaufen, altered the balance of power in Italy and the Mediterranean, and the Interregnum clarified the powers of the electors and opened the way for dynastic territorial strategies such as that of the Habsburgs.

Within Volume II, the Empire contributes an important qualification to any narrative of medieval convergence. The same century that saw English and French kings extend their administrative reach saw the Empire's rulers adjust to a distribution of authority, and both developments belong to the history of Latin Christendom. Chapter 47 now turns to the crusading movement, in which emperors and German princes took part alongside the papacy and the kings of western Europe, and where the political structures examined here shaped the conduct of religious war.

Finally, the chapter has argued for caution about retrospective judgment. The later history of Germany, including unification in the nineteenth century, has often led historians to ask why the medieval Empire failed to become a unified state. A more productive question is how it worked, who benefited from it, and what possibilities it held open. The answers help explain why central Europe developed as a region of many authorities and why its later political forms, examined in Part XII and Part XV, grew from the inheritance of the medieval Empire without being determined by it.
