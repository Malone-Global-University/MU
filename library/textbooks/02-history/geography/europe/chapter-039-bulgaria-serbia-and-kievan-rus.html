Bulgaria, Serbia, and Kievan Rus

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

Rulers who bore Turkic titles carved their boasts in Greek, a prince on the Dnieper was baptized in the shadow of Byzantine diplomacy, and a Serbian ruler's son became a monk on Mount Athos before returning to organize an autocephalous Serbian church. Each of these political worlds borrowed heavily from Constantinople, and none of them became a copy of it.

In the middle of the tenth century, the Byzantine emperor Constantine VII Porphyrogenitus, or "born in the purple," had a handbook of statecraft prepared for his son, now known by its Latin title De Administrando Imperio. Among its many topics is a description of how the Rus, a warrior and merchant elite based around Kyiv, gathered hollowed tree-trunk boats each spring, floated them down the Dnieper past a series of dangerous rapids, and made their way along the northern shore of the Black Sea to Constantinople. The same handbook describes the Serbs as a Slavic people settled in the western Balkans, and it discusses the Bulgarians as neighbors who had recently been, in the emperor's own lifetime, a menace to his capital. A single Byzantine text thus placed three emerging political communities in view at once, and it did so from the perspective of a court that regarded all of them as potential partners, clients, or threats.

This chapter studies those three communities as political histories, and it asks what rulers, aristocracies, churches, and merchants actually did with the religious and cultural resources examined in Chapter 38. That chapter traced the missionary activity of Cyril and Methodius and their disciples, the creation of Slavonic literacy and liturgy, and the conversion negotiations of the Bulgarian ruler Boris. Chapter 39 begins where those developments become political. It follows the Bulgarian state from its formation on the lower Danube through its imperial peak under Simeon and its subjugation by Basil II in 1018. It follows the Serbian lands, which did not yet form a single state, from fragmentary communities described by Byzantine sources through the consolidation under Stefan Nemanja and his sons by the early thirteenth century. And it follows Rus from its origins in a network of river routes through the baptism of Vladimir, the reign of Yaroslav, and the political fragmentation that characterized the later eleventh and twelfth centuries.

The chapter's chronological span is therefore long, from the formation of the Bulgarian khanate in 681 to about 1219, when Sava secured an autocephalous Serbian archbishopric, with glances forward to the Mongol conquest of the Rus lands in the 1230s and 1240s. Its geographic span runs from the Adriatic highlands to the Volga, and from the Baltic to the Black Sea. The three political worlds differed greatly in origin, structure, and fortune. Bulgaria was founded by a steppe confederation that merged with Slavic populations, and it grew into a rival empire to Byzantium before being absorbed into it. The Serbian lands were mountainous, divided among competing dynasties, and pulled between Constantinople, the Adriatic cities, Hungary, and papal Rome. Rus was a polity of rivers and towns, built from trade, tribute, and dynastic rule, with a Scandinavian military elite that was gradually Slavicized in language and custom.

What these communities shared was a relationship with Byzantium that mixed dependence, rivalry, and selective borrowing. All three developed important political and ecclesiastical institutions within the Byzantine-Orthodox sphere, though the Serbian lands also maintained durable Latin Christian institutions and ties with Rome and the Adriatic. They took over titles, court ceremony, architecture, law, and artistic forms, and intermarried with imperial families. They also fought Byzantium repeatedly and negotiated with it from strength, and they sometimes sought recognition from Rome as a counterweight. They never became uniform. The chapter's central argument is that "Orthodox Slavic Europe" was not one political civilization but a family of connected, distinct, and competing polities.

The evidence is uneven, and students should be prepared for that. Bulgaria is documented by Byzantine chronicles, Greek inscriptions carved by its own rulers, archaeological excavation at Pliska and Preslav, and Slavonic literary works. Early Serbian history rests on a very small body of evidence, including Constantine VII's handbook, later hagiography, a problematic and disputed medieval chronicle associated with Duklja, and monastic foundations. Rus is dominated by one great narrative, the Primary Chronicle, compiled in Kyiv in the early twelfth century, supported by treaties, Byzantine and Arabic reports, coin hoards, and excavated trading settlements. Each body of evidence has its own distortions, and every section below asks who produced the evidence and why.

Finally, the chapter fits into its Part and Volume in a specific way. Chapter 38 explained how Christianity and Slavonic literacy spread. Chapter 40 will examine the institutional and theological division between Latin and Greek Christendom, and Chapter 41 will turn to the Fourth Crusade, the Ottomans, and the end of Byzantium. Chapter 39 supplies the political bridge between them, showing that the Orthodox world of eastern and southeastern Europe was not merely an object of Byzantine missionary activity but a set of active states with their own histories, and it prepares the ground for the argument of Volume II that medieval Europe cannot be reduced to Latin Christendom alone.

  3. Analyze the causes and consequences of the Byzantine conquest of Bulgaria between 971 and 1018, distinguishing military, fiscal, and ecclesiastical outcomes.

Historical Context

By the late seventh century, the Roman Empire ruled from Constantinople had lost most of its Balkan hinterland. The Danube frontier, studied in Chapter 27 and Chapter 28, had given way during the sixth and seventh centuries to Slavic and Avar settlement and raiding. Slavic-speaking groups, organized in small and shifting communities that Byzantine authors called sklaviniai, occupied much of the peninsula, while Byzantium clung to the coasts, to Thessaloniki, to Constantinople, and to scattered inland strongholds. Chapter 36 and Chapter 37 traced how the empire survived this contraction, reorganized its provinces into military districts called themes, and slowly recovered Balkan territory after the eighth century. The political communities of this chapter emerged in the spaces left by that contraction and recovery.

North of the Black Sea, a different system was forming. The Khazar Khaganate, a Turkic steppe power whose ruling elite, according to tradition, adopted Judaism, controlled trade and tribute across the southern Russian steppe from the seventh century until its decline in the tenth. To the north, forest zones were inhabited by Slavic, Finnic, and Baltic populations. Scandinavian traders and warriors, moving through the Baltic and up the rivers of the east European plain, connected these forests to the wealthy markets of the Abbasid caliphate and Constantinople. The geography of this region, introduced in Chapter 2, shaped everything that followed: the forest-steppe frontier, the rivers flowing toward the Baltic, the Black Sea, and the Caspian, and the portages linking them created a corridor of exchange rather than a barrier.

Byzantium's own policy toward its neighbors was shaped by limited manpower and abundant diplomatic skill. The court used titles, subsidies, trade privileges, marriage alliances, baptism, and the offer of imperial recognition to manage peoples it could not always defeat. Christianization was a central instrument, as Chapter 38 explained, but conversion also carried political risks for Constantinople, since a Christian ruler could press claims to imperial rank or ecclesiastical independence. In the ninth and tenth centuries, the empire was also managing conflict with Muslim powers in the east and later enjoying a military resurgence under the Macedonian dynasty, whose eastern campaigns and Balkan priorities competed for resources.

Latin Christendom was also present in this world. Rome sent missionaries, claimed jurisdiction over Illyricum, and was approached by Balkan rulers who wished to bargain with Constantinople. The kingdom of Hungary, formed after the Magyars settled in the Carpathian Basin as discussed in Chapter 32 and Chapter 35, became a neighbor and rival in the west and north of the Balkans. Along the Adriatic, Dalmatian towns and Venice moved between Latin and Byzantine orbits. The picture is therefore not of an Orthodox world isolated from the Latin West. It is of a zone of overlapping influences in which Byzantium was the strongest but not the only cultural, ecclesiastical, and political reference point.

The historian's materials are varied but fragmentary. Byzantine historians such as Theophanes, the continuators of his chronicle, Leo the Deacon, and John Skylitzes in the late eleventh century describe Bulgaria and Rus from outside. Slavonic writers produced saints' lives, sermons, and translations. Greek inscriptions, seals, coins, and the imposing ruins of Pliska and Preslav give independent material evidence for Bulgaria. For Rus, Arabic geographers and travelers such as Ibn Fadlan, Scandinavian runic inscriptions, and excavations at Staraya Ladoga, Gnezdovo, Novgorod, and Kyiv supplement the Primary Chronicle. Later periods produced their own memory of these centuries, and modern national movements in the nineteenth century projected ethnic and linguistic unity back onto medieval polities that did not think of themselves in such terms.

Section 39.1

Southeastern and Eastern Europe after the Slavic Conversions

The world examined in this chapter lay between three larger systems. To the south and southeast stood the Byzantine Empire, still the largest Christian state in Europe and the principal source of titles, law, liturgy, and artistic models. To the west lay the Latin Christian kingdoms and the papacy, whose interests in Illyricum, Dalmatia, and Pannonia repeatedly overlapped with those of Constantinople. To the east and northeast stretched the open grasslands of the Pontic steppe, a corridor discussed in Chapter 2 along which Turkic and other pastoral peoples, including Khazars, Pechenegs, and later Cumans, moved, traded, and fought. Within these three systems, the Danube, the Balkan mountains, the Dinaric highlands, the Dnieper and its tributaries, and the northern shore of the Black Sea formed the stage on which Bulgaria, the Serbian lands, and Rus developed.

The Balkan Peninsula presented rulers with a fragmented geography. The Balkan range, called Haimos in Greek sources, divided the lower Danube plain from Thrace and the Aegean approaches. The Dinaric highlands of the west, with their karst valleys and narrow river corridors, favored local lordship and made unified rule difficult. The Morava and Vardar valleys provided north to south corridors connecting the Danube to Thessaloniki and the Aegean. Along the Adriatic coast, Dalmatian cities and offshore islands maintained Roman and later Byzantine traditions and traded with Italy. Control of these corridors and passes, rather than of vast territory, often mattered most to medieval rulers, and the relative scarcity of large cities in the interior meant that power depended on fortified residences, monasteries, and the loyalty of regional elites.

Eastern Europe's geography was different. The East European Plain offered long river systems, thick forests in the north, and open forest-steppe and steppe in the south. Rivers such as the Dnieper, Volkhov, and Volga, linked by portages, joined the Baltic region to the Black and Caspian Seas. Political authority along these rivers rested on control of trading stations, tribute collection, and the ability to field mobile armed retinues, rather than on the control of agricultural land alone. The same plain that facilitated the movement of merchants also exposed the settled lands to steppe raiders, and the forest-steppe frontier became one of the defining features of Rus history.

Historians approach all three political worlds with a shared caution. Modern Bulgarian, Serbian, Russian, Ukrainian, and Belarusian national histories each claim these medieval polities as ancestors, and nineteenth and twentieth century political movements often projected modern ethnic and linguistic unity backward. This chapter treats the medieval states as historical entities in their own right. The population of the first Bulgarian state combined Turkic-speaking Bulgar elites and Slavic-speaking majorities, with a Greek-speaking and Christian Roman population in some districts. The early Serbian lands were home to Slavic communities with distinct political leaders and shifting alliances. The Rus elite spoke Old Norse at first and Slavic later, ruled over Slavic, Finnic, and Baltic populations, and cannot be identified with any single modern nation. Words such as "Bulgarian," "Serb," and "Rus" meant different things at different moments, sometimes designating a ruling group and sometimes a whole territory or people, and the sections that follow try to preserve that fluidity.

Historical Vocabulary
Khan, Tsar, Knyaz, and Župan: Titles of Rule

The titles used by rulers in these three worlds reveal their sources of legitimacy. Khan (and the related khagan) is the conventional modern rendering of a Turkic steppe title, and this chapter follows convention in calling the early Bulgar rulers khans. Their own Greek inscriptions, however, attest forms such as kanasubigi and the Greek title archon, and how those forms relate to a reconstructed khan is debated. Khagan was the title of the Khazar rulers, and some sources also applied it to Rus leaders. Tsar, from Latin Caesar, became the Slavic word for emperor, applied by Simeon of Bulgaria to claim imperial rank, though Byzantium resisted recognizing it. Knyaz (often rendered "prince") was the Slavic title used for Rus rulers, possibly derived from a Germanic root, and veliky knyaz ("grand prince") emerged to distinguish the senior ruler from other Rurikid princes. Župan designated a Serbian regional lord, and veliki župan ("grand župan") the senior one among them.

These terms matter because titles were political claims. A Bulgarian ruler who called himself "tsar" challenged Byzantium's monopoly on the imperial title, while a Serbian župan accepted a lesser rank within a Byzantine hierarchy. Modern translations, which often render everything as "king" or "prince," can conceal these differences, so students should always ask which title was used, by whom, and who recognized it.

Section 39.2

The Formation of the Bulgarian State

The Bulgars were a Turkic-speaking steppe people, organized in tribal groupings under khans, who in the seventh century occupied territory north of the Black Sea. Pressure from the Khazars and the fragmentation of an earlier steppe polity led groups of them to move west. Under a leader whom Byzantine and Bulgarian traditions call Asparukh, one group crossed the lower Danube in the 670s and settled in the region between the river and the Balkan range, an area already inhabited by Slavic communities and containing remnants of the old Roman provincial population. A Byzantine campaign against the newcomers failed, and in 681 the emperor Constantine IV acknowledged the settlement and, in Byzantine terms, paid a form of tribute. The date is conventionally treated as the founding of the Bulgarian state, though the processes of settlement and political consolidation took generations.

What formed in the following century was a political community of two principal populations. The Bulgar warrior aristocracy, bearing titles such as khan, boila, and bagain, held military and judicial authority, while Slavic communities, often organized by tribe or district, provided the agricultural majority and, increasingly, the base for the state's military and fiscal needs. Slavic groups appear in the Byzantine sources as allies, subjects, and sometimes rebels. Over time, linguistic and cultural fusion occurred, so that by the ninth century the Bulgar elite was increasingly Slavic-speaking. The early state also dealt with Byzantine subjects and prisoners who were settled in Bulgarian territory, and the rulers made extensive use of Greek in inscriptions, showing the administrative influence of the empire even before Christianization.

The capital at Pliska, a vast fortified enclosure of earth and later stone ramparts, containing palaces, a monumental stone building, and a large open space, reflects the combination of steppe and Roman traditions. Excavation has revealed a plan designed for a mobile military court with ceremonial structures, combined with stone construction on a scale that required Byzantine-trained builders. The cliff relief at Madara, depicting a horseman with accompanying inscriptions in Greek, is often attributed to the early eighth century and is another sign of a ruling elite that combined steppe symbolism with the written conventions of the empire. Because the Bulgar elite did not write narrative histories in this period, these monuments and inscriptions are the main source for how rulers presented themselves.

The relationship with Byzantium swung between cooperation and war. Khan Tervel intervened in Constantinople in 705 to restore the exiled emperor Justinian II, receiving a high Roman court title in return, and he later helped relieve the great Arab siege of the capital in 717 and 718. Later eighth century rulers fought repeated wars with Emperor Constantine V, whose campaigns reflected both imperial recovery in the Balkans and the instability of Bulgarian succession, in which khans were frequently deposed or killed. The state's fortunes revived under Khan Krum around 803. He expanded westward and northward, taking advantage of the collapse of Avar power in the Carpathian Basin, and in 811 he destroyed an imperial army that had entered Bulgaria. The emperor Nikephoros I was killed, and Byzantine sources, writing with evident shock, record that Krum had his skull fashioned into a drinking cup, a story that may reflect steppe custom as well as hostile elaboration. Krum then took Adrianople and threatened Constantinople itself before his death in 814.

His successor Omurtag concluded a thirty year peace with Byzantium in 816 and turned to consolidation. He built extensively at Pliska, left inscriptions in Greek proclaiming his rule as granted by God and detailing his building works, and negotiated border arrangements in Thrace. A series of anti-Christian measures, including the execution of prisoners who refused to abandon their faith, shows that Bulgarian rulers regarded the Christian communities within their realm as potentially disloyal, and also that the religious future of the state had become a real political question. The ninth century Bulgarian khanate was therefore a territorial state with administrative reach, a hybrid elite, and a clear sense of its rivalry with Constantinople. Its next transformation, examined in the following section, would come through religion.

Section 39.3

Boris-Michael, Christian Monarchy, and the Bulgarian Church

Chapter 38 explained how Boris, who ruled from 852, accepted baptism around 864 to 866, with the Byzantine emperor Michael III as godfather, taking the Christian name Michael. This section does not retell that story. It asks what Christianization meant for the Bulgarian state. The decision brought immediate political benefits, since a Christian ruler could seek recognition as an equal in the community of Christian monarchs. It also brought dangers. A church under Constantinople might become an instrument of Byzantine influence, and the Bulgarian aristocracy, whose authority was bound up with older steppe religious traditions, might resist. Boris acted with deliberate care on both fronts.

He suppressed an aristocratic revolt in the mid 860s, which Latin and Byzantine sources describe as a pagan reaction and which was met with severe reprisals. He then pursued a strategy of negotiating with both Rome and Constantinople for an autonomous ecclesiastical organization. Around 866 he sent questions to Pope Nicholas I about Christian practice and church organization, and the pope's lengthy reply survives as evidence of how Latin Christianity attempted to shape the new church. Latin missionaries entered Bulgaria. But the papacy would not grant Boris the autonomous archbishop he wanted, and in 870 a council at Constantinople determined that Bulgaria belonged to the Byzantine ecclesiastical sphere, with an archbishop sent from the Greek church. The decision was shaped by a larger rivalry between Rome and Constantinople over jurisdiction, which Chapter 40 examines as part of the growing division of Christendom.

The question of language proved decisive. A Greek clergy ministering in Greek to a Slavic-speaking population risked leaving Christianity an elite or foreign faith. After 885 or 886, when disciples of Cyril and Methodius expelled from Moravia arrived in Bulgaria, Boris found a different resource. Clement and Naum, as Chapter 38 described, helped build a Slavonic-language church. Clement worked in the southwest, around Ohrid, training clergy and teaching, while Naum worked in Preslav. This effort gave Bulgaria a native-language clergy and a literary culture that could support a Christian monarchy and also reduce its reliance on Greek priests. In 889, Boris abdicated and retired to a monastery, a pattern that would recur in Orthodox Slavic rulers. His son Vladimir attempted a return to older practices, and in 893 Boris left his monastery and removed Vladimir, and Simeon became ruler. Later tradition and modern historical reconstruction have associated these events with an assembly at Preslav and with the growing replacement of Greek by Slavonic in church and court culture, and with Preslav's rise as the principal capital. The political and linguistic transformation was real, but the evidence does not securely document a single council decree accomplishing all of these changes.

Christianization thus transformed the Bulgarian monarchy in several ways. It supplied a theory of kingship in which the ruler was God's appointed protector of his people, adopted from Byzantine practice but fitted to local needs. It created a church that held land, trained scribes, and could support the state's administration. It gave the ruler a new set of ceremonies and building programs, including churches and monasteries, replacing the old steppe cult sites. And it connected Bulgaria to the wider Christian world while leaving it room to bargain. The accompanying cultural transformation was gradual and uneven. Archaeological and literary evidence suggests that older customs persisted among parts of the population for generations, and the rural Slavic majority probably experienced Christianity first through local priests and monasteries rather than through court ceremonies.

Historical Vocabulary
Autocephaly

Autocephaly, from Greek words meaning "self-headed," describes a church whose head, whether archbishop or patriarch, is chosen within that church and not appointed by another ecclesiastical authority. In medieval Orthodoxy, the status of a church was tied to recognition by the Patriarchate of Constantinople, and the title of its head (archbishop, patriarch, or metropolitan) signaled rank. The Bulgarian rulers sought autocephaly for political reasons, because a church answerable to a foreign patriarch could undercut royal authority. A Bulgarian archbishop recognized in the 870 settlement, a patriarch recognized in 927, and the Archbishopric of Ohrid preserved after 1018 mark stages in that effort.

The concept matters throughout this chapter, since the Serbian church obtained autocephaly in 1219 and the Rus church remained a metropolitanate of Constantinople. Modern equivalents can mislead, because autocephaly was a negotiated and sometimes reversible status granted by a particular authority at a particular moment, not a permanent national right.

Section 39.4

Simeon and Imperial Bulgaria

Simeon ruled from 893 to 927, the longest and, for Bulgaria, the most ambitious reign of the early period. Byzantine sources, which are hostile, report that he had been educated in Constantinople and had studied rhetoric and Greek letters, and they were uncomfortable with a ruler who knew their culture so well. His reign combined four related projects: the expansion of Bulgarian territory in the Balkans, the elevation of his title to that of emperor, the creation of a Slavonic literary culture at Preslav, and the reduction of Byzantine influence over his church.

The first war with Byzantium, in 894 to 896, began over trade. Byzantine merchants had moved a market for Bulgarian goods from Constantinople to Thessaloniki, with increased duties, and Simeon, whose realm benefited from the trade, invaded Thrace. The imperial government responded by calling on the Magyars, then living north of the Black Sea, to attack Bulgaria from the northeast. Simeon, after initial success, was defeated by the Magyars and then, in 896, won a major victory at Boulgarophygon in Thrace over the Byzantine army. The peace that followed demonstrated that Bulgaria could not be ignored. A later crisis came in 913, when the emperor Alexander died and left a child heir, Constantine VII, under a regency. Simeon marched on Constantinople, and the patriarch Nicholas Mystikos, in negotiations, crowned him in a ceremony outside the walls. Byzantine writers later insisted this was a lesser rite, not an imperial coronation, but Simeon seems to have regarded it as acknowledgment of imperial status and proposed a marriage alliance between his daughter and the boy emperor.

The proposal failed. The regency was overthrown, and Romanos Lekapenos, an admiral and commander, became first guardian and soon co-emperor of Constantine VII. Simeon renewed the war, and in 917 his army crushed a Byzantine force at Anchialos near the Black Sea coast, in one of the largest Byzantine defeats of the century. He pressed his attack for years but could not take Constantinople, which lay behind great walls and was supplied by sea, where Bulgaria had no fleet. In 924, Simeon met Romanos in a personal conference at the walls, and the encounter reflected a stalemate. Simeon continued to use the title that Byzantium refused to accept, which Slavic sources give as "tsar of the Bulgarians and Romans," and his church leaders probably used the title of patriarch, though the date of that rank's recognition is debated and Byzantium granted it only after his death.

His expansion westward also affected neighboring Slavic polities. Simeon deposed Serbian princes who had shifted allegiance toward Byzantium and attempted to absorb the Serbian lands, as discussed below in Section 39.6. A campaign against the Croats, led by King Tomislav, appears to have ended in a Bulgarian defeat in 926, though the details come from fragmentary and later evidence. The long wars drained the state, and Simeon's death in 927 left the realm financially strained and vulnerable to attack from Magyars and Pechenegs.

Culturally, Simeon's reign is often called a golden age. At Preslav, Simeon rebuilt the capital on a monumental scale, with a circular church decorated with ceramic tiles and marble, known from excavations as the "Golden Church," and a palace complex. Scholars, including John the Exarch and Constantine of Preslav, translated Greek theology, history, and scientific texts into Slavonic and produced original sermons and poetry. Anthologies of excerpts from Greek authors made for the ruler show the court's aims of building a Christian Slavonic literary culture that could support royal authority. Historians dispute how closely the Preslav movement imitated Byzantine models, and how independent it was, but most agree that the combination of political ambition and literary effort made Bulgaria, for about a generation, a rival to Constantinople in prestige. Its decline was equally rapid, which shows how dependent the state's strength was on the person of its ruler and on continued military success.

Section 39.5

Peter I, Samuel, and the Byzantine Conquest of Bulgaria

Simeon's son Peter, who ruled from 927 to 969, chose peace. He concluded a treaty with Romanos Lekapenos in 927, married the emperor's granddaughter Maria, and received recognition of his imperial title and of a Bulgarian patriarchate, a remarkable concession. For four decades Bulgaria enjoyed stability and prosperity, but the cost was that the state became increasingly bound to Byzantium by marriage, trade, and ecclesiastical ties. Peter's reign also saw internal strains. Magyar raids across the Danube, Serbian independence under Časlav, and the pressure of landless peasants and monastic reform all shaped a period of uncertainty. A contemporary priest named Cosmas described the spread of a dualist heresy later called Bogomilism, which rejected much of the church's material structure and ritual, and criticized the state of the church and the aristocracy. Historians read his sermon as evidence of both a religious movement and social discontent, though the extent of Bogomil organization in this period is debated.

The balance changed when the Byzantine emperor Nikephoros II Phokas, preparing to concentrate on the east, refused to pay the customary subsidy and, around 966, instead invited the Rus prince Sviatoslav of Kyiv to attack Bulgaria. Sviatoslav invaded in 968, and again in 969, and occupied much of northeastern Bulgaria. The invitation backfired. Sviatoslav intended to remain, reportedly preferring the Danubian region to Kyiv, and threatened Byzantine Thrace itself. Emperor John Tzimiskes, who came to power in 969, drove him back, defeated him at Dorostolon (Silistra) on the Danube in 971, and brought eastern Bulgaria under direct Byzantine administration. The Bulgarian ruler Boris II, Peter's son, was taken to Constantinople and deprived of his insignia. In the east, the Byzantine government established a military province and extended its fiscal and ecclesiastical system.

But Bulgaria did not end in 971. In the west, a family of provincial commanders, known collectively as the Cometopuli after their father, a count named Nicholas, rejected Byzantine rule. The brothers David, Moses, Aaron, and Samuel led resistance, and Samuel emerged as leader, ruling at first in the name of the captive Boris II's surviving relatives and then, after about 997, as tsar. His state centered on the lakes of Prespa and Ohrid, in what is now North Macedonia, Albania, and northern Greece, and it extended at its height from the Adriatic to the Black Sea hinterland, including parts of Thessaly, Epirus, and Serbian lands. Church leadership was located at Ohrid, which became the seat of the Bulgarian patriarchate in the west. Historians debate whether to call this state the continuation of Simeon's Bulgaria or a new western Bulgarian state with a different base. The Byzantine sources call it Bulgarian, as did its rulers, and it drew on the Slavonic Christian culture of the earlier state.

Samuel's state fought Basil II, who had come into his own as emperor after suppressing aristocratic revolts and who turned his attention to the Balkans around 986. In that year a Byzantine expedition into the Balkan passes toward Sofia ended in defeat at the pass known as Trajan's Gates, after which Basil is said to have resolved never again to underestimate the Bulgarians. In the following decades, he conducted methodical campaigns, taking fortresses, devastating territory, and wearing down the Bulgarian armies. On 29 July 1014, at the pass of Kleidion near the Belasitsa mountains, Basil's forces trapped Samuel's army. Later Byzantine tradition, recorded generations after the event, said that Basil blinded most of the captured soldiers, leaving a one-eyed man to guide each hundred back, and that Samuel died of shock at the sight in October 1014. The sources give very large numbers, and modern historians treat both the figures and the story with caution, though they accept that harsh treatment of prisoners occurred.

Samuel's successors held out until 1018, when the remaining Bulgarian nobles accepted imperial terms. Basil II's settlement was pragmatic. He integrated Bulgarian aristocrats into the Byzantine hierarchy, awarding titles and offices, and kept the Bulgarian lands under a system of provinces. He did not abolish the church at Ohrid but reduced its rank, making it an autocephalous archbishopric subordinate in dignity to the patriarchate of Constantinople, with an archbishop who in practice was soon appointed from Greek clergy. Taxes were set in kind rather than coin in some districts, reflecting the less monetized economy. The conquest ended the first Bulgarian state and brought the Balkan frontier of the empire back to the Danube for the first time in centuries. But Byzantine rule was not uniformly welcomed, and a major rebellion under Peter Delyan in 1040 and 1041 showed the resentment of fiscal changes and cultural dominance. Bulgarian independence would be restored in 1185 and 1186, as part of a later transformation that belongs to Chapter 41 and is not narrated here.

Section 39.6

Early Serbian Political Communities

The early Serbian lands present a very different historical problem from Bulgaria. There was no single capital, no continuous dynasty over several centuries, and no body of royal inscriptions or court literature from the ninth and tenth centuries. The principal contemporary source is Constantine VII's handbook, compiled in the middle of the tenth century, which includes a section on the Serbs and neighboring Slavic peoples. According to this account, the Serbs had been settled in the western Balkans in the time of the emperor Heraclius in the seventh century, accepted baptism, and were governed by archons, or princes, who were clients of the empire. Modern historians treat the account as a mixture of older tradition and the court's own diplomatic assumptions, including the idea that the emperor distributed lands to peoples. Archaeological and linguistic evidence suggests a gradual and complex Slavic settlement rather than a single organized migration.

Constantine describes several distinct communities. Raška, in the upper Ibar valley, was the heartland of the group the emperor called Serbs. Neighboring regions included Zahumlje, around the Neretva and the southern Dalmatian coast, Travunija near modern Trebinje and Dubrovnik, Zeta and the coastal region of Duklja around Lake Skadar, and the Narentines, whose maritime power along the central Adriatic made them a threat to Venice. Bosnia appears as a distinct and smaller region in the upper reaches of the river that bears its name. Each had its own leaders and local connections. The distinction matters because the history of "Serbia" in the early period is in fact the history of several communities whose later union was far from inevitable, and whose rulers often fought each other as often as they resisted outsiders.

Political life in these communities was shaped by geography and by the three powers around them. The mountainous interior favored local chieftains and hill fortresses, with limited towns. Byzantium maintained influence by conferring titles, giving gifts, and offering baptism and protection, while from the north and east Bulgaria demanded tribute or loyalty. The mid ninth century ruler Vlastimir, according to Constantine, repelled a Bulgarian invasion under Presian, and his descendants held power for a time. In the early tenth century, Simeon of Bulgaria intervened repeatedly, deposing princes who leaned toward Byzantium and, according to Constantine's account, carrying off Serbian elites and annexing the territory. Časlav, who had spent years in Bulgarian custody or exile, returned after Simeon's death in 927 with Byzantine support and rebuilt a Serbian principality for some decades, extending his influence over neighboring communities. His death, in circumstances that sources record only vaguely, and later conflict with Magyar power ended this consolidation.

Religious affiliation was equally complex. Christianity probably reached these communities from several directions, including Byzantine missionaries, Latin clergy from Dalmatian towns, and, from the later ninth century, Slavonic clergy from the schools around Ohrid. The Dalmatian coast was within the Latin sphere, but Byzantium retained a presence, and the boundary between the Greek and Latin churches ran through the area in ways that would later shape Serbian politics. Evidence for parish life or for the daily experience of conversion is almost entirely lacking for this early period, a gap that students should recognize rather than fill with later assumptions.

For these reasons, historians now avoid speaking of a tenth century Serbian "state" in the sense of a continuous medieval kingdom. Later Serbian historical writing, much of it shaped by nineteenth century nationalism and by the memory of the Nemanjić dynasty, projected the unity of the high medieval period backward. The better approach is to describe a series of political communities, sometimes aligned and sometimes in conflict, whose leaders used Byzantine titles and favors, accepted Christianity at varying paces, and were drawn into the rivalry of Constantinople, Bulgaria, and the Adriatic powers. The long process by which one of these communities, Raška, absorbed the others is the subject of the next two sections.

Section 39.7

Between Byzantium and the Adriatic: Duklja and Raška

After the Byzantine conquest of Bulgaria in 1018, the empire's authority extended to the borders of the Serbian lands, and Byzantine officials in the Balkan provinces claimed overlordship. In the eleventh century, the political center of gravity in the western Balkans lay not in Raška but in Duklja, later called Zeta, a coastal and highland principality around Lake Skadar. Its rulers could draw on Adriatic trade, on proximity to Dalmatian towns, and on the possibility of playing Latin and Byzantine authorities against each other. About 1040, Stefan Vojislav led a revolt against Byzantine overlordship, and after a period of defeat and imprisonment he reestablished himself and secured Duklja's autonomy in the following years.

His son Michael, who ruled from around 1050, turned to Rome. In the 1070s, the papacy of Gregory VII, engaged in the reform movement examined in Chapter 44 and seeking allies at the edge of the Byzantine sphere, sent a crown, and Michael obtained recognition as king of the Slavs in 1077. His son Constantine Bodin pursued a more adventurous policy. In 1072 he took part in a Bulgarian uprising against Byzantine rule led by Georgi Voyteh, was proclaimed tsar under the name Peter in Prizren, and was captured and later released. He subsequently expanded Duklja's control to Raška and parts of Bosnia and Zahumlje, and in 1089 the see of Bar was raised to an archbishopric under Latin authority, giving Duklja a Latin ecclesiastical hierarchy. The privilege is associated with the imperial antipope Clement III (Wibert), and scholars debate both the document and how fully a metropolitan jurisdiction operated, with some placing its effective establishment only in 1199. The elevation reflected the region's position on the Adriatic and the willingness of rulers to seek papal recognition when Constantinople was unresponsive, and it shows that the religious geography of the western Balkans was not yet fixed along the lines that would later divide Latin and Orthodox Christendom, as Chapter 40 discusses.

The history of Duklja at this time is clouded by a problematic source, the Chronicle of the Priest of Duklja, a highly contested Latin narrative traditionally dated to the later twelfth century, though its date, authorship, and textual formation remain disputed and some scholarship places its surviving form around 1300. It offers a detailed narrative of the dynasty, including legends about early Slavic migration and conversion. Historians generally regard it as a valuable but unreliable source, written long after the events, probably shaped by Latin church interests at Bar, and containing invented episodes and genealogies. It has to be used with other sources, such as Byzantine histories, papal letters, and charters, and where it stands alone its claims must be treated as uncertain.

Duklja's dominance did not last. After Bodin's death around 1101, dynastic quarrels weakened the principality, and Raška, in the interior, emerged under local lords with the title župan. Raška lay on routes between the Danube plain and the Adriatic and between Byzantium and Hungary, and its rulers survived by shifting among powers. The twelfth century brought a resurgence of Byzantine power under the Komnenian emperors. John II Komnenos and then Manuel I Komnenos campaigned in the Balkans, and Manuel, whose policies aimed at asserting imperial authority from Hungary to southern Italy, forced the župans of Raška to accept overlordship and supply troops. At the same time Hungary, a Latin Christian kingdom that expanded southward, competed for influence in the region. Raška's princes therefore rose in a space defined by great power competition, using alliances, marriage, and rebellion to extend their authority, and their ability to appeal to Hungary or to Byzantium determined which of them succeeded.

The point for historians is that medieval Serbia did not emerge suddenly with the Nemanjić dynasty. It emerged from a prolonged contest among local rulers, in which Duklja held the lead for a time, Latin and Greek ecclesiastical influences competed, and Constantinople alternated between pressure and neglect. When Stefan Nemanja gathered power in the later twelfth century, he did so on the basis of earlier experiments and amid a changing international setting.

Section 39.8

Stefan Nemanja, Sava, and the Formation of Medieval Serbia

Stefan Nemanja was born into the ruling family of Raška and, after a period of conflict with his brothers, who were backed in part by the emperor Manuel I, emerged as the leading župan in the 1160s. The emperor's military campaigns forced him to submit, and in 1172 Nemanja appeared before Manuel in a humiliating ceremony, an event recorded in Byzantine and later Serbian sources. This subordination ended with Manuel's death in 1180. Byzantium, torn by succession struggles and then by the Hungarian king Béla III's invasion of the Balkans, was unable to restrain him. Nemanja expanded his rule over Zeta and the coast, parts of Kosovo, and the Morava valley, making use of the opportunity presented by imperial weakness and the disruptions of the Third Crusade, which passed through the region in 1189. He met Frederick Barbarossa near Niš, reportedly offering an alliance against Byzantium, though nothing came of it.

The imperial response was a campaign by Isaac II Angelos, who defeated Nemanja in 1190 near the Morava. The settlement left him in control of his core territories while recognizing Byzantine suzerainty, and it included a marriage between Nemanja's son Stefan and a member of the imperial family. Nemanja's policy thus fitted a long pattern in which a Balkan ruler grew strong during a period of Byzantine weakness, then agreed to terms that preserved his gains within a framework of formal imperial overlordship. What distinguished him was the durability of what he built, which owed much to the institutions he founded and the family structures he created. He divided power among his sons in a way that avoided the fatal quarrels of Duklja, giving the eldest, Vukan, a territory in Zeta while designating Stefan as his successor, though a conflict between brothers still occurred later.

Monastic patronage was central to the Nemanjić political program. Nemanja founded the monastery of Studenica in the 1190s, with a church of white marble whose architecture combined Romanesque elements from the Adriatic with Byzantine decoration and whose frescoes, completed in the early thirteenth century, rank among the major works of the period. In 1196, he abdicated in favor of Stefan, took monastic vows as Simeon, and in time joined his youngest son Rastko on Mount Athos, where the monastery of Hilandar was refounded for Serbian monks in 1198 with the support of the emperor Alexios III. He died there in 1199. Nemanja was later canonized, and his relics were returned to Studenica, an act that established the dynasty's sacred legitimacy. The pattern of the abdicated ruler entering a monastery was borrowed from Byzantine and Bulgarian precedent, and the dynasty's family saints became a central resource for later rulers.

Rastko, who became the monk Sava, shaped the church. After years on Athos, he returned to Serbia, mediated between his brothers, and in 1208 became abbot of Studenica. Stefan, who ruled as grand župan, sought a royal crown. In 1217 he received one, according to Serbian and Latin tradition, from a legate of Pope Honorius III, which accounts for his later epithet "First-Crowned," though the details of the ceremony are disputed. Sava, however, aimed to ensure the church remained Orthodox and independent. In 1219, in the circumstances created by the Fourth Crusade, when Latin crusaders held Constantinople and the Byzantine emperor and patriarch had withdrawn to Nicaea in Anatolia, Sava traveled there and obtained from Emperor Theodore I Laskaris and the patriarch the status of an autocephalous archbishopric, with himself as first archbishop. This arrangement, tied to Chapter 41's discussion of the Latin conquest of Constantinople, gave Serbia an Orthodox church governed from within.

The Nemanjić state that emerged by about 1220 therefore rested on a recognizable set of institutions: a dynasty with a mythology of sacred ancestry, a monarchy recognized by Latin and Byzantine authorities, an autocephalous church, a network of royal monasteries that served as landholders, centers of learning, and burial sites, and legal and administrative practices drawing on Byzantine models. It was still a composite realm, with regional elites and Latin Christian populations along the coast, and it remained a modest power. The great expansion under later Nemanjić rulers, including the creation of a Serbian empire under Stefan Dušan in the fourteenth century, belongs to the later Balkan transformation examined in Chapter 41. What this chapter emphasizes is that medieval Serbia was built by combining local political experience with Byzantine religious and ceremonial forms, and by skillful use of the rivalry among its neighbors.

Section 39.9

The River World of the Rus: Varangians, Slavs, Khazars, and Trade

Rus history begins with rivers. In the eighth and ninth centuries, long distance trade connected the Baltic and North Sea to the Islamic world through the rivers of the east European plain. Scandinavian and other traders moved from the Gulf of Finland up the Neva and Volkhov to Lake Ladoga and Lake Ilmen, and then by portage to the upper reaches of the Dnieper and the Volga. Along the Volga they reached the Khazar and Bulgar (Volga Bulgar) markets and, beyond them, the Caspian and Abbasid lands. Along the Dnieper they reached the Black Sea and Constantinople. The commodities included furs, honey, wax, slaves, and amber moving south and east, in exchange for silver coins, silks, glassware, and other luxury goods flowing north, and the evidence for this trade is visible in thousands of Islamic silver dirhams found in hoards across northern Europe and in the Baltic.

Archaeology has revealed trading and craft settlements on these routes, at Staraya Ladoga, Rurikovo Gorodishche near Novgorod, Gnezdovo near Smolensk, and Kyiv. They contain Scandinavian objects, burial practices, and Slavic and Finnic material culture side by side, indicating mixed communities. The term Rus, which appears in Byzantine, Arabic, Latin, and Slavonic sources from the ninth century, designated at first a group of warriors and traders operating on these routes. Most scholars derive the word from a Scandinavian term associated with rowing, perhaps via a Finnic form for Sweden, though that etymology is disputed. In the sources it can refer to a ruling elite, to its followers, or to the territory they controlled, and its meaning shifted as the elite became Slavicized.

The region's peoples included Slavic tribal groups such as the Polianians around Kyiv, the Severians, the Drevlians, and the Krivichi, along with Finnic peoples in the north and Baltic peoples to the west. Politically, they lived under local leaders and in some cases paid tribute to the Khazars. The Khazar Khaganate, based on the lower Volga and northern Caucasus, controlled the steppe trade routes and levied tribute, protected merchants, and, according to later sources, included a ruling elite that had converted to Judaism. Rus traders appear in Arabic reports as active at Khazar markets and on the Caspian, and Arabic writers such as Ibn Rusta and, in the 920s, Ibn Fadlan describe their appearance, trading practices, and funerals, with the usual limitations of accounts by outsiders, who sometimes emphasized exotic customs. In the late ninth and tenth centuries Khazar power faded under pressure from new steppe peoples such as the Pechenegs and from the Rus themselves.

For the political narrative, historians depend heavily on the Primary Chronicle, also known as the Tale of Bygone Years. It was compiled in Kyiv, probably in the early twelfth century and reached its present form in the 1110s, drawing on earlier chronicles, oral traditions, treaties, and Byzantine material. Later monastic tradition ascribed it to a monk of the Kyiv Caves Monastery named Nestor, though modern scholars regard it as the product of several editors. The chronicle's account of the beginning of Rus, which tells of Slavic and Finnic tribes inviting Varangian princes to rule over them in the year it dates to 862, is a literary narrative composed generations after the events, shaped by the interests of the ruling dynasty and by Christian historical thinking. It is indispensable, and it must be read critically.

Historical Debate

The Origins of Rus: Scandinavian Founders or Local Development?

The question: Who created the early Rus polity, and how important were Scandinavian warriors and traders compared with the Slavic and Finnic populations of the region? The question became politically charged in the eighteenth and twentieth centuries, when claims of foreign foundation or native origin were tied to national pride.

Interpretation A: Scandinavian elites founded the ruling structure. Proponents, often called "Normanists," point to the Primary Chronicle's account of Varangian princes, the Scandinavian forms of many early Rus names such as Oleg, Igor, and Olga (from Old Norse Helgi, Ingvar, and Helga), the Scandinavian names of the Dnieper rapids in Constantine VII's handbook, the Scandinavian-style burials and objects in trading sites, and Byzantine, Latin, and Arabic references to Rus as northmen or distinct from Slavs. In this view, Scandinavian warrior-merchants created a political and tribute system on the rivers and gradually merged with the Slavic majority.
Interpretation B: The polity grew from local and regional developments. Critics emphasize that the region already had Slavic and Finnic societies, trading centers, and political leaders, that Scandinavian numbers were probably small, that the chronicle's account is a late and stylized narrative, and that the polity's institutions, language, and religion were overwhelmingly Slavic and Byzantine by the eleventh century. Some also stress the role of the Khazar system and steppe interaction in shaping tribute and political organization. This view rejects the idea that a foreign elite "created" a state out of a passive population.

Evidence: The debate draws on the Primary Chronicle, Byzantine and Arabic reports, treaty texts that preserve Scandinavian names, runic inscriptions, archaeological finds from trading settlements, and linguistic analysis. Each category has limitations. Names show elite ancestry but not the size of a population, and burial customs show cultural affiliation in specific places.

Why it matters: Most current scholars hold that Scandinavians played a significant role in the formation of the early elite and trade networks, and that the polity quickly became Slavic in language and culture. The remaining disagreement concerns the degree of Scandinavian influence and the weight given to local development and steppe contacts. The debate matters because modern national histories of Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus have each claimed Rus, and because it shows how historians separate evidence from later political memory.

Section 39.10

From Novgorod to Kyiv: The Early Rus Polity

According to the Primary Chronicle, the first prince, Rurik, established himself in the north, in the Ladoga and Novgorod region, and after his death, his kinsman Oleg moved south, took Kyiv around 882, and united the northern and southern territories under a single dynasty. Archaeologically, the consolidation of Kyiv as a major center occurred around the tenth century, and the chronicle's tidy dynastic sequence probably simplifies a more complex process in which several leaders and groups competed. The Rurikid dynasty, which later claimed descent from the founder, is real as a ruling lineage in the tenth century, but its early genealogy cannot be verified in detail.

The prince's power depended on a retinue of armed men, the druzhina, and on tribute collected from subject communities. The annual circuit of tribute, called polyudie in Slavic sources, involved the prince and his followers traveling through dependent territories in the winter to collect furs, honey, wax, and other goods, then transporting them in the spring along the river route described in Section 39.9. Control of the route and its centers was therefore essential, and early Rus rulers fought to bring neighbors under tribute and to deny the Khazars and others access. The system produced wealth for the elite but gave limited administrative depth, and when rulers pressed too hard it could provoke revolt. The chronicle reports that Igor, around 945, was killed by the Drevlians when he returned to collect additional tribute.

Historical Vocabulary
Druzhina

The druzhina (from a Slavic root meaning "companion") was the prince's armed retinue. Its members lived with the ruler, shared in feasts and spoils, served as military commanders, administrators, and judges, and advised him on policy. Early druzhina members included Scandinavian and Slavic warriors and, later, men of steppe origin. The institution reflects a personal bond of loyalty and reward, rather than a bureaucracy, and it helps explain why the death or defeat of a prince could shake his realm. As the Rus state developed, senior druzhina members emerged as the boyars, a landed aristocracy with its own estates, though the process was gradual and regionally varied. The term should not be translated as "army," since the druzhina was a core of professional fighters who were supplemented by levies and allies.

Relations with Byzantium were central. In 860, according to Byzantine sources, a Rus fleet appeared before Constantinople, and the patriarch Photios described the attack in a sermon. Later expeditions are known from the chronicle and from Byzantine reports. The chronicle describes a campaign by Oleg in 907 that ended with a treaty, though no Byzantine source mentions it, and historians debate whether it occurred as described. A treaty of 911, whose text is preserved in the chronicle in a Slavonic translation of a Greek original, is accepted as authentic in outline. It regulated trade, set penalties for violence and theft, covered inheritance of Rus who died in Constantinople, shipwrecks, and the treatment of captives, and allowed Rus to serve in imperial armies. In 941, Igor led a large expedition that was defeated by Byzantine ships using the incendiary weapon known as Greek fire, as reported by Byzantine and Latin sources, and in 944 a new treaty was concluded. This second treaty shows that some Rus already swore by the Christian God while others still invoked Perun, a sign of religious pluralism within the elite.

After Igor's death, his widow Olga acted as regent for their son Sviatoslav. The chronicle depicts her revenge on the Drevlians, in tales that mix folklore and dynastic memory, and her reorganization of tribute, including fixed collection points. She traveled to Constantinople, where a Byzantine ceremonial text describes her reception by Constantine VII, probably in 957, and she was baptized, though the circumstances and date are debated. Her conversion did not convert Rus, since Sviatoslav is said to have refused baptism, citing the mockery of his retinue. His reign, from about 962 to 972, shows the other side of Rus power. He campaigned against the Khazars, destroying their fortress of Sarkel and weakening the khaganate around 965, against Volga Bulgars and steppe peoples, and then, at the invitation of Byzantium in 968, against Bulgaria. His ambition to rule from the Danube, noted in Section 39.5, ended with defeat by John Tzimiskes. On the return to Kyiv in 972, he was killed by the Pechenegs, who now controlled the steppe route near the Dnieper rapids. The tragedy illustrates both the reach of Rus power and its exposure to steppe peoples.

In these decades, the Rus polity evolved from a loose collection of river settlements and tribute territories into something closer to a dynastic realm centered on Kyiv. It was nevertheless fragile. Succession was unsettled, the prince's authority depended on military success, and the Slavic and Finnic subjects had their own local leaders. The next stage, under Vladimir and Yaroslav, would give the polity a church, a legal tradition, and a capital with monumental buildings.

Section 39.11

Vladimir and the Christianization of Rus

After Sviatoslav's death in 972, his sons fought for the succession. Yaropolk ruled in Kyiv, Oleg in the Drevlian lands, and Vladimir, in Novgorod. Vladimir fled to Scandinavia, returned with Varangian warriors, defeated his brothers, and by about 978 to 980 held Kyiv. The chronicle, writing within a Christian dynastic tradition, presents his early rule as pagan, with the erection of idols and, in one story, a human sacrifice that was ended when the victims were Christians. Historians read these stories with care, though they accept that Vladimir at first tried to organize a state cult around Perun and other deities, an attempt that reflected the political need for a unifying religion for a polity whose subjects included Slavs, Finns, Balts, and Scandinavians.

The conversion that followed is told in the chronicle as a moving narrative in which Vladimir investigated the religions of Muslim Volga Bulgars, Latin Christians, Khazar Jews, and Greek Christians and chose the Greek faith after his envoys were overwhelmed by the beauty of the liturgy at Constantinople. This story, which bears the marks of a literary construction, is a tradition about how later generations wished to remember the decision. More reliable evidence comes from Byzantine, Armenian, and Arabic sources. They indicate that in 987 the emperor Basil II, facing the rebellion of the general Bardas Phokas, asked Vladimir for military help and that Vladimir sent a force, reported by later sources as roughly six thousand men, that helped to defeat the rebels. In return, Vladimir was to marry Basil's sister Anna, a remarkable concession, since Byzantine princes born in the purple were not normally given in marriage to foreigners, and Vladimir was to accept baptism. When the emperor delayed, Vladimir seized the Byzantine city of Cherson (Chersonesos) in the Crimea, and the marriage and baptism took place around 988 or 989. The Rus force became the nucleus of the Varangian Guard, the elite imperial corps in which Scandinavians and Rus served for generations.

The conventional date of 988 comes from the chronicle's dating and is widely used, though some historians prefer 989 for the capture of Cherson, and the sequence of events is debated. What matters is that the conversion was a political decision that formed part of an alliance with Byzantium. It brought Vladimir recognition as a Christian monarch, kinship with the imperial family, a place in the Byzantine hierarchy of rulers, and access to craftsmen, clergy, and books. For the emperor it secured military support and brought a powerful northern neighbor into the Christian sphere, which promised to reduce Rus raiding. The cost was that Rus would be a Christian state but not an equal to Byzantium, and its church would remain subject to Constantinople.

Vladimir's baptism started a process that took generations. He ordered the destruction of pagan idols in Kyiv and, according to the chronicle, the mass baptism of the townspeople in the Dnieper, an account that may combine fact and symbolic narrative. He brought clergy from Byzantium and perhaps Bulgaria, and Slavonic liturgical books from Bulgaria, which the Rus could use because the language was close to their own, an application of the Cyrillo-Methodian heritage discussed in Chapter 38. He established a church organization tied to Constantinople (the precise structure and earliest succession of its hierarchy are poorly documented, though Rus subsequently developed as a metropolitanate under the patriarch of Constantinople), and built stone churches, most notably the Church of the Virgin, known as the Tithe Church, in the 990s, supported by a tenth of his revenue. He also issued silver and gold coinage with Christian imagery, in imitation of Byzantine types, though it was struck in limited quantities. Archaeology shows that pagan burials continued in some regions long after, and that the Christianization of the countryside, where ritual practice combined old and new forms, proceeded slowly. Historians use the term "dual faith" (dvoeverie) for the coexistence of Christian and older practices, while debating how strongly this concept should be applied.

Vladimir's later years saw the extension of Kyivan authority to the west and north, where he placed his sons in key towns as governors, a system that tied the dynasty to regional centers but also created potential rivals. After his death in 1015, the pattern of civil war returned. His son Sviatopolk, supported by some of the druzhina, killed his brothers Boris and Gleb, who were later venerated as the first native Rus saints, representing a distinctive tradition of non-violent sacrifice that was important for the dynasty's religious identity. Their murder opened a struggle that Yaroslav, Vladimir's son and governor of Novgorod, would win, as the next section explains.

Section 39.12

Yaroslav, Law, Church, and Dynastic Diplomacy

Yaroslav, called "the Wise" in later tradition, ruled Kyiv from 1019, after defeating Sviatopolk with the support of Novgorodian townspeople and Scandinavian mercenaries, to his death in 1054. For much of his reign he shared power with his brother Mstislav of Chernigov, with whom he divided the Dnieper lands in 1026, and he became sole ruler only after Mstislav's death in 1036. In that year he also defeated the Pechenegs outside Kyiv, a victory that eased the steppe threat for a generation and was commemorated by the building of the cathedral of St. Sophia, begun around 1037, with its mosaics and frescoes, modeled on Constantinople's Hagia Sophia in conception and decoration. The Golden Gate and a rebuilt city wall expressed the ambition to present Kyiv as a Christian capital in the manner of Constantinople.

Yaroslav is associated with the earliest layer of Rus law, the so-called Rus' Justice or Pravda. It is a compilation of customary rules, fines, and procedures, covering homicide, theft, injury, and property, with distinctions of status. The "short" version, attributed to Yaroslav and his sons, is preserved in later manuscripts, and expanded versions were produced in the twelfth century. Its provisions, which assign monetary penalties rather than corporal punishment and limit blood vengeance, suggest a legal system based on customary practice adapted by princely authority. Church law also developed, with the church holding jurisdiction over matters of marriage, family, and religious offenses. Historians debate the date and authorship of the text's layers, so it must be used as evidence for legal practice in the eleventh and twelfth centuries, not necessarily for Yaroslav's own time alone.

The church grew under his patronage. Yaroslav founded monasteries, supported translation of Greek texts into Slavonic, and in 1051 installed Hilarion, a Rus cleric, as metropolitan of Kyiv, without the usual appointment by the patriarch in Constantinople. Hilarion's sermon on Law and Grace, an early work of Rus Christian rhetoric, celebrated the conversion of Rus and Vladimir. In the same period the Kyiv Caves Monastery, founded by the monks Antony and Theodosius, became the leading religious and intellectual center, and it was in this milieu that the chronicle tradition took shape. The episode of 1051 is sometimes read as an assertion of Rus ecclesiastical independence, though the metropolitanate was soon filled again by Greek appointees, and it did not become an autocephalous church. Kyiv's religious relationship to Byzantium remained subordinate.

Yaroslav's dynastic diplomacy was exceptionally wide, which shows that Rus was integrated into the European political world beyond Byzantium. He married Ingegerd, daughter of King Olaf of Sweden. His daughter Elisiv married Harald Hardrada, later king of Norway, his daughter Anna married King Henry I of France, and his daughter Anastasia married Andrew, who became king of Hungary. His sister married Casimir I of Poland, and several of his sons and grandsons married into Polish, German, and Byzantine families. Scandinavian exiles took refuge at his court, and the Rus supplied troops to others. This web of connections reflects the prestige of Kyiv and the Rurikid dynasty and shows the Rus lands as part of an eleventh century Europe that included Latin Christian kings, a connection that complicates the idea of a sharp division between East and West before the later eleventh century, though Chapter 40 will examine the growth of that division. Rus relations with Byzantium were not uniformly peaceful. In 1043, a Rus naval expedition against Constantinople, commanded by one of Yaroslav's sons, failed, and the Byzantine writer Michael Psellos recorded the event. Afterward, peace and marriage ties were restored.

Yaroslav's reign is often seen as the height of Kyivan Rus, with a capital of monumental churches, a legal tradition, a Christian literary culture, and prestigious dynastic ties. Its limits are equally important. The realm was a large federation of towns and territories held together by dynastic authority rather than by a bureaucratic state, and on his death Yaroslav divided his lands among his sons, with the oldest, Izyaslav, holding Kyiv. The arrangement, which gave seniority to the eldest while assigning lands to the others, was intended to preserve unity, but it also embedded the principle that the realm was the property of the whole dynasty.

Section 39.13

Society, Economy, Steppe Frontiers, and the Fragmentation of Rus

Rus society was stratified and varied by region. At the top stood the prince and his kin, the druzhina and, increasingly, a landholding boyar class, and senior churchmen. Townspeople, including merchants and artisans, formed an important and sometimes politically active group, especially in Novgorod and Kyiv, where assemblies called veche met in times of crisis and could accept or reject a prince. Peasants, the majority of the population, owed tribute and labor to princes, boyars, and monasteries, though the degree of dependence is difficult to measure. The law codes distinguish free men, various categories of dependents, and slaves (kholopy), who were acquired through war, debt, or birth and who were also a significant commodity in trade with Byzantium and the Islamic world. Women, depicted in the law and chronicle mainly through their families, could hold property and, in the case of princesses such as Olga, wield political power, though Church rules on marriage reshaped their legal position.

The economy combined agriculture, forest products, and trade. Along the southern edge, in the black earth regions near Kyiv, farming supported denser settlement, while in the north, forest zones produced furs, honey, and wax. Towns served as military, administrative, and commercial centers. Archaeology at Novgorod, where waterlogged soil has preserved wooden streets, buildings, and hundreds of letters written on birch bark since the eleventh century, has opened a rare window onto everyday literacy, with messages about debts, household management, and personal affairs. These documents show that literacy extended beyond clergy and princes into merchant and artisan circles, at least in Novgorod. The church and monasteries were also economic actors, acquiring land and labor through donations, and, as in Bulgaria and Serbia, becoming major landholders.

The steppe frontier shaped political life continually. After the decline of the Khazars, the Pechenegs dominated the steppe north of the Black Sea until about the 1030s, when they were displaced by the Oghuz (Torks) and Cumans (known to the Rus as Polovtsy). The Cumans, a Turkic nomadic confederation, raided and traded with the Rus lands from the 1060s onward. Defeat by Cuman forces at the Alta river in 1068 provoked unrest in Kyiv. Relations were not only hostile. Rus princes made marriage alliances with Cuman leaders, hired steppe auxiliaries, and drew on their cavalry, and Cuman khans took part in Rus dynastic struggles. A fortified frontier line of earthworks and towns along the southern rivers, constructed over decades, expressed the effort of princes to protect settled territory and the commerce of the Dnieper route, and the later epic Tale of Igor's Campaign, which is set in a failed expedition of 1185 against the Cumans, remembers the frontier in poetic form, though its date and authenticity have been debated.

Political fragmentation followed the death of Yaroslav's sons. The system of seniority gave each prince a claim to rotate among thrones as vacancies arose, but it was cumbersome and led to repeated conflict. In 1097, at a meeting at Lyubech, princes agreed that each would hold his father's patrimony, a step toward territorial dynastic lines that tended to localize authority. Vladimir Monomakh, who ruled in Kyiv from 1113 to 1125, and his son Mstislav (to 1132) temporarily restored cohesion, drawing on Byzantine ties, since Monomakh's mother was a Byzantine princess, and on military success against the Cumans. After 1132, regional principalities, including Chernigov, Smolensk, Galicia and Volhynia in the southwest, Vladimir-Suzdal in the northeast, and the republican Novgorod, became the principal centers, and Kyiv remained a prize and a ritual capital but no longer the unquestioned political center. Its sack by a coalition of princes in 1169, led by Andrei Bogolyubsky of Vladimir-Suzdal, exemplifies the change. The Mongol invasion of 1237 to 1240, which captured Kyiv in December 1240, fell on a fragmented political world, and its consequences belong primarily to the separate MGU Russian History textbook and are recalled later where they intersect with European developments.

Cause and Effect

The Fragmentation of Rus after the Mid Eleventh Century

Long-term causes

A polity built on tribute, river trade, and a personal retinue, with limited administrative machinery, and a ruling dynasty that regarded the realm as the shared property of its members.

Short-term causes

Yaroslav's division of the realm among his sons, disputes over seniority and succession, the growth of regional boyar and urban interests, and the pressure of Cuman raids from the 1060s.

Event or transformation

The shift from a Kyiv-centered dynastic polity to a set of regional principalities linked by kinship, church, and shared law, with Kyiv retaining prestige but not decisive authority after about 1132.

Immediate effects

Recurrent princely wars, shifting alliances with steppe peoples, the rise of regional centers such as Galicia, Vladimir-Suzdal, and Novgorod, and the weakening of Kyiv's economic position as trade routes changed.

Long-term consequences

Distinct regional political traditions that later shaped Ukrainian, Belarusian, and Russian history, and political disunity at the time of the Mongol invasion.

Historical complexity

Fragmentation was not simply decline. Regional principalities flourished economically and culturally, and the church and law maintained a shared Rus identity. Scholars also caution against reading the weakness of the 1230s backward as the inevitable result of the 1130s.

Historical complexity: Several factors are often proposed for the decline of Kyiv, including the weakening of the Dnieper trade route after the rise of Italian commerce in the Mediterranean and the effects of the Crusades, as examined in Chapter 43 and Chapter 47. Historians differ about how much weight to give such economic changes compared with dynastic and political causes.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living in the Orthodox Slavic Lands

For most inhabitants of Bulgaria, the Serbian lands, and Rus, daily life centered on the village, the household, and the agricultural year. Cultivators grew cereals, kept livestock, and in the highlands practiced seasonal pastoralism, and they owed rent, labor, or tribute to princes, nobles, monasteries, or in some regions the state. Pliska and Preslav in Bulgaria, and Kyiv and Novgorod in Rus, were centers of crafts, from pottery and metalwork to jewelry, and the ceramic workshops of Preslav and the wooden streets of Novgorod show the material world of artisans. Archaeology at village and cemetery sites reveals diet, health, and burial practices, including the gradual replacement of older funeral customs, such as cremation or grave goods, with Christian burial.

Religious life was local. Priests, who were usually married parish clergy in the Orthodox tradition, baptized children and conducted burials, while monks and hermits held a special prestige and often served as advisers and mediators. Veneration of local saints, relics, and holy places spread with the churches. Popular belief preserved older elements, from agricultural rituals to the veneration of spirits, so that practice was often a blend. The evidence for women's lives is thin and filtered through male writers, but law, hagiography, and birch bark letters show women as property owners, household managers, and participants in trade, and the roles of princesses and abbesses demonstrate that elite women could shape religious and political life. Slavery and dependency were common, and captives from wars and steppe raids were a regular part of the economy, as the evidence of law codes and treaties demonstrates. Disease, famine, and raiding were persistent threats, and the Pecheneg and Cuman raids brought particular danger to the southern frontier.

Section 39.14

Three Orthodox Political Worlds: Connection without Uniformity

The three polities examined in this chapter borrowed from Byzantium in similar ways. All three developed major political and ecclesiastical institutions within the Byzantine-Orthodox sphere, though the Serbian lands in particular also contained durable Latin Christian institutions and maintained important ties with Rome and the Adriatic. Each took over titles and ceremonial, constructed churches and monasteries on Byzantine models, and drew on Byzantine law, literature, and art. The rulers of each used marriage ties, baptism, and recognition from Constantinople as political resources, and each used the Slavonic language as the vehicle of liturgy and literature, giving them a shared textual world in which saints' lives, sermons, and translated Greek works circulated across political boundaries. Scholars once spoke of a "Byzantine Commonwealth" to describe this world of shared religion and culture, and the concept highlights real connections, though its critics argue that it can overstate Byzantine control and understate the autonomy and conflict of the peoples involved.

The differences were equally striking. Bulgaria was the first of the three to become a territorial state, and its rulers, with a steppe origin and a fusion with Slavic subjects, achieved imperial pretensions and the creation of a patriarchate before suffering conquest. Its history shows how close dependence on a single ruler's military success could be, and how Byzantine integration could absorb a rival state, though it left Slavonic Christianity as a lasting legacy that later Serbian and Rus culture inherited. The Serbian lands never had a first great state in this period and instead developed through competing principalities, mountainous fragmentation, and the interplay of Latin and Greek influences, achieving consolidation late through a dynasty that fused monarchy with monasticism. Rus, by contrast, was a polity of rivers and towns, with a Scandinavian elite assimilating into a Slavic society, a vast and weakly institutionalized realm that relied on dynastic kinship, and a church that remained within the Byzantine metropolitan system.

The relationship between ruler and church also varied. Bulgarian rulers sought a patriarchate and autocephaly from Constantinople, and they used the church to build a Slavonic literary culture that served the court. Serbian rulers, notably Sava and the Nemanjići, tied the monarchy to monastic foundations and obtained autocephaly in the exceptional circumstances of 1219. Rus princes accepted a metropolitan sent from Constantinople, though they patronized the church generously and attempted in 1051 to appoint a native metropolitan. In all three, the model of the abdicating ruler who becomes a monk, and the cult of saintly princes, influenced the shape of monarchy, though in different ways.

Their relations with neighbors were equally varied. Bulgaria fought Byzantium, the Magyars, the Serbs, and the Rus. Serbian princes bargained among Byzantium, Hungary, the papacy, and Venice. Rus princes dealt with Scandinavians, Poles, Hungarians, Byzantines, Cumans, and others, including through dynastic marriage. Latin Christian, Muslim, Jewish, and steppe communities were part of this world, as the Khazar and Volga Bulgar encounters, the Latin church at Bar, and the Jewish merchants and communities known from Kyiv and other centers demonstrate. The idea of a closed "Orthodox Slavic Europe" therefore does not capture historical reality.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Rulers and Christianity in Bulgaria, the Serbian Lands, and Rus

What Changed

  • Rulers who had derived authority from steppe, tribal, or warrior traditions came to claim a place in a Christian order, with ceremonies, titles, and building programs on Byzantine models
  • Slavonic became a written language of liturgy, law, and chronicle, creating new literate groups of clergy and scribes
  • Churches and monasteries became landholders, centers of learning, and instruments of royal policy
  • Dynasties used marriage, baptism, and the cults of saintly rulers to link themselves to Byzantine and other Christian courts

What Stayed the Same

  • Personal loyalty to the ruler and his retinue remained a basis of power, and military success still determined a ruler's standing
  • The geography of rivers, mountains, and steppe frontiers continued to shape politics, trade, and warfare
  • Rural communities carried on their agricultural routines and often kept older customs alongside Christian practice
  • The rivalry and cooperation with Byzantium continued as a central relationship

Who experienced change most directly? Courts, aristocracies, and clergy experienced the most direct transformation, since they gained new sources of legitimacy, but also new obligations. Who experienced greater continuity? Peasants and herders, for whom the church initially meant a new priest and calendar added to existing practice. Why the pattern matters: It shows that the "conversion" of a state is different from the conversion of a society, and that rulers borrowed selectively from Byzantium to serve local aims.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined three Orthodox and Slavic political worlds, Bulgaria, the Serbian lands, and Rus, as active states and communities rather than as recipients of Byzantine culture. It began from the political consequences of the conversions described in Chapter 38, and it asked what rulers, aristocracies, and churches did with the religious, literary, and ceremonial resources they acquired. The answer varied by place, and the variations are the chapter's principal subject.

Bulgaria originated in the late seventh century when a Bulgar confederation settled south of the Danube and combined with Slavic populations to form a territorial state with its capital at Pliska. Under Krum and Omurtag it defeated Byzantine armies and became a durable power. Boris's acceptance of Christianity, his negotiations with Rome and Constantinople, and the reception of Slavonic clergy created a Christian monarchy and an ecclesiastical organization that served the state.

Under Simeon, Bulgaria reached a peak of ambition. He fought Byzantium over trade and imperial recognition, defeated its army at Anchialos in 917, claimed the title of tsar, and made Preslav a center of Slavonic letters. But he could not take Constantinople, and the strain of war weakened his realm. Peter I's long peace brought recognition of a patriarchate, along with growing dependence, internal religious and social tension, and vulnerability to Rus and Magyar attack.

The Byzantine annexation of eastern Bulgaria in 971, the survival of Samuel's western state around Ohrid, and Basil II's conquest completed in 1018 ended the first Bulgarian state. The settlement was pragmatic, preserving an archbishopric at Ohrid and integrating Bulgarian elites, but it was resented, and later revolts and the eventual restoration of Bulgarian independence in the late twelfth century showed that the political and cultural memory of the first state had survived.

The Serbian lands followed a different path. Constantine VII's handbook shows a set of communities, among them Raška, Zahumlje, Travunija, and Duklja, led by princes tied to Byzantium and sometimes to Bulgaria. In the eleventh century Duklja rose and obtained Latin church recognition, while Raška, in the interior, grew under župans who bargained among Byzantium, Hungary, and others. Stefan Nemanja, taking advantage of Byzantine weakness after 1180, consolidated Raška and neighboring regions and founded the dynasty that built medieval Serbia. His son Stefan received a crown, and his son Sava, through a monastic and ecclesiastical career, secured an autocephalous Orthodox church in 1219.

Rus began as a network of river routes connecting the Baltic with the Black and Caspian Seas, along which Scandinavian, Slavic, and Finnic traders and warriors exchanged goods and built tribute systems. The princes of Kyiv, Oleg, Igor, Olga, and Sviatoslav, fought and traded with Byzantium, challenged the Khazars, and entered Balkan politics. The Primary Chronicle is the central source, and it has to be read as a later and shaped narrative. Vladimir's baptism around 988 or 989, bound to an alliance with Basil II, began the Christianization of Rus, a process of generations that left the countryside with a blend of Christian and older practice.

Under Yaroslav, Kyiv reached its height, with the cathedral of St. Sophia, a legal tradition, a Christian literary culture, and dynastic marriages across Europe. But the realm rested on dynastic kinship, tribute, and a personal retinue. After Yaroslav's death, the system of seniority, regional interests, and Cuman pressure encouraged fragmentation, and by the mid twelfth century regional principalities had replaced the primacy of Kyiv, leaving the Rus lands politically divided when the Mongols arrived.

Comparison shows common features and real differences. All three polities used Christianity, Slavonic literacy, Byzantine titles, and marriage ties as political resources. They differed in origin, structure, and relationship between ruler and church, and in the nature of their relations with Byzantium, the Latin West, and the steppe. The chapter has also shown the importance of source criticism, from Byzantine court writings to the later chronicle tradition, and of avoiding projections of modern national identities onto medieval polities. Together, these histories show that the Orthodox world of eastern and southeastern Europe was a set of connected but distinct political experiences, a conclusion that prepares for Chapter 40's study of the division of Christendom and Chapter 41's study of Byzantium's end.

The status of a church whose head is chosen within it and not appointed by another authority, a status negotiated with Constantinople.
Patriarchate
The highest rank of Orthodox church governance, whose recognition for Bulgaria in 927 marked political as well as ecclesiastical status.
Metropolitan
A bishop with authority over a province of bishops; the head of the Rus church was a metropolitan of Kyiv under Constantinople.
Slavonic liturgy
Worship and church writing in Church Slavonic, enabling Slavic populations to hear and read Christian texts in a language they understood.
Boyar
A member of the Bulgarian or Rus landed and military aristocracy, a term also used for Bulgarian nobles.
Druzhina
The armed retinue of a Rus prince, bound to him by personal loyalty and reward, and a source of administrators and later aristocrats.
Veche
An assembly of townspeople in Rus cities, especially Novgorod and Kyiv, which could act in political crises.
Varangian
A term used in Rus and Byzantine sources for Scandinavians, including those who served in the Byzantine imperial guard.
Rus
A name that designated a warrior and merchant elite of Scandinavian origin, then the polity they ruled, and then its Slavic-speaking population.
Khazar Khaganate
A Turkic steppe empire controlling trade and tribute north of the Caucasus and Black Sea until its decline in the tenth century.
Pecheneg
A Turkic nomadic people who dominated the Pontic steppe in the tenth and early eleventh centuries and threatened Rus and Byzantium.
Cuman
A Turkic nomadic confederation, called Polovtsy by the Rus, which dominated the steppe from the mid eleventh century.
Bogomilism
A dualist religious movement reported in tenth century Bulgaria, critical of the church hierarchy and the material world, whose organization is debated.
Theme
A Byzantine military and administrative province, whose system was extended to conquered Bulgarian territory.
Chrysobull
A Byzantine imperial charter sealed in gold, used to grant privileges to monasteries and others.
Polyudie
The annual circuit of a Rus prince and his retinue to collect tribute in goods from subject communities.
Primary Chronicle
The early twelfth century Kyiv compilation, also called the Tale of Bygone Years, which is the main narrative source for early Rus and must be read critically.
Rus' Justice (Pravda)
The earliest compilation of Rus legal customs and penalties, associated with Yaroslav but expanded over later generations.
Dvoeverie
A scholarly term for the coexistence of Christian and older religious practices in Rus, whose strength and extent is debated.
Composite polity
A realm made up of distinct regions or communities under a common ruler, such as the Nemanjić realm or the federation of Rus principalities.
Varangian Guard
An elite unit of the Byzantine army of Scandinavian and Rus origin, formed in the late tenth century from forces sent by Vladimir.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it keeps eastern and southeastern Europe at the center of the medieval story. A narrative that treats Latin Christendom as the whole of medieval Europe would pass over states that rivaled or outlasted many western kingdoms in power and cultural achievement. Bulgaria's imperial claims, Serbia's monastic monarchy, and Kyiv's Christian capital and dynastic network were not marginal episodes. They were parts of the same medieval world that produced Charlemagne's successors and the Norman kings, and they developed through contact with the same Mediterranean and steppe systems.

The chapter also reveals larger processes that appear across Europe in this period. In each case, a ruling group with older traditions adopted Christianity, a literate culture, and ceremonial forms from a more established neighbor and used them to build a durable state. In each case, the move created tension between independence and dependence, between a ruler's wish for autonomy and the claims of a church or empire. Similar processes can be seen in Poland, Hungary, and Scandinavia, as Chapter 35 explained, and the comparison shows that the spread of Christianity was also a story of political choice and negotiation.

Several developments from this chapter continued beyond it. The Slavonic Christian culture preserved at Ohrid and in the monasteries of the Balkans and Rus remained a shared resource for later Orthodox societies. The Nemanjić dynasty's institutions formed the basis of later Serbian expansion, and the regional principalities of Rus shaped distinct political traditions. The question of how the Latin and Greek churches related to one another, which surfaced in the Balkan and Rus contexts here, becomes the central subject of Chapter 40, and the pressures that would eventually undo the Byzantine order in the Balkans, examined in Chapter 41, were already visible in the shifting balance between the empire and its neighbors.

For the argument of Volume II, the chapter contributes a decentered picture of medieval Europe. The medieval world was formed by many centers, among them Constantinople, Rome, Aachen, Preslav, Kyiv, and Studenica, and by many peoples, among them Franks, Greeks, Bulgars, Slavs, Scandinavians, Khazars, Magyars, and Cumans. Understanding how these centers and peoples interacted, competed, and borrowed from one another is essential to understanding what Europe was in the Middle Ages, and why the history of Europe cannot be written as the history of a single church, empire, or nation.
