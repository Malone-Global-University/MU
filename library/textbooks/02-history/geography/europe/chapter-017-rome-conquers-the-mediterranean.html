Rome Conquers the Mediterranean

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

Roman power in the Mediterranean was not the execution of a single plan. It was the cumulative result of wars, settlements, appeals, suspicions, and improvised arrangements, each of which created new relationships that could draw Rome into the next intervention.

In 196 BCE, at the Isthmian Games held near Corinth, a herald announced to a crowd of Greeks that Rome and its commander, Titus Quinctius Flamininus, had defeated King Philip V of Macedon and declared a large group of Greek communities free, ungarrisoned, and untaxed, entitled to live under their own laws. Fifty years later, in 146 BCE, Roman forces under the consul Lucius Mummius stormed and plundered Corinth itself, ending a war against the Achaean League and leaving Greek political life under a heavy and permanent Roman shadow. The same year saw the Roman destruction of Carthage in North Africa. Between these two moments lay one of the most consequential transformations in ancient history: a republic organized to govern a city and an alliance of Italian communities became the dominant political power of the Mediterranean.

Chapter 16 explained how Rome acquired the citizen manpower, treaty network, and institutional resilience that allowed it to survive Hannibal and defeat Carthage. This chapter asks a different question. Once Rome possessed that power, what did it do with it? Why did a state that emerged from the Second Punic War exhausted and still largely concerned with Italy, Sicily, and Iberia become, within roughly seventy years, the arbiter of the Greek world, the destroyer of Macedonian and Carthaginian power, and the governor of provinces from the Atlantic coast of Iberia to the Aegean? And how, given a very small administrative apparatus, did Romans actually govern what they had won?

The chapter's central argument is that Roman expansion after 201 BCE was neither a preplanned program of world conquest nor a series of purely accidental defensive reactions. Security fears, treaty obligations, appeals from allies and rivals, senatorial debate, aristocratic competition for commands and triumphs, the attraction of booty and indemnities, and the consequences of each earlier settlement interacted in changing combinations. Each war ended in an arrangement, and each arrangement created obligations, resentments, and opportunities that shaped the next crisis. Students should watch for this recurring mechanism without treating it as an inevitable conveyor belt toward empire. At many points the Senate hesitated, withdrew armies, or declined to annex territory it had conquered.

The chapter also insists on a distinction between conquest and administration. Rome did not turn defeated territory into identically governed provinces. Some regions, such as Sicily and, after 197 BCE, the two Spanish provinces, were placed under annually assigned Roman magistrates. Others were left as kingdoms, leagues, or free cities bound to Rome by treaty, indemnity, hostage taking, and the expectation of deference. Numidia remained a kingdom, Egypt a Ptolemaic monarchy, Rhodes an independent republic, and most Greek cities continued to govern themselves for decades after Roman armies first crossed the Adriatic. The Roman imperial system that existed by about 130 BCE was a patchwork of direct rule, dependency, and diplomacy.

The people who shaped these outcomes were not only Romans. Macedonian kings, the Aetolian and Achaean Leagues, the Seleucid ruler Antiochus III, the kings of Pergamum, the Rhodians, the Numidian king Masinissa, Carthaginian councils, Lusitanian and Celtiberian war leaders, and countless individual cities all made calculations about Rome. Many invited Roman intervention against a local rival. Others resisted it with great skill and at great cost. Roman power grew in the space created by the rivalries of others as much as through Roman initiative.

Historians reconstruct these events from an uneven evidence base. The Greek historian Polybius, who was taken to Rome as a hostage after the Third Macedonian War and knew many of the leading Romans personally, is the best contemporary witness, though his surviving text is incomplete and he wrote partly to explain Roman success to Greek readers. The Roman historian Livy, writing in the Augustan period, preserves detailed narrative for the years down to 167 BCE, largely drawing on Polybius and Roman annalistic tradition, but the books that covered later years survive only in summary. Inscribed decrees of Greek cities, treaties, and letters from Roman magistrates, coins, archaeological surveys of Roman siege works at Numantia, excavations at Carthage, and evidence for silver mining in Iberia supplement the literary tradition. Almost all the narrative sources reflect the perspective of victors, of Greek intellectuals living within the Roman orbit, or of much later writers, and the voices of Iberian and North African communities must largely be recovered indirectly.

Within Part III, this chapter marks the point at which the history of Rome ceases to be primarily the history of a city and its Italian neighbors and becomes the history of a Mediterranean power. It provides the geographic and institutional setting for the social transformations of Chapter 18 and the political crises of Chapter 19, and it lays the foundation for the imperial system whose mature form is examined in Chapters 21 and 22. Within Volume I, it also shows how the western Mediterranean, Iberia, the Balkans, Greece, and Anatolia became linked in a single political system, a change that would shape the histories of nearly every region examined in the chapters that follow.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the balance of power in the Mediterranean in 201 BCE and explain why Roman supremacy over the whole basin did not yet exist.
  2. Explain the main motives historians identify for Roman intervention after the Second Punic War and distinguish which best fit particular episodes.
  3. Trace the sequence of Roman wars and settlements in Macedon, Greece, and the Seleucid sphere from 200 to 146 BCE.
  4. Analyze the meaning and political uses of the "freedom of the Greeks" and the contradiction between proclaimed autonomy and Roman arbitration.
  5. Explain why the conquest of Iberia was a prolonged process shaped by geography, local resistance, and Roman political and military practice.
  6. Distinguish the Second and Third Punic Wars and explain the Roman decision for war in 149 BCE and the creation of the province of Africa.
  7. Describe how Roman provincial government worked, including governors, taxation, local elites, and the limits of the Roman administrative apparatus.
  8. Compare provinces, free cities, treaty allies, and subordinate kingdoms as different forms of Roman domination.
  9. Evaluate competing historiographical explanations for Roman expansion, including defensive imperialism, aggressive militarism, and international anarchy.
  10. Assess the agency of non Roman actors, including Greek states, Hellenistic kings, Numidians, Carthaginians, and Iberian communities.
  11. Interpret literary and inscriptional evidence for Roman expansion while identifying its perspective and limitations.
  12. Connect Mediterranean conquest to the social and political problems examined in Chapters 18 and 19 without narrating those chapters in advance.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

Historical Context

Chapter 15 examined the institutions through which the Roman Republic governed itself: annually elected magistrates holding imperium, the Senate as a standing deliberative body dominated by former magistrates, and popular assemblies that voted on legislation, elections, and declarations of war. Chapter 16 traced the extension of Roman power across Italy through citizenship, colonies, and bilateral treaties with Latin and Italian allies, the socii, and showed how that system supplied the manpower to withstand Hannibal's invasion. By 201 BCE, following Scipio's victory at Zama in 202 and the peace that ended the Second Punic War, Rome controlled Italy, Sicily, Sardinia and Corsica, and a substantial part of the Iberian coast.

The peace of 201 BCE stripped Carthage of its Iberian holdings and its remaining overseas territory, required the surrender of most of its warships and its war elephants, imposed an indemnity of ten thousand talents payable over fifty years, and forbade the city to make war outside Africa, and within Africa without Roman consent. Carthage remained a wealthy city with a territory in North Africa. Beside it, Rome's ally Masinissa, whose Numidian kingdom had been enlarged by the war, occupied a position that would later matter a great deal. Only a few paragraphs of transition are needed here, because the campaign narrative belongs to Chapter 16.

The wider Mediterranean was not a Roman world. The eastern basin was dominated by the Hellenistic kingdoms that had emerged from the fragmentation of Alexander the Great's empire in the late fourth century BCE. Chapters 11 and 12 examined the classical Greek city states, but this textbook's outline does not devote a separate chapter to the Hellenistic period, so the essential background is summarized here. Three great monarchies competed for territory and prestige: the Antigonid kingdom of Macedon, ruling Macedonia and exercising influence over parts of Greece; the Seleucid kingdom, whose enormous territory stretched from Anatolia and Syria eastward; and the Ptolemaic kingdom of Egypt, wealthy from the Nile valley and holding overseas possessions. Smaller powers, such as the kingdom of Pergamum in western Anatolia and the maritime republic of Rhodes, exercised influence far beyond their size. In mainland Greece the Aetolian and Achaean Leagues, federations of cities, negotiated with kings and each other, while Athens, Sparta, and many lesser cities pursued their own interests.

This was a competitive, diplomatically sophisticated state system with its own traditions of arbitration, alliance, hostage taking, and rhetoric about the freedom of cities. Rome had already entered it in a limited way. During the Second Punic War, Philip V of Macedon allied with Hannibal, and Rome fought an indecisive First Macedonian War, ending in the Peace of Phoenice in 205 BCE, largely in alliance with the Aetolians. That conflict left Roman commanders with knowledge of Greek politics, Greek states with knowledge of Roman power, and Philip with unfinished ambitions in the Aegean.

In the west, the Iberian Peninsula was not an empty space awaiting occupation. Its inhabitants included the Iberian communities of the Mediterranean coast and southern valleys, the Celtiberians of the central plateau, the Lusitanians of the west, and many other peoples with distinct languages, political organizations, and military traditions, some organized in fortified towns, others in dispersed hill settlements. Mineral wealth, especially silver in the south, made the peninsula extremely valuable to Carthage before Rome, as examined in Chapter 16. Geography, described in Chapter 2, mattered: a high interior plateau, mountain barriers, arid and semi arid zones, and a scattered population made conquest from the coast slow and costly.

Finally, this chapter depends on institutional arrangements from the earlier republic. Roman magistrates served for a single year, generals were senators seeking public distinction, and the Senate decided assignments, funds, and diplomatic policy. The republic had no professional bureaucracy and no standing imperial civil service. Roman armies were largely composed of citizen soldiers supplemented by Italian allied contingents. Understanding how this modest institutional toolkit was stretched across three continents is a central task of the chapter.

Section 17.1

The Mediterranean after the Second Punic War

In 201 BCE a Roman observer might reasonably have described the world as one in which Rome had been fortunate to survive. Italy had been ravaged for more than a decade. Rural districts had been devastated, tens of thousands of citizens and allies had died, and some Italian allies had defected to Hannibal before being brought back. Rome had, however, emerged with a large and experienced army, a navy that had gained mastery of the western seas, and possession of Sicily, Sardinia, Corsica, and the eastern and southern coasts of Iberia. The Carthaginian empire in the west had been dismantled.

That victory did not make Rome master of the Mediterranean. The Seleucid and Ptolemaic kingdoms were vastly larger in territory and revenue than the Roman state, and their courts were sophisticated diplomatic centers with long traditions of statecraft. Macedon remained a formidable military power. Carthage retained its urban population, agricultural hinterland, and commercial networks, and it recovered economically with remarkable speed. Roman senators regarded these powers as neighbors, rivals, potential allies, or potential threats, but not as subjects.

The states of the eastern Mediterranean were themselves in motion. Philip V of Macedon was expanding his influence in the Aegean, attacking Rhodes, Pergamum, and cities in Thrace and the Aegean coast. Antiochus III, after campaigns in the east, was reasserting Seleucid claims over Syria and Anatolia against the weakened Ptolemaic kingdom. Rhodes and Pergamum, feeling threatened, looked for allies. Some of them turned to Rome. That fact is critical: the first decisive Roman entry into eastern politics after 201 came as a response to appeals, not as a Roman initiative alone.

For Rome the settlement of 201 had also created unfinished business in the west. Iberia now had to be held, since the departure of Carthage did not eliminate the local communities who had negotiated, fought, and cooperated with Rome and Carthage alike. In Africa, the treaty with Carthage entrusted Masinissa with a position that created lasting tension. For the next half century Roman policy would have to address all three regions, west, east, and Africa, at once, and the Senate's attention was constantly divided between them.

Section 17.2

Why Did Rome Continue to Expand?

Any explanation of Roman expansion must begin with the institutions that shaped Roman decisions. War was declared by the popular assembly on the recommendation of the Senate, but the Senate controlled diplomatic contact, allocated provinces and armies, and determined whether victory would be followed by annexation, withdrawal, or settlement. Magistrates were elected annually and, in the senatorial elite, public standing depended on military distinction. Consuls and praetors sought commands, victories, and triumphs, the processions celebrating successful generals, and wealth from booty and indemnities could be redistributed to soldiers, spent on public works, or used to build political influence. Such incentives did not compel any specific war, but they created a political culture in which military success was the most valued form of public achievement.

A second factor was security. Roman senators had recently faced the invasion of Italy and had reason to fear the emergence of another power capable of threatening Rome from across the sea. Philip V's alliance with Hannibal was a fresh memory, and Roman suspicion of Macedonian and later Seleucid ambitions was real, even if some Roman perceptions of danger were exaggerated or manipulated. The Roman conception of security was also unusually expansive. Under the practice of amicitia, formal friendship, and under treaties, Rome accumulated allies whose safety it was expected to protect. Anyone attacking a Roman friend could be represented as attacking Rome.

A third factor was the role of appeals. Greek states, Hellenistic kings, and Iberian and North African communities routinely sought Roman support against rivals. Envoys from Pergamum, Rhodes, and Athens came to Rome in 201 and 200 BCE to complain about Philip. Later, Eumenes II of Pergamum would denounce Perseus. Masinissa would press claims against Carthage. The Senate could decline these appeals, and sometimes did, but responding to them increased Rome's involvement and reputation as a powerful patron.

Finally, expansion had cumulative consequences. Treaties imposed obligations of enforcement. Hostages, indemnities, and territorial settlements had to be monitored. Defeated enemies who felt aggrieved might revolt, as in Iberia or Macedonia. Each victory also enhanced Rome's prestige, which made it more likely that others would seek Roman arbitration and more costly, in terms of reputation, for Rome to ignore disputes. Roman power was thus partly reproduced through the expectations it created.

These factors did not operate identically in every case. A war in Iberia, where governors fought for personal glory and silver, differed from a war in Greece, where the Senate deliberated over alliances, and from a war against Carthage, where the decisive influence appears to have been a combination of fear, resentment, and elite pressure. Modern historians have therefore moved away from single explanations, a debate examined in the Historical Debate feature in this chapter. Students should approach each intervention by asking what mixture of motives best explains it.

Section 17.3

Iberia: From Carthaginian War Zone to Roman Provinces

Chapter 16 described the campaigns through which Roman armies, most famously under Scipio, drove Carthaginian forces from Iberia. What that chapter could not treat is what followed: the transformation of a wartime occupation into a lasting territorial presence. When Carthage abandoned Iberia, Roman commanders were left in control of the coastal zone and some interior districts. The Senate at first kept commanders there through prorogation, the extension of a magistrate's authority beyond his year in office. In 197 BCE, it created two provinces, Hispania Citerior, "Nearer Spain," along the northeastern and eastern coast, and Hispania Ulterior, "Further Spain," in the south. The number of praetors was increased to six so that magistrates could be assigned to them annually.

The formation of these provinces did not mean that Rome controlled Iberia. It meant that Rome had assigned responsibility for territory to particular magistrates and intended to collect revenue from it. The immediate response was revolt. Uprisings in 197 BCE among communities in both provinces required Roman military reinforcement. In 195 BCE, the consul Marcus Porcius Cato campaigned in Hispania Citerior, defeating opponents, disarming communities, and reorganizing mining and revenue arrangements. Ancient accounts credit him with a large scale settlement, but they also show that Roman control remained fragile.

The Roman presence in Iberia rested on several institutions. Governors, holding imperium, commanded armies, held courts, and negotiated with local communities. Iberian communities were assessed a fixed tribute, stipendium, which resembled Carthaginian practice, and were also subject to occasional requisitions of grain and to military levies of auxiliary troops. Local elites, whose authority Rome recognized and sometimes reinforced, served as intermediaries. Rome also exploited the mineral wealth of the peninsula, especially the silver mines around Carthago Nova (New Carthage, modern Cartagena) and in the south, sometimes through state contracts awarded to Roman contractors and worked in part by enslaved and hired labor, matters explored further in Chapter 18.

Settlements in the region also began to reflect a Roman presence. According to Appian's later tradition, Scipio settled wounded soldiers at Italica, near modern Seville, in 206 BCE, though the nature of this earliest settlement is debated, and the site provides one possible early example, and Roman army camps, roads, and fortified positions marked the landscape. But throughout the first half of the second century BCE the region remained a zone of intermittent war, negotiated agreements, and shifting alliances. Tiberius Sempronius Gracchus, the father of the famous tribunes of Chapter 19, negotiated a well known series of treaties with Celtiberian communities around 179 and 178 BCE, and these arrangements were remembered as fair. Their stability, however, depended on the behavior of later governors.

The essential point is that Iberia was never conquered in a single stroke. Roman authority expanded through repeated campaigns, local treaties, surrenders, revolts, and re-subjugations that continued into the 130s BCE and, in the far northwest, much longer. The remaining narrative of that resistance appears in Section 17.10 after the chapter has surveyed the eastern wars that competed for the Senate's attention.

Section 17.4

Macedon, Greece, and the Second Macedonian War

The Second Macedonian War began in 200 BCE, only a year after the peace with Carthage, and its origins have long been debated. Philip V was pressing campaigns against Pergamum, Rhodes, and cities along the Aegean and Thracian coasts. Envoys from Pergamum, Rhodes, and Athens, which had been attacked by Macedonian allies, appealed to Rome. Polybius also reports that Philip and Antiochus III had concluded a secret agreement to divide Ptolemaic possessions, a claim that modern scholars treat with caution because the evidence is difficult to verify, though it may reflect real Roman fears.

The Roman decision was not smooth. The popular assembly, weary of war after Hannibal, initially voted against war in 200 BCE and only accepted it after senatorial argument. Roman motives probably included concern about Macedonian power, a wish to punish Philip for his cooperation with Hannibal, and responsiveness to the appeals of friends. The first campaigns were inconclusive. In 198 the consul Titus Quinctius Flamininus took command, a young senator who spoke Greek, admired Greek culture, and had ties to Greek diplomatic practice. Flamininus succeeded in detaching Achaean support from Philip and in building an anti Macedonian coalition including the Aetolian League, which contributed cavalry and light troops.

The decisive battle occurred at Cynoscephalae ("Dog's Heads") in Thessaly in 197 BCE. Philip's Macedonian phalanx, a dense formation of pikemen, was effective on level ground but vulnerable on broken terrain. On the hills of Cynoscephalae, fighting began in confused conditions and Roman legionaries, organized in more flexible units, exploited gaps in the phalanx, aided by Roman elephants and Aetolian cavalry. The Macedonian army was routed. The battle mattered less as an example of "legion versus phalanx" than as proof that the Antigonid monarchy could not defend its position in Greece against a coalition including Rome.

The peace terms were carefully judged. Philip was required to withdraw from all of his Greek holdings, surrender most of his fleet, pay an indemnity of one thousand talents, and give hostages, including his son Demetrius, who spent time in Rome. Macedon itself was not annexed and Philip remained king. Some Aetolians, expecting a share of the gains, were disappointed, and Aetolian resentment would later feed Roman conflict with Antiochus. The settlement also reflected Roman calculations that weakening Macedon without destroying it would keep the Greek world stable and prevent the Aetolians from becoming too strong.

Section 17.5

The "Freedom of the Greeks"

At the Isthmian Games of 196 BCE, Flamininus arranged for a herald to announce that the Senate and he had declared free, ungarrisoned, exempt from tribute, and entitled to their ancestral laws a set of Greek communities formerly subject to Philip V. The proclamation was received, according to Polybius and later writers, with extraordinary enthusiasm. Its language was familiar. Hellenistic kings had for generations proclaimed the "freedom" of Greek cities as a diplomatic gesture, often in competition with one another, and cities themselves prized eleutheria, freedom from foreign domination, and autonomia, the right to govern themselves by their own laws.

What the announcement meant in practice was less clear. Freedom in Hellenistic diplomacy did not mean the absence of powerful patrons. It usually meant that a city was not under a garrison or paying tribute to a king, but the same city might be bound by treaties to a stronger power and expected to act in accordance with its wishes. A benefactor who granted freedom acquired prestige and often a claim to gratitude. For Rome, proclaiming Greek freedom served several purposes at once. It represented the withdrawal of Macedonian garrisons as a liberation, appealed to Greek sentiment, weakened rivals who might claim to be the true protectors of Greek liberty, and allowed Rome to intervene later as guardian of the freedom it had granted.

Skeptical Greek observers, especially in the Aetolian League, argued from the start that Romans were merely replacing Macedonian domination with Roman domination. Other Greeks took the proclamation at face value. Flamininus withdrew Roman troops from Greece in 194 BCE after a war against Nabis, the ruler of Sparta, and the withdrawal reinforced the view that Rome sought no territory. Yet the Roman commander had also reorganized political arrangements in some cities, favoring elites who supported Rome, and the practice of Greek states sending embassies to Rome for arbitration grew steadily.

The result was an unstable equilibrium. Rome asserted that Greek communities were free but expected them to accept Roman guidance in foreign affairs. Greek states that failed to follow Roman preferences risked Roman displeasure. Because the Senate had committed itself publicly to Greek freedom, it faced reputational costs if it appeared to violate that freedom, but it also had the diplomatic prestige to decide what freedom meant. The contradiction between proclaimed autonomy and increasing Roman supervision was not a hypocrisy invented later. It was built into the Roman settlement of 196.

Historical Vocabulary
Freedom (eleutheria), Friendship (amicitia), and Clientela

Eleutheria and autonomia were Greek diplomatic terms describing a city's status as free from garrison, tribute, and external interference in its internal government. They did not mean complete independence in a modern sense. Amicitia, Latin for friendship, described formal relations between Rome and other states or rulers, involving mutual expectations of goodwill and, in practice, deference to the stronger party. The related Roman idea of clientela described personal relations of patronage and obligation, and modern historians often speak of "client kingdoms" or "client states" to describe rulers who were nominally independent but bound to Rome by such ties. Ancient writers did not use a single technical term for these relationships, and the modern label can imply a more uniform system than existed.

These vocabularies matter because misunderstandings between Romans and Greeks arose partly from differing expectations. Romans could regard "friendship" as a hierarchical relationship in which Rome's wishes prevailed, while Greek states used the same word to describe relations among roughly equal partners. Understanding the terms helps explain why interventions that seemed legitimate to Roman officials could seem oppressive to Greek observers.

Polybius, Histories, Book 18, on the Proclamation at the Isthmian Games, 196 BCE

Creator: Polybius of Megalopolis, Greek statesman and historian, later hostage and resident in Rome · Date: written in the mid second century BCE, describing events of 196 · Type: Contemporary Greek historical narrative, surviving partly in excerpts preserved in later collections. Livy, writing in Latin in the late first century BCE, gives a parallel account that depends on Polybius or a related tradition.

Context: Polybius came from the Achaean League and saw Roman power from both Greek and Roman perspectives. His account of the proclamation was composed decades later, when Rome's dominance was evident, and part of his purpose was to explain how the Mediterranean had come under Roman control.

Paraphrase, not a quotation Polybius reports that a herald announced that the Roman Senate and Titus Quinctius, the Roman commander, having defeated King Philip and the Macedonians, declared the named Greek communities free, without garrisons, without tribute, and living under their own ancestral laws. He describes the crowd's astonished and prolonged reaction, and the way the audience pressed forward to honor Flamininus, and he presents the moment as a striking demonstration of Roman conduct toward the Greeks.

Analysis: The passage shows how Roman policy was framed in the political vocabulary of Greek diplomacy and how contemporaries understood it as a public act with significance beyond the immediate settlement. It also shows that a well informed Greek writer treated the moment as important. But the source has limits. Polybius is not neutral. He writes from within the Roman orbit, and the lists of communities and the emotional description of the crowd may have been shaped by later interpretation. The proclamation describes Roman intentions, not their long term effect, and it does not record how Aetolian critics, Macedonian subjects, or the ordinary inhabitants of the liberated cities experienced Roman arbitration.

Questions for students:

  1. What does this source say, and what kind of status does it attribute to the named Greek communities?
  2. Who created it and for what possible purpose, and how might Polybius's position in Rome have shaped his account?
  3. What can historians learn from it about Greek and Roman political language and about Roman diplomacy?
  4. What can historians not establish from this source alone about the actual consequences of Roman "freedom" for Greek cities?
Section 17.6

Antiochus III and Rome Enters the Eastern Mediterranean

The withdrawal of Roman forces from Greece did not end Roman involvement. Antiochus III, restoring Seleucid authority in Anatolia and crossing into Thrace in 196 BCE, appeared to Roman observers to be reestablishing the kind of power Philip had held. Rome's Anatolian friends, Pergamum and Rhodes, feared him, and Roman envoys who met with Antiochus discovered that each side claimed rights the other rejected. Antiochus argued that Thrace and Anatolian cities were part of his ancestral inheritance. Rome maintained that the autonomy of Greek cities in Anatolia should be respected. The negotiations failed, but neither side wanted an immediate war.

The trigger came from Greece. Aetolian leaders, dissatisfied with Roman arrangements, invited Antiochus to become the liberator of Greece from Rome, a striking reversal of the 196 proclamation. Antiochus crossed to Greece in 192 with a small force, expecting broad support that did not materialize. In 191 BCE a Roman army under the consul Manius Acilius Glabrio defeated him at Thermopylae, and Antiochus withdrew to Asia Minor. Hannibal, who had taken refuge at Antiochus's court, urged bolder strategy but was largely ignored and played a limited role. Roman naval operations, supported by Rhodes and Pergamum, secured the Aegean.

Rome then took the war into Anatolia. The consul Lucius Cornelius Scipio, accompanied by his brother Scipio Africanus as adviser, led an army across the Hellespont. In the winter of 190 or early 189 BCE, at Magnesia ad Sipylum, the Roman and Pergamene army defeated the Seleucid army in a large battle. The Treaty of Apamea, concluded in 188 BCE, required Antiochus to withdraw from Anatolia west of the Taurus Mountains, pay an indemnity totaling fifteen thousand Euboic talents (with 500 due at once, 2,500 on ratification, and the remaining 12,000 in twelve annual installments of 1,000), surrender his war elephants and refrain from keeping them in future, restrict his fleet to ten decked warships, and provide hostages. Among the hostages was his son, later Antiochus IV.

Roman policy in the aftermath is revealing. Rome did not annex Anatolian territory. Instead, it granted large areas to its allies, including much of the former Seleucid possessions to Eumenes II of Pergamum and territory in Caria and Lycia to Rhodes. Roman commanders under Gnaeus Manlius Vulso campaigned against the Galatians, a Celtic speaking people who had settled in central Anatolia, and imposed their submission. The Aetolian League was reduced to a subordinate alliance. Rome withdrew its armies. The immediate result was a Mediterranean in which Roman victory had been overwhelming but Roman administration had barely begun.

The significance was not the battles but the new position Rome occupied. After Apamea, Roman decisions could determine the borders of Anatolian kingdoms, and states from Rhodes to Egypt sent embassies to Rome. Although Rome held no eastern province, it had become the ultimate arbiter of eastern disputes. A famous demonstration came in 168 BCE, when the Roman envoy Gaius Popilius Laenas met Antiochus IV during his invasion of Egypt and, according to ancient tradition, ordered him to withdraw before leaving the circle he had drawn around the king. Even if the story is embellished, it captures how contemporaries came to view Roman authority. That authority also affected regions beyond the Greek world. Around 161 BCE, the Judean leader Judas Maccabeus, in revolt against Antiochus IV's successors, sent an embassy to Rome and concluded a friendship agreement, as recorded in the biblical First Book of Maccabees, an example of how Jewish communities and leaders also treated Rome as a potential source of support.

Section 17.7

Perseus, Pydna, and the Destruction of Macedonian Power

Philip V died in 179 BCE and was succeeded by his son Perseus, who sought to restore Macedonian standing through diplomacy, marriage alliances, and cultivation of Greek goodwill. Perseus gained sympathy in parts of Greece, sometimes amid conflicts between poorer citizens and pro Roman elites, although historians dispute how consistently support for Macedon followed social or class divisions. This alarmed Rome's allies. Eumenes II of Pergamum, in particular, traveled to Rome in 172 to denounce Perseus, and the Senate, influenced by these accusations and its own suspicions, moved toward war. The Roman claims that Perseus was preparing aggression rest largely on accusations, and modern scholars often regard them as exaggerated.

The Third Macedonian War (171 to 168 BCE) began badly for Rome. Early commanders were ineffective, and some Greek communities, including Roman friends, hedged their loyalties. The turning point came in 168 with the election of Lucius Aemilius Paullus, an experienced general who reorganized discipline and supply and maneuvered Perseus out of his defensive position. At Pydna, on the coast of Macedonia, the Roman legions again defeated the Macedonian phalanx. The battle was decisive and swift, and Perseus, captured soon afterward, was later displayed in Paullus's triumph in 167 and died in Italian custody.

The settlement of 167 BCE was much harsher than that of 197. The Antigonid monarchy was abolished. Macedonia was divided into four separate republics, with restrictions on marriage and property ownership across their borders and a requirement to pay Rome an annual tribute equal to half the royal taxes previously collected. Mining was initially restricted. Illyria, where King Gentius had allied with Perseus, was divided into three districts. Ancient sources report that Paullus, on senatorial instructions, ordered the sack of a number of towns in Epirus that had sided with Perseus and the enslavement of a very large number of inhabitants, a figure preserved in ancient sources as 150,000. Such ancient numbers are not reliable as precise data, but the episode indicates that Roman treatment of opponents had become more severe.

The war reshaped the Greek world in other ways. Roman commissioners investigated Greek communities, and pro Macedonian leaders were removed, executed, or deported. Around a thousand Achaean leaders were taken to Italy without trial and held for years, among them Polybius. Rhodes, which had attempted to mediate, was punished by the loss of its Anatolian territories, and the Romans made Delos a free port under Athenian control in 166 BCE, redirecting commercial traffic and damaging Rhodian revenues. Even Pergamum found that Roman favor could be withdrawn. Roman treatment of its allies had become more transactional and coercive.

Section 17.8

From Hegemony to Provincial Rule in Greece

The arrangements of 167 did not endure. Resentment among Macedonians and dissatisfaction with divided government created fertile ground for a challenger. In 149 BCE, a man named Andriscus claimed to be a son of Perseus and raised a Macedonian revolt, defeating a Roman force before being defeated by Quintus Caecilius Metellus in 148. This Fourth Macedonian War convinced the Senate that the four republics model had failed. Macedonia was reorganized as a Roman province, governed by a Roman magistrate, with a fixed administrative framework. Ancient tradition and modern scholarship differ on the exact date of the provincial organization, but it followed the collapse of Andriscus's revolt, and the province was certainly in existence by the mid 140s.

Meanwhile, the Achaean League, which had originally cooperated with Rome, was increasingly divided. Among the many disputes was a controversy over Sparta's attempt to leave the League, in which Roman envoys were involved. Anti Roman leaders such as Critolaus, elected general of the League in 147, raised support among poorer citizens and rejected Roman mediation. War followed. The Achaean forces were defeated by Metellus and then by Lucius Mummius, who took Corinth in 146 BCE. Roman sources describe the sack of the city, the transportation of its artworks to Italy, and the sale of much of its population into slavery, though the scale and details are difficult to verify. The League was dissolved and its members were placed under Roman supervision, some communities paying tribute and others sustaining new constitutional restrictions.

It is essential to describe carefully what happened to Greece. Greece was not immediately organized as a province in 146 BCE. Roman commissioners, including Polybius, who helped craft acceptable arrangements, established a settlement in which Greek cities retained local institutions but recognized Roman supervision. The governor of Macedonia held oversight. Some cities remained formally free and exempt from tribute, for example Athens and Sparta, which had supported Rome. Others paid tribute. The differentiated treatment illustrates a Roman method: reward compliance, punish resistance, and vary the constitutional arrangement community by community. A province of Achaea in the formal sense, as a distinct administrative unit, emerged only later: Julius Caesar formed one temporarily in 46 BCE, and Augustus re-established Achaea as a separate province in 27 BCE.

The destruction of Corinth had considerable symbolic significance in later memory. Roman writers and later Greek writers recalled 146 as a moment of finality, and modern textbooks often use it that way. But what actually changed was the structure of power. The Greek leagues had lost the ability to act independently. Roman magistrates were now present in the Aegean as a permanent feature of political life. Greek civic elites redirected their attention to Rome, and the practice of sending embassies, seeking honors, and cultivating Roman patrons grew.

Section 17.9

Carthage after Hannibal and the Third Punic War

It would be a serious distortion to treat the Third Punic War as a postscript to Hannibal. Carthage after 201 BCE was not a defeated ruin. It was a functioning republic with an agricultural hinterland, seaborne commerce, a substantial civilian population, and a political system that continued to adapt. The city paid its indemnity in installments and, according to Livy, offered to pay the whole balance early in 191, a sign of its economic vitality. Hannibal himself held office as a magistrate in the 190s and attempted financial reforms before political enemies denounced him to Rome and he fled east, eventually dying in Bithynia in the early 180s BCE.

Tension arose from the position of Numidia. Masinissa, whose kingdom the treaty had strengthened, repeatedly encroached on territory the Carthaginians considered theirs, and Carthage repeatedly appealed to Rome. Roman commissions typically found in ways that favored Masinissa or left disputes unresolved. Because the 201 treaty prohibited Carthage from waging war without Roman permission, Carthage could not defend itself effectively. In about 151 BCE, after years of provocation, Carthaginian forces fought Masinissa, a step that Rome could portray as a violation of the treaty.

In 149 BCE Rome declared war. Later Roman tradition, especially the later stories about the elder Cato closing every senatorial speech with a call for the city's destruction, made the decision seem the product of fanatical hatred. The attribution of the famous phrase is uncertain, and even if the tradition contains a kernel of truth, it cannot alone explain policy. Fear of a recovering commercial power, the interests of Roman landowners and traders, the calculations of ambitious senators, and the desire to settle the Masinissa problem all may have contributed. The Roman demands escalated. Carthage surrendered hostages and then its weapons, only to be told that the city must be abandoned and its inhabitants resettle inland, away from the sea. The Carthaginians refused, and the war began.

The siege lasted three years. It ended after Scipio Aemilianus, elected consul in 147 in unusual circumstances because he was below the normal age, took command, blockaded the harbors, and stormed the city in spring 146 BCE after fierce street fighting. Much of Carthage was burned and its surviving population enslaved. The modern story that Romans plowed the ground and sowed it with salt is not supported by ancient evidence and appears to be a later invention. Carthage was destroyed, but archaeological and historical work shows that the site was later reoccupied, and Julius Caesar and Augustus refounded a Roman colony there.

Rome established a province of Africa in the former Carthaginian territory, with Utica, which had defected to Rome, as its base. Cities that had sided with Rome kept a degree of freedom. Numidia remained a kingdom, so that Roman power in North Africa combined a small directly administered province with a dependent neighboring monarchy. Punic language, religion, and civic institutions did not vanish. They continued in North African towns for centuries under Roman rule, a reminder that the destruction of a city was not the destruction of a culture.

Section 17.10

Resistance and the Long Conquest of Iberia

The wars in Iberia in the mid second century BCE were among the longest and most difficult fought by Rome in this period. They occurred at the same time as the Third Macedonian War, the Fourth Macedonian War, and the Third Punic War, and their length cannot be understood without considering how thinly Roman military resources were stretched. Two large conflicts dominated: the Lusitanian War in the west and the Celtiberian or Numantine wars on the central plateau.

In the Lusitanian War, which continued from about 155 to 139 BCE, Lusitanian raiders attacked Roman provincial communities, and Roman commanders suffered defeats. In 150 BCE the governor Servius Sulpicius Galba, after promising to settle Lusitanians on good land, disarmed a group that came to negotiate and had many of them killed or enslaved, an act of treachery denounced even in Rome. Galba escaped serious punishment. Viriathus, reputedly a survivor of the massacre or connected to its victims, became a leader from about 147. He defeated several Roman armies through mobility, ambush, and skilled use of terrain, and in 140 BCE a treaty was reached, and ratified at Rome, recognizing him as a friend of the Roman people. His success alarmed many senators. The next commander, Quintus Servilius Caepio, broke the peace and arranged the murder of Viriathus by his own companions in 139. Lusitanian resistance collapsed thereafter, though the wider region remained unstable.

In the central plateau, Celtiberian communities had fought Rome sporadically since the 180s. The renewed Celtiberian war of 153 to 151 BCE began when the Roman Senate objected to the expansion of the walls of the town of Segeda, and it exposed the sensitivity of Iberian communities to what they regarded as broken agreements. The most famous struggle was the Numantine War (143 to 133 BCE). The hilltop city of Numantia, in the upper Douro region, held out against a series of Roman commanders. In 137 BCE, Gaius Hostilius Mancinus was trapped and negotiated a treaty saving his army. The Senate repudiated the treaty and delivered Mancinus to the Numantines, who declined to accept him, a striking example of Roman willingness to disavow diplomatic commitments made under pressure.

Only in 134 BCE, with the appointment of Scipio Aemilianus, elected consul again and given the command, did Rome adopt an approach that succeeded. Scipio restored discipline to a demoralized army, isolated Numantia by an elaborate ring of siege works and forts, and starved the city into surrender in 133. Archaeological investigation of the siege camps around Numantia, first systematically explored in the early twentieth century, corroborates the literary tradition of extensive circumvallation. According to the surviving tradition, Scipio reserved fifty captives for his triumph, sold the remaining survivors into slavery, and destroyed the settlement. Ancient writers, including Roman authors, later drew from the city's resistance an image of Iberian courage that sometimes became part of modern Spanish national memory, and modern students should recognize that such uses of Numantia belong to later history rather than to the events themselves.

Even after 133 BCE, Rome's hold on Iberia was incomplete. The northwestern and northern regions remained unconquered until the campaigns of Augustus in the late first century BCE. The historical significance of these wars was twofold. They demonstrated the extraordinary difficulty of conquering decentralized societies in difficult terrain, and they revealed the weakness of Roman institutions when annual commands, ambitious generals, and short political horizons combined with treachery and incompetence. Iberia was also a training ground in which Roman commanders learned methods of siege warfare, logistics, and control of countryside that would shape later wars.

Section 17.11

How Roman Provincial Government Worked

The word provincia originally referred to the sphere of responsibility assigned to a magistrate with imperium, whether a war to be fought or an area to be governed. Only gradually did it come to mean a territorial unit governed for the long term. By the mid second century BCE, several regions, including Sicily, Sardinia and Corsica, the two Spains, Macedonia, and Africa, were provinces in this newer, territorial sense. Provincial organization was flexible and piecemeal. Arrangements could emerge from senatorial decisions, treaties, ordinances issued by commanders or governors, the continuation of existing local law and tax systems, and sometimes the work of senatorial commissions. Older scholarship spoke of a standard lex provinciae drawn up at each conquest, but the evidence does not support a universal, predictable procedure, and even the concept is disputed. The variety reinforces this chapter's argument that Rome built its empire without a single blueprint.

The senior official was the governor, a praetor or a promagistrate who held imperium for a year, sometimes extended. The governor commanded any troops in the province, exercised judicial authority, decided disputes, negotiated with communities, and was expected to defend the province. He was assisted by a quaestor, responsible for finances, and by a small staff of legates, friends, clerks, and attendants, a very small administrative entourage. It is essential to recognize how small this apparatus was. A province could contain hundreds of communities and hundreds of thousands of people. Roman governors could not directly administer them. They depended on local institutions, city councils, magistrates, and elites who collected taxes, kept order, and acted as intermediaries.

Provincial communities were treated differently according to their history. Some were stipendiariae, paying fixed sums or a portion of the harvest. Others were liberae et immunes, free and exempt from tribute, often because they had been early allies. Others were bound by treaties, foederatae. In Sicily, Rome retained an earlier system of taxation dating to the Syracusan king Hiero II, involving a tenth of the harvest. In Iberia and Africa, fixed tribute and levies were more common. The Roman state also relied on contractors, the publicani, private partnerships that bid for state contracts, from mining to customs to supplying armies. Over time, the involvement of such firms in provincial revenue would become politically and economically significant, as later chapters show.

The most serious weakness was accountability. Governors held office for one year, were largely unsupervised, and often regarded the province as an opportunity for wealth and prestige. Complaints of extortion were frequent. Provincial communities could send embassies to the Senate, but they required a Roman patron, and the Senate's response depended on politics. In 149 BCE, the Lex Calpurnia established a standing court at Rome to hear cases of extortion, a significant response to provincial complaints, though its jurors were senators and its effectiveness was limited. The court's establishment shows both that abuses were recognized and that the system relied on senatorial self regulation.

What emerges is a system of low cost, high dependence government. Rome extracted revenue and military resources and maintained order with minimal personnel, relying on local elites who gained status and protection from cooperation. That arrangement made Roman rule durable in many places, but it also embedded inequality, gave provincial elites leverage, and created continual opportunities for abuse. Chapter 22 will examine how imperial government later refined these methods, and Chapter 142 will place Roman provincial rule within the longer comparative history of European empires.

Historical Vocabulary
Provincia

Provincia originally meant the task or sphere of authority assigned to a Roman magistrate holding imperium, such as a war against a particular enemy, judicial duties in Rome, or the administration of a region. During the third and second centuries BCE, as Roman commanders were assigned to Sicily, Sardinia, and Iberia for extended periods, the word increasingly referred to the territory itself. The modern English "province," meaning a territorial administrative unit, derives from this later sense.

The distinction matters because it warns students against assuming that a Roman "province" was created at a single moment as a fully formed unit with fixed borders and a bureaucracy. In the second century BCE, provincial organization was gradual and improvised, and the difference between a magistrate's command and a permanent territorial government could be unclear.

Section 17.12

An Empire without a Single Imperial Blueprint

By about 130 BCE, the Mediterranean political system had changed profoundly. Rome ruled Italy through citizenship and alliance, governed several provinces directly, and exercised influence over a broad range of kingdoms, leagues, and cities through treaties, arbitration, hostages, and the threat of intervention. It had ended the Antigonid monarchy and the power of Carthage, humbled the Seleucid kingdom, and become the reference point for the diplomacy of the Ptolemaic kingdom, Rhodes, Pergamum, and the leagues. Yet it did not yet rule the whole Mediterranean, and it did not conceive of that as its goal.

The bequest of Pergamum illustrates how varied Roman acquisition remained. Attalus III, the last king of Pergamum, died in 133 BCE, leaving his kingdom to the Roman people in a will. The reasons for the bequest are uncertain: he may have hoped to protect the position of the royal family or Greek cities, or to prevent civil conflict. The Senate accepted the inheritance, but the transfer prompted a revolt led by Aristonicus, a claimant of royal descent, drawing support from parts of the population who resented the transfer and from those seeking social change. Roman armies and allied kings suppressed the revolt by 129 BCE, and Manius Aquillius organized the province of Asia. A Roman province was created in western Anatolia, not by conquest but by inheritance followed by war, and the details of taxation and administration continued to develop afterward.

The overall picture is of empire as a set of relationships, not a uniform system. Some regions were provinces under governors. Others were kingdoms whose rulers cultivated Roman favor. Others were formally free but subject to influence. The Senate improvised, and the vocabulary that later writers used to describe an empire did not fully exist. The period also saw Rome learning that expansion involved administrative and moral problems that its institutions had not been designed to address. Provincial governors accumulated wealth. Armies operated far from Rome for years. Rome now held vast numbers of enslaved captives, enormous revenues, and a network of dependent communities.

These consequences form the subject of the chapters that follow. Chapter 18 examines how conquest transformed Roman society, its households, its dependence on enslaved labor, its landholding, and its conceptions of citizenship. Chapter 19 turns to the political crisis that emerged as competition for command, wealth, and land strained republican institutions. Here it is enough to note that by the later second century BCE Rome faced a new kind of political problem: how a citizen republic, an Italian alliance, overseas provinces, dependent communities, and an unprecedented flow of resources and people could coexist within institutions designed for a much smaller world.

Historical Debate

Why Did Rome Conquer the Mediterranean?

The question: Was Roman expansion after the Second Punic War driven primarily by defensive concerns, by an aggressive militaristic culture, by responses to the requests and rivalries of others, or by a combination of these? Ancient writers such as Polybius and Livy supplied narratives, but modern historians have argued about how far Roman motives can be reconstructed from them.

Position 1, Mommsen: defensive and reluctant expansion. Theodor Mommsen, in the nineteenth century, gave the most influential statement of the view that Rome was drawn into empire by real threats and obligations to allies rather than by a program of conquest.
Position 2, Gruen: Hellenistic context and Greek agency. Erich Gruen emphasized the Hellenistic diplomatic world into which Rome entered, the role of Greek states in seeking Roman involvement, and Rome's frequent avoidance of direct annexation. His argument is less a restatement of defensive imperialism than a shift of attention toward local and regional dynamics.
Position 3, Harris: militaristic republic. William Harris stressed the social, political, and economic incentives that Roman militarism created, including glory, plunder, and triumphs, and argued that Roman claims of defense often masked aggressive expansion.
Position 4, Eckstein: multipolar anarchy. Arthur Eckstein responded to arguments that Rome was uniquely aggressive by contending that militarism was widespread in a competitive Mediterranean system with no overarching authority, and that Rome's distinctive strengths were its alliance network and capacity to mobilize manpower.

Evidence: These positions rely on the same narrative sources, treaties, diplomatic exchanges, and patterns of behavior. Defensive interpretations point to real Roman fears, senatorial debate, and moments of withdrawal. Aggressive and systemic interpretations point to repeated wars, the punitive settlements after 168 and 146, the political rewards of victory, and the Senate's readiness to break agreements, as in Iberia.

Why it matters: How historians assess Roman motives affects whether Roman power is understood as a series of reactions, as an expression of Roman institutions, or as an outcome of a wider Mediterranean system. It also affects how much responsibility is assigned to Roman decisions as opposed to Greek, Carthaginian, and Iberian choices.

Multiple Perspectives

Confronting an Expanding Rome

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor, and groups did not have equal power to shape events.

A Roman senator or commander: Foreign policy meant managing obligations to allies, judging threats, and seeking honor. Victory could bring triumph, wealth, and political standing, and reputations depended on visible success. A commander might sincerely believe that Rome protected its friends while also benefiting personally from war.
A Greek civic elite in a league city: Roman power offered protection against rivals and a chance to gain influence, but it also meant that local political success depended on Roman favor. Leaders faced choices among cooperation, hedging, and resistance, and mistakes could lead to deportation or destruction, as the fates of the Achaean detainees and of Corinth show.
An Iberian community on the central plateau: Roman governors arrived demanding tribute, grain, and soldiers, negotiated treaties, and sometimes broke them. Communities might accept Roman arrangements to preserve autonomy or fight to defend land, and the surrender of arms could expose them to massacre, as the Lusitanian episode of 150 BCE shows.
A Numidian or Carthaginian actor in North Africa: For Masinissa and his supporters, cooperation with Rome offered territory and security. For Carthaginians, Roman arbitration appeared systematically biased, and the treaty limits on defense made Rome's decisions decisive. The final Roman demand to abandon the city meant the destruction of a civic community.
An enslaved captive or displaced person: Wars ended in sales of captured populations, from Epirus to Carthage. For those seized, conquest meant loss of family, homeland, and legal status. Their experience is rarely recorded directly, and the evidence must be reconstructed from Roman narrative and later legal and economic sources.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Italian Alliance to Mediterranean Empire

What Changed

  • Rome's power extended from Italy to Iberia, North Africa, Macedonia, Greece, and Anatolia
  • The Italian model of citizenship, colonies, and bilateral alliance could not simply be reproduced overseas, so Rome improvised provinces, indemnities, hostage taking, tribute, and client relationships
  • Roman settlements became more punitive after about 168 BCE
  • Roman officials and armies were absent from Rome for long periods, and revenue from provinces and indemnities entered the Roman economy on a new scale

What Stayed the Same

  • The Senate, annual magistrates, and citizen armies remained the institutional core of Roman decision making
  • Rome continued to rely on local elites and existing institutions rather than creating a large imperial bureaucracy
  • The principle of differentiated treatment, rewarding cooperation and punishing resistance, continued from the Italian settlement

Who experienced change most directly? Defeated communities in Macedonia, Greece, Carthage, and Iberia faced the sharpest changes, including tribute, loss of autonomy, deportation, and enslavement. Senators gained wealth and prestige. Who experienced greater continuity? Rural populations in many provinces continued agricultural life under new tax collectors, and many Greek cities kept their own councils and festivals. Why does the pattern matter? It shows that empire was created by adapting old institutions to new tasks, and that the strains of that adaptation would shape the republic's later crisis.

Long-term causes

Rome's Italian alliance system, aristocratic competition for military honor, experience of invasion by Hannibal, the fragmentation of the Hellenistic world into competing states, and Rome's growing reputation as a patron.

Short-term causes

Appeals from Pergamum, Rhodes, and Athens; suspicion of Philip and Antiochus; Aetolian disappointment; Masinissa's pressure on Carthage; revolt and treachery in Iberia; senatorial debates and the ambitions of particular magistrates.

Event or transformation

A sequence of wars and settlements produced Roman provinces and a network of dependent states and communities across the Mediterranean.

Immediate effects

Indemnities, hostages, territorial grants to allies, destruction of Macedonian and Carthaginian power, and the extension of Roman influence to Anatolia and North Africa.

Long-term consequences

Growth of provincial administration, new forms of wealth and enslaved labor in Italy, and political pressures that Chapters 18 and 19 examine.

Historical complexity

The recurring mechanism was victory, settlement, new obligations, intervention, and new settlement. It was not an inevitable conveyor belt. The Senate sometimes withdrew, refused annexation, or hesitated, and outcomes depended on decisions by many actors.
Everyday Life and Social History

Living through Conquest

For Roman and Italian soldiers, Mediterranean expansion meant long absences from farms and families. Citizen soldiers, including those who served in Iberia, might be away for years, and evidence from Roman sources suggests that service in Iberia was sometimes feared and that levies were at times difficult to raise. Allied Italian contingents carried a heavy share of the burden, a matter with later political significance discussed in Chapter 19. Soldiers might gain booty, but many bore the costs of campaign, disease, and injury.

For provincial populations, Roman rule appeared as tax collectors, grain requisitions, garrisons, and courts. In Iberia, communities were required to supply auxiliary soldiers and grain and faced punishment for revolt. Mining districts brought together enslaved workers, hired laborers, and Roman contractors in harsh working conditions. In Greece, ordinary citizens of poorer social standing sometimes saw Rome as the ally of wealthy elites, while elites competed for Roman backing. Captives of war, from Epirus to Carthage, were sold into slavery, and slave markets such as Delos, made a free port in 166 BCE, became major centers of trade. The social effects of large scale enslavement, together with changes in landholding and family life, are examined in Chapter 18.

Evidence for all these experiences is uneven. Literary sources focus on generals, senators, and kings, while inscriptions and archaeology occasionally illuminate local life. The voices of Iberian villagers, Greek laborers, Punic townspeople, and enslaved captives have to be reconstructed indirectly, and historians should be cautious about assuming that elite descriptions represent the experience of the majority.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Rome, after defeating Carthage in the Second Punic War, became the dominant power of the Mediterranean and how it governed the territories it acquired. The central argument is that Roman expansion between 201 and about 129 BCE was neither a single predetermined plan nor a chain of merely defensive accidents. It emerged from an interaction of security fears, obligations to allies, appeals from other states, aristocratic competition, the attraction of wealth and prestige, and the cumulative consequences of earlier settlements.

In 201 BCE Rome was the strongest western Mediterranean power but not the ruler of the basin. The Hellenistic kingdoms, especially the Seleucid and Ptolemaic monarchies, were larger and wealthier, and Carthage retained economic strength. Rome's involvement in the East began through appeals from Pergamum, Rhodes, and Athens and through suspicion of Philip V. The Second Macedonian War ended at Cynoscephalae in 197, and Flamininus's proclamation of Greek freedom in 196 used the language of Hellenistic diplomacy to establish Rome as guardian of Greek liberty.

The proclamation created a durable contradiction. Freedom meant absence of garrison and tribute, not absence of influence, and Rome expected deference. When the Aetolians invited Antiochus III into Greece, Rome defeated him at Thermopylae and Magnesia, and the Treaty of Apamea in 188 removed Seleucid power from most of Anatolia. Rome then rewarded Pergamum and Rhodes rather than annexing territory, showing that expansion of influence did not always mean expansion of provincial government.

After 168 BCE, Roman behavior became harsher. The victory at Pydna ended the Macedonian monarchy, and the settlement divided Macedon, enslaved many Epirotes according to ancient sources, deported Achaean leaders, and punished Rhodes. The Fourth Macedonian War led to the province of Macedonia, and the Achaean War ended with the sack of Corinth in 146 and the dissolution of the League. Greece was placed under Roman supervision but was not yet formally a province, and Roman treatment varied community by community.

In the west, Roman provinces were created in Iberia in 197, but conquest proved slow. Lusitanian and Celtiberian resistance, especially the revolt of Viriathus and the defense of Numantia, exposed the limits of Roman military organization and the consequences of treachery and treaty violation. Numantia fell in 133 after a carefully organized siege under Scipio Aemilianus, though the northwest remained unconquered. Iberia demonstrates how misleading a rapid narrative of Roman conquest can be.

In North Africa, Carthage recovered economically after 201 while Masinissa pressed its borders and Roman arbitration favored him. Roman fear, senatorial pressure, and opportunity produced a decision for war in 149. The Third Punic War ended in 146 with the destruction of Carthage and the creation of the province of Africa, while Numidia remained a dependent kingdom. Later stories about salting the earth are not supported by ancient evidence.

Roman provincial government was small in scale and dependent on local institutions. Governors, quaestors, and small staffs commanded troops, administered justice, and collected tribute through local elites and, in some cases, contractors. Communities held different statuses: tribute paying, free, or treaty allies. The system was cheap for Rome but vulnerable to abuse, as the creation of an extortion court in 149 indicates. The bequest of Pergamum in 133 and the organization of Asia after a revolt showed that expansion continued through diverse means.

Throughout the chapter, non Roman actors appear as decision makers. Greek leagues, Hellenistic kings, Numidians, Carthaginians, and Iberian communities all sought to use, resist, or accommodate Rome, and Roman power grew in the political spaces created by their rivalries. Historians debate how to weigh these factors, and the historiographical discussion in the chapter emphasizes that different interventions require different explanations.

The chapter also shows the limits of a simple narrative of Roman triumph. Roman armies were repeatedly defeated in Iberia, Roman decisions were sometimes reversed, and Roman institutions were strained by the burdens of overseas government. By the later second century BCE, Rome had become an imperial power without a corresponding imperial administration. That imbalance, together with the flow of wealth, captives, and command opportunities into the Roman elite, created the social and political problems examined in Chapters 18 and 19.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Provincia
Originally the sphere of responsibility of a Roman magistrate with imperium; by the second century BCE, increasingly a territory governed by an annually assigned magistrate or promagistrate.
Imperium
The supreme authority to command troops and exercise judicial power held by Roman consuls, praetors, and promagistrates.
Prorogation
The extension of a magistrate's imperium beyond his year in office, allowing continuity of command in wars and provinces.
Praetor
A Roman magistrate below the consuls who held judicial duties and, from 197 BCE, was increasingly assigned to provincial governance; the number of praetors rose to six that year.
Quaestor
A junior magistrate responsible for financial administration, often assigned to assist a provincial governor.
Socii
The Italian allies of Rome, bound by treaties and required to supply troops; distinct from provincial subjects.
Hellenistic kingdom
One of the monarchies, such as the Antigonid, Seleucid, Ptolemaic, and Attalid states, that arose after Alexander the Great and dominated the eastern Mediterranean.
League (koinon)
A federation of Greek cities, such as the Achaean or Aetolian League, coordinating foreign policy and military affairs.
Eleutheria
Greek term for freedom of a city from foreign control, understood in Hellenistic diplomacy as freedom from garrison and tribute rather than absolute independence.
Amicitia
Formal friendship between Rome and another state or ruler, involving mutual expectations and, in practice, Roman superiority.
Client kingdom
A modern term for a kingdom nominally independent but dependent on Rome for security and legitimacy; ancient writers did not use a single equivalent term.
Indemnity
A payment imposed on a defeated state, usually in installments, as at Carthage in 201 and at Apamea in 188 BCE.
Talent
A large ancient unit of weight, commonly used for silver, in which indemnities were reckoned; modern conversions vary and should be treated as approximate.
Hostage
A person, often of royal or elite family, held by Rome as a guarantee of compliance with a treaty.
Triumph
A Roman procession celebrating a general's major victory, awarded by the Senate and a source of political prestige.
Legati
Senatorial envoys or deputies, including the commissions of ten who helped organize settlements after conquest.
Stipendium
A fixed tribute or payment imposed on provincial communities, particularly in Iberia and Africa.
Publicani
Private contractors who bid for state contracts, including mines, customs, and supplies.
Free city
A community granted privileged local autonomy under Rome, often because of early alliance, sometimes accompanied by immunity from taxation; freedom and tax exemption were related but not identical privileges, and either could be revoked.
Lex provinciae
A modern term for the charter or settlement of a province; older scholarship assumed a standard document for each, but current work stresses that provincial arrangements varied and that the concept is disputed.
Phalanx
The dense pike formation used by Macedonian and other Hellenistic armies, effective on level ground but less flexible than the Roman legion in broken terrain.
Defensive imperialism
An interpretation, associated with Mommsen and others, holding that Rome expanded chiefly in response to perceived threats; now widely regarded as incomplete.
Extortion court
The standing court established by the Lex Calpurnia in 149 BCE to hear provincial complaints of illegal exactions by Roman officials.
Celtiberians
The peoples of the central Iberian plateau, who spoke a Celtic language and resisted Rome for generations.
Lusitanians
The peoples of western Iberia, opponents of Rome in the mid second century BCE, best known through the resistance of Viriathus.
Numidia
The North African kingdom of Masinissa, an ally of Rome that remained a dependent kingdom after the destruction of Carthage.
Circumvallation
A ring of siege works built around an enemy city to cut it off, used by Scipio Aemilianus at Carthage and Numantia.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains how a Mediterranean political system came into being without a master plan. The transformation of Rome from an Italian power into the arbiter of the Mediterranean occurred through decisions that were often responsive, contested, and improvised, yet whose cumulative effect was to bind Iberia, Greece, Macedonia, North Africa, and Anatolia into a single sphere of Roman power. Understanding that process guards against two opposite errors: treating Roman conquest as inevitable, and treating it as accidental.

The chapter also reveals how empire depended on the actions of those who were not Roman. Greek leagues, Hellenistic kings, Masinissa, Carthaginian councils, and Iberian war leaders each contributed to the pace and shape of Roman expansion, sometimes inviting intervention, sometimes resisting it heroically, sometimes misjudging Roman intentions. Their history is not merely a background to Roman triumph. It is part of the history of the ancient Mediterranean as a shared space, of which the European regions of Iberia, the Balkans, and Greece were integral parts alongside North Africa and Anatolia.

For Volume I, the chapter marks a transition from the world of independent Greek city states, Punic Carthage, and Hellenistic monarchies to a Mediterranean increasingly organized by Roman power. Many features that later Europeans would associate with Rome, provincial administration, Latin as a language of empire, and law adapted to diverse communities, grew from the improvised arrangements described here. But Roman rule in this period was uneven, sometimes brutal, and always dependent on local partners, and the chapter has tried to show that later ideas of a seamless Roman empire should not be projected backward.

Chapter 18 now turns from the map of empire to the society living within it, asking how conquest transformed Roman households, slavery, landholding, and citizenship. Chapter 19 will then examine the political crisis produced when institutions designed for a city republic confronted the scale of the empire it had acquired. The story of Roman expansion in this chapter is therefore not an ending but the beginning of the problems that would eventually transform the republic itself.
