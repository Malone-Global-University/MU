Orthodox Christianity and the Slavic World

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

In 862 or 863 a ruler in central Europe asked a Byzantine emperor for teachers. What arrived, with two brothers from Thessalonica, was not simply a church but a new way of writing: an alphabet fitted to Slavic speech, a body of translated scripture and prayer, and a generation of disciples who would carry that achievement far beyond the court that had requested it.

The ruler was Rastislav of Great Moravia, whose power lay in the river valleys north of the middle Danube, in lands now divided among the Czech Republic, Slovakia, Austria, and Hungary. The emperor was Michael III, whose government in Constantinople had only recently emerged from the long controversy over images examined in Chapter 37. The two brothers were Constantine, later known by his monastic name Cyril, and Methodius. Archaeologists have uncovered stone churches, fortified residences, and cemeteries at Moravian sites such as Mikulčice and Staré Město, and these remains show that Christian worship already existed in Rastislav's realm before the brothers came. The mission therefore cannot be described as the conversion of an unchristianized people by a Byzantine government, and much of this chapter is devoted to explaining why that older picture is inadequate.

Chapter 37 ended with the Byzantine state in the 840s again possessing the institutional confidence to project clergy, books, liturgy, learning, and diplomacy beyond its borders. Chapter 38 examines what happened when that capacity met Slavic-speaking societies. The assigned subject is missionary activity, Cyril and Methodius, literacy, liturgy, political conversion, and cultural transmission. The central argument is that Byzantine Christianity spread through translation and adaptation as much as through political power. The brothers helped create a written Slavic Christian culture that crossed what would later become the boundary between Latin and Orthodox Europe. That culture suffered an institutional defeat in Moravia after the death of Methodius in 885, but was reborn and made durable farther south, above all in Bulgaria.

The chapter title contains a deliberate tension. "Orthodox Christianity" is partly a retrospective label. In the 860s there was no clearly demarcated Catholic church on one side and Orthodox church on the other. The Moravian mission was sponsored by Constantinople, but its leaders sought and received authorization from Rome, worked in lands claimed by Bavarian and Frankish bishops, and left a literary tradition that would flourish in Orthodox Bulgaria, Catholic Croatia, and eventually Rus. The later institutional and theological separation of Latin and Greek Christianity belongs to Chapter 40. Here, the careers of the brothers serve as one of the book's best demonstrations that ninth-century ecclesiastical relationships do not fit the later confessional map.

Equally, "the Slavic world" is a convenient phrase rather than a description of a single medieval society. Slavic-speaking communities lived under Frankish overlordship, Moravian princes, Bulgar khans, Byzantine officials, Adriatic duke-princes, and local leaders whose authority left almost no written trace. They shared related languages, not a common state, church, or political identity. The chapter therefore treats the spread of Christian literacy as the entanglement of several distinct regional histories: Moravia and Pannonia, Bohemia, the Adriatic and Croatian lands, and Bulgaria.

The evidence is unusual and demands careful handling. The principal narrative sources are the Slavonic Lives of Constantine and Methodius, saints' lives composed within the circle of the brothers' followers. They are valuable and relatively early, but they are hagiography and not neutral reports. They can be checked against papal correspondence, Latin sources produced in the Bavarian church, Byzantine accounts of Bulgaria, linguistic evidence, early manuscripts, and archaeology. Reading these sources against one another is one of the chapter's methodological lessons.

The chapter's place in Part VI is that of a hinge. Chapters 36 and 37 traced the survival and recovery of the Byzantine state. Chapter 38 shows recovery turning into cultural transmission. Chapter 39 will then examine the political communities, Bulgaria, Serbia, and Kievan Rus, that built states on foundations partly laid here, and Chapter 40 will examine the widening separation between Latin and Greek Christianity. Within Volume II, which traces the formation and diversification of medieval Latin, Byzantine, Orthodox, northern, central, and eastern Europe, the chapter shows that eastern and central Europe were created through exchanges that moved in several directions at once.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Moravian mission of 863 cannot be understood as the conversion of an unchristianized population by Byzantium.
  2. Distinguish among "Slavs," Slavic-speaking communities, and the medieval states and polities in which they lived.
  3. Describe the backgrounds of Constantine-Cyril and Methodius and the Byzantine missionary and diplomatic world that prepared them.
  4. Analyze the political and ecclesiastical motives that shaped Rastislav's request to Constantinople, including competition with Frankish and Bavarian clergy.
  5. Explain the creation of Glagolitic and Old Church Slavonic, and correct the common claim that Cyril invented the Cyrillic alphabet.
  6. Assess the significance of translation and vernacular liturgy, and evaluate the dispute over liturgical languages without exaggerating its place in western church doctrine.
  7. Interpret the relationship of the mission to Rome and show why an "Orthodoxy against Catholicism" reading is anachronistic.
  8. Trace the career of Methodius, his conflicts with Bavarian clergy, and the dispersal of his disciples after 885.
  9. Evaluate Boris of Bulgaria as an active ruler negotiating among Constantinople, Rome, and other powers rather than as a passive recipient of Byzantine culture.
  10. Explain why the Slavonic literary tradition became institutionally durable in Bulgaria, and identify the roles of Clement, Naum, Preslav, and Ohrid.
  11. Contextualize cultural transmission as translation, selection, and adaptation rather than one-way Byzantinization.
  12. Compare the different Christian futures of Bohemia, Croatia, Bulgaria, and the later Orthodox Slavic world, and use source criticism to evaluate saints' lives as historical evidence.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did missionary diplomacy, vernacular literacy, translation, liturgy, and ruler conversion transform the relationship between Byzantium and Slavic-speaking societies?
  2. Why did the Cyrillo-Methodian legacy develop differently in Moravia, Bohemia, Croatia, and Bulgaria?
  3. What does the existence of churches in Moravia before 863 change about how historians should describe the Byzantine mission?
  4. In what sense is "Orthodox" an accurate, and in what sense a misleading, label for the Christianity examined in this chapter?
  5. Why might a ruler such as Rastislav or Boris prefer a vernacular church, and what political risks and benefits did that choice carry?
  6. How should historians weigh the evidence of saints' lives against papal letters, Latin ecclesiastical sources, manuscripts, and archaeology?
  7. Why did a literary movement that failed institutionally in Moravia become permanent in Bulgaria?
  8. How does the creation of Slavonic Christian literature challenge the idea that cultural transmission from Byzantium meant simple Greek replacement?

Historical Context

By the mid ninth century the lands between the Elbe, the Adriatic, the Danube, and the Black Sea were a zone where the interests of several powers overlapped. To the west, the Carolingian world examined in Chapter 31 had fragmented after the 840s into successor kingdoms, among them the realm of Louis the German, whose bishops and counts dominated the Bavarian frontier. Louis's church was strongly shaped by the archdiocese of Salzburg and the bishopric of Passau, which regarded the Danubian and Pannonian lands as their missionary and jurisdictional field. Chapter 32 examines the pressures of this period on western Europe, and Chapter 35 examines the expansion of Latin Christendom into Bohemia, Poland, and Hungary. The present chapter explains why that expansion was not the only possibility in the center of the continent.

To the south and east lay the Byzantine Empire, which had weathered the territorial losses and religious controversies discussed in Chapters 36 and 37. Constantinople's effective power in the Balkans was uneven. It held coastal cities on the Adriatic and Aegean and the Thessalonian hinterland, but much of the interior was inhabited by Slavic-speaking communities, in many places organized under local leaders, and to the northeast lay the Bulgar polity south of the Danube, which had absorbed a large Slavic-speaking population. Byzantium was thus already a state that governed, taxed, and sometimes Christianized Slavic speakers. Thessalonica, a major city whose hinterland was heavily Slavic-speaking, was a place where Greek and Slavic speech met daily.

Rome in the ninth century was a papacy with ambitions but without the administrative reach of later centuries. The popes of the period, Nicholas I, Adrian II, and John VIII, sought to assert Roman authority over regional churches, including the Balkans and the Danube, and they competed in that effort with both Frankish bishops and the patriarchs of Constantinople. The contested jurisdiction of Illyricum, the mixed Latin and Greek ecclesiastical heritage of the Balkan provinces, was one source of tension. Another was the dispute between Pope Nicholas I and Patriarch Photios in the 860s, which shows that conflicts between Rome and Constantinople were real but should not be mistaken for a final rupture. Chapter 40 returns to the longer trajectory of separation.

The wider religious setting included the aftermath of iconoclasm. The restoration of images in 843, treated in Chapter 37, strengthened the confidence of the Byzantine church and encouraged its patriarchs and emperors to see missionary expansion as a sign of renewed legitimacy. Learning also revived, particularly in Constantinople, where Photios and others cultivated classical and patristic scholarship. The brothers from Thessalonica emerged from this intellectual environment, even if they were not themselves part of the highest ecclesiastical circle.

The evidence base is distinctive. Slavic-speaking societies of this period produced almost no writing before the mission, so their own earlier voices are largely invisible. For Moravia, historians rely on archaeology, Latin ecclesiastical writing from Bavaria, papal letters preserved in later collections, and the Slavonic Lives of Constantine and Methodius. For Bulgaria they use Byzantine sources, papal responses to Bulgarian questions, and later Slavonic manuscripts. Dating those manuscripts, and identifying the places where they were copied, remains a specialized field. Students should remember that most of what is known about the Cyrillo-Methodian mission comes from people committed to its memory.

Section 38.1

Christianity and the Slavic-Speaking World before Cyril and Methodius

Any account of Slavic Christianity must begin by dismantling the blank-slate model, in which pagan Slavs wait passively for Byzantium to offer them the faith. By the 860s Christianity had already reached many regions where Slavic was spoken, through Latin, Greek, local, and mixed networks. In the Balkans, Slavic-speaking communities had been settling for generations in areas that were formerly Roman provinces with Christian bishoprics, and the Christian population of the coastal cities of Dalmatia and the Aegean never disappeared. In the Adriatic hinterland, Frankish expansion under Charlemagne and his successors encouraged baptism and church foundation among Croatian and other leaders. In Pannonia and Moravia, missionaries from Bavaria, Salzburg, Passau, and Aquileia had been active for decades. Slavic rulers on the Danube, such as Pribina, who built a church at Nitra consecrated by the archbishop of Salzburg in the 820s, belonged to a Christian world by choice and alliance. In 845 a group of Bohemian leaders was baptized at Regensburg, an event that shows the western reach of Frankish missionary diplomacy.

Great Moravia itself possessed churches before Cyril and Methodius arrived. Excavations at Mikulčice, Staré Město, Modrá, and other sites reveal stone churches, graves with Christian features, and the material culture of a ruling class in contact with Frankish, Italian, and Danubian networks. These remains do not tell historians what the inhabitants believed, how deep Christianity went among ordinary people, or how many continued older practices. They do establish that a Christian infrastructure and a Latin clergy existed, and that Rastislav was already participating in a Christian political culture. This is why scholars now regard older descriptions of the mission as the conversion of pagans as inadequate.

The point about Christianity's presence is joined to a point about political and linguistic variety. The expression "the Slavs" suggests a single people, but the sources and archaeology reveal many communities of related languages living under very different conditions. Some were subjects of the Byzantine emperor in the Thessalonian hinterland. Others lived under Bulgar rulers, Frankish overlords, Moravian princes, or the Croatian dukes of the Adriatic coast. A Christian from Salzburg and a Christian from Thessalonica might each regard Slavic-speaking neighbors as a missionary field, and neither group shared a common ecclesiastical loyalty with the other. Understanding those competing missionary claims is essential to the chapter.

Finally, the chapter title itself needs qualification. "Orthodox" in modern usage identifies a family of churches in communion with Constantinople and with one another and separate from the Roman Catholic church. In the ninth century, no such clean division existed. The churches of Rome and Constantinople were in contact, in disagreement over particular questions, and in rivalry over specific territories, but they still belonged, in the minds of their members, to one Christian church. The adjective "Orthodox" has traditionally also meant "right believing," a claim every Christian community made for itself. The brothers' mission was Byzantine in sponsorship, language, and training, but it is better described as eastern Christian in origin and Roman, Greek, and Slavic in operation. Chapter 40 will explain how the later separation emerged.

Section 38.2

Constantine, Methodius, and the Byzantine Missionary World

The brothers who led the mission came from Thessalonica, the second city of the Byzantine Balkans, a port with a Greek urban elite and a countryside heavily settled by Slavic speakers. Their family was apparently of official standing, and this background provided education and connection. Methodius, the elder, served in some administrative capacity in a Slavic-speaking district before entering monastic life, according to the Life that bears his name, and so had practical experience of Byzantine government and of the lands where Slavic speech was used. Constantine was educated in Constantinople, taught philosophy there, and was associated with the learned circle around the court and patriarchal school. His familiarity with the Slavic speech of the Thessalonian environment later proved decisive.

The name "Cyril" requires care. Constantine assumed it as a monastic name only near the end of his life in Rome, in 869. For his earlier career, historians use "Constantine" or "Constantine-Cyril," and this chapter does the same. The traditional pairing "Cyril and Methodius" is therefore a convention shaped by later memory. It is, nevertheless, the established English form and is used for the combined mission and its legacy, as in "Cyrillo-Methodian."

Before Moravia, the brothers had participated in Byzantine diplomatic and missionary activity. Constantine's mission to the Khazars, a steppe polity north of the Caucasus and the Black Sea whose elite had adopted Judaism, is known mainly from the Life of Constantine. It is valuable here only for what it shows about preparation. Constantine engaged in religious debate, learned to operate in multiple linguistic environments, and, according to the tradition, recovered relics associated with Pope Clement at Cherson in the Crimea, relics he later brought to Rome. The episode also reminds students that the Byzantine missionary world extended to the Black Sea and Caucasus. Missionary diplomacy was an ordinary instrument of imperial relations: emperors sent clergy along with envoys, presents, and titles, and rulers who accepted baptism entered a relationship with the emperor that the Byzantine court understood in political as well as religious terms.

Neither brother should be turned into a modern national figure. They were Byzantine subjects who wrote and spoke Greek, had mastered Slavic, and acted within Roman imperial and Christian institutions. The later veneration of "Saints Cyril and Methodius" by many Slavic nations, and their designation as patrons of Europe in the late twentieth century, are examples of historical memory rather than evidence for ninth-century national consciousness. The Master Instructions for this series ask that later memory be kept distinct from historical reconstruction, and the distinction matters especially here.

Section 38.3

Rastislav's Request and the Moravian Mission, 862–863

Rastislav had become prince of Moravia in the mid 840s with Frankish support, and by the early 860s he sought to reduce Frankish control. Louis the German's armies had campaigned against him, and Bavarian bishops provided much of the clergy for his churches. Dependence on Latin clergy carried political consequences, because bishops and priests from Salzburg and Passau were also agents of Frankish influence, held property and rights in the region, and prayed for the Frankish king. A ruler who wanted greater independence had reason to look for ecclesiastical support elsewhere, and the Byzantine emperor, whose own interests lay in cultivating allies against the Franks and Bulgars, was a plausible source.

According to the Life of Constantine, Rastislav asked Michael III to send a teacher who could instruct his people in their own language. The Life presents the request in religious terms, and the later Life of Methodius similarly emphasizes the instruction of a Christian people. Historians do not dismiss this religious dimension. Christian instruction in the vernacular could meet a real pastoral need, because Latin services conducted by foreign clergy were poorly understood by Slavic congregations. They also recognize that the political dimension was at least as strong. A church using Slavic books and trained Slavic clergy would be less dependent on Bavarian priests and less open to Frankish jurisdictional claims.

Michael III's government responded, within an ecclesiastical and intellectual environment strongly influenced by Patriarch Photios, by sending Constantine and Methodius. The Byzantine government gained an opportunity to extend its influence into central Europe, pressure Frankish power at a distance, and enhance the prestige of Constantinople, just as the Bulgar ruler to the south was weighing his own religious alignment. This interest was also real, but it did not mean that the mission was an instrument of imperial conquest. Constantinople had no army in Moravia and no authority to appoint bishops there. The mission offered resources, trained teachers, and a Slavic written and liturgical program, and it could succeed only if Moravian rulers found them useful.

The mission began in 863 in a political environment that was complex and unstable. The Bavarian clergy had cause for resentment. The brothers' arrival was a challenge to established claims, and Latin clergy resisted the use of Slavonic worship. For the first years the brothers taught pupils, translated, and began to train clergy. The mission's cultural work, treated in the next sections, proved more lasting than the political settlement that had occasioned it. Students should recall that Rastislav himself was deposed in 870 by his nephew Svatopluk and handed over to the Franks, an outcome that shows how limited the protection of a Moravian ruler was.

Section 38.4

Glagolitic and the Creation of a Slavic Literary Language

The mission's most original achievement was the creation of a writing system for Slavic. The tradition holds that Constantine devised, or led the creation of, an alphabet today called Glagolitic. Scholars accept the association of Glagolitic with the Moravian mission, even though no manuscript survives in Constantine's hand and the details of how the alphabet was constructed are uncertain. The alphabet was designed with Slavic sounds in mind, and it handled sounds, such as certain palatalized and nasal vowels, that Greek and Latin letters did not easily represent. It was an alphabet with letter forms that look unfamiliar to readers of Greek or Latin, rounded and looped in the earliest surviving specimens, and it drew on a variety of graphic inspirations rather than simply adapting Greek.

The language written in this script is conventionally called Old Church Slavonic, and it is the earliest Slavic literary language. Its linguistic base was a South Slavic dialect, associated with the speech of the Thessalonian region, which Constantine and Methodius knew from childhood. But Old Church Slavonic became more than a local dialect. It was a standardized literary and liturgical language, shaped by the demands of translation, and usable across a wide area of Slavic speech in the ninth century, when the differences among Slavic dialects were still small. The label should not be confused with any modern language. Old Church Slavonic was not Russian, Bulgarian, Serbian, or Macedonian in the modern sense, and later national traditions that claim it as exclusively their own project their own boundaries backward.

A crucial correction must be made explicit. Cyril did not invent the Cyrillic alphabet. Glagolitic is the script associated with his mission. Cyrillic developed later, especially in the Bulgarian cultural environment of the late ninth and early tenth centuries, on a predominantly Greek graphic foundation, with additional signs for Slavic sounds. It was named in honor of Cyril by later tradition, but the attribution is not historical. Its extensive use of familiar Greek letterforms made it more closely compatible with Greek scribal practice, and it ultimately became the dominant script of the Orthodox Slavic world. Glagolitic, however, did not vanish. It survived in Croatian Catholic contexts for centuries and remains a reminder that Slavic literacy began with an alphabet that most later readers do not recognize.

Historians should also be careful about the meaning of literacy. The invention of a script did not produce mass literacy. The first readers were clergy, court officials, monks, pupils in mission schools, and members of the elite. Books were expensive, made of parchment copied by hand, and used in churches and monasteries. Even so, giving a language its own written form had effects that went beyond clerical usage. It established a standard for spelling and grammar, created a vocabulary for abstract ideas, and enabled the creation of a body of texts that later generations could copy and extend.

Section 38.5

Translation, Scripture, and the Slavic Liturgy

Writing mattered because it made it possible to translate. The brothers and their pupils translated portions of scripture, especially the Gospel readings used in worship and the Psalms, along with liturgical books, prayers, and other texts needed to conduct services. Tradition holds that the work began with the opening of the Gospel of John, and although the details are reported in a saint's life rather than a neutral record, it is plausible that a mission needing liturgical texts began with the readings that formed the core of worship. The translators also trained clergy to use Slavonic in worship, which was as important as the books themselves.

Translation was not mechanical substitution. Slavic had no established vocabulary for much of Christian theology, scripture, law, and ritual, and the translators had to coin or adapt terms. They used Slavic roots, formed calques on Greek expressions, and borrowed from Greek and Latin where no equivalent was available. They also had to decide how much to adapt the syntax of their sources. These decisions created the lexicon of the new literary language. When historians say that Old Church Slavonic was a creation and not merely a transcription, they mean that translation was an act of cultural production, in which theology, law, and abstract thought were made possible in a language that had not previously expressed them in writing.

The introduction of Slavonic worship provoked conflict. Some clergy argued that the proper languages for liturgy were Hebrew, Greek, and Latin, the languages of the inscription on the cross according to the Gospel narrative. The Life of Constantine presents this view as a "heresy" of those who recognized only three languages, and portrays Constantine answering that God sends rain and sun on all peoples alike. This argument must be placed in perspective. The "three languages" position was not official doctrine of the western church. It was advanced by particular clerical opponents, partly for jurisdictional reasons, and it was criticized by the Cyrillo-Methodian circle and, at important moments, by Rome itself. Many Latin clergy accepted that vernacular preaching and instruction were permissible, and the question of whether the liturgy itself could be celebrated in a vernacular was disputed and was resolved differently at different times.

The debate shows that the issue was not simply a contest between a Greek East that permitted vernaculars and a Latin West that forbade them. The Constantinopolitan church generally used Greek, but Eastern Christianity more broadly already included long established Georgian, Armenian, Syriac, and other vernacular literary and liturgical traditions, and Byzantine Christians themselves lived in multilingual environments. The innovation was Slavonic worship itself. It reflected a missionary strategy that made the vernacular a vehicle of pastoral care and political attachment, and it depended on patrons willing to support it.

Section 38.6

Rome, Papal Approval, and the Problem with an East-West Story

After several years in Moravia and Pannonia, Constantine and Methodius set out for Rome in the later 860s. The reasons were practical. Their disciples needed ordination, and the Bavarian bishops, who regarded the mission as an intrusion, were unlikely to provide it. Venice, where the brothers stopped on the way, was the setting of a dispute with clergy who rejected Slavonic worship. According to the Life of Constantine, he answered them there with arguments from scripture. The location also shows how the Adriatic ports linked Constantinople, Rome, and the Slavic interior.

In Rome the brothers were received by Pope Adrian II, who had succeeded Nicholas I in 867. The sources report that the relics of Pope Clement, which Constantine had brought from Cherson, helped to secure a favorable welcome, and that the pope sanctioned the Slavonic books, which were placed on the altars of Roman churches, and ordained the brothers' disciples. These details derive largely from the Slavonic Lives and must be treated with their usual caution. The central fact, that Rome accepted Slavonic books and worship in this period and ordained Slavic clergy, is supported by the later papal letters discussed in section 38.7. Constantine, by then seriously ill, entered a monastery, took the name Cyril, and died on 14 February 869. He was buried in Rome, in the church of San Clemente according to tradition.

The lesson is explicit. A Byzantine sponsored mission could simultaneously seek and receive Roman authorization. It worked with Rome because Rome was the highest ecclesiastical authority to which the mission could appeal against the Bavarian bishops' jurisdictional claims, and because Roman recognition gave the Slavonic project legitimacy in the West. Rome, in turn, saw an opportunity to extend papal authority in a region contested by Frankish bishops and by Constantinople. Both sides pursued advantage, and neither can be described as representing a fixed confessional camp. Describing Cyril and Methodius as Orthodox missionaries struggling against Catholicism is therefore inaccurate. The brothers were Christians from the Byzantine world who worked within, and sought legitimacy from, the whole of Christendom as they understood it.

This episode also happened in the same decade as the conflict between Pope Nicholas I and Patriarch Photios over Bulgaria and other matters. The two situations show different aspects of the same relationship. Rome and Constantinople could quarrel bitterly over jurisdiction while cooperating over a mission, and the existence of one dispute did not preclude the other. Students should avoid reading the later schism, examined in Chapter 40, into either.

Section 38.7

Methodius, Pannonia, Moravia, and Ecclesiastical Conflict

After Cyril's death Methodius became the central figure of the mission. Pope Adrian II sanctioned his episcopal mission for the Slavic lands around 869 to 870, in close connection with Kocel, the Slavic prince of Pannonia, who had supported the brothers and who sought clergy independent of Salzburg. The sources associate Methodius with the ancient see of Sirmium and with the Pannonian and Moravian regions, and call him an archbishop. Students should treat the exact title, territorial jurisdiction, and location of his see cautiously, because scholarship has long disputed these details. He should not be described simply as the "Byzantine archbishop of Moravia." His authority came from the pope, it was exercised over an area that Salzburg and Passau regarded as their own, and its precise definition was part of the conflict.

The reaction of the Bavarian church was hostile. Salzburg's clergy produced a memorandum around 870, the Conversio Bagoariorum et Carantanorum, that defended the archdiocese's historic missionary role in Pannonia and implicitly rejected Methodius's claims. Methodius was tried at a synod in Bavaria, imprisoned for a period in Swabia, and held for roughly two to three years until Pope John VIII, who succeeded Adrian II in 872, demanded his release. Chronology here is approximate, with the detention usually placed between about 870 and 873. The incident shows that the question was not Slavonic worship alone. It was also a struggle over who held authority over a territory and over the revenues, clergy, and prestige that went with it.

After his release Methodius resumed his work, but the political situation had changed. Svatopluk, who had replaced Rastislav in 870 and was a more vigorous ruler, controlled Moravia, and had ties to Frankish interests while also being capable of independent action. Papal policy toward Slavonic liturgy also fluctuated. John VIII restricted the use of Slavonic in the liturgy at one stage, then in 880 sent letters that, as scholars generally reconstruct them, affirmed Methodius's orthodoxy and permitted Slavonic worship under conditions, such as reading the Gospel first in Latin and then in Slavonic. The letter also reported that John VIII had consecrated Wiching, a Latin cleric sent to Rome by Svatopluk, as bishop of Nitra and as a suffragan of Methodius, and Wiching later became hostile to him. The combination shows that papal support was real but hedged. Scholars have debated details of the textual tradition of these papal documents.

The point is not that Rome supported Slavonic while Germany opposed it in every circumstance. Alliances and policies changed, rulers shifted allegiances, and bishops pursued interests that were local as well as doctrinal. Methodius's last years included continuing arguments over jurisdiction and Slavonic worship, accusations about doctrine, and conflict with Wiching. The tradition also credits him with translating further biblical and legal texts, including a Slavonic law code and canon law materials, which indicates that the mission's work extended beyond liturgical books toward the intellectual equipment of a church.

Section 38.8

The Death of Methodius and the Dispersal of His Disciples

Methodius died on 6 April 885. According to the Life of Methodius, he had designated Gorazd, a disciple, as his successor, but the Moravian church was already vulnerable. Wiching, who represented the Latin party, secured the support of Pope Stephen V, who in a letter of about 885 prohibited the use of Slavonic in the liturgy in Svatopluk's territory. The disciples, left without episcopal leadership or princely protection, were expelled, imprisoned, or dispersed. Later tradition states that some were sold into slavery and later ransomed, a detail that cannot be independently confirmed but is consistent with the violence of political conflict in the region. Latin ecclesiastical influence became dominant in Moravia.

The Moravian mission did not therefore produce a permanent Slavonic Moravian church. After Svatopluk's death in 894 the Moravian state itself weakened, and the arrival of the Magyars in the Carpathian Basin in the following years, treated in Chapter 32, brought its political collapse in the early tenth century. The surviving evidence for Slavonic liturgy in the region is thin, and the later history of Bohemia and neighboring lands was written within Latin Christianity. By conventional institutional measures the mission was defeated.

Yet its apparent failure generated a second phase of transmission. The disciples carried books, language, liturgy, and training elsewhere. Some moved toward the Croatian and Adriatic sphere, where Glagolitic took root in western Christian contexts. Others, including Clement, Naum, and Angelarius, made their way south and east toward Bulgaria, where they were received at the court of Boris. Scholarship identifies this dispersal as crucial to the larger historical impact of the mission. The intellectual capital that the brothers and their pupils had built was portable in a way that political control of Moravia was not. Books could be copied, pupils could teach, and a language prepared for worship could be moved to a ruler who valued it.

Section 38.9

Boris, Bulgaria, and Political Conversion

While the Moravian mission was beginning, a parallel and politically more consequential conversion was under way to the south. Boris, ruler of the Bulgar polity south of the Danube, accepted Christianity in the mid 860s, conventionally dated to 864 or 865. His conversion occurred with Byzantine participation: Emperor Michael III is reported to have acted as his godfather, and Boris took the baptismal name Michael. Political conversion of this kind was a recognized pattern. A ruler's baptism brought him into a relationship with a Christian overlord or ally and could consolidate his authority over a diverse population, which in Boris's case included Bulgar elites, Slavic-speaking communities, and Christians who were already present. This chapter treats conversion as a political event while recognizing that it was also a religious one.

Boris was not a passive recipient of Byzantine culture. Almost immediately he sought to ensure that his church would be independent of Constantinople. He negotiated actively with Rome, sending questions to Pope Nicholas I in 866 about matters of belief and practice, and the pope's detailed answers survive. Roman missionaries were active in Bulgaria for a time, and the Bulgarian ruler seems to have wanted an archbishop or patriarch of his own. When Rome did not grant this, Boris turned back to Constantinople. The council held in Constantinople in 869 to 870 assigned Bulgaria to the jurisdiction of the Constantinopolitan patriarchate, and Greek clergy replaced the Latin ones. Boris's choice was dictated by political calculation as well as theological preference, and he continued to seek the highest degree of autonomy he could obtain.

The date 864 or 865 cannot be taken to mean that every Bulgar or Slavic subject became Christian immediately. The conversion of a population was a process of decades, accompanied by resistance. According to sources hostile to the pagan party, there was a revolt around 866, which Boris suppressed with severity. Christianization involved the construction of churches, the education of clergy, the displacement of older cults, and changes in the practice of rulership. Because the clergy appointed from Constantinople used Greek, the new religion remained, for many Slavic-speaking subjects, a language of the court and the elite. That problem would be addressed after 885.

This section limits itself to the aspects of Bulgaria that bear on missionary activity, liturgy, and cultural transmission. The development of Bulgarian political power, the reign of Boris's son Simeon, the wars with Byzantium, and the later imperial claims of Bulgarian rulers belong to Chapter 39 and are not narrated here.

Section 38.10

From Moravia to Bulgaria: Clement, Naum, Preslav, and Ohrid

The arrival of the exiled disciples in Bulgaria, around 886, offered Boris an opportunity. A church staffed by Greek clergy and using Greek in worship risked making his realm culturally dependent on Constantinople. A church that could use Slavonic, with trained clergy and written texts, would give him greater control over the religious life of his people and a language of rule that most of his subjects understood. Boris therefore welcomed the disciples and gave them patronage. This is the main reason the literary project survived. The disciples came with skills, books, and pupils, and the Bulgarian ruler supplied resources, security, and a mandate.

Clement was sent to the southwestern region around Ohrid, in the lands later called Macedonia, where he established a teaching center and trained a large number of clergy in Slavonic, according to the tradition. He is later described as a bishop, around 893. Naum worked in the Bulgarian capital area at Pliska and Preslav and later also at Ohrid, where he founded a monastery. Preslav, the new capital that Boris's son Simeon expanded, became the setting of a vigorous literary culture. Writers associated with it, including Constantine of Preslav, John the Exarch, and the author called Chernorizets Hrabar, who defended the Slavic alphabet, translated Greek theological and historical works, composed original sermons and poetry, and produced works that no longer reflected only the Moravian inheritance.

The later ninth and early tenth centuries saw an enormous expansion of Slavonic Christian literary culture. Translation widened from liturgical books to include homilies, saints' lives, chronicles, and legal and scientific texts. In this setting the Cyrillic alphabet emerged, apparently through the adaptation of Greek uncial letterforms supplemented with signs needed for Slavic sounds. Cyrillic's creator is unknown, and it should not be assigned personally to Cyril. Glagolitic and Cyrillic coexisted for a time, and Glagolitic never disappeared. Some scholars suggest that the transition between the scripts occurred around the turn of the tenth century, but the chronology and geography of the shift remain debated.

A council held at Preslav in 893 is frequently said to have established Slavonic in place of Greek in the Bulgarian church. The cultural implications of the Preslav assembly should be described cautiously. The change to Slavonic as the language of the Bulgarian church was real, but it was gradual, and later claims that a single decree dismissed Greek clergy are not securely documented. What is clear is that by the early tenth century Bulgarian rulers had a church language of their own, and that this language was a powerful instrument of both religious and political identity, a matter explored further in Chapter 39.

The evidence for these centers needs careful handling. Much of what is said about Clement's and Naum's work comes from later saints' lives, written to honor them. The earliest Slavonic manuscripts survive in copies of uncertain place and date, and scholars use language, script forms, and content to assign them to regions, so references to a "Preslav school" and an "Ohrid school" are modern reconstructions of overlapping scribal and teaching activity, not descriptions of formal institutions documented as such. Early inscriptions show the new script in use, but the mechanics of how Cyrillic emerged, and who first used it, are not documented. Historians therefore describe Preslav and Ohrid as conventional labels for real but incompletely known literary centers.

Section 38.11

Cultural Transmission without Simple Byzantinization

This section concerns what moved in the Cyrillo-Methodian and Bulgarian settings: scripture, liturgical books, hymnography, saints' lives, homilies, legal texts, and the clerical education that made them usable. Broader questions of architecture, painted decoration, monastic organization, and court ceremony are better treated with the political communities of Chapter 39, and they varied greatly by region. In Bulgaria and other southeastern settings closely connected to Constantinople, Byzantine models did shape church building, rulership, and titles. The same cannot be assumed for Moravia, Bohemia, or Croatia, where Latin and local models were at least as influential.

The literary record shows selection. Translators in Moravia and later in Bulgaria rendered Gospel readings, Psalms, and liturgical books first, then homilies, saints' lives, and historical and theological works from Greek, and the surviving legal material, including Slavonic texts of church and secular law traditionally connected with Methodius's circle, drew on Greek sources while serving local jurisdictional needs. Which Greek works were chosen reflected the needs of a young church more than any Byzantine curriculum. Texts were abridged, rearranged, combined with original compositions, and occasionally translated from Latin sources. Original Slavonic writing, such as the sermons and defenses of the alphabet associated with Preslav, shows that translators became authors.

The creation of Slavonic Christian literature is therefore itself evidence against a model of Greek cultural replacement. A new language of learning came into being, one that allowed Slavic speakers to take part in Christian intellectual life without learning Greek. Bilingual clergy and scribes continued to read Greek, and Constantinople remained a source of models and prestige, so transmission was a conversation and not a one-way imposition.

The same point applies to later Rus. Rus inherited a substantial body of Byzantine Christian literature, liturgical vocabulary, and book culture through South Slavic mediation, while also maintaining direct ecclesiastical and political connections with Constantinople. How Bulgarian and Ohrid derived traditions mattered there is a subject for Chapter 39. For this chapter the key conclusion is that Slavonic Christianity gave later societies a shared ecclesiastical language without giving them a shared state.

Section 38.12

One Mission, Several Christian Futures

The Cyrillo-Methodian tradition did not lead all Slavic-speaking peoples toward the same ecclesiastical or literary future. In Bohemia, where Christianity had come through Bavarian and Moravian channels, the Slavonic liturgy persisted for a time but was increasingly eclipsed by Latin worship, and Bohemia developed within Latin Christianity, a process examined in Chapter 35. Croatian communities on the Adriatic retained important Glagolitic and Slavonic traditions within the western church, and inscriptions and liturgical books in Glagolitic remained in use for centuries. Bulgaria became a major center of Slavonic Christianity aligned with Constantinople. Later Serbia and Rus would participate in related eastern Christian networks.

Modern scholarship emphasizes that the early Glagolitic tradition crossed the later division between what scholars call Slavia Orthodoxa, the Slavic lands within the Byzantine sphere, and Slavia Latina, the Slavic lands within the Latin sphere. It was in this sense a shared heritage, and the existence of such a shared heritage in the ninth century is one reason historians resist projecting later religious divisions backward. The tradition's career shows a church culture that began in one setting, was sanctioned by Rome, was developed in an Orthodox state, and was preserved in a Catholic region.

The conclusion is therefore that the Cyrillo-Methodian mission created possibilities, not predetermined confessional destinies. The choices of rulers, the policies of popes and patriarchs, the activity of disciples, and the shifting boundaries of political power determined which possibilities were realized. The literary and ecclesiastical traditions described here would become foundational to political communities examined in Chapter 39, and the wider story of how Latin and Greek Christianity grew apart, which this chapter has deliberately not told, is the subject of Chapter 40.

Historical Vocabulary
"Slavs" and "Slavic-Speaking"

"Slavs" is a conventional name for the many communities that spoke related languages of the Slavic family and whose settlement across central, eastern, and southeastern Europe is traced through archaeology, linguistics, and the reports of Greek and Latin authors. The term does not denote a single medieval state, ethnicity, or political community. Ninth-century Slavic speakers lived under the Franks, Moravian princes, Bulgar khans, the Byzantine emperor, and local leaders. This chapter prefers "Slavic-speaking" when it needs to emphasize linguistic connection without implying political or ethnic unity. The distinction matters because the Cyrillo-Methodian project depended on the closeness of Slavic dialects in the ninth century, not on any shared Slavic state.

Historical Vocabulary
Old Church Slavonic, Glagolitic, and Cyrillic

Old Church Slavonic is the earliest Slavic literary language, based on a South Slavic dialect associated with the Thessalonian region and standardized through the translation of scripture and liturgy. Glagolitic is the earlier alphabet, associated with the Moravian mission and Constantine. Cyrillic is the later alphabet, developed in the Bulgarian environment on a largely Greek foundation and named for Cyril by later tradition, not invented by him. None of these terms should be equated with modern national languages or scripts, and the history of the two alphabets shows that the Slavic written tradition began in more than one form.

Historical Vocabulary
Slavia Orthodoxa and Slavia Latina

Modern scholars use Slavia Orthodoxa for the Slavic-speaking regions whose Christianity was linked with Constantinople and used Slavonic in worship, and Slavia Latina for Slavic-speaking regions within the Latin church. These are analytical labels for later medieval situations, not ninth-century identities. They are valuable because they show that the early Glagolitic tradition belonged to both spheres, and they warn against reading the later division back into the 860s.

Primary Source Window

The Life of Constantine and Pope John VIII's Letter of 880

Creator: An anonymous author, traditionally associated with the circle of the brothers' disciples (the Life of Constantine); Pope John VIII (the letter) · Date: The Life is traditionally dated relatively close to Constantine's lifetime, and influential scholarship places an early Slavonic form in the later ninth century, but its precise date, authorship, textual development, and original language remain debated; the papal letter dates to 880 · Type: A saint's life, and an administrative papal letter

Context: The Life of Constantine narrates the career of Constantine-Cyril, including his disputes about the use of Slavonic. It was composed to present and honor Constantine's sanctity and missionary career, and it includes an important defense of Slavonic worship; its precise original setting and polemical purposes remain debated. Pope John VIII's letter to Svatopluk was a piece of papal correspondence that dealt with Methodius's standing and with Slavonic liturgical practice. Its textual tradition has been debated by scholars.

Paraphrase of the argument in the Life of Constantine In the controversy the Life describes, Constantine answers the claim that worship is proper only in Hebrew, Greek, and Latin. He argues that God gives rain, sunlight, and air to all people alike, and that it is therefore unjustifiable to restrict praise of God to three languages. The author portrays the opponents as people who cling to a narrow rule. This is a paraphrase of the argument and is not a translation.
Paraphrase of the papal letter of 880 As the letter is commonly interpreted, John VIII accepts the use of Slavonic letters and the praise of God in the Slavonic language, but expects that the Gospel be read first in Latin and then in Slavonic, and he confirms Methodius's soundness in faith while restricting some practices. The exact wording and the details of the text are debated, and this summary reflects a common scholarly reading.

Analysis: Read together, the two sources show a hagiographic self-presentation and a papal administrative position. The Life is eloquent about principle but is a work of praise, and its speeches follow literary convention. The papal letter shows what Rome was willing to authorize and under what conditions, which is more limited and more political than the Life suggests. Neither source reports the views of the ordinary Slavic-speaking congregations, nor does either reveal the perspective of the Bavarian clergy except as an opponent.

Source Criticism

Can Saints' Lives Tell Us Missionary History?

The Lives of Constantine and Methodius are the main narrative sources for the mission, and their value and limits illustrate a method that runs across Part VI. Chapter 37 showed how victorious iconophile narratives shaped the memory of iconoclasm. The same caution applies here. Students should distinguish among several kinds of material:

  • Events independently corroborated by papal correspondence, Latin sources from Bavaria, or archaeology, such as the existence of earlier churches in Moravia, Methodius's papal sanction, and his detention.
  • Theological speeches shaped by literary convention, such as the debates with opponents, which express positions but are not verbatim records.
  • Miraculous and exemplary material, which reflects the aims of saint-making and not events a historian can verify.
  • Later memory of disputes, in which the disciples' sense of grievance shaped the portrayal of opponents.
  • The relatively early date traditionally assigned to important portions of the tradition, which would make the Lives unusually valuable even though they are partisan. Their precise dates, authorship, textual development, and original language remain debated, so even this strength should be stated with care.

The proper conclusion is neither to trust the Lives uncritically nor to discard them. Historians use them where they can be cross-checked and treat the rest as evidence about how the movement understood itself.

Questions for students:

  1. Which claims in this chapter rest on sources other than the Lives?
  2. How does the Life's portrayal of opponents illustrate hagiographic purpose?
Historical Debate

What Was the Cyrillo-Methodian Mission Really For?

The question: Why did Rastislav ask for the mission, why did Constantinople send it, and what were the brothers trying to accomplish?

Interpretation A: evangelization and pastoral instruction. Some accounts, following the Lives, emphasize the instruction of a people in the faith in a language they understood.
Interpretation B: ecclesiastical autonomy. Others emphasize Rastislav's search for a church independent of Frankish and Bavarian institutions, with Slavonic worship as a means to that end.
Interpretation C: Byzantine diplomatic strategy. Still others stress that Constantinople used missionary activity as an instrument of foreign policy, extending influence against the Franks and Bulgars.
Interpretation D: vernacular Christian literacy as an independent project. A fourth view emphasizes that the brothers' translation and literary work acquired a life beyond either Moravian or Byzantine political goals.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on the Lives, papal letters, Latin sources from Bavaria, Byzantine diplomacy, and the archaeological evidence that Moravia possessed churches before 863, which shows that the mission cannot be reduced to converting pagans.

Why it matters: The correct conclusion is not to choose one exclusive motive. Religious conviction, diplomacy, ruler strategy, jurisdictional competition, and cultural translation interacted. The debate also illustrates why historians avoid single-cause explanations for missions.

Student task: Choose two interpretations and identify what additional evidence would help determine which better explains Rastislav's request.

Multiple Perspectives

The Moravian Mission as Experienced by Different Groups

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives based on patterns visible in the sources. They are not quotations from named individuals, and the groups did not have equal power.

From the perspective of Moravian rulers such as Rastislav: The mission offered a possible way to reduce dependence on Frankish clergy and to strengthen the independence of the realm, but it also depended on the goodwill of distant patrons and could be undone by changes of rulers, as Rastislav's own fate showed.
From the perspective of Bavarian clergy: The mission challenged jurisdiction and revenues they regarded as theirs, and Slavonic worship raised doctrinal and practical objections, though some of the opposition was probably motivated by institutional interest.
From the perspective of Slavic-speaking congregations: Hearing the liturgy and scripture in a familiar language could make worship comprehensible, though evidence for the experience of ordinary believers is scarce and comes mostly through the clerical sources.
From the perspective of the disciples: After 885 they lost protection and livelihood in Moravia, but in Bulgaria they found patrons, pupils, and a chance to build the schools their teacher had not been able to secure.
From the perspective of the papacy: The mission offered a chance to assert authority over a contested region, and policy toward Slavonic worship changed with circumstances and with the pope.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Christian Worship and Literacy in Central and Southeastern Europe, c. 850 and c. 900

What Changed?

  • Around 850, Christian worship in much of central and southeastern Europe depended heavily on Greek or Latin clerical and literary traditions.
  • By around 900, a durable Slavonic Christian literary culture existed, with scripts, translated texts, schools, and original writing.
  • That culture had moved geographically and institutionally, from Moravia to Bulgaria and the Adriatic.

What Remained Continuous?

  • Christianity itself, and the importance of episcopal and monastic institutions.
  • The central role of rulers as sponsors, and the dependence of missions on political patronage.
  • Rivalry among Rome, Constantinople, and Frankish churches over jurisdiction.

Who experienced change most directly? Clergy, scribes, pupils, and court elites, who gained a new language of learning. Who experienced greater continuity? Rural populations, for whom worship and daily life changed slowly and unevenly. Why does the pattern matter? It shows that the creation of a written language was a revolution in a narrow social circle first, with wider effects emerging over generations.

Cause and Effect

Why Did the Slavonic Literary Tradition Survive after Its Moravian Defeat?

Long-term causes

Byzantine intellectual preparation, the brothers' familiarity with Slavic speech, and the Byzantine tradition of missionary diplomacy gave the project tools and experience.

Short-term causes

Glagolitic literacy, trained disciples, papal legitimization at important moments, and the political patronage of Boris provided the means of survival.

Event or transformation

After 885 the disciples were expelled from Moravia, migrated, and established schools and scriptoria in Bulgaria and the Ohrid region.

Immediate effects

Translation continued and expanded, new centers trained clergy, and the Cyrillic script emerged alongside Glagolitic.

Long-term consequences

A durable Slavonic literary and liturgical tradition became available to later Orthodox Slavic societies, while Glagolitic survived in Croatian Catholic contexts.

Historical complexity

No single cause explains survival. Moravia was where the mission became famous, but Bulgaria was where much of its literary inheritance became institutionally durable.
Everyday Life and Social History

Hearing and Reading the Faith

For most inhabitants of ninth-century Moravia, Pannonia, and Bulgaria, religion was experienced through the rhythms of feast days, burials, baptisms, and local sacred places, and not through books. The liturgy in a language they did not understand, conducted by foreign clergy, was a feature of many Christian regions. Slavonic worship altered that experience for those who lived near mission centers, since prayers, readings, and responses could be understood. Evidence for the response of ordinary people is slight, and historians should not assume a uniform welcome. Older customs persisted in many places, and conversion, as the Bulgarian revolt of the 860s shows, could be resisted.

Literacy remained a specialized skill. Books were made by scribes on parchment, a costly material, and copied in monasteries and cathedral or court schools. Pupils learned the new alphabet, memorized texts, and practiced translation. Church construction used local labor, and rulers financed buildings as part of their authority. For the families of the elite, sending sons to the mission schools offered a path to clerical office. Women appear rarely in the sources of the period, but they participated as worshippers, as members of ruling families whose marriages built alliances, and, in later centuries, as patrons and as members of convents.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Byzantine Christianity spread among Slavic-speaking societies, and has argued that it spread through translation and adaptation as much as through political power. It began by dismantling the blank-slate model. Christianity had reached the Balkans, the Adriatic, Pannonia, Bohemia, and Moravia through Latin, Greek, local, and mixed networks before 863, and Great Moravia already possessed churches. "The Slavs" were not a single people, and "Orthodox" in the chapter's title is partly a retrospective label, since no clean Catholic and Orthodox divide existed in the ninth century.

The mission was led by two brothers from Thessalonica, Constantine, who took the name Cyril shortly before his death, and Methodius. They were prepared by Byzantine education, administrative and monastic experience, familiarity with Slavic speech, and earlier diplomatic missions. They came at the request of Rastislav of Moravia, who wanted instruction in his people's language and also greater independence from Frankish and Bavarian clergy. The motives were therefore religious and political together, and Constantinople's willingness to respond reflected its own diplomatic interests.

The brothers' most original achievement was the creation of a literary language. Constantine is associated with the creation of Glagolitic, an alphabet suited to Slavic sounds, and the language written in it, Old Church Slavonic, was based on a South Slavic dialect and shaped by translation. Cyril did not invent Cyrillic, which developed later in the Bulgarian environment. Translation produced a new vocabulary for theology, scripture, law, and ritual, and was therefore an act of cultural production. Literacy remained initially clerical and elite, and the invention of a script did not create mass literacy or a modern national identity.

Slavonic worship provoked conflict, including the argument that only Hebrew, Greek, and Latin were fit liturgical languages. That position was never official doctrine of the western church. It was an argument used by particular opponents and criticized by the brothers' circle and, at important moments, by Rome. The mission sought and received papal approval from Adrian II, and Methodius continued under papal authority. The episode demonstrates that a Byzantine sponsored mission could win Roman authorization, and it warns against reading the later schism, examined in Chapter 40, back into the ninth century.

After Cyril's death in 869, Methodius carried on in Pannonia and Moravia as a papally sanctioned bishop or archbishop, whose exact title and jurisdiction are disputed. He faced the hostility of Bavarian clergy, who imprisoned him for a time until papal intervention secured his release. Papal policy under John VIII was supportive but conditional, and the Moravian ruler Svatopluk shifted among interests. When Methodius died in 885, his opponents prevailed, Slavonic worship was restricted, and his disciples were expelled or dispersed.

The Moravian defeat produced a second phase of transmission. Disciples such as Clement and Naum found patronage in Bulgaria, where Boris, who had accepted Christianity around 864 or 865 and had negotiated actively with both Rome and Constantinople, saw the advantage of a church that could use the language of his subjects. Schools and scriptoria at Preslav and Ohrid expanded translation and original writing, and Cyrillic emerged. Boris should be understood as an active ruler choosing among ecclesiastical relationships, and not as a passive recipient of Byzantine culture. The date of his baptism did not convert his whole population in a day.

The chapter has emphasized that cultural transmission was not simple Byzantinization. Scripture, liturgy, saints' lives, legal texts, hymnography, and clerical learning moved through Byzantine and Slavonic networks, but local societies selected, translated, and transformed them. The creation of Slavonic Christian literature is evidence against a model of Greek cultural replacement. Later Rus received a substantial body of Byzantine Christian literature, liturgical vocabulary, and book culture through South Slavic mediation while also maintaining direct ecclesiastical and political connections with Constantinople.

The mission's legacy was varied. Bohemia developed within Latin Christianity. Croatian communities preserved Glagolitic within the western church. Bulgaria became a major center of Slavonic Christianity aligned with Constantinople. Later Serbia and Rus would participate in related eastern Christian networks. The early Glagolitic tradition crossed what scholars call Slavia Orthodoxa and Slavia Latina, and the mission therefore created possibilities and not predetermined confessional destinies.

Methodologically, the chapter has used the Lives of Constantine and Methodius as valuable but partisan evidence, and has shown how they can be checked against papal letters, Latin sources, manuscripts, and archaeology. It has also kept to its boundaries. The political development of Bulgaria, Serbia, and Kievan Rus belongs to Chapter 39, and the longer separation of Latin and Greek Christianity belongs to Chapter 40.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Great Moravia
A central European polity of the ninth century, centered north of the middle Danube, whose rulers dealt with Frankish, papal, and Byzantine powers.
Slavic-speaking
Describing communities who spoke related Slavic languages, without implying a single state or ethnicity.
Mission
An organized effort, usually sponsored by a church or ruler, to establish or reform Christian practice in a region.
Vernacular
The everyday spoken language of a community, as opposed to a classical or learned language.
Liturgy
The prescribed public worship of a church, including readings, prayers, and chants.
Glagolitic
The earliest Slavic alphabet, associated with the Cyrillo-Methodian mission and designed for Slavic sounds.
Cyrillic
A later Slavic alphabet, developed in the Bulgarian environment on a largely Greek foundation and not invented by Cyril.
Old Church Slavonic
The earliest Slavic literary language, based on a South Slavic dialect and standardized through translation of scripture and liturgy.
Hagiography
Writing about saints, composed to honor and instruct, and therefore requiring critical reading as historical evidence.
Jurisdiction
The territory and population over which a bishop or church claims authority.
Archbishop
A bishop with authority over other bishops in a province or region; Methodius's exact rank and jurisdiction are debated.
Patriarch
The senior bishop of a major church center, such as Constantinople or Rome.
Ordination
The rite by which clergy are consecrated; the need for ordaining disciples was a reason for the journey to Rome.
Scriptorium
A place in a monastery or church where manuscripts were copied.
Gospel lectionary
A liturgical book containing the Gospel readings arranged for the church year.
Slavia Orthodoxa
A scholarly label for Slavic-speaking regions within the sphere of Constantinople and Slavonic worship.
Slavia Latina
A scholarly label for Slavic-speaking regions within the Latin church.
Political conversion
The acceptance of Christianity by a ruler, with consequences for diplomacy, legitimacy, and the gradual Christianization of the population.
Translation
The creation of equivalent texts in another language, which for the mission included inventing vocabulary and conventions.
Cultural transmission
The movement of ideas, texts, practices, and institutions between societies, involving selection and adaptation.
Byzantinization
A term for the spread of Byzantine forms and influence, which is misleading when it implies one-way replacement of local cultures.
Autonomy
The degree of ecclesiastical or political self-direction a ruler or church could achieve.
Illyricum
A historic ecclesiastical and administrative region of the Balkans over which Rome and Constantinople contested authority.
Conversio Bagoariorum et Carantanorum
A Salzburg memorandum of about 870 defending that church's missionary role in Pannonia.
Calque
A loan translation that builds a word in one language on the model of a word in another.
Cyrillo-Methodian
Relating to the mission and tradition of Cyril and Methodius; a modern scholarly adjective.
Khazars
A steppe polity north of the Caucasus and Black Sea with which Constantine had diplomatic and religious contact.
Pliska and Preslav
Successive Bulgarian capitals, the second a major center of Slavonic literary culture.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a church culture can move across political and ecclesiastical boundaries. Byzantine recovery, traced in Chapters 36 and 37, was not only a matter of defending an empire. It also produced a capacity for cultural transmission, and the Slavic mission shows that capacity at work. At the same time, the chapter reveals the limits of imperial initiative. The mission depended on Moravian, papal, Bulgarian, and monastic actors, and it succeeded most lastingly where a ruler chose to support it.

The chapter also contributes to a larger argument about Europe. A written Slavic Christian culture emerged from the exchange of Greek learning, Roman authority, Frankish pressure, and Slavic speech. Its roots lay in the center of the continent, its institutional survival in the southeast, and its later influence in the east and north. Such a history cannot be told as the story of one church, one empire, or one nation. It shows that the cultural map of Europe was made by negotiation, translation, and contingency.

Later developments shaped by these events include the Slavonic church traditions of Bulgaria, Serbia, and Rus, the Glagolitic heritage of Croatia, and the long modern memory of Cyril and Methodius as patrons of several nations. Each of those later uses of the brothers belongs to the history of memory, and should be distinguished from the ninth-century evidence. Chapter 39 now turns to the political communities that built states with these traditions, and Chapter 40 to the widening separation between Latin and Greek Christianity. Students should carry forward the lesson that the later confessional map was not drawn in advance.
