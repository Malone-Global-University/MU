Archaic Greece and Mediterranean Colonization

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

By the eighth century BCE, the small, still forming communities described in Chapter 8 were no longer confined to their own valleys and coastal plains. Greek speaking men and women were sailing westward to Italy and Sicily, northward through the Hellespont into the Black Sea, and southward to Libya and Egypt, carrying with them not a finished civilization but a restless, competitive, and unevenly organized way of life that both transformed the wider Mediterranean world and was, in turn, transformed by it.

Consider a small island just off the Bay of Naples, called by later Greeks Pithekoussai. Archaeologists working there have uncovered, layered beneath centuries of later habitation, an eighth century BCE settlement whose graves and workshops contain pottery from Corinth, from the island of Euboea, from Phoenician cities of the Levant, and from local Italian communities, alongside substantial evidence of ironworking and other metallurgy, including material linked to iron sources on the island of Elba, although the exact balance between imported ore, worked bloom, and secondary smithing at the site remains debated among archaeologists. Whoever lived on that island did not think of themselves as founding a distant outpost of a unified Greek nation, since no such nation existed. They were, instead, part of a loosely connected world of trading partners, craftsmen, and settlers who developed their community within western Mediterranean networks in which Phoenician traders were already active, and who lived alongside, traded with, and sometimes intermarried with people whose language and customs differed from their own.

Multiply that island many times over, along the coasts of Sicily and southern Italy, around the shores of the Black Sea, on the North African coast at Cyrene, and at the Egyptian administered river port of Naukratis on the Canopic branch of the Nile, and something of the scale of Archaic Greek movement across the Mediterranean and Black Sea worlds begins to come into view. This chapter examines that movement, not as a single organized migration directed from a Greek homeland, but as a long, uneven, and regionally varied process in which particular communities, driven by particular pressures and opportunities, sent out settlers who founded new, politically independent communities among peoples who were often already there.

This same period, conventionally called the Archaic age of Greek history and dated approximately from 800 to 500 BCE, also saw profound change within the communities that stayed at home. Chapter 8 described how the polis emerged, gradually and unevenly, out of the disorder that followed the collapse of Mycenaean palace society. This chapter picks up that story and asks what happened next. Aristocratic families competed for wealth, status, and office. Population pressures, land shortages, debt, and factional conflict strained many communities from within. Some poleis experimented with written law. A number of them, at different times and for different reasons, fell under the rule of individual strongmen whom the Greeks called tyrants. And across many communities, warfare increasingly came to depend on citizens who could afford a shield, helmet, and spear, tying military service, land, and civic standing together in ways that would matter for the rest of Greek history.

None of these developments, overseas settlement, commercial growth, changing warfare, aristocratic competition, or tyranny, occurred in isolation from the others. A family short of land at home might send a son overseas as a settler; a community weakened by internal conflict might expel a faction that then founded a new polis elsewhere; a city grown wealthy from trade with Etruscan or Phoenician partners might see that wealth reshape who could arm and fight for the community; an ambitious member of an aristocratic family, prevailing in a struggle against his own peers, might seize sole power and, according to later tradition, draw on wider dissatisfaction with entrenched elite rule. This chapter treats these threads as interconnected without collapsing them into a single, tidy narrative of cause and effect.

It is equally important to state, from the outset, what this chapter does not claim. It does not claim that a rising population alone drove Greeks overseas, since the evidence for demographic pressure varies enormously from region to region and period to period. It does not claim that Greek settlers arrived in empty lands, since nearly every region they settled was already inhabited by other peoples, Etruscans, Phoenicians, Sicels, Sicanians, Elymians, Italic communities, Thracians, Scythians, Egyptians, Libyans, and Iberians among them, with whom Greeks traded, fought, intermarried, and exchanged ideas. It does not claim that the development of heavily armed infantry warfare mechanically produced political equality or democracy, since the pace and political consequences of that military change remain genuinely debated among historians. And it does not claim that tyranny was a necessary stepping stone on a predetermined road toward democratic government, since many Greek communities never experienced tyranny at all, and those that did followed no single script.

This chapter, in short, takes the polis that Chapter 8 has just brought into view and places it into motion: across the sea, into contact with other peoples, into an expanding commercial economy, into new forms of warfare, and into the political conflicts that would, in the following chapter, help explain why Sparta and Athens, communities that shared this same broad Archaic environment, developed into such strikingly different political orders.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the general chronological and geographic scope of the Archaic Greek world, c. 800 to 500 BCE, while recognizing regional variation.
  2. Explain the internal pressures, demographic, agricultural, and political, that historians associate with Archaic Greek communities.
  3. Evaluate multiple explanations for why Greek communities sent out overseas settlers, rather than accepting a single cause such as overpopulation.
  4. Distinguish among an apoikia, a metropolis, an oikist, and an emporion, and explain why these categories could overlap in practice.
  5. Identify major regions of Greek overseas settlement, including southern Italy, Sicily, the western Mediterranean, the Black Sea, North Africa, and Egypt.
  6. Analyze the range of relationships, commercial, cultural, and violent, between Greek settlers and the non-Greek societies they encountered.
  7. Explain how commerce, craft production, and the later spread of coinage connected Greek poleis to a wider Mediterranean economy.
  8. Describe the development of hoplite warfare and assess, with appropriate caution, its relationship to citizenship and political change.
  9. Define Archaic tyranny historically and distinguish it from its later, purely negative popular meaning.
  10. Explain how Archaic citizenship connected land, military service, cult, law, and political participation, while remaining exclusionary.
  11. Assess the strengths and limitations of archaeological, material, and later literary evidence for reconstructing Archaic Greek expansion.
  12. Contextualize Archaic Greek expansion within the wider Mediterranean world of Phoenician, Etruscan, and other contemporary societies.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What combination of pressures and opportunities led particular Greek communities to send settlers overseas during the Archaic period?
  2. Why should historians treat Archaic overseas settlement as a heterogeneous process rather than a single organized movement?
  3. How did relations between Greek settlers and non-Greek populations vary across different regions of the Mediterranean and Black Sea worlds?
  4. In what ways did overseas settlement connect to changes in commerce, aristocratic competition, and warfare within Greek communities themselves?
  5. Why is the relationship between hoplite warfare and political change more complicated than a simple cause and effect story?
  6. What did Archaic Greeks mean by the word tyrannos, and how does that meaning differ from the modern English word tyrant?
  7. How were civic membership and political participation defined and contested during the Archaic period, and how did access vary by status and by polis?
  8. What kinds of evidence, archaeological and textual, allow historians to reconstruct Archaic Greek expansion, and what are the limitations of each?

Historical Context

Chapter 8 traced the slow recovery of Greek speaking communities after the collapse of Mycenaean palace society, examining renewed settlement, population growth in some regions, the revival of long distance contact, and the gradual emergence of the polis as an increasingly common, though not universal, form of political community. By the eighth century BCE, a number of Greek communities, including Corinth, Chalcis, Eretria, and Megara, possessed the organizational capacity, agricultural surplus, and maritime experience needed to send groups of settlers to found new communities overseas.

This chapter's chronological center, conventionally called the Archaic period and dated approximately from 800 to 500 BCE, was a time of substantial transformation across the Greek world, though the pace of change differed considerably from one community to another. Some regions of the Greek world, including much of Sparta's Peloponnesian neighborhood, remained comparatively insulated from overseas settlement, while others, particularly Euboea, Corinth, Megara, and several Ionian cities of Asia Minor, were deeply involved in it from an early date. Historians should resist treating the Archaic Greek world as a single, uniform society moving through identical stages at the same pace.

The wider Mediterranean world into which Greek settlers moved was not empty, nor was it unfamiliar with long distance contact. Phoenician cities of the eastern Mediterranean coast, particularly Tyre and Sidon, had already established trading posts and settlements across the central and western Mediterranean, including at Carthage on the North African coast, by the time Greek overseas settlement intensified in the eighth century BCE. Etruscan communities in central Italy possessed sophisticated metalworking, agriculture, and trade networks of their own. Egypt, under its own long established dynasties, had for centuries maintained diplomatic and commercial contact with the wider eastern Mediterranean. Greek expansion, in other words, entered a Mediterranean world already crossed by other peoples' ships, goods, and settlements, and Greek communities frequently competed, cooperated, and intermingled with these other Mediterranean societies rather than encountering an untouched frontier.

The evidence available for this chapter differs importantly from the evidence available for Chapter 8. Archaeology remains fundamental, particularly for tracing pottery styles, burial practices, settlement layouts, and craft production across the Mediterranean and Black Sea worlds. Inscriptions, though still relatively rare before the sixth century BCE, become increasingly informative. Later Greek literary sources, particularly the historian Herodotus writing in the fifth century BCE, preserve foundation traditions and narratives about the Archaic period, but these accounts were composed generations, and sometimes centuries, after the events they describe, and historians must weigh them carefully against archaeological evidence rather than treating them as straightforward eyewitness testimony.

Section 9.1

The Archaic Greek World, c. 800–500 BCE

Historians conventionally call the period from roughly 800 to 500 BCE the Archaic age of Greek history, a label that, like the periodization terms discussed throughout this textbook, should be treated as an analytical convention rather than a natural division of time. During these three centuries, the polis, whose gradual emergence Chapter 8 examined, became the principal, though never exclusive, framework within which many Greek communities organized territory, religious cult, political participation, and collective military action. It would be a mistake, however, to imagine that a fully formed, universally recognized polis system sprang into existence at some fixed date around 800 BCE. Development remained profoundly uneven. Some communities, particularly in Ionia and on Euboea, developed sophisticated political and commercial institutions relatively early, while others, including many communities in more mountainous or agriculturally marginal regions, developed more slowly or along different lines entirely.

The evidence for this period comes overwhelmingly from archaeology for its earlier decades, supplemented increasingly by inscriptions from the seventh century BCE onward, and by later literary accounts, composed in most cases well after the events they describe, for its social and political history. Excavated cemeteries reveal changing burial practices that may reflect changing ideas about status and community. Sanctuary sites, including major Panhellenic centers such as Olympia and Delphi, preserve dedications from across the Greek world that illustrate both religious practice and the competitive display of wealth among aristocratic families. Pottery, traded widely across the Mediterranean, allows archaeologists to trace commercial contact even where no written record survives.

This chapter treats the Archaic period as the setting within which two closely related processes unfolded together: the movement of Greek settlers across the Mediterranean and Black Sea worlds, examined in Sections 9.3 through 9.7, and the internal political, social, military, and economic transformation of Greek communities themselves, examined in Sections 9.8 through 9.10. These two processes were not separate stories that happened to occur at the same time. They were, in many cases, directly connected, as later sections of this chapter will show.

Section 9.2

Population, Land, Aristocracy, and Social Competition

Before examining why particular Greek communities sent settlers overseas, it is necessary to understand the internal pressures that historians associate, with varying degrees of confidence, with Archaic Greek society. Archaeological evidence, particularly the number and density of burials and settlements in some regions, suggests demographic growth in parts of the Greek world during the eighth and seventh centuries BCE, though this evidence is uneven and should not be generalized uncritically to the entire Greek world. Where population did grow, it placed pressure on the limited agricultural land available in many Greek regions, whose mountainous terrain, discussed in Chapter 2, left comparatively little level, fertile ground for cultivation.

Competition for that land, and for the wealth, status, and political influence that land ownership conferred, took place chiefly among aristocratic families, whose members already dominated political office, religious priesthood, and military leadership in most Archaic communities. These families competed with one another through marriage alliances, hospitality networks called xenia, lavish dedications at religious sanctuaries, elaborate funerary display, and, when peaceful competition failed, through political conflict. Some communities also show evidence of debt and dependence among poorer members of the population, though the precise legal and social mechanisms of this dependence varied by region and are not equally well documented everywhere; Chapter 10 will examine one particularly well attested case, the crisis addressed by Solon's reforms in Athens, in greater depth.

Internal civic conflict, which the Greeks called stasis, recurred within many Archaic communities, arising from combinations of aristocratic rivalry, popular resentment against elite privilege, disputes over land and debt, and disagreement over political participation. Stasis should not be imagined as a single uniform crisis experienced identically everywhere; its causes and intensity differed from polis to polis. It is this unsettled internal environment, combined with genuine, if unevenly distributed, pressure on land and resources, that forms the essential background against which Section 9.3 examines the reasons particular communities sent settlers overseas.

Section 9.3

Why Did Greeks Establish Communities Overseas?

Popular accounts of Archaic Greek expansion often reduce the entire movement to a single explanation, that Greece became overpopulated and therefore sent its surplus population abroad. This chapter treats that explanation as insufficient. Modern scholarship, drawing on archaeological, comparative, and textual evidence, describes Archaic overseas settlement as a genuinely heterogeneous process, in which different communities, at different times, acted for different, and sometimes overlapping, reasons.

Access to agricultural land was, in many well documented cases, a significant motive, particularly for settlements in southern Italy and Sicily, where later tradition and archaeological evidence together suggest that new arrivals sought fertile ground unavailable, or unavailable in sufficient quantity, in their home communities. Internal political conflict provided another documented motive: a faction defeated in a struggle for power at home might depart, sometimes under compulsion and sometimes by more voluntary arrangement, to found a new community elsewhere, a pattern consistent with the stasis discussed in Section 9.2. Individual exile, whether for political, religious, or personal reasons, could also lead directly to the founding of a new settlement, as later tradition claims for a number of foundations.

Commercial opportunity provided a further, and in some cases primary, motive, particularly for settlements established at strategic maritime locations offering access to metal ores, agricultural surplus for export, or control over trade routes, a pattern examined further in Section 9.8. Some settlements, such as Naukratis in Egypt, appear to have originated principally as trading arrangements rather than as agricultural communities at all. Strategic and logistical considerations, including control of a harbor, a river mouth, or a narrow strait, also shaped where settlements were placed, since a well situated site could generate wealth and influence regardless of its agricultural potential.

Historians disagree, and the surviving evidence does not allow a single confident resolution, about the relative weight that should be assigned to land hunger as against commercial motive in explaining the overall movement. Some scholars emphasize agricultural settlement as the dominant pattern, particularly in the west, while others stress the commercial and exploratory dimension of much early Greek maritime activity, particularly in the earliest, most ambiguous settlements such as Pithekoussai, examined in Section 9.4. This textbook does not resolve that disagreement by fiat. Instead, it treats Archaic overseas settlement as a phenomenon produced by multiple, often locally specific causes, that varied from one founding community and one destination to another, and it returns to this genuine historiographical disagreement directly in this chapter's Historical Debate feature.

Section 9.4

Apoikiai, Metropoleis, Oikists, and Emporia

Careful historical vocabulary matters a great deal in this chapter, because the conventional English word colonization, borrowed from the very different context of early modern and modern European overseas empire, can mislead students into imagining eighth and seventh century BCE Greek settlement as a centrally directed imperial project administered permanently from a home government. It was not. The Greek term most often translated as colony, apoikia, literally means a home away from home, and an apoikia normally developed as a politically autonomous community in its own right, with its own government, laws, and citizen body, rather than a permanent dependency administered by the community that had sent it out, even though the process of establishing that autonomy could itself be gradual and, in some cases, mixed with more purely commercial arrangements.

The community that sent out settlers was called the metropolis, literally the mother city, and the relationship between an apoikia and its metropolis, while often marked by continuing religious, commercial, and sentimental ties, including shared cults and calendars, did not generally involve ongoing political subordination of the kind later European colonial empires imposed on their overseas territories. An apoikia might, in later centuries, find itself allied with, at war with, or entirely indifferent to its metropolis, depending on political circumstance rather than any fixed constitutional relationship.

An individual traditionally credited with organizing and leading a new settlement was called an oikist, or oikistes. Later Greek tradition often remembered the oikist as a heroic founder, sometimes receiving a formal foundation oracle from the sanctuary at Delphi and, after death, honored with a hero cult at the new settlement. Historians should treat these later foundation traditions with some caution, since they were often shaped, retold, and embellished over generations to serve later political or religious purposes, a point developed further in this chapter's Primary Source Window on Cyrene.

A distinct term, emporion, described a settlement whose primary purpose was trade rather than full scale agricultural settlement and political independence. An emporion might house a permanent or semi permanent community of merchants operating under specific arrangements with a local non-Greek authority, as at Naukratis in Egypt, examined in Section 9.6, rather than constituting a fully autonomous polis of the kind an apoikia typically became. In practice, however, the line between an apoikia and an emporion could be genuinely blurred, and Pithekoussai, examined in the following section, is an excellent illustration of a settlement whose character, agricultural community, trading post, or some combination of both, remains debated among archaeologists and historians.

Historical Vocabulary
Apoikia, Metropolis, Oikist, and Emporion

An apoikia was a new Greek community established overseas by settlers from an existing polis. The English word colony, though conventionally used to translate apoikia, carries later imperial connotations, subordination, administration from a distant capital, extraction of resources for the benefit of the ruling power, that do not accurately describe most Archaic Greek apoikiai. Most Archaic apoikiai normally developed as politically autonomous communities rather than permanent dependencies of their metropoleis, although the process of settlement and institutional formation could itself be gradual, and, in some cases such as Pithekoussai, mixed with more purely commercial arrangements.

The metropolis, or mother city, was the community from which settlers departed. Ties between an apoikia and its metropolis often persisted through shared religious cult, calendar, dialect, and sentiment, and sometimes through commercial or military cooperation, but these ties did not generally amount to continuing political rule.

An oikist, or oikistes, was the individual traditionally credited with leading and organizing a new settlement, often remembered in later tradition as a heroic founder honored with cult after death. Foundation stories involving an oikist and, frequently, a consulting oracle at Delphi should be read as historically informative but not necessarily as precise or unembellished contemporary record.

An emporion was a settlement, or a district within a larger settlement, organized principally around trade, sometimes operating under an arrangement with a local non-Greek authority rather than functioning as a fully independent polis. Naukratis provides the clearest Archaic example, though it should be understood not as a Greek town in its own right but as an Egyptian administered, multiethnic port within which particular Greek cities maintained a recognized emporion presence, including sanctuaries and commercial privileges, alongside Egyptian, Cypriot, Phoenician, and other communities. In practice, the categories of apoikia and emporion could overlap, and some settlements combined features of both.

Section 9.5

Greeks in Italy, Sicily, and the Western Mediterranean

The Bay of Naples provides one of the earliest and most instructive cases of Greek presence in the central Mediterranean. Archaeological excavation on the island of Pithekoussai, generally dated to the first half of the eighth century BCE, has revealed a settlement whose graves and workshops contain pottery from Euboea, from Corinth, from Phoenician cities of the Levant, and from local Italian communities, alongside substantial evidence of ironworking, including material associated with iron sources on Elba, though scholars continue to debate how much of this activity represented primary smelting as opposed to the secondary working of already processed iron. Whether Pithekoussai should be understood primarily as an agricultural apoikia, a commercial emporion, or, more plausibly, a mixed community combining elements of both, remains debated among archaeologists, and this textbook presents that uncertainty honestly rather than resolving it artificially. Nearby Cumae, whose archaeological record places its founding around the middle of the eighth century BCE and whose later tradition associated it with settlers connected to Pithekoussai and to Chalcis on Euboea, developed into one of the earliest and most influential Greek poleis on the Italian mainland, though its precise literary foundation chronology is considerably less secure than its material record.

Sicily attracted intensive Greek settlement from the later eighth century BCE onward. Naxos, traditionally the earliest Greek foundation on the island, and Syracuse, founded by settlers from Corinth and destined to become one of the most powerful cities of the ancient Mediterranean, illustrate the agricultural and strategic advantages that drew Greek settlers to Sicily's fertile coastal plains and defensible harbors. Southern Italy, whose Greek communities later Greeks and Romans came to call, collectively, Magna Graecia, a term that should be understood as a later descriptive label for a region containing numerous separate and often rival poleis rather than the name of any unified Greek state, likewise attracted extensive settlement, supporting cities that grew wealthy from grain, livestock, and trade with neighboring Italic communities.

Greek settlement extended still further west. Phocaea, an Ionian city on the coast of Asia Minor, sent settlers who, according to later tradition, founded Massalia on the southern coast of Gaul around 600 BCE, extending Greek commercial and settlement networks into the western Mediterranean and establishing contact with Celtic and Iberian peoples of the region, a connection examined further in Section 9.7. Phocaean and other Greek mariners are also associated with more limited, and less securely documented, contact along the coasts of Iberia during this period.

Across this western Mediterranean network, patterns of settlement varied by region: agricultural apoikiai predominated in the fertile coastal plains of Sicily and southern Italy, while more purely commercial motives appear to have shaped settlement further west and in mixed communities such as Pithekoussai. In every case, however, Greek settlers arrived in a landscape already inhabited by other peoples, a reality examined directly in Section 9.7.

Section 9.6

The Black Sea, North Africa, and Egypt

Greek overseas settlement was never simply a westward movement, and this section deliberately turns eastward, southward, and toward Africa to correct that common misimpression. Beginning in the seventh century BCE, Miletus, a wealthy Ionian city on the coast of Asia Minor, is traditionally credited by later sources with founding a very large number of settlements around the Hellespont, the Propontis, and the shores of the Black Sea. These settlements gave Greek communities access to grain producing regions of the northern Black Sea coast, to fish, timber, and metal resources, and to trade routes reaching deep into the Eurasian steppe world discussed in Chapter 2, connecting Greek commercial life to peoples such as the Scythians, examined further in Section 9.7.

To the south, Cyrene, on the coast of North Africa in what later became known as Libya, was founded, according to a traditional date of approximately 631 or 630 BCE, by settlers from the small Aegean island of Thera. This tradition is preserved both in Herodotus's later narrative and in a surviving inscription from Cyrene itself, known to modern scholars as the Foundation Decree of Cyrene, a fourth century BCE inscribed document that purports to reproduce the text of a much earlier Theran oath or decree governing the settlers' obligations. Historians continue to debate how faithfully this later inscription preserves genuine seventh century material as opposed to later reconstruction or embellishment. This chapter's Primary Source Window examines that layered tradition in detail, comparing later literary memory, the inscribed decree, and archaeological findings from the site, precisely because the complicated nature of the Cyrene evidence offers students an unusually clear lesson in how historians evaluate foundation narratives.

Egypt presented Greek settlers and traders with a different situation entirely, since it was governed by a long established and powerful state whose authority Greek arrivals had to negotiate rather than displace. Naukratis, established late in the seventh century BCE on the Canopic branch of the Nile in the western Delta under the authority of Egypt's Saite Dynasty, functioned as an Egyptian administered port and cosmopolitan trading center serving Mediterranean commerce, home to Egyptian, Greek, Cypriot, Phoenician, and other communities rather than to Greek merchants alone. Within this Egyptian controlled port, particular Greek cities established recognized sanctuaries and trading privileges, a Greek presence that operated inside, rather than in place of, Egyptian sovereignty. Naukratis illustrates especially clearly the point made in Section 9.4: Greek overseas presence did not take a single institutional form, and the relationship between Greek arrivals and the societies among whom they settled depended heavily on the political and military strength of the host society, a point developed further in Section 9.7.

Section 9.7

Greeks and Non-Greek Societies: Encounter, Exchange, Conflict, and Entanglement

No section of this chapter matters more for avoiding a distorted picture of Archaic Greek expansion than this one. Every region examined in Sections 9.5 and 9.6 was already inhabited, in most cases by well established societies with their own political organization, agriculture, craft production, and long distance contacts of their own. Writing Greek expansion as though settlers arrived in empty or passive territories, waiting to receive a superior civilization, would misrepresent the historical record and the substantial body of modern scholarship built on archaeological and comparative evidence from these encounters.

In Italy and Sicily, Greek settlers encountered Etruscan communities in central Italy, whose sophisticated metalworking, urban organization, and long distance trade networks predated intensive Greek settlement and whose commercial and cultural exchange with Greek cities, visible in shared artistic styles, imported pottery, and the adoption of the Greek alphabet by Etruscan scribes, proved extensive and mutually significant rather than one directional. In Sicily, Greek settlers encountered Sicels, Sicanians, and Elymians, indigenous peoples whose relationships with incoming Greek communities ranged from trade and, in some documented cases, intermarriage, to competition for land and, at various points, warfare and displacement. Phoenician communities, already established at sites such as Motya in western Sicily before intensive Greek settlement began, added a further layer of competition and exchange, particularly in the western part of the island, where Greek and Phoenician spheres of influence bordered and sometimes clashed.

Around the Black Sea, Greek settlements developed close, and often economically essential, relationships with Scythian and other steppe peoples, whose grain, hides, and other goods supplied Greek markets in exchange for wine, oil, and craft products, a commercial relationship examined further in Section 9.8. In North Africa, Cyrene's Greek settlers interacted with Libyan populations through a combination of trade, intermarriage, according to later tradition, and, at various points, conflict over land and grazing rights. In Egypt, as Section 9.6 explained, Greek traders and settlers operated within limits set by a powerful and long established Egyptian state, a relationship of negotiated access rather than Greek dominance. Further west, Phocaean settlers at Massalia established trading and, in some cases, more personal relationships with Celtic and Ligurian peoples of southern Gaul and with Iberian communities further along the coast.

Current archaeological scholarship increasingly emphasizes that these encounters produced mixed communities, new hybrid material cultures, and locally specific outcomes rather than a single uniform pattern of Greek expansion into non-Greek space. Pithekoussai's mixed grave goods, examined in Section 9.5, exemplify this point vividly. Historians should therefore resist two equally misleading generalizations: that Greek and non-Greek relations were everywhere and always peaceful and cooperative, and that they were everywhere and always violent and exploitative. Both patterns occurred, often within the same region at different times, and local agency, on the part of both Greek settlers and the societies they encountered, shaped the outcome in each specific case.

Section 9.8

Commerce, Wealth, and an Expanding Mediterranean Economy

Overseas settlement and Mediterranean commerce reinforced one another throughout the Archaic period, though this chapter does not claim that commerce alone explains why any particular settlement was founded. Greek communities, both in the Aegean homeland and in the new apoikiai examined in Sections 9.5 and 9.6, exported wine, olive oil, and finely decorated pottery, and imported grain, metals, timber, and a range of luxury goods, including textiles, ivory, and precious metalwork obtained through contact with Phoenician, Etruscan, Egyptian, and steppe trading partners. Craft production, particularly fine pottery workshops in cities such as Corinth and, somewhat later, Athens, expanded considerably during this period, supplying markets across the Mediterranean and Black Sea worlds and providing archaeologists with an unusually detailed record of changing trade patterns.

The introduction of coinage, emerging in western Anatolia in the late seventh century BCE, especially among Lydia and neighboring East Greek communities whose relative priority cannot be securely established from the surviving evidence, and adopted by an increasing number of Greek poleis from the sixth century BCE onward, marks an important, though later, stage in this commercial transformation. Students should note carefully that the earliest and most intensive phase of Greek overseas settlement, examined in Sections 9.5 and 9.6, substantially preceded the widespread use of coined money, and did not depend upon it; earlier trade and settlement operated through barter, weighed metal, and other exchange mechanisms that predate coinage by well over a century in most regions. The spread of coinage during the sixth century BCE should therefore be understood as a development that intensified and reshaped an already substantial Mediterranean commercial economy, not as its origin.

Wealth generated through this expanding commercial economy did not distribute evenly across Greek society, though the precise pattern of that distribution varied considerably from one polis to another. In many communities, established elite households, with existing landholding, shipping capacity, and overseas connections, benefited disproportionately, a pattern that could intensify the social competition and stasis discussed in Section 9.2 even as it helped finance the temples, dedications, and monumental building projects that increasingly marked major Archaic sanctuaries such as Delphi and Olympia. At the same time, commercial growth created new sources of wealth outside traditional landed elites for at least some merchants, craftsmen, and sailors, though the political effects of this new wealth, whether it translated into greater political influence, and for whom, differed significantly by polis, a development that would matter considerably for the social and political conflicts examined in Section 9.10.

Section 9.9

Warfare, Hoplites, and the Armed Community

Beginning in the late eighth and continuing through the seventh century BCE, a growing number of Greek communities adopted a distinctive style of heavy infantry warfare associated with an equipment set that modern historians call the hoplite panoply: a large round shield, bronze helmet, breastplate, greaves, and a thrusting spear, worn and wielded by citizen soldiers who fought in a close, organized formation known as a phalanx. This bronze equipment was costly to acquire and maintain, and hoplite service was generally associated with citizens possessing enough property to equip themselves, tying military obligation to landholding and civic status in many Archaic poleis, though the specific relationship among wealth, military service, and political participation differed considerably from one community to another.

Older historical accounts sometimes described a sudden seventh century hoplite revolution, in which the appearance of phalanx warfare immediately and directly produced demands for political equality and, eventually, democratic government, since the argument ran that men who fought shoulder to shoulder for the community would no longer accept exclusion from its government. This chapter presents that formula with considerable caution rather than as settled fact. Historians continue to debate the chronology itself: some recent scholarship traces integrated bronze panoply equipment and massed close order fighting as far back as the late eighth century BCE, while other accounts describe a more gradual seventh century development, and the debate over dating remains genuinely active rather than settled in either direction. What can be said with more confidence is that the relationship between hoplite warfare and political change varied significantly from one polis to another. Sparta, examined in detail in Chapter 10, adopted heavy infantry warfare relatively early yet developed a highly restrictive rather than broadly participatory political system, a case that should itself caution students against assuming any single, automatic link between military change and political equality.

What can be said with greater confidence is that Archaic warfare increasingly connected military obligation to property, citizenship, and communal identity. Fighting for the polis, on land that the community's citizens themselves owned and cultivated, reinforced a growing sense that political membership, land, cult participation, and defense of the community belonged together as interconnected dimensions of citizenship, a theme this chapter carries forward into Section 9.10 and that Chapter 10 will examine in far greater depth through the specific and contrasting cases of Sparta and Athens.

Section 9.10

Aristocracy, Stasis, Tyranny, and the Definition of Citizenship

This final section returns the chapter's overseas and economic narrative to developments within the polis itself, bringing together the aristocratic competition and social strain described in Section 9.2, the commercial transformation described in Section 9.8, and the military change described in Section 9.9. Aristocratic families, whose wealth, landholding, and inherited status had long dominated Archaic political life, continued to compete intensely with one another for office, religious authority, and public prestige throughout this period, while facing, in a number of communities, growing pressure from citizens outside the traditional aristocracy who had gained wealth through trade, craft production, or hoplite military service and who increasingly resented their exclusion from political power.

This pressure, combined with recurring stasis, produced, in a number of Greek poleis during the seventh and sixth centuries BCE, the rise of individual rulers whom the Greeks called tyrannoi. Corinth and Sicyon provide well known seventh and sixth century examples. Students must understand this Archaic term historically rather than through its later, purely negative English derivative. A tyrannos was, in its original Archaic sense, an individual who seized and exercised one man rule outside, or in addition to, a community's normal inherited constitutional structures. Archaic tyrants were typically themselves members of aristocratic families who had prevailed in intense struggles against their own peers, rather than outsiders rising against the aristocracy as a whole. Later tradition sometimes portrays particular tyrants as drawing on wider dissatisfaction with entrenched elite rule or enjoying support outside the established aristocracy, but the extent and nature of any such broader support is difficult to reconstruct with confidence and should not be generalized into a single explanatory mechanism for tyranny across the Greek world. Some tyrants governed with considerable public building, patronage of religious cult, and economic development; others ruled harshly and were remembered by later generations, often hostile aristocratic or philosophical sources, in overwhelmingly negative terms. Tyranny should not be imagined as either universal, since many poleis never experienced it, or as a required evolutionary stage that automatically or inevitably led to democracy, since the outcomes following a period of tyranny varied enormously from one community to the next.

A related institutional development, distinct from tyranny but arising from some of the same pressures toward greater civic regulation, was the appearance of publicly inscribed law. From about the middle of the seventh century BCE, a number of poleis began recording rules and procedures in durable, publicly displayed form rather than relying solely on unwritten custom and aristocratic memory. The earliest known surviving example of this kind, an inscription from Dreros on Crete, regulates the community's kosmos office, limiting how often the same individual could hold it. Written law of this kind did not by itself imply democracy or broad political participation, and Dreros itself remained governed by a narrow aristocratic elite; its significance lies instead in showing that communal rules, including limits on officeholding, could be made durable, public, and, at least in principle, binding on powerful individuals as well as ordinary citizens. Publicly inscribed law would remain an important, if unevenly adopted, feature of Greek civic life throughout the remainder of the Archaic period and beyond.

Across this turbulent Archaic environment, civic membership and political participation were being defined, and contested, differently from one polis to another, rather than following a single path of gradual broadening. Citizenship increasingly bound together landholding, military obligation, participation in communal religious cult, standing before the community's laws, and some form, however limited, of political voice. Formal political participation, including office holding, deliberation in assembly, and service on juries where such institutions existed, was normally concentrated among free adult male citizens. Women of citizen families, however, could possess a recognized civic status of their own, established through descent, marriage, household role, inheritance, and participation in civic religious cult, even where they were excluded from the same political rights exercised by male citizens; the precise legal content of that status is best documented for later Athens and should not be assumed to have been identical across every Archaic polis. Resident non-citizens and enslaved people occupied still different, and generally more restricted, legal and civic positions outside the male political citizen body. This chapter deliberately stops at this general, comparative level. The detailed contrast between Sparta's militarized and highly restrictive citizenship and Athens's distinctive path toward broader civic participation, a contrast the Authoritative Chapter Outline assigns specifically to the next chapter, belongs to Chapter 10, which now takes up that comparison directly.

Historical Vocabulary
Tyrannos and Archaic Tyranny

The Greek word tyrannos described an individual who held sole power in a polis outside the community's normal inherited constitutional arrangements, generally without the negative moral connotation that the derived English word tyrant later acquired. Archaic tyrants typically emerged from within the aristocracy itself, prevailing in intense competition against rival elite families rather than rising as outsiders against the aristocratic order as a whole, although later tradition sometimes portrays particular tyrants as drawing on wider popular dissatisfaction, a claim that is difficult to verify and should not be generalized into a single mechanism for tyranny across the Greek world. Some Archaic tyrants governed capably and sponsored public building and religious cult; others ruled through fear and coercion, and later Greek political thought, particularly among philosophers writing after the Archaic period, developed a strongly negative theoretical account of tyranny as inherently unjust rule. Historians must therefore read Archaic tyranny on its own contemporary terms rather than automatically importing this later, more hostile philosophical judgment.

Primary Source Window

The Founding of Cyrene: Herodotus's Narrative and the Foundation Decree

Creator: Herodotus of Halicarnassus, writing in the fifth century BCE, describing events traditionally dated to about 631 or 630 BCE; a separately surviving inscription from Cyrene itself, inscribed in the fourth century BCE and known to modern scholars as the Foundation Decree of Cyrene, which purports to reproduce the text of a much earlier Theran oath or decree · Date: the traditional foundation date is approximately 631 or 630 BCE; Herodotus composed his account roughly two centuries afterward; the surviving inscription was carved some three centuries after the events it claims to record · Type: historical narrative and inscribed civic document of contested date and authenticity

Context: Herodotus recounts that settlers from the small Aegean island of Thera, responding to drought and hardship at home and, according to his narrative, prompted by an oracle from Delphi, sent settlers under an oikist to found a new community on the North African coast, a settlement that became Cyrene. A separate inscribed text, carved at Cyrene in the fourth century BCE, presents itself as a faithful copy of the original seventh century agreement between Thera and its settlers, but historians debate how far this much later inscription preserves authentic early wording as opposed to later reconstruction, elaboration, or outright invention serving fourth century civic purposes.

Paraphrase of Herodotus, Histories, Book IV, and the Cyrene Foundation Decree According to Herodotus, drought on Thera and the counsel of the Delphic oracle led the community to select settlers by lot for a distant expedition to Libya, an expedition that initially settled a small island before eventually establishing the mainland city of Cyrene under an oikist whom later Cyrenean tradition honored with heroic cult. The fourth century inscribed decree presents itself as the original agreement governing the settlers' obligations to Thera and to one another, including provisions for those who refused to go. This is a paraphrase and summary of material spread across several passages and a separate inscribed document, not a continuous quotation of either source.

Analysis: This case is unusually valuable for teaching historical method because it layers three distinct kinds of evidence, a fifth century literary narrative, a fourth century inscribed document claiming to preserve a seventh century agreement, and the archaeological record of Cyrene itself, none of which is strictly contemporary with the traditional late seventh century founding. Historians must ask whether the inscription accurately preserves an authentic early document or reflects later reconstruction for fourth century purposes, whether Herodotus drew on genuine local tradition or later embellishment, and how far archaeological evidence from Cyrene supports or complicates either textual account. No single source here can be taken at face value as a straightforward contemporary record.

Questions for students:

  1. Why does the gap of roughly two centuries between the traditional founding of Cyrene and Herodotus's account, and of roughly three centuries before the surviving inscription was carved, require historians to treat both sources with caution?
  2. What kinds of details in a foundation decree, such as provisions for those who refused to emigrate, might a much later community wish to preserve, invent, or embellish, and why?
  3. How might archaeological evidence from Cyrene be used to test claims made by the literary and inscribed textual traditions?
  4. Why does this layered case illustrate the general caution this textbook applies to foundation traditions across the Archaic Greek world?
Historical Debate

Why Did Greeks Establish Overseas Settlements?

The question: What combination of causes best explains the wave of Greek overseas settlement across the Archaic Mediterranean and Black Sea worlds, and how much weight should historians assign to agricultural land hunger as against commercial motive?

Interpretation A — Land and demographic pressure as the primary driver: Some historians emphasize archaeological and later textual evidence suggesting population growth and limited agricultural land in certain home communities, arguing that the search for new farmland best explains the pattern of settlement in fertile regions such as Sicily and southern Italy, where later tradition consistently describes agricultural motives.
Interpretation B — Commerce, exploration, and elite and political motives as equally or more significant: Other historians point to the earliest and most ambiguous settlements, such as Pithekoussai, as well as clearly commercial cases such as Naukratis, arguing that trade, access to metals, strategic control of maritime routes, and internal political conflict, including stasis and exile, drove much of the movement independently of any simple land shortage, and that the evidence for a general Greek overpopulation crisis is uneven and regionally inconsistent.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same archaeological record and the same body of later literary foundation traditions, but differ in how much explanatory weight to give agricultural motive relative to commercial, political, and exploratory motives, and in how far later traditions can be trusted to reflect authentic seventh and eighth century circumstances.

Why it matters: How historians resolve this question shapes whether Archaic Greek expansion is understood chiefly as a response to internal crisis at home or as an outward looking commercial and political movement, a distinction that affects how the entire Archaic period, and its connection to later political change, should be interpreted.

Student task: Using evidence from Sections 9.3 through 9.6, identify at least one specific settlement that supports each interpretation, and explain why relying on a single explanation for all Archaic Greek settlement would be historically insufficient.

Multiple Perspectives

Settlement from Several Sides

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor.

From the perspective of a younger son of a landholding family in a crowded home polis: With little prospect of inheriting sufficient land at home, joining an expedition overseas offers a real chance at landholding and standing in a new community, even at the cost of leaving family, cult, and familiar ground behind.
From the perspective of a Corinthian merchant financing a settlement expedition: A new settlement at a well chosen harbor secures trading partners, raw materials, and a foothold in distant markets, a commercial investment as much as an act of civic generosity.
From the perspective of a Sicel farmer in eastern Sicily: Newcomers arriving by sea have begun claiming coastal land long used by our communities, and whether they become trading partners, unwelcome neighbors, or something in between depends on how far inland their settlements eventually push.
From the perspective of an Egyptian official overseeing Naukratis: Greek and other foreign traders bring valuable goods and revenue to this Egyptian administered port, and granting particular Greek cities recognized sanctuaries and commercial privileges within it allows the kingdom to benefit from that trade while Egyptian authority over the port and its surrounding land remains unquestioned.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Emerging Polis to Mediterranean Network

What Changed

  • Greek communities moved from largely localized settlement, as described in Chapter 8, to a settlement network spanning the western Mediterranean, the Black Sea, and North Africa
  • Commerce expanded from local and regional exchange toward a Mediterranean wide economy connecting Greek, Phoenician, Etruscan, Egyptian, and steppe trading partners
  • Warfare shifted toward organized hoplite formations tying military obligation to property and citizenship
  • A number of poleis experienced tyranny as aristocratic dominance faced growing internal challenge

What Stayed the Same

  • The polis remained the basic, though not universal, unit of Greek political organization, in the homeland and in new apoikiai alike
  • Aristocratic families continued to dominate landholding, religious authority, and political office in most communities
  • Formal political participation remained concentrated among free adult male citizens, and resident foreigners and the enslaved occupied restricted legal and civic positions outside that body, though the precise extent of participation and exclusion varied considerably by polis
  • Political fragmentation among independent, often rival, poleis persisted rather than dissolving into a unified Greek state

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both expansion and persistent local political fragmentation prepares students to understand why the Greek world entered the fifth century BCE as a vast network of independent, competitive communities rather than a single unified civilization.

Cause and Effect

Warfare, Land, and Citizenship

Long-term causes

Limited fertile land in much of the Greek homeland, combined with expanding overseas settlement and commerce, created new sources of wealth for citizens outside the traditional aristocracy.

Short-term causes

The spread of costly bronze hoplite equipment and organized phalanx tactics, whose precise chronology remains debated, tied heavy infantry service to citizens able to equip themselves.

Effect

Military obligation, landholding, and civic standing became increasingly linked, though in different ways in different poleis, and this shift intersected with intra elite conflict to produce tyranny in some communities or, in later periods examined in Chapter 10, broader civic participation in others.

This chapter's caution

The link between hoplite warfare and political change was neither immediate nor uniform, and Sparta's contrasting trajectory, examined in Chapter 10, shows that similar military developments did not produce identical political outcomes.

Historical complexity: Overseas settlement, commercial wealth, hoplite warfare, and tyranny interacted differently in each polis, and no single causal chain explains political change across the entire Archaic Greek world.

Everyday Life and Social History

Leaving Home, Arriving Somewhere New

Behind the political and economic patterns examined in this chapter lay ordinary human experience: families deciding whether a son should join a settlement expedition, farmers adapting unfamiliar land and crops to techniques learned at home, craftsmen finding new markets and new materials among unfamiliar neighbors, and religious communities carrying familiar cults, calendars, and festivals to newly founded sanctuaries far from the sanctuaries of the metropolis. Marriage between settlers and local non-Greek women is attested, with varying frequency, in the archaeological and later literary record for several regions of settlement, complicating any simple picture of a purely Greek population in the new apoikiai.

Daily life in a new settlement required adapting inherited Greek agricultural knowledge, house design, and craft techniques to unfamiliar soils, climates, and neighboring populations, while participation in symposia, athletic competition, and communal religious festival helped maintain a sense of shared Greek identity across a settlement network that stretched, by the end of the Archaic period, from the Black Sea to the western Mediterranean. Understanding these ordinary adaptations helps ensure that the political history of tyranny, warfare, and citizenship examined in this chapter does not obscure the lived experience of the men, women, and families who actually crossed the Mediterranean or received newcomers on their own shores.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has taken the emerging polis described in Chapter 8 and placed it into motion across the Archaic Mediterranean and Black Sea worlds, examining both the overseas settlement of Greek communities and the internal social, economic, military, and political transformations that accompanied that expansion between roughly 800 and 500 BCE.

The chapter began by establishing the internal pressures, uneven demographic growth, limited agricultural land, aristocratic competition, and recurring stasis, that formed the background against which particular communities sent settlers overseas. It then examined, with deliberate care, the range of explanations historians offer for that movement, rejecting any single cause such as generalized Greek overpopulation in favor of a heterogeneous account combining land hunger, political conflict, exile, and commercial opportunity.

Careful attention to vocabulary distinguished the apoikia, generally a politically independent new community, from the metropolis that sent it out, the oikist traditionally credited with its founding, and the emporion, a settlement organized principally around regulated trade. The chapter then surveyed Greek settlement across Italy, Sicily, and the western Mediterranean, including Pithekoussai, Cumae, Syracuse, Naxos, and Massalia, before turning eastward and southward to the Black Sea, Cyrene, and Naukratis, deliberately correcting any impression that Greek expansion moved only westward.

A central section of the chapter examined relationships between Greek settlers and the Etruscan, Phoenician, Sicel, Sicanian, Elymian, Thracian, Scythian, Egyptian, Libyan, and Iberian societies among whom they settled, emphasizing genuine variation, trade, intermarriage, competition, and conflict, rather than a single uniform pattern of Greek expansion into passive or empty territory.

The chapter then returned to developments within Greek communities themselves, examining the growth of Mediterranean commerce and the later emergence of coinage in western Anatolia, the debated chronology and complicated, non automatic relationship between hoplite warfare and political change, and the recurring appearance of Archaic tyranny as a product chiefly of intra elite competition rather than a simple popular revolt against the aristocracy. It also introduced the early development of publicly inscribed law, exemplified by the seventh century regulation from Dreros. It closed by describing how civic membership and political participation were being defined and contested differently across Archaic poleis, deliberately stopping short of the detailed comparison between Spartan and Athenian political development that Chapter 10 now takes up.

By the close of the Archaic period, around 500 BCE, the Greek world had become a dense network of several hundred politically independent poleis and apoikiai stretching from the Black Sea to the western Mediterranean, deeply entangled with the non-Greek societies among whom Greek settlers had made their homes, and internally transformed by commerce, changing warfare, and political conflict. This transformed, expanded, and internally contested Greek world is the setting within which Chapter 10 now examines why Sparta and Athens, sharing this same broad Archaic environment, developed into such strikingly different political and social orders.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has carried the still forming polis of Chapter 8 outward, across the sea, into contact with other peoples, into an expanding commercial economy, into new forms of warfare, and into new political conflicts. None of this movement was centrally organized, uniformly experienced, or free of genuine violence and displacement, and none of it can be reduced to a single cause. Land hunger, commercial ambition, political exile, and strategic calculation each played a documented role in different settlements at different times, and the societies Greek settlers encountered, Etruscan, Phoenician, Sicel, Scythian, Egyptian, Libyan, and Iberian among them, were active participants in these encounters rather than a passive backdrop.

At the same time, the internal life of Greek communities was itself being transformed by this same period of expansion. Aristocratic competition, stasis, the gradual spread of hoplite warfare, and the recurring appearance of tyranny all reflect a Greek world working out, unevenly and without any predetermined outcome, what citizenship, land, military service, and political power should mean for a community. This chapter has deliberately stopped at the point where that working out becomes most historically interesting, the sharply contrasting paths taken by Sparta and Athens, leaving that comparison to Chapter 10.

Students who understand that Archaic Greek expansion was heterogeneous, entangled with non-Greek societies, and internally connected to political and military change at home are well prepared to read Chapter 10's detailed account of Spartan and Athenian development, and, further ahead, Chapter 11's account of how this same expanded and interconnected Greek world confronted the Persian Empire. The Mediterranean and Black Sea network this chapter has described did not simply set the stage for later Greek history; it was itself one of the most consequential transformations of the Archaic age.
