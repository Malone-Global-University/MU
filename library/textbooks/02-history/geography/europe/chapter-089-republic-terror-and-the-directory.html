    • Chapter Opening

    On the morning of August 10, 1792, the bells of Paris sounded the alarm, columns of armed citizens converged on the royal palace, and a monarchy that had existed for more than a thousand years was suspended within hours. Seven years later, on November 10, 1799, soldiers cleared a legislative hall at Saint-Cloud, and a republic that had been created to prevent the return of arbitrary power gave way to a new concentration of authority.

    This chapter studies the seven years between those two days. In that time France became a republic, tried and executed its king, fought a war against much of Europe, suppressed rebellions within its own territory, abolished slavery in its colonies, built the most extensive system of emergency government that Europe had yet seen, overthrew that system, wrote a new constitution, and governed itself through an executive of five Directors until a coup replaced them. These events are often told as a sequence leading toward Napoleon Bonaparte, but the chapter treats them as a historical problem in their own right.

    The problem is how the republic tried to reconcile ideals that were not easily compatible: liberty and equality, popular sovereignty and the rule of law, national survival and individual rights, political participation and political stability. Each answer produced new difficulties. The Convention of 1792 promised a republic of equal citizens but governed through committees. The Constitution of 1793 offered a democratic framework but was suspended. The Constitution of 1795 was built to prevent both dictatorship and popular insurgency, but it lacked a procedure to resolve conflict. Understanding why these experiments failed requires attention to war, civil conflict, economic insecurity, colonial slavery, and the choices of individuals and groups, not only to ideology.

    The chapter treats the republic as something experienced differently by different people. A Parisian artisan in a section assembly, a woman standing in a bread line, a peasant in the bocage of western France, an enslaved cultivator in Saint-Domingue, a deputy of the Plain trying to survive the Terror, and a property owner in a provincial town after Thermidor did not see the same republic. The evidence is correspondingly varied: debates in the Moniteur and the Archives parlementaires, laws and decrees, police reports, section records, tribunal files, letters, memoirs, newspapers, engravings, and paintings. Each type of evidence has biases, and the chapter attempts to show how historians weigh them.

    The chapter also asks students to avoid two simplifications. The first is to treat the period as a series of executions followed by Napoleon's inevitable rise. The Directory lasted four years, passed through several political realignments, restructured state finances, and presided over military expansion, and its collapse resulted from specific decisions. The second is to treat the Terror as the expression of a single will. Revolutionary government involved competing committees, local officials, popular militants, and a Convention that voted for the measures that later became the subject of its own recriminations.

    The chapter belongs to Part XIV, Revolution and the Destruction of the Old Regime, and to Volume IV, Enlightenment, Revolution, Industry, and Empire. It follows Chapter 87, which examined the fiscal and Atlantic conditions leading to 1789, and Chapter 88, which traced the Revolution from the Estates-General to the breakdown of constitutional monarchy. It leads to Chapter 90, on Napoleon and the French Empire, to Chapter 91, on resistance, nationalism, and the Napoleonic wars, and to Chapter 92, on the Congress of Vienna and the revolutionary legacy. The ideological consequences of the period are examined in Chapter 97 and Chapter 98.

    Finally, the chapter connects the French Revolution to the wider world. The republic's wars changed the political map of the Low Countries, the Rhineland, Italy, and Switzerland. The revolution in Saint-Domingue and the Convention's decree of 1794 tie the story to the Atlantic slave economy discussed in Chapter 70, and the Egyptian expedition links it to the Ottoman Empire and the Mediterranean. The sources of the revolutionary period were also the sources of later political memory, so the chapter regularly distinguishes what the records show from how later generations remembered and mythologized the Terror, Robespierre, and Thermidor.

    Learning Objectives

    By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

    1. Explain the sequence by which royal authority ended between August and September 1792, distinguishing suspension, abolition, and the creation of republican institutions.
    2. Analyze the Convention's divisions among Girondins, Montagnards, and the Plain without treating them as modern political parties.
    3. Trace the trial and execution of Louis XVI and assess its constitutional and political consequences.
    4. Explain how war, conscription, and the levée en masse transformed the republic and its citizens.
    5. Compare the Vendée uprising and the federalist revolts and evaluate the republic's responses.
    6. Distinguish the Constitution of 1793, which was never applied, from the emergency government that actually ruled.
    7. Assess the relationship between colonial insurrection, military necessity, and the abolition of slavery in 1794.
    8. Analyze the institutions, laws, and regional patterns of the Terror while distinguishing categories of political violence.
    9. Interpret Thermidor and its aftermath, including the White Terror and the popular uprisings of 1795.
    10. Evaluate the Constitution of Year III and the Directory's political, financial, and military record.
    11. Contextualize the coup of Brumaire within French institutional history rather than as the product of one man's genius.
    12. Interpret primary sources, including laws, constitutions, and a painting, identifying their limitations and silences.

    Guiding Historical Questions

    Questions for Historical Inquiry

    1. How did the republic emerge from an insurrection and a military emergency, and what did contemporaries think they were creating?
    2. Why did representatives who agreed on republican government disagree so sharply about participation, property, and coercion?
    3. How did war, civil conflict, and scarcity interact to produce the institutions of emergency government?
    4. To what extent was the Terror a response to circumstances, and to what extent did ideology and political choices shape it?
    5. How did enslaved people, colonial commissioners, and the Convention each contribute to the abolition of slavery in 1794?
    6. Why did the end of Jacobin rule in Thermidor not bring political stability or an end to violence?
    7. Which weaknesses in the Constitution of Year III contributed to the Directory's instability, and which problems came from outside the constitution?
    8. How should historians distinguish the evidence of laws and speeches from the experience of ordinary people and the memory of later generations?

    Historical Context

    Chapter 88 explained how the Revolution of 1789 to 1792 created a constitutional monarchy, abolished feudal privileges, nationalized church lands, and divided the clergy over the Civil Constitution. By early 1792 the king's flight to Varennes in June 1791 had undermined confidence in the monarchy, and the declaration of war on Austria in April 1792 placed the new regime under military pressure. Neither the constitution of 1791 nor the Legislative Assembly that it created had a means of resolving a conflict between a distrusted executive and a suspicious legislature, a problem that anticipated later difficulties.

    The republic was created in a context of fiscal and economic strain. The Revolution had abolished many old taxes and replaced them with new ones that were slow to produce revenue. The state financed itself by issuing assignats, paper money secured on confiscated lands, and as the volume of paper increased its value declined. War multiplied expenses, and the nationalization of church property had created a large body of owners who had a material interest in the survival of the Revolution. These conditions help explain why many deputies hesitated to restore any form of monarchy that might return the land to its old owners.

    The political culture of the period was shaped by the Enlightenment, examined in Chapters 82 and 83, and by the American Revolution and Atlantic debates described in Chapter 87. Ideas of natural rights, popular sovereignty, and civic virtue, often derived from readings of Rousseau, Montesquieu, and classical republics, provided the vocabulary, but revolutionaries used them in competing ways. The popular movement of the Paris sections added another element, a tradition of direct action, grain regulation, and neighborhood solidarity that predated the Revolution and gave the sans-culottes their distinctive politics.

    The colonial dimension was equally significant. Saint-Domingue, the most profitable colony of the French empire, depended on the labor of several hundred thousand enslaved people. The uprising of August 1791 and the Legislative Assembly's decree of April 1792 granting political equality to free men of color, discussed in Chapter 88, left the colony in turmoil, and the arrival of war with Britain and Spain in 1793 internationalized the struggle. The Atlantic economy examined in Chapters 70 and 73 therefore formed part of the republic's political problems, not a separate story.

    The sources for this chapter are exceptionally rich but must be used critically. Parliamentary debates were recorded in the Moniteur and later collected in the Archives parlementaires; laws appear in printed collections; police agents reported on public opinion in Paris; representatives on mission wrote to the committees; tribunal and prison registers survive; and newspapers, pamphlets, engravings, and memoirs offer partisan views. After Thermidor, many participants wrote to justify themselves, and their accounts often exaggerate Robespierre's role. The memory of the Terror, the Vendée, and Thermidor was later used by royalists, republicans, liberals, and socialists in arguments about the meaning of the Revolution, and a central task of historians is to separate documented events from these later narratives.

    Division I · Sections 89.1 to 89.4 · August 1792 to early 1793

    The Overthrow of Monarchy and the Foundation of the French Republic

    This division explains how the constitutional monarchy created in 1791 collapsed, and why the republic that replaced it confronted, from its first days, crises of legitimacy, political violence, and war. The republic was not the product of a prepared plan. It emerged from an insurrection in the capital, a military emergency on the frontier, and a series of improvised decisions by deputies who disagreed about what a republic should be.

    Historical Vocabulary
    Historical Vocabulary 1: National Convention, Girondins and related terms
    National Convention
    The assembly of 749 deputies elected in September 1792 under a broad male franchise to draft a republican constitution; it governed France, through its committees, until October 1795.
    Girondins
    A loosely connected group of deputies around Brissot, Vergniaud, and Roland who defended legality, provincial voices, and representative government against the Paris Commune; a retrospective label, not a party.
    Montagnards
    Deputies who sat on the high benches of the left, including Robespierre, Danton, and Marat; they favored cooperation with the Paris popular movement and emergency measures. They were never politically uniform.
    The Plain
    The large body of uncommitted deputies, also called the Marsh, who decided most votes and generally supported whichever group seemed able to secure order.
    Popular sovereignty
    The principle that authority belongs to the people; republicans disagreed over whether it was exercised only through elected representatives or also through direct action, petitions, and insurrection.
    Section 89.1

    The Insurrection of August 10, 1792, and the Fall of Constitutional Monarchy

    Chapter 88 ended with a constitutional monarchy that few of its participants trusted. The Constitution of 1791 had made Louis XVI a hereditary executive officer with a suspensive veto, yet the king had attempted to flee in June 1791, had vetoed measures against émigrés and refractory priests, and had presided over a court that many deputies suspected of seeking foreign intervention. When the Legislative Assembly declared war on Austria on April 20, 1792, the decision was supported by very different groups for opposite reasons: Brissot and his allies expected war to expose treason and spread liberty, while the court hoped that military defeat might restore royal authority. The first months of fighting favored neither hope. French armies, disorganized by the emigration of many noble officers, collapsed in early engagements, and by midsummer Prussia had joined Austria in the field.

    The political temperature in Paris rose accordingly. In June 1792 crowds invaded the Tuileries Palace, and the king's dismissal of Girondin-leaning ministers and his veto of measures for a camp of provincial volunteers deepened suspicion. In July the Assembly declared the nation in danger, an emergency measure that authorized local authorities to act, recruited volunteers, and legitimized popular vigilance. Provincial National Guards, known as fédérés, converged on Paris, among them a battalion from Marseille whose marching song would later become the national anthem. Then, in the first days of August, news arrived of the Brunswick Manifesto, issued in the name of the commander of the allied armies, the Duke of Brunswick, and drafted in the circle of the émigrés. It threatened Paris with severe punishment if the royal family were harmed. Historians generally agree that the document, whatever its intention as deterrence, served mainly to confirm the revolutionaries' belief that the king was bound up with the invaders.

    The insurrection was organized less by the Legislative Assembly than by the Paris sections, the forty-eight districts into which the city was divided, whose general assemblies had been radicalized by the war emergency and increasingly opened to men whom the Constitution of 1791 had classed as passive citizens. In the night of August 9 to 10 delegates from the sections replaced the legal municipality with an insurrectionary Paris Commune, and the tocsin sounded. Columns of National Guards from the working districts, joined by the fédérés, marched on the Tuileries at dawn. The king, advised that the palace could not be held, crossed to the Assembly hall with his family, leaving the defense to the Swiss Guards, several hundred Swiss soldiers who formed part of the royal household troops, and to a number of armed nobles and National Guards. Firing began at the palace. The Swiss, who had initially held their ground, were overwhelmed or withdrew in disorder; many were killed during the fighting or in the reprisals that followed. Estimates of the dead vary, as they do for most events of this kind, though the Swiss losses were heavy and several hundred attackers also died.

    The Legislative Assembly, meeting while the fighting continued, did not abolish the monarchy. It voted, under pressure, to suspend the king from his functions, to hold him and his family provisionally under guard, and to summon a National Convention to draw up a new constitution. The deputies decided that the Convention would be elected by a broader electorate than the Constitution of 1791 had allowed, setting aside the distinction between active and passive citizens for male citizens who were not domestic servants. The royal family was lodged, by the Commune's insistence, in the Temple, a medieval fortress in the north of Paris, rather than in the more comfortable quarters the Assembly had proposed. The episode shows the pattern that would recur throughout the republic: a legal body ratified an outcome shaped by the capital's armed population, and the Commune and the sections claimed a share of sovereignty that the deputies could neither fully accept nor safely deny.

    It is important to distinguish the successive stages by which royal authority ended. On August 10 the king was suspended, not deposed; executive power passed to a provisional council of ministers, which included Danton as minister of justice; and the monarchy survived, in law, until the Convention met. The overthrow of the monarchy was therefore a two-stage process, with a popular insurrection and a provisional suspension in August, and a formal constitutional decision in September. Many Parisians who acted on August 10 did so out of immediate fear of betrayal rather than from a developed republican program, and republicanism, still a minority position in 1791, became the dominant language of politics only through the experience of the summer.

    The insurrection also had consequences beyond the capital. General Lafayette, commanding an army on the northeastern frontier, refused to recognize the events of August 10 and, after failing to persuade his troops or local authorities, crossed the frontier on August 19 and was taken prisoner by the Austrians. Other officers followed the opposite course, accepting the new authorities, and the Legislative Assembly dismissed or arrested many officials considered unreliable. Meanwhile the allied armies advanced: Longwy fell on August 23 and Verdun, the last fortress of significance on the road to Paris, on September 2. Evidence for these events comes from several kinds of source, including the debates recorded in the newspaper the Moniteur universel and later collected in the Archives parlementaires, municipal and section registers, police reports, letters, and later memoirs. Each carries bias: the Moniteur summarized proceedings with editorial choices, the memoirs of royalist survivors emphasized the killing of the Swiss, and republican writers framed the day as a justified defense of the nation.

    Primary Source Window 1

    Legislative Assembly Proceedings on the Suspension of Louis XVI, August 10, 1792

    Issuing authority or creator: Legislative Assembly of France · Date: August 10 to 11, 1792 · Document type: Parliamentary decrees recorded in the Moniteur universel and the Archives parlementaires · Jurisdiction: France, the Constitution of 1791 in force

    Context: The Assembly was meeting in the Manège while fighting continued at the Tuileries. Its decrees were passed under pressure from delegates of the insurrectionary Commune and under the sound of gunfire.

    Editorial paraphrase (not a verbatim translation)The Assembly provisionally suspended the king from his functions, ordered that he and his family be kept under guard pending the Convention's decision, summoned a National Convention to be elected by male citizens aged twenty-one and over who were not domestic servants, and left executive power to a provisional council of ministers.

    Analysis: The decrees show that the Assembly framed the event as a provisional and legal measure rather than a revolution against monarchy as such. They also reveal how broad the new franchise was in comparison with that of 1791, and how far the deputies were ratifying decisions already made in the streets.

    Limitations: The printed record condenses debates and does not reveal the pressures in the hall. It is silent about the fighting outside, about the Swiss Guards, and about how ordinary Parisians understood the day.

    Questions for students:

    1. What does the Assembly decide, and what does it leave undecided?
    2. Who issued the decrees, and under what pressure?
    3. What can historians learn about the legal form given to an insurrection?
    4. What can this source not establish about the motives of the insurgents or the course of the fighting?

    Documentary reference: Archives parlementaires, legislative-assembly proceedings; consult relevant 1792 volume and cross-check transcription

    Section 89.2

    The September Massacres, Valmy, the National Convention, and the Birth of the Republic

    The fall of Longwy and the approach of the Prussian army in the last days of August created conditions of extreme fear in Paris. The Commune ordered house searches for hidden weapons and suspects, and thousands of people, including nobles, refractory priests, and others, were arrested and held in the city's prisons. On September 2, as news of Verdun's fall reached the capital, the alarm was sounded and volunteers were urged to march to the front. Rumors spread that prisoners, many of whom were supposed to be royalist conspirators, would break out and attack the city as volunteers left. Between September 2 and 6, groups of armed men, some of them National Guards and fédérés and some of them local activists, entered the prisons of the Abbaye, the Carmelite convent, La Force, the Conciergerie, the Châtelet, the Bicêtre, and the Salpêtrière. Informal tribunals interrogated some prisoners and acquitted a number of them; many others were killed on the spot.

    Modern estimates place the number killed at roughly 1,100 to 1,400, which would amount to about half the prison population of Paris at that time. The victims included priests, aristocrats, and royal Swiss officers, but a majority were ordinary prisoners, including common criminals, women held in the Salpêtrière, and adolescents in the Bicêtre. The massacres therefore cannot be understood as a purely political purge. The responsibility of public authorities is a subject of careful distinction. The Commune and its surveillance committee did not issue a straightforward order for a general slaughter, though some members, notably Marat in his journal and in a circular sent from the Commune's committee to provincial authorities, justified or encouraged popular justice. The Legislative Assembly and the Girondin minister Roland condemned the violence in speeches but did little to stop it, and Danton, as minister of justice, has been criticized by contemporaries and historians for failing to act. This pattern, in which a revolutionary regime did not clearly order violence but declined to prevent it and then defended its legitimacy, is the first of several episodes in this chapter requiring care in assigning responsibility.

    Valmy, on September 20, 1792, offered an unexpected release from the military emergency. The Prussian advance toward Paris was met in Champagne by French armies under Dumouriez and Kellermann, mixing regulars of the old royal army, volunteers, and artillery that had survived the emigration of many officers. The engagement was a cannonade rather than a massed infantry battle, and French casualties were modest. Its significance was strategic and political. The allied commander, the Duke of Brunswick, faced bad weather, sickness among his troops, supply problems, and a French army that did not collapse, and he began negotiations and withdrew. Historians still debate how far the withdrawal was determined by military reasoning, by diplomatic considerations concerning Poland, or by the unexpected resilience of the French artillery. What is clear is that the invasion halted, the capital was spared, and revolutionaries came to see the day as proof that citizens defending their country could defeat the armies of kings.

    On the same day, September 20, the National Convention met for the first time. Elections had been held under a broad male franchise, in a two-stage process, with participation that was low, perhaps well under a majority of those eligible, partly because of fear, partly because of the political climate, and partly because many were uninterested or unwilling to vote under open conditions. The Convention included 749 deputies, among them many men who had sat in earlier assemblies, and a large number of lawyers and local officials. The Convention's first major act, on September 21, was to abolish royalty in France. The proceedings, recorded in the Archives parlementaires, show the motion presented by Collot d'Herbois, supported by Grégoire, and adopted without reported opposition. The decree stated a decision, rather than a constitution, and the Convention did not at once define the republic in positive terms. On September 22 it decreed that public acts should be dated from the first year of the republic, and the formula was later extended to the revolutionary calendar, which was formally adopted in October 1793 and applied retroactively, so that September 22, 1792, became the first day of Year I. On September 25 the Convention declared the republic one and indivisible, a phrase intended to answer the charge that republicans wished to divide France.

    The creation of the republic did not mean that republican political identity was immediately universal. In 1792 many provincial communities experienced the change through proclamations, festivals, and the replacement of royal symbols, while ordinary people continued to think about taxes, grain, conscription, and the church in terms of their own local concerns. Evidence about popular opinion comes from reports sent by administrators, letters from representatives, police reports on public opinion, and petitions, all of which are filtered through the concerns of officials. Still, the republic's early weeks were marked by a sense of possibility, and also by the memory of the September Massacres, which gave opponents a durable argument that republican government had been born in violence. The Girondins, in particular, used the massacres to attack the Parisian leaders, and the Montagnards, in reply, argued that fear of invasion explained the crowd's anger and that the Girondins were using the memory to discredit Paris. The dispute became one of the principal axes of Convention politics.

    Section 89.3

    The Trial and Execution of Louis XVI: Sovereignty, Justice, and the Fate of Kingship

    Once the monarchy was abolished, the question of what to do with the former king could not be avoided. Louis XVI, now addressed as Louis Capet, remained a prisoner in the Temple, and in the autumn of 1792 the Convention was divided over whether he could or should be judged. The question was not simple. The Constitution of 1791 had declared the king's person inviolable, and a few deputies argued that the Convention, as a constituent body, lacked the authority to try a man whom the constitution had placed beyond prosecution for acts committed in office. Others replied that the monarchy had ceased and that the king's inviolability had ended with it. Still others argued that a nation at war with a king's allies could not treat him as an ordinary defendant.

    The turning point came on November 20, 1792, when a locksmith named Gamain revealed the existence of a hidden iron cabinet, the armoire de fer, in the Tuileries. The documents recovered from it, along with papers found elsewhere in the palace, were examined by the Convention and showed the king's contacts with figures such as Mirabeau and with royalist and foreign agents. They did not show a complete plan of treason, and historians have noted that the evidence was selective and sometimes ambiguous, but they were taken by most deputies as confirmation of the king's duplicity. The Convention decided on December 3 that it would itself judge the king, and the trial before the full Convention began on December 11. The king appeared with defense counsel, among them Malesherbes, Tronchet, and de Sèze, who argued that the king had been constitutionally inviolable, that the accusations lacked sufficient proof, and that the nation should not judge a man whom it had previously declared above the law.

    Saint-Just's speech of November 13, an early contribution to the debate, held that the king should be judged not as a citizen but as an enemy, since every king was a rebel and usurper, and that a trial would imply that the king might be innocent and the Revolution guilty. Robespierre, speaking on December 3, took a similar position, though with a different emphasis, arguing that the king need not be tried at all, since the people had already judged him on August 10, and that the nation's survival required that he die. Opponents of immediate execution, many of whom were Girondins, argued that a trial ought to follow legal forms, that the king's execution might isolate France in Europe, and that the people should be consulted. They proposed an appeal to the people, that is, ratification of the verdict by the primary assemblies of voters. Their critics replied that such an appeal would risk civil war.

    The Convention proceeded by roll-call votes, taken in public, in January 1793. On January 15 deputies answered a series of questions. They overwhelmingly found the king guilty, with a few abstentions and no recorded votes for acquittal. They rejected the appeal to the people by a vote in the range of 424 to 287. The decision on the penalty followed on January 16 and 17, in a session that lasted almost twenty-four hours. The result was very close: a narrow majority voted for death, with the exact margin depending on how conditional votes, some linked to a reprieve or to postponement, are counted. On January 19 the Convention considered a reprieve, which it rejected by a vote of roughly 380 to 310. The king was executed on January 21, 1793, in the Place de la Révolution, after a night spent with his family and his confessor. Historians use the Archives parlementaires and the Moniteur for the proceedings, but they have also drawn on diaries and letters of deputies, and later memoirs, which sometimes differ about what pressures deputies felt, including the presence of crowds in the galleries and rumors of threats.

    The execution altered French politics in several ways. At home, the vote revealed the division between those who sought to end the issue and those who feared its consequences, and the Girondins were weakened by their association with the appeal to the people, which their opponents used to depict them as hesitant or secretly royalist. Abroad, the execution hardened opposition among monarchies. Britain expelled the French ambassador, and the Convention soon declared war on Britain and the Dutch Republic. Historians generally agree that the war was already becoming general before January, and the execution was one factor among others, including French annexations and the opening of the Scheldt, rather than the sole cause. In religious terms, many Catholics, particularly in western France, interpreted the king's death as a sacrilege, and royalists developed a martyrology around Louis that survived through the nineteenth century.

    The trial also raised a larger question about the relationship between justice and revolutionary legitimacy. Was the Convention exercising judicial power, or was it acting as sovereign, making a political decision under judicial forms? The answer given by the Montagnards, that sovereignty permitted exceptional action, would later reappear in the justification of emergency institutions. Critics at the time and since have argued that the trial confused legislative, judicial, and political functions. Defenders have replied that no preexisting institution could try a king in a republic, and that the public votes gave the proceeding a transparency unusual in the history of royal trials. Students should distinguish judgments about the king's conduct, which many contemporaries and historians regarded as conspiratorial, from the separate constitutional and moral question of whether a revolutionary assembly could legitimately execute him.

    Section 89.4

    The National Convention: Girondins, Montagnards, the Plain, and Competing Republican Visions

    The National Convention was at once a legislature, a constituent assembly, and, through its committees, an executive government. It met first in the Manège, the former riding hall next to the Tuileries, and from May 1793 in the Tuileries Palace itself. Its 749 deputies sat in an open hall with galleries that allowed spectators from the Paris sections to attend, applaud, and intimidate. The Convention did not simply legislate; it also appointed and dismissed ministers, sent its own members as representatives on mission to the armies and departments, supervised the Paris sections indirectly, and judged cases of treason. The republican constitution it had been summoned to write would be delayed for months, so that the republic existed without a settled constitutional framework.

    Contemporaries and historians group deputies into three broad tendencies. The Girondins, a label that derived partly from the deputies of the Gironde department but was applied to a wider group around Brissot, Vergniaud, Roland, Condorcet, and others, emphasized legality, representative government, the liberty of provincial France, and the protection of property, and they distrusted the Paris sections and Commune. Many had supported the war in 1792 and many believed Paris exercised excessive influence. The Montagnards, so named because they sat on the high benches of the left, included Robespierre, Danton, Marat, Saint-Just, Couthon, Billaud-Varenne, Collot d'Herbois, and many deputies of Paris. They argued that the representatives must work with the popular movement, that the republic must defeat its enemies by exceptional measures, and that the people's vigilance was a necessary check on those who held office. Between them sat the largest group, often called the Plain or the Marsh, whose members voted according to circumstances, often supporting whichever side seemed to offer security.

    These labels are useful but must be used with care. The Girondins and the Montagnards were not disciplined modern political parties with fixed membership, platforms, or voting discipline. They were networks of acquaintances, shaped by friendships, personal rivalries, regional ties, and the experiences of the previous assemblies, and many deputies moved between positions. Brissot and Robespierre had once been allies in the Jacobin Club, and the break between them over the war in early 1792 was personal as well as political. The Montagnards were not politically uniform either. Marat was a journalist whose language of denunciation alarmed even his allies, Danton was a pragmatic orator who sought compromise with the Girondins in early 1793, Robespierre emphasized virtue and the sovereignty of the people, and Saint-Just emphasized institutions and the republic's moral foundation. The Plain was not a single bloc and included many men who had served in local government and who feared disorder on both sides.

    Why did men who agreed on republican government disagree so sharply? First, they disagreed over the relationship between representation and popular sovereignty. The Girondins held that the Convention represented the nation and that the sections of Paris were a single department among eighty-three. The Montagnards, while also defending the Convention, tended to hold that the people retained the right to supervise and, in an emergency, to intervene. Second, they differed on property and economic regulation. Most deputies held that property was a fundamental right and shared the assumption that the market in grain should be free, but the Montagnards were more willing to accept regulation in a crisis, particularly when pressed by popular demands for subsistence. Third, they differed over coercion: the Girondins feared that exceptional institutions would create a dictatorship, while the Montagnards held that without them the republic would perish.

    Their conflict was sharpened by personal accusations and by the pressure of events. The Girondins accused Robespierre of aspiring to dictatorship and Marat of inciting murder, and they pressed the Convention to prosecute those responsible for the September Massacres. The Montagnards accused the Girondins of having provoked the war, of having protected Dumouriez, and of dividing the republic by attacking Paris. The Convention's departments, the provincial clubs, and the press carried the quarrel into every region. Historians have used the Convention's debates, the correspondence of deputies, the pamphlets and newspapers of the period, and police reports to reconstruct the conflict, but they differ over how far the struggle expressed fundamental social or ideological division and how far it reflected the dynamics of faction, personality, and fear. Older interpretations that treated the Girondins as representatives of a commercial bourgeoisie and the Montagnards as spokesmen of the popular movement have been qualified by prosopographical work showing that both groups were drawn from similar professional and propertied backgrounds.

    By the early spring of 1793 these quarrels were intertwined with military defeat, the outbreak of civil war, and the first emergency institutions. The Girondins, who had held the ministries in the winter of 1792 to 1793, lost influence as the military situation worsened. Danton, who had tried to mediate, was himself criticized for his contacts with Dumouriez, and the Convention turned to institutions that placed more power in its own committees. The conflict among republicans would not remain a matter of speeches. It would lead, in the journées of May 31 to June 2, to the removal of the Girondin leadership by force, and it would raise the question that haunts the whole republican period: whether political opposition within a republic could be legitimate or whether, in a state of emergency, it would be treated as treason.

War, rebellion, factional struggle, and slave emancipation were not separate stories.     Section 89.5

    International War, the First Coalition, and the Mobilization of Revolutionary France

    After Valmy, the French armies took the offensive. In October 1792 they occupied Savoy and Nice and advanced on the Rhine, taking Mainz, and on November 6 General Dumouriez defeated the Austrians at Jemappes and entered Brussels. The Convention's decree of November 19, offering fraternity and assistance to all peoples wishing to recover their liberty, and the opening of the river Scheldt to navigation, were interpreted in London and The Hague as challenges to the established order. The annexation of Belgium and the Rhineland, declared by the Convention in the winter on the basis of votes held in occupied territories under the shadow of French arms, reinforced the sense that the republic was not merely defending itself. Revolutionary war therefore had a double character, an ideological campaign for liberty and a more traditional contest over borders and strategic security, and French policy oscillated between the two.

    The execution of the king and the French declarations of war on Britain and the Dutch Republic on February 1, 1793, and on Spain on March 7, expanded the conflict. The coalition that resulted, which historians call the First Coalition, was not a unified alliance. Austria and Prussia remained preoccupied with the partition of Poland, Britain relied on naval power and subsidies, Spain and Piedmont-Sardinia pursued their own frontier interests, and the governments had different aims, from the restoration of the monarchy to the limitation of French power and the acquisition of French colonies. That lack of unity gave the republic time, but in the spring of 1793 the military position looked grim. The Austrians defeated Dumouriez at Neerwinden on March 18, and the French were driven out of Belgium. Dumouriez, who had been in contact with the Austrians and who blamed the Convention for the defeat, tried to lead his army against Paris and, failing to persuade his troops, defected to the Austrians on April 5, taking with him a number of officers and the young Duke of Chartres, the future King Louis-Philippe.

    The defection of a victorious general confirmed fears of treason in the army and strengthened the argument that military command required political supervision. The Convention responded by multiplying representatives on mission, deputies sent with extensive powers to the armies and departments. These men could dismiss officers, requisition supplies, arrest suspects, and enforce recruitment. The Convention had already, on February 24, 1793, decreed a levy of 300,000 men, to be raised by departmental quotas, and that levy, as the next section shows, provoked resistance in several regions. The republic also reorganized the army through the amalgam, the merger of the line regiments of the old royal army, which retained professional training, with the volunteer battalions raised since 1791, which often had higher enthusiasm but less experience. The amalgam, carried out unevenly in 1793 and 1794, gave the revolutionary army a more coherent structure and promoted capable officers, often from modest backgrounds, in ways that the old army had not.

    The most far-reaching measure was the levée en masse, decreed on August 23, 1793. The decree declared that from that moment until enemies had been driven from the territory of the republic, all French people were in permanent requisition for the service of the armies. Young unmarried men were to go to the front, married men were to forge arms and transport supplies, women were to make tents and clothing and serve in hospitals, children were to prepare lint, and the old were to be carried to public squares to arouse the courage of the warriors and teach hatred of kings. In practice, the decree did not mobilize the entire population in the way its rhetoric suggested. It specified conscription of unmarried men and widowers without children between eighteen and twenty-five, and the administration of recruitment, supply, and arms manufacture was uneven and often improvised. Even so, it created a precedent for universal military obligation and for the state's direction of production, and it helped raise the armies of the republic to a size unprecedented in modern European history; paper strength in 1794 reached figures approaching three-quarters of a million, though actual effectives were considerably fewer because of desertion, sickness, and administrative inflation.

    The mobilization was linked to a new conception of citizenship. The republic argued that military service was a duty of citizens and that the citizen's liberty depended on defending the nation. The ideological appeal was real, and many young men served willingly, but conscription also relied on coercion, the surveillance of communities, and penalties for evasion and desertion. Families resented the loss of labor, some regions resisted, and local authorities sometimes protected their own communities. The republic's military success in the autumn of 1793 came from the combination of mass, supply organization under Lazare Carnot and the Committee of Public Safety, aggressive tactics, and improved leadership. French armies broke the siege of Dunkirk after Hondschoote in September, relieved Maubeuge after Wattignies in October, recaptured Toulon in December, where a young artillery officer named Napoleon Bonaparte first drew attention, and won a decisive victory at Fleurus on June 26, 1794, which secured the reconquest of Belgium. Students should treat these victories as the result of institutional and logistical change as much as of revolutionary enthusiasm, and should recognize that the mobilization strained the economy and increased domestic repression.

    Section 89.6

    The Vendée, Federalist Revolts, and the Republic's Internal Wars

    The republic confronted rebellion within France while fighting abroad. The most serious uprising began in March 1793 in western France, in a region of Anjou, Poitou, and Brittany south of the Loire, which came to be known as the Vendée after one of its departments. Its origins were complex. The region had a pattern of dispersed rural settlement and strong parish institutions, and many peasants and rural artisans had benefited little from the sale of church lands, which had been bought largely by townspeople. The religious settlement imposed by the Civil Constitution of the Clergy, examined in Chapter 88, divided communities, and priests who refused the oath were replaced by constitutional clergy whom many parishioners rejected. The execution of the king further alienated some. The immediate trigger, however, was the levy of 300,000 men, which in March 1793 provoked riots across France and, in the west, turned into an armed insurrection.

    The rebels called themselves the Catholic and Royal Army. Their leaders included local nobles such as Charette and La Rochejaquelein, but also commoners such as the peddler Cathelineau and the gamekeeper Stofflet, and the movement's strength came from parish networks and local knowledge of the terrain of hedged fields and sunken lanes, the bocage. The rebels took towns including Cholet and Saumur in the spring and early summer, but were repelled at Nantes in June and failed to establish a secure port or a coherent government. Communities participated in different ways. Some villages rose en masse, others stayed loyal, and many people, including supporters of the Revolution in towns such as Cholet and in the republican Mauges, were victims of rebel violence. Historians therefore resist treating the Vendée as the uprising of a single social or religious body, and emphasize variation among parishes, the role of conscription and local conflict, and the political uses of the uprising by both sides.

    The Republic's response combined military and political measures. After defeats in the summer, republican armies, strengthened by troops from the Rhine garrisons, defeated the main rebel army at Cholet in October, and an immense body of rebels and civilians crossed the Loire and marched north in the so-called Virée de Galerne, hoping to reach a port and British support. They were defeated at Le Mans and Savenay in December 1793. Repression, however, did not end with military victory. In early 1794 General Turreau organized columns that moved through the countryside destroying crops, burning farms, and killing people, an operation commonly called the infernal columns, which devastated regions and killed many civilians. In Nantes, the representative Carrier presided over mass executions, including the drowning of prisoners in the Loire in the winter of 1793 to 1794. These actions were recognized by some contemporaries and by the Convention itself as excesses; Carrier was tried and executed after Thermidor.

    Estimates of the dead in the Vendée war and its repression vary widely, from about 117,000 to more than 200,000 on the basis of different demographic methods, and they include combatants, civilians, republican supporters, and victims of famine and disease. Such figures must be treated with caution, and should not be combined with figures for judicial executions elsewhere. A controversy that began in the 1980s concerns whether the repression constituted a genocide. Most historians of the Revolution have rejected the application of that term, noting the lack of an order to exterminate a population as such, the political rather than strictly ethnic or religious targeting, the variation in local practice, and the differing actions of republican commanders, while recognizing that the violence was extreme, that it included massacres of civilians, and that rhetoric of extermination was used by some leaders. Others, including some historians writing in France, have argued that elements of the repression shared features with later genocidal policies. The debate shows how modern categories and memory shape interpretation of the past.

    The Vendée was not the only region of resistance. In the late spring of 1793, after the expulsion of the Girondin deputies, departments including Calvados, the Gironde, the Rhône, and the Bouches-du-Rhône rose in protest against the Convention's domination by Paris. Contemporary Montagnards called these movements federalist revolts, and the label was used polemically to suggest that the rebels wished to break the republic into autonomous parts. In fact, most rebels claimed loyalty to the republic and protested against what they saw as the usurpation of the Convention by the Paris Commune and the sections; they did not typically propose a federal state. The movements varied. In Normandy the revolt collapsed quickly after a minor engagement at Pacy-sur-Eure, though it provided Charlotte Corday, who traveled from Caen to Paris and killed Marat on July 13. In Lyon, the revolt overthrew the Jacobin municipality in late May, and the city withstood a siege from August until October 9, 1793, with royalist elements gaining influence. In Marseille and Bordeaux, local authorities eventually submitted, and in Toulon, royalists handed the port and fleet to the British in August.

    The Convention's reaction combined military force and political repression. After Lyon fell, it decreed that the city should be destroyed, renamed it Ville-Affranchie, and carried out executions, some by cannon fire, on a scale that shocked some contemporaries. Toulon was recaptured in December. The relationship between local conflict, military repression, and the claim of national unity was central: republicans argued that the republic was one and indivisible and that dissent in wartime was treason, while their opponents argued that the Convention had been coerced and that resistance was legitimate. The consequences included thousands of arrests and executions, damage to economic life in cities like Lyon, and long memories of hostility that would shape regional politics after Thermidor, when the same regions witnessed reprisals against Jacobins.

    Section 89.7

    The Fall of the Girondins and the Constitution of 1793

    The military crisis of March 1793, the uprising in the west, and the economic strains of the spring sharpened the conflict between Girondin and Montagnard deputies. The Girondins attacked the Paris Commune and the sections and in April secured the impeachment of Marat before the Revolutionary Tribunal; he was acquitted and carried in triumph. In May they created a Commission of Twelve to investigate the Commune's activities, and the commission ordered the arrest of Hébert, the editor of the newspaper Le Père Duchesne and a deputy procurator of the Commune. The Paris sections, joined by militant clubs, saw the arrest as an attack on popular liberty. Prices were rising, the assignat was losing value, and bread was scarce.

    The result was a sequence of popular days, journées, between May 31 and June 2. On May 31 a delegation from the sections and the Commune's insurrectionary committee pressed the Convention to dissolve the Commission of Twelve and arrest its leaders, and the Convention yielded only in part. On June 2, armed National Guards under François Hanriot surrounded the hall with cannon, and the Convention, after an attempt by some deputies to leave and an encounter with armed men who refused to let them pass, voted for the arrest of twenty-nine deputies and two ministers. Some of the arrested deputies escaped to the provinces and joined the revolts described in Section 89.6, which in turn increased the Convention's suspicion of Girondin leaders. A large number of the Plain accepted the outcome out of fear and a sense of necessity. The episode demonstrated that the Convention's authority depended on the support or acquiescence of Parisian armed forces, and also that the Montagnards did not control the sections as a simple instrument; the militants had their own aims.

    The expulsion of the Girondins strengthened the case for completing the constitution. A first draft had been prepared by a committee in which Condorcet had played a major role, but the Montagnards had set it aside as too complex and too favorable to the Girondins. In June a new committee, which included Hérault de Séchelles, Saint-Just, and Couthon, produced a text in about a week, which the Convention adopted on June 24, 1793. The Constitution of 1793 was preceded by a new Declaration of Rights, broader than that of 1789. It affirmed equality, liberty, security, and property as rights, declared that society owed subsistence to unfortunate citizens, treated public education as a need of all, and described resistance to oppression as a consequence of other rights. It also included a provision on insurrection that has attracted great attention, stating that when the government violates the rights of the people, insurrection is for the people and for every portion of the people the most sacred of rights and the most indispensable of duties.

    The Constitution established a unicameral legislative assembly elected annually by direct male suffrage, with a very broad electorate, and an executive council of twenty-four members chosen by the legislature from candidates nominated by electoral assemblies of the departments. It also provided for referendums, in which laws proposed by the assembly could be subjected to popular rejection by a proportion of the primary assemblies. It did not abolish slavery or give political rights to women, and it did not address the colonies in any detail. It was submitted to the primary assemblies, which approved it by a large margin, with about 1.8 million voting yes and a very small number against, though participation was limited and approval was influenced by the political climate and the desire for unity. Historians therefore treat the plebiscite as evidence of widespread support for the idea of a republican constitution rather than as a precise measure of opinion.

    The Constitution of 1793 was never put into operation. On August 10, 1793, the first anniversary of the fall of the monarchy, the constitutional text was solemnly deposited in a ceremony on the Champ de Mars, and the Convention received delegations from the primary assemblies. But on October 10 the Convention, on a report by Saint-Just, declared that the provisional government of France would be revolutionary until the peace. The elections that the constitution required were postponed, and the text was placed in an ark in the Convention's hall. The decision was defended on the grounds that war, rebellion, and speculation made normal procedure impossible, and it was criticized by opponents, then and later, as a way of avoiding the constitution's own checks. Its legacy was powerful. Ideas drawn from it, including the right to subsistence and the right of insurrection, would be invoked by radicals in 1795 and by later republican and socialist movements, and the contrast between a democratic constitution that was never applied and an emergency government that governed in its absence became a central theme of later interpretation.

    Section 89.8

    Saint-Domingue, Colonial Emancipation, and the Contradictions of Republican Universalism

    The Revolution's declarations of rights were proclaimed in a state that held roughly half a million enslaved people in its Caribbean colonies, and Saint-Domingue, the richest of them, produced a large share of the sugar and coffee consumed in Europe. As Chapter 88 explained, the revolution in the colony began as a conflict among white planters, free people of color, and metropolitan authorities, and in August 1791 an uprising of enslaved people in the north, organized in part through networks among plantation workers and led by figures such as Boukman and later Jean-François and Biassou, transformed the situation. On April 4, 1792, the Legislative Assembly granted political equality to free men of color, and it sent civil commissioners, Léger-Félicité Sonthonax and Étienne Polverel, with troops to enforce the decree. They arrived in the autumn of 1792 to find a colony divided among planters, free men of color, rebel armies, and contending military forces.

    The outbreak of war with Britain and Spain in 1793 made the colony a theater of international conflict. Spain, which held the eastern part of the island, welcomed rebel leaders into its service, and British forces landed in the southwest and west in September 1793, encouraged by planters who hoped British rule would preserve slavery. In June 1793 a conflict in the northern port of Cap Français between the commissioners and the governor Galbaud, who was supported by white colonists and naval forces, led to fighting and a large fire. In the course of the struggle, Sonthonax offered freedom to enslaved men who would fight for the republic, and on August 29, 1793, he proclaimed the emancipation of all enslaved people in the north, a step prompted by military and political necessity but also acknowledging the demands of the insurgents. Polverel extended similar proclamations to the west and south in September and October. These decisions were taken in the colony, in a context where the commissioners could not rely on metropolitan authority.

    The National Convention acted later. In the autumn of 1793 and the winter of 1794 delegates from the colony, including a white colonist named Dufay, a man of mixed ancestry named Mills, and a formerly enslaved man named Belley, traveled to Paris, and on February 3, 1794, they were admitted to the Convention. The next day, 4 February 1794 (16 Pluviôse Year II), the Convention decreed the abolition of slavery in all the colonies and declared that all men, without distinction of color, domiciled in the colonies were French citizens with the rights guaranteed by the constitution. The decree was passed with little debate, amid enthusiasm, and historians have disagreed about its sources. It was clearly connected to the principles of the Declaration of the Rights of Man, to the arguments of abolitionists such as the Société des Amis des Noirs, to the military need to hold the colony against Britain and Spain, and, above all, to the fact that emancipation had already been proclaimed by the commissioners under pressure from the insurgents. The agency of enslaved people, who resisted, rebelled, negotiated, and fought, was central rather than peripheral.

    Abolition on paper did not mean full freedom in practice. In Saint-Domingue, the new regime of freedom was accompanied by labor policies that bound former slaves to their plantations as cultivators, paid with a share of the produce, in an effort to preserve exports, and these policies provoked tension. In Guadeloupe, the republican commissioner Victor Hugues arrived in 1794, expelled the British, and enforced emancipation while using harsh measures, including the guillotine, against opponents and relying on the labor of former slaves. In Martinique, which was under British occupation, the decree could not be implemented. In the Mascarene Islands in the Indian Ocean, colonists resisted its application, and in other colonies it was dependent on local circumstances. The decree thus had uneven effects, and the legal abolition of slavery, the grant of citizenship, and the continuing coercion of labor must be distinguished.

    Toussaint Louverture, an emancipated man who had been among the leaders of the insurrection and had served under Spanish command, rallied to the French republic in 1794, after the decree of abolition, bringing with him a significant army. His decision, taken for reasons including the Spanish refusal to abolish slavery and the republic's commitment to emancipation, would transform the military balance in Saint-Domingue and begin a career that belongs to the later history of the colony. The full history of the Haitian Revolution and independence is reserved for its own coverage; this chapter's concern is the contradiction at the center of republican universalism. The republic proclaimed universal rights while it depended on colonial wealth, and it abolished slavery under pressure from enslaved people and the demands of war, but without resolving the questions of labor, land, and political power. The metropolitan decision would be reversed under the Consulate in 1802, a development examined in connection with Napoleon, and the connections between colonial slavery and European economic life, discussed in Chapter 70 and Chapter 73, did not disappear with the decree.

    Historical Debate 2

    Why Was Slavery Abolished in 1794?

    The question: Did the abolition of slavery result primarily from revolutionary universalism, colonial resistance, military necessity, or a combination of all three?

    Interpretation A: Some historians emphasize the principles of the Declaration of the Rights of Man and the arguments of abolitionists, and they note that the Convention acted in the name of universal rights.
    Interpretation B: Others emphasize the actions of enslaved people, whose insurrection forced the colonial commissioners to proclaim freedom, and the war with Britain and Spain, which made alliance with the insurgents necessary. Many works now stress that these explanations are interdependent and that the agency of the enslaved was decisive.

    Evidence: The evidence includes the commissioners' proclamations of 1793, the speeches in the Convention, the letters of colonial leaders, and military reports. The sequence, in which colonial proclamations came before the decree, supports the second interpretation.

    Why it matters: The debate affects whether abolition is understood as a gift of the metropole or as the outcome of struggle in which enslaved people were historical actors.

    Revolutionary Government, the Terror, and the Concentration of Power

    This is the chapter's most analytically demanding division. It explains how emergency government was built, how it operated across Paris and the provinces, why political violence intensified, and how the same concentration of authority that was meant to save the republic eventually consumed its own leadership. The word Terror, a label that contemporaries and later writers applied differently, is used here for a system of institutions, laws, and practices rather than for the will of a single man.

    Section 89.9

    The Committee of Public Safety, the Committee of General Security, and Emergency Government

    The institutions of emergency government were created piecemeal between October 1792 and the autumn of 1793, in response to specific crises rather than according to a plan. The Committee of General Security, formed in October 1792, supervised police matters and the surveillance of suspects. In March 1793, after the defeat at Neerwinden and the outbreak of the Vendée uprising, the Convention created a Revolutionary Tribunal in Paris to try crimes against the state, dispatched representatives on mission with broad powers to the departments, and authorized the creation of surveillance committees in each commune to monitor foreigners and suspects. On April 6, the Convention established the Committee of Public Safety, a body of nine members, at first dominated by Danton, whose purpose was to supervise and accelerate the work of the executive ministers.

    The Committee of Public Safety changed in composition and in function. After the defeat of the Girondins, the failures of the spring, and the loss of confidence in Danton's attempts at negotiation, the Convention renewed the committee on July 10, 1793, without him. Robespierre joined it on July 27. The committee that governed until the summer of 1794 consisted of twelve members, including Robespierre, Saint-Just, and Couthon, who dealt largely with political matters and the supervision of the republic's institutions; Carnot and Prieur de la Côte-d'Or, military engineers who organized the armies and the production of arms; Lindet, who handled supplies and food; Barère, who acted as the committee's regular spokesman to the Convention; Billaud-Varenne and Collot d'Herbois, who were associated with the political direction of the Terror and the supervision of the departments; and Jeanbon Saint-André and Prieur de la Marne, who took charge of the navy and of missions to the armies. The committee worked in conditions of shared responsibility, with members specializing but signing orders collectively, and it was subject to renewal each month by the Convention, which usually reconfirmed it.

    Its relationship to the Committee of General Security was complicated. The latter, composed of twelve members elected by the Convention and including Amar, Vadier, and Vouland, controlled the police and the investigation of political suspects, and there were frequent tensions over jurisdiction, with the Committee of General Security resenting the greater prestige of the Committee of Public Safety. The Convention itself remained the sovereign body in principle. The committees were its delegates, its members were deputies, and its decrees gave the committees power. Representatives on mission, in turn, enjoyed wide autonomy, so that policy in the departments was often shaped by the character and temperament of the representative on the spot: some, like Fouché in the Nièvre or Carrier in Nantes, were ruthless, while others, like Lindet in Normandy, were more cautious.

    The decisive move toward centralization came with the law of 14 Frimaire Year II (December 4, 1793), which laid out the organization of revolutionary government. The law placed the departmental and district authorities under the direct supervision of the Committee of Public Safety and its agents, replaced elected local officials with national agents responsible for enforcing the laws, and subordinated the representatives on mission and the revolutionary armies to the committee. It also curtailed the autonomy of the Paris Commune and the independent revolutionary committees. The immediate purpose was to end rivalries and local initiative that threatened the unity of action, but it also removed some of the local elective features of the revolutionary administration established in 1790. The measure illustrates the tension between the republic's claim to popular sovereignty and the centralization demanded by war.

    Robespierre's role must be described with care. He was an influential member of the committee, a powerful orator in the Convention and the Jacobin Club, and a symbol for supporters and enemies alike, but he did not rule alone. Decisions in the committee were collective, the Committee of General Security was independent, the Convention had to vote on measures and could remove committee members, and Robespierre's own commitments, such as the Cult of the Supreme Being, did not always prevail. The image of a dictator is partly the product of later propaganda by those who overthrew him in Thermidor and sought to place responsibility on a single man, and historians now emphasize the collective character of the committees, the competing institutions, and the shifting coalitions of members.

    One way to see what centralization meant in practice is to follow an order from Paris into a department. A committee could set a policy, but an agent or representative on mission had to determine which local officials could be trusted, whether a municipality possessed the registers and personnel to carry it out, and how to obtain supplies or cooperation from people who feared requisition or arrest. Correspondence frequently traveled in both directions: representatives described emergencies and asked for authority, while the committees issued instructions or demanded explanations. The result was not a perfectly unified bureaucracy but a more tightly supervised network dependent on local knowledge. The same decree could be interpreted differently in a frontier fortress, a village affected by the Vendée fighting, and a Paris neighborhood. Historians therefore distinguish the legal chain of command from the contingent exercise of power, particularly when explaining why some regions experienced more extensive coercion than others.

    The sources for the committees include the Convention's decrees, the committee's registers and correspondence, reports of representatives on mission, and the memoirs of survivors, which were written after Thermidor and often aimed to deflect responsibility. The administrative records show a government that tried to organize arms production, food supply, and surveillance with limited resources and that sought to control the passions of the provinces even as it relied on them. The result was an unprecedented concentration of administrative power in a republic whose own constitution of June 1793, as described earlier, had been designed on principles of popular participation. The emergency institutions drew on the language of public safety and popular sovereignty, and they were accepted by many deputies of the Plain because they appeared to offer security.

    Section 89.10

    The Law of Suspects, Revolutionary Justice, Regional Repression, and the Terror

    The term Terror has no single origin. In early September 1793, following a popular journée on September 4 and 5 when crowds pressed the Convention for measures against hoarders and suspects, Convention speakers and Parisian militants called for terror to be placed on the agenda. Historians debate how far this phrase described an official policy rather than a rhetorical demand, but within weeks the Convention passed legislation that built the legal apparatus for mass detention. The Law of Suspects, adopted on September 17, 1793, instructed local authorities to arrest as suspects a broad range of people, including those who by conduct, associations, remarks, or writings had shown themselves partisans of tyranny or federalism, former nobles and the relatives of émigrés who had not consistently demonstrated attachment to the Revolution, people who could not prove their means of existence or the fulfillment of civic duties, officials suspended or dismissed from office, and people who had been refused certificates of civic virtue by their local committees.

    The law's breadth gave local surveillance committees extensive discretion. Recent scholarship stresses that the law did not arise from nowhere: local authorities had been arresting suspects since 1791, and the practices of surveillance, the denunciation of enemies, and the issuing of certificates of civic virtue had developed through the revolutionary years. The law systematized practices that already existed and extended them under national authority. The result was an enormous increase in detention. Estimates of the number arrested during the Terror vary, and some scholarly calculations place the number of people held at some point at several hundred thousand, though the figures include short detentions and were unevenly distributed among departments. Prison conditions were harsh and disease killed many detainees, so that the number who died in prison is a separate figure from those who were executed.

    Revolutionary justice combined ordinary and exceptional procedures. The Revolutionary Tribunal in Paris, created in March 1793, tried political cases, including those of the queen Marie Antoinette, executed on October 16, 1793, of leading Girondins, executed on October 31, and of figures such as Madame Roland, who was executed in November, and the writer Olympe de Gouges, executed on November 3. The tribunal's procedures were increasingly summary, though acquittals continued to occur, and in some periods a significant proportion of defendants were released. In the departments, local tribunals, military commissions, and special courts judged cases, and representatives on mission sometimes ordered executions without formal trial. The distinction between these forms of repression is crucial. A judicial execution after a trial, however flawed, differs from a military execution of captured rebels, from a massacre outside judicial proceedings, and from deaths in prison or in civil war, and an accurate account distinguishes among them rather than speaking of a single total of victims.

    Regional variation was extreme. In Paris, the Revolutionary Tribunal condemned roughly 2,600 people between its creation and the fall of Robespierre, most of them in the last two months. In the departments of the west, the repression of the Vendée and the Chouannerie involved military commissions and military columns as well as tribunals, and the violence in Nantes, with its mass drownings, was the work of a handful of officials acting with local support. In Lyon, after the fall of the city, a commission of the representatives Collot d'Herbois and Fouché ordered mass executions, including shootings of groups of prisoners with cannon and musket, and many were killed. Other regions, such as Brittany outside the areas of insurrection, much of the center, and parts of the south, experienced far fewer executions, and the sets of laws were applied with differing intensity depending on the representatives and local officials. The geographical pattern shows that the Terror was concentrated in regions of civil war and frontier conflict rather than distributed evenly across France.

    Historians have long debated the nature, scale, and responsibility for the Terror. The classic statistical study by the American historian Donald Greer, published in 1935, counted roughly 16,600 death sentences by revolutionary courts and commissions and found that approximately 71 percent fell within Greer’s broader western and southeastern statistical regions, which cannot be equated solely with the Vendée and other battle zones. Greer's work has been refined but not displaced, and later historians stress that his total excludes people killed in combat in the civil war, those who died in prison without trial, and those killed by military columns outside the courts. The study also showed that the victims of judicial executions were drawn largely from the lower and middle classes, with nobles and clergy overrepresented relative to their share of the population but a minority of the total. The debate about causes divides broadly between interpretations that emphasize circumstances, such as the military emergency, the civil war, and popular pressure for action, and those that emphasize ideology, such as the language of virtue, the belief in regeneration, and the suspicion of hidden enemies. Most recent scholarship holds that both mattered and interacted, and it resists attributing the whole to a single cause or a single leader.

    The institutional life of suspicion was also uneven. Certificates of civic standing, denunciations, neighborhood knowledge, and investigations of correspondence could become decisive for a detainee, even when a formal accusation remained vague. Relatives sometimes petitioned for release, offered evidence of military service or patriotic donations, or sought protection from influential local figures. These actions should not be romanticized as a reliable legal remedy: a successful petition depended on officials' decisions and shifting political conditions. Yet they show that people were not merely passive objects of surveillance. An arrest register reveals the authority that ordered confinement and, sometimes, the stated reason; a petition reveals how a family presented its case; a later memoir may recast events to fit the politics of Thermidor. Each form of evidence offers a different perspective, and none alone establishes the guilt, innocence, or political commitments of an accused person.

    Students should also note what the sources can and cannot show. The laws, tribunal records, and committee registers allow reconstruction of procedures, charges, and outcomes. They do not show the experience of detainees, whose letters, memoirs, and testimony survive in smaller numbers and were often written after the fact, nor do they show the motives of local denouncers, who might act from political conviction, personal rivalry, or fear. The memory of the Terror was shaped after 1794 by those who wished to condemn it, those who wished to defend the Revolution, and later historians who used it to argue about the French Revolution as a whole. A careful account therefore separates the documented system from the interpretations laid over it.

    Section 89.11

    The Revolutionary Economy: Price Controls, Requisitions, Popular Politics, and Social Discipline

    Economic conditions shaped the Terror as much as military events did. The republic financed war by issuing assignats, paper currency backed by the value of confiscated church and émigré lands, and by 1793 the assignat had lost a large part of its nominal value. Grain harvests were adequate in some regions but poorly distributed, and urban populations, especially in Paris, depended on rural suppliers who were reluctant to sell for paper money at fixed prices. Armies needed food, horses, shoes, and arms, and the requisitions to supply them competed with civilian needs. Wages rose less rapidly than prices, and many urban laboring households spent most of their income on bread.

    The Parisian popular movement, whose participants were called sans-culottes, a term that originally referred to men who wore trousers rather than the knee breeches of the elite, combined artisans, shopkeepers, journeymen, and wage laborers, often organized through the sections. Their political ideals included direct participation, vigilance against hoarders and aristocrats, a moral economy that treated subsistence as a right, and suspicion of wealth and commercial speculation. They did not demand the abolition of private property, but they wanted its use limited by the needs of the community. Radical journalists and activists, among them the former priest Jacques Roux and the section leader Jean Varlet, were known as the Enragés and demanded price controls and harsher measures against speculators, while Hébert, through Le Père Duchesne, spoke for a more populist and anticlerical radicalism with ties to the Commune and the Cordeliers Club.

    The Convention initially resisted regulation. It established a maximum price for grain on May 4, 1793, under pressure from the countryside and cities, but repealed or modified it after the pressure of the producers, and it struck against hoarding by laws of July. Following the journées of September 4 and 5, the Convention accepted wider controls, and on September 29, 1793, adopted the General Maximum, which fixed the price of a list of essential goods, including grain, meat, wine, fuel, salt, soap, and cloth, at one third above their levels of 1790, and set wages at one half above their 1790 levels. The law was ambitious and difficult to enforce, since prices in 1790 varied from locality to locality, producers evaded the controls by withholding goods or by selling on the black market, and local officials differed in their zeal. Penalties for violation included fines and imprisonment, and the Law of Suspects, discussed earlier, allowed the arrest of hoarders as suspects. The Maximum had different consequences for different groups. Workers in some trades gained from wage rises, others suffered when wage ceilings were applied more strictly than price ceilings, and farmers resented controls that cut their profits.

    Requisitions, the compulsory purchase of grain and supplies at fixed prices, overlapped with the Maximum but had different purposes. They were mainly directed toward the armies and the supply of the cities, and they were enforced by local officials and sometimes by the revolutionary armies, the paramilitary units sent from Paris into the countryside in the autumn of 1793 to ensure deliveries. Rural households experienced these measures as an intrusion by the state and by city dwellers, and in some regions they contributed to hostility to the revolutionary government. Historians therefore resist describing the Maximum as an unqualified success or failure. It helped to sustain the armies and the food supply of the cities during an extreme crisis, and it stabilized prices for a period, but it also depended on coercion, produced shortages and evasion, and contributed to resentment that appeared after Thermidor.

    Women took a prominent part in popular politics. They had been active in the bread protests of 1789, in the march to Versailles in October 1789, and in political clubs and street protests. In May 1793, Claire Lacombe and Pauline Léon founded the Society of Revolutionary Republican Women, which supported the Enragés and demanded measures against speculators. The society's members wore the red cap of liberty and claimed the right to bear arms, and the Convention and some Jacobins regarded their activity as disruptive. On October 30, 1793, the Convention suppressed women's political clubs and societies, in a debate that stressed the supposed incompatibility of political activity with women's domestic roles, and a few days later Olympe de Gouges, author of the Declaration of the Rights of Woman and of the Female Citizen of 1791, was executed. Historians distinguish between this formal exclusion and the persistence of women's participation. Women continued to attend sessions of the sections and the Convention's galleries, to organize bread protests, to petition, to run enterprises, and to take part in the uprisings of 1795, which were largely driven by women, and the Convention responded in May 1795 by forbidding women from attending its sessions or assembling in groups in the street.

    Even within a single market the effects of the Maximum depended on where sellers acquired goods and whether authorities could supervise transport. A legal ceiling might allow town officials to distribute available grain at a predictable price, but if that ceiling lagged behind transport costs or the prices offered in neighboring districts, farmers and intermediaries could redirect goods or postpone sales. Municipal agents faced the difficult task of distinguishing genuine scarcity from withholding without reliable information on stores, harvests, and movements between departments. Price controls were therefore neither simply benevolent relief nor an automatic cause of shortage. They formed part of a contested system of procurement, policing, patriotic obligation, and negotiation with producers. The later repudiation of these controls also had political consequences, because workers who remembered regulated prices judged the new government's promises of liberty against the price of bread they could actually buy.

    The economic policies of the Year II also involved social discipline, as the government used propaganda, festivals, and the regulation of language and dress to promote republican virtue and equality. Citizens were encouraged to address one another with the familiar tu and the title citizen, to wear the tricolor cockade, and to adopt revolutionary first names, and the revolutionary calendar replaced the Christian week with a ten-day décade. These changes were not universal, but they show the effort to build a political culture, and they set the stage for the religious campaigns examined next.

    Section 89.12

    Revolutionary Religion, Political Factions, Robespierre, and the Escalation of the Terror

    Religion was a persistent source of conflict, and in the autumn of 1793 it became linked to the political struggles of the Terror. The Revolution's religious settlement had divided clergy and laity since 1790. The war, the Vendée, and the conviction that refractory priests encouraged resistance led the Convention to adopt measures against them, and in autumn 1793 a movement called dechristianization, led by representatives on mission such as Fouché and Chaumette, the procurator of the Commune, closed churches, melted down bells and church plate, pressured priests to resign or marry, and replaced Christian festivals with civic celebrations. The revolutionary calendar, adopted in October 1793 and beginning with the foundation of the republic in September 1792, eliminated Sundays and saints' days and renamed the months after seasonal features. On November 10, a Festival of Liberty or Reason was celebrated in the cathedral of Notre-Dame.

    The geography of religious change complicates any account centered only on the capital. In some towns officials removed crosses from public buildings and closed churches as visible signs that the republic had replaced the old corporate Christian order. Elsewhere inhabitants negotiated to preserve a church bell, requested permission for local observances, or defended a priest whose ministry had continued through successive governments. The revolutionary calendar could be printed on official notices without displacing Sunday worship in a household. Officials also differed: one representative might interpret a continuing parish festival as evidence of disloyalty, while another feared that coercion would turn otherwise accommodating citizens into opponents. Such examples do not imply that religion was invariably a mask for politics or that popular resistance was always counterrevolutionary. They show why the conflict over conscience cannot be measured solely by the number of churches formally closed or priests who resigned.

    Dechristianization was not directed by a single plan, and it was resisted by many peasants and townspeople, some of whom continued to practice secretly and to shelter priests. It also divided the revolutionary leadership. Robespierre, who believed that atheism was an aristocratic doctrine and that the republic needed a moral and religious foundation, denounced attacks on belief in November 1793, and the Convention reaffirmed freedom of worship by decree on December 6, though the practice of Catholic worship in public was in effect restricted. In the spring of 1794, Robespierre proposed a civic religion. On May 7, 1794, the Convention decreed that the French people recognized the existence of the Supreme Being and the immortality of the soul, and on June 8 a festival of the Supreme Being was held in Paris, with Robespierre presiding as the Convention's president. The Cult of the Supreme Being, like the Cult of Reason, was an effort to create a republican religion, and it was received with ambivalence, as some saw it as an attempt to impose a new orthodoxy and as a sign of Robespierre's personal ambition.

    Political divisions within the revolutionary camp intensified in the winter of 1793 to 1794. Two groups challenged the committees from opposite directions. The Hébertists, associated with the Cordeliers Club and the Commune, demanded more severe measures, including stricter enforcement of the Maximum and a renewed purge of suspects. The Indulgents, associated with Danton and the journalist Camille Desmoulins, whose newspaper Le Vieux Cordelier called for a committee of clemency, argued that the republic's victories justified relaxing the Terror and also had been implicated in corruption scandals, such as the affair of the East India Company. The committees, concerned about threats from both sides, acted against both. The Hébertist leaders were arrested on March 14, 1794, and executed on March 24. Danton, Desmoulins, and other Indulgents were arrested on March 30 and executed on April 5, after a trial in which the defendants were prevented from defending themselves effectively. Saint-Just, who delivered the committees' accusations, and Robespierre, who agreed to the decision, bore much of the responsibility, though others, including Billaud-Varenne and the Committee of General Security, also supported it.

    The purge of rival revolutionary figures introduced another problem of political legitimacy. The trials of the Hébertists and Dantonists were not normal contests among recognized parliamentary parties; defendants could be accused of participating in foreign conspiracy, corruption, or a deliberate effort to weaken national defense. Because members of the Convention had previously endorsed emergency powers, they found it difficult to object to these procedures without appearing to undermine the republic at war. At the same time, the elimination of prominent militants deprived the committees of some channels through which Paris sections had communicated popular grievances. The resulting quiet should not be confused with consensus. It reflected the danger of public dissent in a system that increasingly interpreted criticism as proof of hidden hostility.

    These executions removed rivals but also revealed the dynamics of the Terror. The leaders of the republic were no longer protected by their service to the Revolution, and deputies in the Convention, many of whom had themselves participated in repressive measures, began to fear that they might be next. The committees' power had grown, but their unity weakened. Robespierre, who had defended the Convention's authority against the committees' critics, increasingly felt isolated, and conflicts emerged between him and members of the Committee of General Security, and between Robespierre and Carnot over military matters. The government also dissolved the revolutionary armies and Paris sectional societies and brought the Commune under closer control, reducing the independence of the popular movement that had helped create it. At the same time, military victories in the spring of 1794, culminating at Fleurus on June 26, weakened the argument that emergency measures were needed to save the republic.

    In this setting the Law of 22 Prairial Year II (June 10, 1794) transformed revolutionary justice. Proposed by Couthon and supported by Robespierre, it reorganized the Revolutionary Tribunal and broadened the definition of enemies of the people to include those who sought to destroy liberty by force or ruse, to spread false news, or to debase morality, and it limited the penalties available to the tribunal to acquittal or death. It removed the requirement of preliminary interrogation in many cases, restricted the use of witnesses, and denied defendants the services of counsel, on the argument that patriotic jurors were themselves the defenders of innocent citizens. Its immediate effect was a rapid increase in executions in Paris. In the roughly seven weeks between the law's passage and the fall of Robespierre, the tribunal condemned more than 1,300 people, a larger number than in the previous fourteen months, a period sometimes called the Great Terror.

    The Law of 22 Prairial further changed the relationship between a defendant and the state. Earlier revolutionary proceedings already allowed exceptional prosecutions; the new law enlarged categories of alleged hostility and gave juries still greater latitude to treat moral or political behavior as evidence of enmity. Removing defense counsel and narrowing the use of witness testimony meant that a formal trial offered fewer opportunities to challenge an allegation. The acceleration in death sentences was not simply a matter of a more efficient court processing the same cases. By changing who could be classified as an enemy and how guilt could be demonstrated, the law altered the substance as well as the speed of revolutionary justice. Yet the frequently cited Paris total after June 10 belongs to one tribunal and a defined span of weeks. It should not be represented as an estimate for all deaths throughout France or for the entire period of emergency government.

    The reasons for the acceleration remain debated. Some historians argue that the law responded to practical concerns, including the backlog of cases and the desire for efficiency, others that it reflected a growing fear of conspiracy and the need to eliminate enemies, and others that it expressed an ideological commitment to purifying the republic. The law's passage also coincided with a dispute between Robespierre and his opponents about whether deputies could be sent before the tribunal without the Convention's approval, a question that worried many members. By July 1794, the leadership had created a system that no longer commanded the confidence of its own participants, who feared that the next lists might include them. The consequences of that fear would appear within weeks.

    Cause and Effect

    Cause and Effect: The Establishment of Emergency Government and the Terror

    Long-term causes

    The crisis of royal legitimacy, the polarization over the church, fiscal collapse, fear of counter-revolution, and a political culture that identified opponents as enemies of the nation.

    Short-term causes

    Military defeats in spring 1793, the Vendée, the federalist revolts, inflation and scarcity, the pressure of the Paris sections, and the expulsion of the Girondins.

    Immediate effects

    The committees, Revolutionary Tribunal, Law of Suspects, General Maximum, centralization in December 1793, and mass detention and executions.

    Long-term consequences

    Military mobilization and victory, a legacy of fear and resentment, the discredit of Jacobin government, and a constitutional design in 1795 meant to prevent concentrated power.

    Historical complexity: No single cause explains the Terror. War, civil conflict, ideology, popular demands, factional competition, and administrative improvisation interacted, and regional patterns differed greatly.

    Historical Debate 1

    Was the Terror a Necessary Response to Extraordinary Threats?

    The question: Was the Terror a response forced by the military emergency and civil war, or did ideology and political choice make its escalation avoidable?

    Interpretation A: Some historians argue that the invasion, the Vendée, the federalist revolts, and the economic crisis created circumstances in which the republic's survival seemed to require extraordinary measures. In this view, the timing of the Terror's most severe phases tracks the emergencies of 1793 and spring 1794.
    Interpretation B: Others argue that the Terror outlasted the emergency, since the Great Terror followed the victories of spring 1794, and that the revolutionary language of virtue and conspiracy, together with factional struggles, drove escalation. Recent work often emphasizes the interaction of both, along with popular demands for action.

    Evidence: The debate draws on the chronology of executions, military events, the texts of laws and speeches, the correspondence of representatives, and regional patterns of repression. The fact that the heaviest killing occurred in civil-war regions supports circumstantial explanations, while the Paris acceleration after Fleurus supports ideological and political ones.

    Why it matters: The question shapes how historians judge the Revolution and how later regimes understood emergency powers.

    Section 89.13

    The Ninth of Thermidor: The Overthrow and Execution of Robespierre

    On 8 Thermidor Year II (July 26, 1794), Robespierre delivered a long speech to the Convention, denouncing unnamed conspirators within the committees and the Convention, and calling for a purge of the government. He did not name the men he accused, and the vagueness alarmed many deputies, who each wondered whether he meant them. The Convention first voted to print the speech and then reversed itself, and that evening Robespierre repeated the address at the Jacobin Club, where it was warmly received. Meanwhile, deputies who feared for their safety, including some of the most prominent agents of the Terror such as Fouché, Tallien, Barras, and Fréron, who had been recalled from missions under suspicion of excess or corruption, began to organize. They were joined by members of the committees who had come to resent Robespierre's influence, notably Billaud-Varenne and Collot d'Herbois, and by deputies of the Plain, who had been silent but feared further purges.

    The decisive session began at noon on 9 Thermidor (July 27). Saint-Just rose to deliver a report but was interrupted by Tallien and Billaud-Varenne, who accused Robespierre and his allies of aspiring to tyranny. Robespierre was shouted down and not allowed to speak. The Convention decreed the arrest of Robespierre, his brother Augustin, Saint-Just, Couthon, and Le Bas, and the men were taken to prison. The Paris Commune, whose leaders were Robespierre's allies, sounded the tocsin and attempted to rally the sections. Hanriot, the commander of the National Guard, was arrested by deputies and then freed by his supporters, and the arrested men were liberated and taken to the Hôtel de Ville, where the Commune declared itself in insurrection.

    The outcome of the night was uncertain. The Commune could call on some sections and cannoneers, but many National Guard units hesitated, uncertain whether the Convention or the Commune represented legitimate authority. The Convention declared Robespierre and his associates outlaws, meaning that they could be executed on identification without trial, and appointed Barras to command forces loyal to the Convention. Troops from the sections moved against the Hôtel de Ville and entered it around two in the morning. During the confusion Robespierre was shot in the jaw; whether he attempted suicide or was shot by a gendarme named Merda has been disputed, and the evidence does not settle the question. Le Bas died by suicide, Augustin Robespierre was injured in a fall, and Couthon was found paralyzed.

    On July 28, Robespierre, Saint-Just, Couthon, and nineteen others were guillotined in the Place de la Révolution, and on the following day seventy more members of the Commune were executed, so that more than a hundred people died within three days, a larger number in a short period than at any other point of the Terror in Paris. Historians therefore emphasize that Thermidor was itself a violent event, resolved by force and by a rapid use of the same legal devices that had been used against others, including outlawry. The coalition that overthrew Robespierre was heterogeneous. It included men who had been ardent promoters of the Terror and others who had opposed it, former allies who feared for their lives, and members of the Plain who wanted an end to the uncertainty, and they were not united by any common program.

    Thermidor exposes a central difficulty in assigning political responsibility. Many who denounced Robespierre as a tyrant had previously voted for surveillance measures, sat on committees, or supervised repression outside Paris. Their new claim that the Convention had been held hostage by a few men helped preserve the legitimacy of the assembly itself. It also created a selective memory: practices that had been authorized by collective votes were retrospectively presented as deviations imposed by a faction. Contemporary speeches should consequently be read alongside the record of earlier decisions and the later careers of the speakers. A historian can acknowledge the very real fear felt by deputies in July 1794 without accepting all of their subsequent explanations. In particular, the fact that Robespierre's arrest ended his political career cannot by itself establish that every act of the Terror had been directed by him, or that those who overthrew him had agreed on a liberal constitutional alternative.

    The contemporary explanation that the Convention had delivered the republic from a tyrant, which became the official Thermidorian version, was largely created in the days and months that followed. It served the interests of those who needed to explain their own part in the Terror, and it assigned responsibility to a few leaders. Later historians, in contrast, have debated whether Robespierre had planned a new purge, what he intended in his last speech, and whether he was a sincere defender of the republic's virtue or a politician pursuing power. The surviving evidence, including speeches, notes found among his papers, and the testimony of contemporaries, is limited and often partisan, and students should treat both the Thermidorian image of a monster and the later image of an incorruptible martyr as products of political memory. What can be said is that Robespierre was a central figure of the committees, that his speech threatened many deputies, and that his fall resulted from the interaction of fear, factional rivalry, and the exhaustion of a system that had lost much of its original justification.

    Section 89.14

    The Thermidorian Reaction, the White Terror, and the Retreat from Emergency Government

    The men who overthrew Robespierre did not at first intend to dismantle the revolutionary government. They proclaimed their loyalty to the republic and to the Convention, and they continued the war. But within weeks, pressures from within the Convention and from the public began to undo the institutions of the Terror. The Convention reorganized the committees in August 1794, limiting the terms of their members and spreading their responsibilities among a larger number of committees, so that the Committee of Public Safety lost its dominating position. The law of 22 Prairial was repealed, the Revolutionary Tribunal was reorganized, and most detainees held under the Law of Suspects were gradually released. In November 1794 the Jacobin Club in Paris, which had been a center of republican militancy, was closed after a violent clash with a group of fashionable young men known as the jeunesse dorée, and its affiliates in the provinces were dissolved.

    The Convention also turned against some of those associated with the Terror. Carrier, the representative responsible for the drownings at Nantes, was tried and executed in December 1794, and some surviving Girondin deputies, seventy-three of whom had been imprisoned for protesting the expulsion of their colleagues, were readmitted. Billaud-Varenne, Collot d'Herbois, and Barère, former members of the committees, were denounced, and in March 1795 the Convention decided to prosecute them, though in the end they were deported rather than executed. The trials and denunciations raise the question of responsibility: the members of the Convention who condemned the Terror had often voted for the laws that created it, and the claim that Robespierre alone was guilty allowed them to preserve the legitimacy of the Convention.

    Economic policy shifted toward liberalization. The Convention abolished the General Maximum on December 24, 1794, and gradually removed other controls on prices and trade, on the argument that regulation had discouraged production and promoted evasion. The immediate consequence was a surge of inflation, as the assignat continued to fall, and a harsh winter of 1794 to 1795, with frozen rivers and disruption of transport, produced severe food shortages in Paris and other cities. The retreat from the controls expressed the interests of property owners, merchants, and many provincial elites, who had resented the regulation of the economy, and it undermined the sans-culottes, who saw the Maximum as a protection of the poor.

    The end of the Terror did not end violence. In the departments, and especially in the southeast, a movement known as the White Terror emerged in 1795, in which royalists, former moderates, relatives of victims, and local gangs, some calling themselves Companies of Jehu or of the Sun, attacked former Jacobins and officials, often with the passive support of local authorities. In Lyon, in May 1795, groups of men massacred imprisoned Jacobins in the city's prisons, and similar killings occurred in Marseille, Aix, Tarascon, and other towns of the Rhône valley. The violence was partly an act of vengeance for the repressions of the Year II and partly an expression of the political struggle between local factions. It was also encouraged by the knowledge that the Convention itself had begun to denounce the Jacobins, and by royalist activity linked to émigré networks and to the Chouan insurgents of the west, who had been offered amnesty in early 1795. A royalist landing of émigrés at Quiberon Bay in Brittany in June and July 1795, supported by British ships, was defeated by General Hoche, and the prisoners were executed.

    Outside Paris the Thermidorian reaction proceeded at different speeds. Former revolutionary officials could be removed from office, imprisoned, or attacked by local opponents who remembered earlier requisitions, arrests, or executions. In the southeast, networks of anti-Jacobin activists, royalists, and men seeking personal revenge sometimes acted together, but their objectives were not identical. In other places new administrations attempted to restrain violence and restore confidence in ordinary courts. The label White Terror therefore covers a group of local and regional struggles rather than a centrally designed campaign equivalent in organization to the legislation of Year II. Its use requires chronological care as well: the violence of 1795 should not be confused with the later royalist reaction of 1815. Both republican and royalist commentators claimed to be ending persecution, while their opponents described the same actions as renewed terror.

    Religious policy also became a field of negotiation after Thermidor. The retreat from coercive de-Christianization did not instantly restore the Catholic Church's previous legal standing, and neither the Convention nor the Directory was willing simply to surrender all state supervision of religious practice. Communities disagreed about the use of church buildings, public ceremonies, the position of former constitutional priests, and the return of refractory clergy. These conflicts linked national legislative debates to family practices such as baptism and burial. They also reveal why a political turn away from the Jacobins could attract some former opponents of the Revolution without creating a stable coalition: restoration of worship, defense of property acquired during the Revolution, and loyalty to republican institutions did not always travel together.

    The political landscape was therefore reconfigured. The Thermidorians, as the deputies who had overthrown Robespierre were known, sought to occupy a middle position between former Jacobins and royalists, though neither group trusted them. Property owners and local elites regained influence in many departments, and a mixture of republicans, constitutional monarchists, and royalists contested control. Foreign policy also reflected the change, as the republic, victorious in the north and on the Rhine, made peace with Prussia and the Dutch Batavian Republic and then Spain in 1795, so that the coalition began to disintegrate. The retreat from emergency government, therefore, did not mean a return to the situation of 1791. It created a new situation in which the republic was committed to a constitutional order but remained unsure who would be allowed to participate in it, and in which the winners of Thermidor feared both royalists and radicals.

    Continuity and Change Over Time

    Continuity and Change Over Time: Sovereignty and Power, 1792 to 1799

    What changed?

    France moved from a constitutional monarchy to a republic, from a Convention with broad male suffrage to a regime restricted by property, and from emergency committees to a five-man executive. Executions of a king, a queen, and thousands of others replaced the royal justice of the old regime, and slavery was abolished in law, if unevenly in practice.

    What remained continuous?

    Fiscal weakness, regional division, fear of conspiracy, the reliance on the army, and the claim that the nation was sovereign persisted through every regime. The sale of national lands and the abolition of feudal rights were never reversed.

    Who experienced change most directly?

    Parisian militants, nobles, priests, soldiers, enslaved people in the colonies, and residents of rebel regions experienced the sharpest changes. Many rural families experienced greater continuity in agriculture and local life, although war and requisitions touched them too.

    Why does the pattern matter?

    It shows that revolutionary regimes combined radical rupture with persistent structural problems, so that political form changed more rapidly than the conditions that threatened it.

    Section 89.15

    Popular Protest, Economic Crisis, and the Struggle over the Revolution's Legacy, 1794 to 1795

    The winter and spring of 1795 were among the hardest in the republic's history. The abandonment of price controls, the continued collapse of the assignat, and the shortage of grain, worsened by bad weather and by the disruption of transport and trade, produced a crisis of subsistence in Paris and other cities. By the spring, the assignat was trading for a small fraction of its face value, wages lagged behind prices, and daily bread rations in Paris were reduced to a low level. Contemporary police reports describe long queues for bread, anger at the wealthy and at the deputies, suicides, and rumors of speculation, and they show how many Parisians interpreted the crisis in moral terms, as the result of hoarders and the abandonment of the Maximum.

    The popular movement attempted to respond. On 12 Germinal Year III (April 1, 1795), crowds from the faubourgs entered the Convention, demanding bread and the implementation of the Constitution of 1793, which many activists regarded as the legitimate framework of the republic and as a guarantee of the right to subsistence. The Convention, supported by units of the National Guard from the wealthier sections, cleared the hall and decreed the deportation of Billaud-Varenne, Collot d'Herbois, and Barère. A far greater uprising followed on 1 Prairial Year III (May 20, 1795), when crowds, led largely by women who sounded the alarm in the faubourgs, again invaded the Convention. A deputy, Féraud, was killed, and his head was paraded on a pike. A small group of deputies of the left, the last Montagnards, supported the demands of the crowd. The Convention, again with the help of troops, restored order, and in the days following the army disarmed the Faubourg Saint-Antoine and arrested hundreds of activists.

    The consequences were far-reaching. A military commission tried and condemned a number of the Montagnard deputies who had supported the insurgents, and several of them, including Romme and Goujon, killed themselves rather than be executed. Women were barred from attending the Convention's sessions and forbidden to gather in the streets in groups, on pain of arrest. The sections were purged, the National Guard was reorganized to favor property owners, and the popular movement, which had provided much of the energy of the revolutionary days between 1789 and 1794, lost its organized power. The sans-culottes had been weakened by the dissolution of their clubs, the arrest of their leaders, and the end of the policies that they had supported, and after Prairial they did not again exercise the same political influence.

    The insurrections of Germinal and Prairial revealed the difficulty facing the Thermidorian republic. On one side were demands for bread, social protection, and a democratic constitution, supported by the urban poor and former militants. On the other were those who wanted stability, property protection, and the return of order, and who feared that the revolutionary ideals of the Year II would lead to anarchy or dictatorship. The Convention, which had to feed a capital and an army, was compelled to choose, and it chose to restrict political participation and to rely on military force. This choice had a lasting impact on the republic's legitimacy, because the Convention suppressed the movement that had put it in power and set the precedent for the use of the army to resolve domestic disputes.

    The events of Germinal and Prairial are best understood through the interaction of subsistence politics and constitutional expectation. Protesters did not merely ask for a cheaper loaf; some demanded the implementation of the Constitution of 1793, regarding a democratic charter and the right to livelihood as mutually reinforcing. Women's prominent role in food queues, neighborhood communication, and demonstrations shows why a history restricted to electoral office misses much of revolutionary participation. The Convention and its military defenders increasingly treated such mobilization as a danger to representative sovereignty, especially after deputies were threatened in the chamber. The repression therefore concerned more than crowd control. It helped narrow the range of legitimate popular action and allowed authorities to describe the demand for bread and democracy as evidence of disorder. The eventual defeat of the movement altered the social balance of the republic before the Constitution of Year III came into force.

    Evidence for popular hardship must be handled carefully. A nominal wage recorded in paper assignats cannot be read as purchasing power without comparing its date and location with prices and the note's value. Likewise, dramatic accounts of starving crowds were written by observers with political commitments and cannot stand as population-wide statistics. Municipal market records, relief petitions, letters, police reports, and local price series can be cross-checked to reconstruct pressures on particular households. Families might respond by selling possessions, relying on kin, changing foods, or joining political demonstrations, but those strategies differed by occupation and access to local support. The period illustrates how economic distress could magnify constitutional conflict without automatically producing a single political response.

    The sources for the popular movement of 1795 include police reports compiled by agents who sampled opinion in cafés, markets, and bread lines; the records of the sections; pamphlets and newspapers of the period; and the testimony and trial records of those arrested. These sources, like all police sources, reflect the perspective of those who gathered them, and they should be read as evidence of what officials thought important and what they feared. They show nonetheless that people used the language of rights, the constitution, and the Revolution's promises, and they provide evidence for the role of women, who were prominent in the bread riots and who were later excluded from formal politics, though they did not cease to participate in public life.

    Everyday Life and Social History

    Everyday Life under the Republic

    For most people, the republic arrived as bread queues, conscription lists, certificates of civic virtue, festivals, and changes in names and dates. Households in Paris spent most of their income on bread and watched prices and rations closely. Women stood in lines, shared news, and protested, while men served in the National Guard or the armies. In the countryside, families lost sons to conscription and faced requisitions of grain and horses, and in many places the closing of churches and the disappearance of Sundays disrupted communal rhythms.

    The revolutionary calendar, the tu form of address, new first names, and civic festivals sought to create republican habits, but people adopted them unevenly. After Thermidor, social life shifted again: theaters and balls reopened, fashion changed, and many people turned away from politics. Evidence for everyday life comes from police reports, petitions, letters, diaries, and administrative records, which show more about officials' concerns than about private feelings.

    Section 89.16

    The Constitution of Year III, the Vendémiaire Uprising, and the Establishment of the Directory

    With the popular movement suppressed and the royalist threat apparent, the Convention turned to constitution-making. A commission of eleven, which included Boissy d'Anglas and Daunou, drafted a text intended to avoid the dangers perceived in both the Constitution of 1793 and the dictatorship of the Year II. The Constitution of Year III, adopted by the Convention on August 22, 1795 (5 Fructidor Year III), was preceded by a Declaration of Rights and Duties, which retained the principles of equality before the law, liberty, security, and property, but omitted the right to subsistence and the right of insurrection that had appeared in 1793, and added a list of duties. It restored the distinction between citizens who paid taxes and other citizens, so that the right to vote depended on property or payment of a direct tax, and it created an indirect electoral system.

    The constitution distributed power among a legislature of two chambers and an executive of five Directors. Primary assemblies of citizens met annually to elect electors, who were required to meet higher property qualifications and who formed electoral assemblies in each department; the number of electors was only a small fraction of adult males, a few tens of thousands. The electoral assemblies chose the members of the legislature, a Council of Five Hundred, whose members had to be at least thirty years old and who alone could propose laws, and a Council of Ancients of 250 members, at least forty years old and married or widowed, who could approve or reject the proposals but not initiate them. The legislature was to be renewed by one third each year. The executive Directory was composed of five Directors, chosen by the Council of Ancients from a list prepared by the Council of Five Hundred, with one member replaced each year.

    The framers sought to prevent both tyranny and the dominance of the legislature by separating powers rigidly. The Directors could not propose laws, dissolve the councils, or sit in the legislature. The councils could not dismiss the Directors or control the executive's appointments, and neither side had a constitutional mechanism to resolve a dispute with the other. If the legislature refused to vote funds, the executive had no legal remedy, and if the executive acted illegally, the councils could not remove it except by impeachment. The French National Assembly's own historical account observes that this rigid separation became a major source of later instability. The framers' intention was to avoid concentrating power, a goal that reflected the lessons of the Terror, but the absence of mechanisms for compromise meant that when conflicts occurred, as they did, the only remedies were extra-constitutional.

    Many deputies feared that free elections would bring royalists to power. The Convention therefore adopted two decrees, of 5 and 13 Fructidor, requiring that two thirds of the members of the new legislature be chosen from among the outgoing Convention. The constitution and the decrees were submitted to the primary assemblies in September. The constitution was approved by a large margin, while the decrees on the two thirds were more contested, though they also passed, and in Paris a number of sections, where royalists and moderates had influence, protested. The sections rejected the decrees and mobilized the National Guard in the center of the city, and on 13 Vendémiaire Year IV (October 5, 1795) some thousands of men marched against the Convention.

    The Convention charged Barras with its defense, and Barras appointed a young general, Napoleon Bonaparte, who had been noticed at Toulon, to take command of the artillery. Bonaparte brought cannon to the approaches of the Tuileries, and when the sectionaries advanced near the church of Saint-Roch the cannon fired, killing and wounding a considerable number, and the uprising collapsed. Later accounts described this as a whiff of grapeshot. The episode marked the first time that the republic's regular military forces had been used against a royalist and moderate challenge in the capital, as opposed to the popular movement in Prairial, and it elevated Bonaparte's reputation. It also confirmed that the republic's survival depended on the army, a fact with long-term consequences.

    The Constitution of Year III was an institutional answer to memories of both monarchy and the Terror. Its authors feared an executive that could impose arbitrary rule, but also feared a single elected assembly claiming unlimited sovereignty. The two councils were designed to slow legislation through successive consideration, while the five Directors shared executive authority and were renewed gradually rather than all at once. These safeguards offered no simple method for overcoming sustained disagreement between the branches. They assumed that elections would produce enough shared commitment to the constitutional settlement for officials to tolerate temporary defeat. When that assumption failed, the constitution supplied strong incentives to regard opponents as existential threats instead of legitimate rivals. The Directory's later coups were therefore not mechanically dictated by the text, but the rigidity of the system made political compromise harder to sustain.

    The first transition also raised a question of continuity. The requirement that two-thirds of the initial legislative councils be drawn from the outgoing Convention was defended as protection against an abrupt royalist restoration. Opponents viewed it as a means by which incumbent deputies prevented voters from making a decisive choice. The October 1795 royalist rising in Paris arose in this contentious setting as well as from broader hostility to the revolutionary order. The defense of the Convention by troops associated with Bonaparte demonstrated the immediate effectiveness of force, but it also established an uncomfortable precedent: soldiers were summoned to resolve a conflict over who possessed legitimate political authority. Neither the new constitution nor military victory alone could settle that dispute.

    The Convention dissolved itself at the end of October 1795, and the new legislature met, and the first Directory was installed on November 2. The five Directors were Barras, Reubell, Letourneur, La Révellière-Lépeaux, and Carnot, who had been a member of the Committee of Public Safety, and who was chosen in part for his military expertise. All five were former members of the Convention who had voted for the king's death, a requirement imposed by the Convention's concerns about royalists, and so the regime was inaugurated in the hands of men whose political commitments, in a time of widespread reaction, were identified with the Revolution. The establishment of the Directory ended the Convention's rule and promised a return to constitutional government. Its design, however, left the new regime with a narrow social base, a divided executive and legislature, and a heavy dependence on the very forces that the republic was trying to control.

    This division gives the Directory an independent history. It should not be read merely as the prelude to Napoleon.

    Section 89.17

    Governing Under the Directory: Political Institutions, Finance, Society, and Republican Opposition

    The Directory governed through five Directors, a legislature divided between the Council of Five Hundred and the Council of Ancients, and a body of ministers who were responsible to the executive. The Directors, who met in the Luxembourg Palace, divided tasks among themselves, with Carnot, until 1797, handling military operations, Reubell foreign affairs and finance, and Barras, who had the greatest social presence, the management of political contacts. Their authority was limited. They appointed ministers and the central commissioners who supervised local administration, and they chose generals, but they could not initiate legislation, their budgets depended on the councils, and they governed a country where local government, after the experience of the Revolution, was both fragmented and sometimes unreliable. Annual elections, because they renewed one third of the legislature, meant that the political balance could shift every year, and the regime therefore faced an electoral test every spring.

    Finance was the most pressing problem. The state's revenue had collapsed during the Revolution, since the old indirect taxes had been abolished, the new direct taxes were collected slowly, and the sale of national lands, on which the assignat was based, had been overwhelmed by the quantity of paper issued. The assignat lost nearly all its value in 1795 and early 1796. In February 1796 the Directory ceased issuing assignats, and the printing plates were symbolically destroyed in Paris. It replaced them with territorial mandates, a new paper secured on national lands, which also depreciated and were abandoned in early 1797. In the same year the government restored the use of metallic currency and, in September 1797, declared a partial bankruptcy, reducing the public debt by repaying two thirds of it in bonds that rapidly lost value, so that creditors suffered heavy losses while the state's finances were stabilized. The regime also reorganized tax collection, creating a body of direct tax administrators and reviving indirect taxes such as the registration and stamp duties, and, increasingly, relied on contributions extracted from occupied territories, which supplied a significant proportion of revenue and supported the armies.

    Society under the Directory was marked by contrasts. The disorder of the previous years had created new fortunes, based on speculation in national lands, army contracting, and the assignat's collapse, and a new urban social life developed in Paris, symbolized by fashions that deliberately evoked antiquity, by balls and salons, and by the figures of the merveilleuses and the incroyables, who dressed in elaborate and often extravagant costume. Alongside this display, many people suffered from poverty, rural brigandage, and the continuing effects of war. The regime was identified with the Revolution's gains, such as the abolition of feudal rights and the sale of national lands, which created a large group of owners with a material interest in preventing a royalist restoration. It also faced the persistence of religious divisions, as the Directory enforced laws against priests who refused the oath while permitting private worship, and its failure to settle the religious question left a legacy for the Consulate.

    The Directory's opponents included royalists, who hoped to restore a Bourbon king, and radicals, who looked back to the Year II or to the Constitution of 1793. The most important radical movement was the Conspiracy of the Equals, organized by François-Noël Babeuf, who called himself Gracchus, and by former Jacobins including Buonarroti and Darthé. Babeuf's journal denounced the inequality that persisted under the republic and demanded a community of goods and labor, in which private property would be abolished, and in the winter of 1795 to 1796 the Society of the Pantheon, a club of supporters, attracted several thousand members until the government, using Bonaparte as an executor, closed it in February 1796. The conspirators planned an insurrection and were arrested on May 10, 1796, after a police agent had infiltrated the group. Tried at Vendôme before a high court, Babeuf and Darthé were condemned and executed on May 27, 1797. The conspiracy has been interpreted as an early form of communism, though historians stress that its ideas drew on eighteenth-century egalitarian thought and that its size was small. A larger episode of neo-Jacobin activity, the attempt by a group of radicals to rouse a military camp at Grenelle in September 1796, was quickly crushed, and military commissions executed a number of participants.

    The financial and administrative recovery of the Directory deserves separate attention from its coups. Abandonment of the assignat did not instantly restore a dependable currency, while government suppliers expected payment in forms they could use and administrators still faced the difficult task of assessing and collecting taxes across unequal local economies. New or reorganized direct taxes and central fiscal controls aimed to make the state less dependent on improvised wartime expedients. The regime also relied heavily on contributions extracted from territories occupied by French armies. These revenues could relieve immediate pressure on the treasury while making peace more costly to the government and burdening populations outside France. Financial measures consequently connected the internal problem of maintaining a republican state with the external logic of expansion. A local official charged with raising taxes and recruiting men could experience the Directory as a more persistent administrative power even when Parisian political factions were changing rapidly.

    The Directory therefore governed between two oppositions, and it adopted measures against each: laws on émigrés and priests to reassure republicans, and surveillance of radical clubs to maintain order. In explaining the regime's difficulties, historians increasingly emphasize that the Directory was not simply corrupt or inept, as the Napoleonic regime and later conservative writers portrayed it, but that it faced a constitutional structure that left it with little room for maneuver, war pressures that demanded resources, and a political class that was divided about whether the republic should be defended by legal or extra-legal means. It also created institutions that survived it, including a more efficient administration of direct taxation and a system of central commissioners in the departments, which prefigured the prefects of the Consulate.

    Section 89.18

    Elections, Coups, Political Purges, and the Erosion of Republican Legitimacy, 1797 to 1799

    The annual elections produced political shifts that the Directors could not accept. In the spring of 1797, the first free elections for the renewal of one third of the legislature, in the months of Germinal Year V, brought a large number of moderates and royalists to the councils, many of them former émigrés or men sympathetic to monarchist ideas, and the councils began to repeal laws against priests and émigrés and to criticize the Directory's finances and foreign policy. Carnot, who was moving toward the moderates, was removed from consideration, and a new Director, Barthélemy, a diplomat who was not hostile to the opposition, replaced Letourneur. The royalist and moderate majority met in a club, the Clichy Club, and some of its members were in touch with royalist agents, one of whom, General Pichegru, president of the Council of Five Hundred, was suspected of treasonable contacts.

    The three Directors who feared the consequences, Barras, Reubell, and La Révellière-Lépeaux, decided to act. With the support of General Augereau, whom Bonaparte sent from the Army of Italy, and with the help of troops in Paris, they carried out the coup of 18 Fructidor Year V (September 4, 1797). Soldiers occupied the legislative halls, arrested some deputies, and the councils, purged of their opposition members, annulled the elections in dozens of departments, invalidated the election of numerous deputies, deported Pichegru, Barthélemy, and others to Guiana, and forced Carnot to flee. The councils then restored the laws against priests and émigrés, increased press controls, and gave the government extraordinary powers. The coup was justified as a defense of the republic against a royalist conspiracy, and the evidence for such a conspiracy was real, but the Directors' response violated the constitution that they had been appointed to defend.

    Fructidor set a precedent. In the following year, the electoral process produced a different result, because the political reaction against the royalists encouraged the election of neo-Jacobins, former supporters of the Year II, who gained seats in the legislature. The Directory, concerned about a leftward shift, passed a law on 22 Floréal Year VI (May 11, 1798), which annulled the elections of a large number of deputies, more than a hundred, whom it considered to be anarchists, and replaced them. This intervention differed from Fructidor in that it was carried out through legislative procedure rather than military force, but it also demonstrated that the Directory could not tolerate unfavorable electoral outcomes. By alternating between the two flanks, the regime lost the support of those who wished to see it as a constitutional republic.

    A third crisis occurred in 1799. The elections of that spring strengthened the neo-Jacobins and other critics of the Directory, and the Council of Five Hundred, in an atmosphere of military defeat abroad and brigandage and royalist insurrections at home, forced three Directors out in the coup of 30 Prairial Year VII (June 18, 1799). The crisis invalidated Treilhard's appointment and forced La Révellière-Lépeaux and Merlin de Douai to resign. Louis Gohier replaced Treilhard, while Roger Ducos and Jean-François Moulin replaced the other two. Sieyès had already entered the Directory in May 1799 as Reubell's successor, before the June crisis. The councils then passed a series of radical measures, among them a progressive forced loan on the wealthy and a law of hostages, which allowed the authorities to take relatives of émigrés and rebels into custody, and the Jourdan-Delbrel law of September 1798 on conscription, which created a systematic annual draft, was implemented more vigorously. These measures revived some of the policies of the Year II but were unpopular and did not solve the underlying crisis. A neo-Jacobin club met in the Manège, and conservatives, in turn, feared a return to the Terror.

    The personnel chronology of 1799 reveals why the phrase 'the Directory changed hands' conceals several distinct events. In May Sieyès replaced Reubell through the regular rotation of an outgoing Director. During the political crisis of June, Treilhard's earlier election was invalidated and Louis Gohier replaced him; La Révellière-Lépeaux and Merlin de Douai were pressured to resign and were replaced by Roger Ducos and Jean-François Moulin. Barras remained in office. This was a legislative victory against sitting executives, not the simultaneous appointment of four replacements for three removed Directors. The succession matters because Sieyès entered the government before the June upheaval and subsequently sought still more sweeping constitutional change. It also reminds readers that a 'coup' need not look the same in every case: 1797 involved direct military intervention, 1798 the annulment of elections, and 1799 a combination of parliamentary pressure and contested appointments.

    The result by the summer of 1799 was a sense that the republic had lost its legitimacy. Each change had been justified as a defense of the republic, yet all of them had breached the constitution or its spirit. The use of the army to purge the legislature, the annulment of election results, and the manipulation of laws to exclude opponents undermined faith in the constitutional order, and the army's increasing role in politics created new possibilities. A government designed to prevent arbitrary executive power was repeatedly dependent on extraordinary actions to preserve itself. This pattern is a key argument of the chapter: the Directory's instability does not prove that republican government in France could not have succeeded. It reflected specific constitutional weaknesses, such as the lack of a mechanism for resolving conflicts between the branches, as well as a high degree of factional conflict, war pressures, fears of royalist restoration and of Jacobin revival, and political decisions that were made by individuals and groups who had alternatives. Historians continue to debate whether a more flexible constitution, a sustained peace, or a more tolerant political culture might have allowed the regime to survive.

    Section 89.19

    Revolutionary Expansion, Napoleon Bonaparte, the Second Coalition, and the Coup of Brumaire

    The Directory depended on war. Military campaigns supplied money through contributions from occupied territories, provided employment for the army and a means of keeping generals occupied abroad, and delivered successes that bolstered the regime's prestige. France formally annexed Belgium in October 1795, while its forces occupied much of the left bank of the Rhine, whose diplomatic and legal disposition remained unsettled; the treaties of Campo Formio in 1797 and Lunéville in 1801 were later stages in that settlement. French intervention in the Dutch Republic also helped establish the Batavian Republic in January 1795. The Directory's plan for 1796 involved three armies, in Germany and Italy, and in the spring it appointed Bonaparte, aged twenty-six, as commander of the Army of Italy, a poorly supplied force that had been assigned a secondary role. Bonaparte's appointment owed something to the support of Barras and to his marriage to Barras's former companion Joséphine de Beauharnais, though his competence was also recognized by Carnot.

    In a rapid campaign in northern Italy between April 1796 and April 1797, Bonaparte defeated the armies of Piedmont-Sardinia and of Austria in a series of engagements, including Lodi, Arcole, and Rivoli, forced Piedmont to sign an armistice, occupied Lombardy, and advanced into the Austrian heartland. He imposed armistices and contributions on Italian states, sent works of art to Paris, and conducted negotiations, often beyond the Directory's instructions, in his own name. By the Treaty of Campo Formio on October 17, 1797, Austria recognized the French gains in Belgium and accepted the creation of a Cisalpine Republic in northern Italy, in return for receiving most of the territory of the Republic of Venice, which had been occupied by French troops and ended its independent existence. The destruction of Venice, a centuries-old republic, was criticized even by some French republicans as a violation of their principles. A Ligurian Republic was created at Genoa, and in 1798 and 1799 French armies established a Roman Republic, a Helvetic Republic in Switzerland, and a Parthenopean Republic at Naples. These client states, ruled by local republicans under French supervision, adopted constitutions modeled on that of Year III and paid for the French armies' presence, and they produced both enthusiasm among some local reformers and resentment at requisitions, anticlerical measures, and the loss of independence.

    In 1798, Bonaparte persuaded the Directory to authorize an expedition to Egypt, designed to threaten British interests, to open a new theater, and to establish French influence in the eastern Mediterranean. He sailed from Toulon in May with about 35,000 troops and a group of scholars, and after capturing Malta he landed in Egypt in July and defeated the Mamluk forces at the Battle of the Pyramids on July 21. On August 1 and 2, a British fleet under Nelson destroyed the French fleet at Aboukir Bay, in the Battle of the Nile, stranding the army. The Ottoman Empire, of which Egypt was nominally a province, declared war on France, and Bonaparte's campaign in Syria in early 1799 failed before Acre. The expedition generated extensive scholarly observations; their celebrated multi-volume publication, the Description de l'Égypte, began only in 1809, as a later consequence of the campaign, and had a lasting effect on European knowledge of Egypt, but it also resulted in military failure and Egyptian resistance, including the revolt in Cairo in October 1798, and illustrated the connection between revolutionary expansion and imperial ambition.

    Meanwhile, the Directory's expansion provoked a new coalition. The Second Coalition, formed in 1798 and 1799, included Britain, Austria, Russia, the Ottoman Empire, and Naples. In 1799, the French suffered setbacks. In northern Italy, the Austro-Russian forces under Suvorov defeated French armies, and General Joubert was killed at Novi in August. The murder of French diplomats at Rastatt in April, after negotiations with the German states, outraged opinion, and royalist risings, the renewed Chouannerie in the west, and brigandage in the south added to the pressure. In the autumn the military situation improved: Masséna's victory at Zurich on September 25 and 26 and a stalemate in the Netherlands removed the immediate threat of invasion. But the political crisis was by then well advanced, and many of the political elite believed that the republic needed a stronger executive.

    Sieyès, one of the authors of the revolutionary theory of national sovereignty and a Director since May 1799, concluded that the constitution should be revised and that a military figure was needed to support the change. He looked first to General Joubert, who was killed, and then to Bonaparte, who left Egypt in August, evaded the British fleet, and landed at Fréjus on October 9, to the acclaim of crowds. Bonaparte, who had not been authorized to return, was welcomed in Paris by politicians who sought a solution, among them Sieyès, Talleyrand, Fouché, who was minister of police, Roger Ducos, and Lucien Bonaparte, who was president of the Council of Five Hundred. The conspirators planned to use the constitution's provision permitting the Council of Ancients to move the legislature outside Paris, in order to remove it from popular pressure, and to force a revision of the constitution.

    On 18 Brumaire Year VIII (November 9, 1799), the Council of Ancients, informed of an alleged Jacobin conspiracy, decreed that the legislative councils would meet the next day at Saint-Cloud, outside Paris, and appointed Bonaparte to command the troops in the capital. Barras was persuaded to resign, and Gohier and Moulin were placed under guard. The next day, 19 Brumaire (November 10), the councils met at Saint-Cloud, in a palace surrounded by soldiers. In the Council of Ancients, Bonaparte's address was confused, and in the Council of Five Hundred, where there were many neo-Jacobin deputies, he was shouted down and some deputies called for him to be outlawed. Lucien Bonaparte, who presided, left the chair and, with the support of soldiers led by Murat, had the hall cleared. Later that night a rump of the Council of Five Hundred and the Ancients voted to replace the Directory with a provisional executive of three consuls, Bonaparte, Sieyès, and Roger Ducos, and to appoint commissions to draft a new constitution.

    The coup should be explained through French political and institutional history rather than as the inevitable product of Bonaparte's genius. It was planned by civilian politicians who had been repeatedly frustrated by the constitution of Year III, it depended on the legal pretext provided by the constitution itself, and it succeeded partly because of luck, the improvisation of Lucien Bonaparte, and the army's loyalty, and partly because much of the political class and public preferred order to a renewed revolutionary struggle. The consequence was that a revolution that had repeatedly tried to create legitimate representative institutions ended this phase under a new concentration of executive and military power. The consolidation of that power, the Constitution of Year VIII, and the Napoleonic state are examined in Chapter 90, and the wars that followed are treated in Chapter 91.

    Analysis: The decree shows how the coup was given a constitutional appearance. It created the conditions for the events of November 10, though it did not itself abolish the Directory or create the Consulate; those measures came from a rump of the councils later that night.

    Limitations: A decree cannot reveal the private plans of the conspirators or the evidence for the alleged plot. Accounts of Saint-Cloud, written later by participants, are partisan and conflict on details.

    Section 89.20

    Chapter Review and Historical Synthesis

    This section draws together the chapter's principal arguments. Between 1792 and 1799 the French Republic was founded in an insurrection and a military emergency; it executed its king; it fought a continental war that made it a mass-mobilized state; it faced rebellion in the west and revolts in provincial cities; it adopted a democratic constitution that was never applied; it abolished slavery in its colonies under pressure from the enslaved and from war; it built a system of emergency government and terror; it overthrew that system in Thermidor; it created a constitution of checks in 1795; and it governed through the Directory until a coup in Brumaire replaced it with a new concentration of executive and military power.

    The central question, how the Republic tried to reconcile liberty, popular sovereignty, equality, national survival, and political stability, can be answered by comparing the republic's three constitutional moments. The Convention of 1792 was elected by a broad male franchise but ruled through committees; the Constitution of 1793 promised democracy but was suspended; the Constitution of 1795 limited participation and divided power but provided no way to resolve conflict. In each case the authors responded to the dangers they most feared, and in each case the response created new problems. Emergency government was justified as a defense of the nation, but it concentrated power; the Directory sought to prevent concentration but depended on coups.

    A comparative evaluation therefore shows a recurring movement between claims of popular sovereignty and practices of coercive or extraordinary government. War, civil conflict, scarcity, unequal rights, factional rivalry, and political choice interacted, and no single factor explains the pattern. Students should be able to distinguish judicial executions from civil-war deaths, constitutional design from practice, and the abolition of slavery in law from freedom in practice. They should also recognize that Napoleon Bonaparte's rise was part of the Directory's history, grounded in French politics, though the consolidation of his rule belongs to Chapter 90.

    Chapter Summary

    This chapter has traced the French Republic from the insurrection of August 10, 1792, to the coup of Brumaire in November 1799. The republic was not created by a single act. The monarchy was suspended in August, abolished in September, and followed by the king's trial and execution in January 1793. The National Convention governed a republic that existed before it had a constitution, and its deputies disagreed deeply about the relationship between representation and popular action.

    War shaped every aspect of the period. French victories after Valmy led to expansion and to a coalition of European powers, and defeats in 1793 produced the defection of Dumouriez, the levy of 300,000 men, and the levée en masse. The mobilization created large armies, strengthened the revolutionary state, and linked citizenship to military service, but it also provoked resistance, notably in the Vendée, and required coercive administration.

    The republic faced rebellion in the west and revolts in Lyon, Marseille, Bordeaux, Caen, and Toulon. The fall of the Girondins in June 1793 transformed the Convention's balance of power, and the Constitution of 1793, though approved, was never put into effect. In October 1793 the Convention declared the government revolutionary until peace, and the committees, representatives on mission, surveillance committees, and the Revolutionary Tribunal became the instruments of emergency rule.

    The Terror was a system rather than the act of a single person. The Law of Suspects, the Revolutionary Tribunal, military commissions, and local committees produced mass detention and thousands of executions, concentrated in regions of civil war. Historians distinguish judicial executions, military killings, massacres, and deaths in prison, and they debate whether the Terror was a necessary response to extraordinary threats or the result of ideological and political choices. The General Maximum and requisitions linked repression to economic policy, and women's activism and the suppression of their clubs illustrate the limits of the republic's universalism.

    The same tension appears in the colonies. Enslaved people in Saint-Domingue had already forced commissioners to proclaim emancipation when the Convention decreed abolition on February 4, 1794. The decree extended citizenship to all men in the colonies without distinction of color, but its implementation varied, labor remained coerced, and the government that decreed abolition also restricted rights at home. The decision came from the interaction of revolutionary principle, war, and insurrection.

    By spring 1794 the committees had eliminated the Hébertists and Dantonists, and the Law of 22 Prairial accelerated the Terror even as the military situation improved. The fall of Robespierre on 9 Thermidor was the work of a heterogeneous coalition of men who feared for their own safety. It ended Jacobin emergency government but not repression: the White Terror, economic crisis, and the popular uprisings of Germinal and Prairial 1795 showed that the republic was still divided.

    The Constitution of Year III attempted to avoid both dictatorship and popular insurgency by creating an indirect electoral system, two legislative councils, and a five-member Directory, strictly separated. The royalist uprising of Vendémiaire was defeated by the army, and the regime began with an expectation of stability. The Directory restructured finances, ended the assignat, defended the republic against royalists and neo-Jacobins such as Babeuf's conspirators, and built a ring of sister republics.

    However, the Directory's design offered no means of resolving conflicts between executive and legislature, and when elections produced unwelcome results the Directors turned to coups in Fructidor Year V, Floréal Year VI, and Prairial Year VII. These repeated interventions eroded republican legitimacy and made the army a political actor. Military setbacks in 1799 and renewed internal disorder encouraged civilian politicians such as Sieyès to look for a general.

    On 18 and 19 Brumaire, Sieyès, Bonaparte, and their allies used the constitution's own provision to move the legislature to Saint-Cloud, and with the army's help they replaced the Directory with a provisional Consulate. The coup resulted from French political and institutional history, including a long record of extra-constitutional action, and cannot be explained only by Bonaparte's ambitions.

    The chapter's governing conclusion is that between 1792 and 1799 French revolutionaries repeatedly tried to establish a durable order based on republican sovereignty, constitutional rights, and national independence. Their efforts produced major political and social transformations, but war, civil conflict, economic insecurity, unequal rights, factional competition, and reliance on extraordinary power undermined them. The Directory inherited these contradictions and did not resolve them before Brumaire. The consolidation of Napoleonic power belongs to Chapter 90.

    Chapter Reflection

    This chapter matters because it shows a political community trying to build a republic under conditions of extreme pressure and discovering how difficult it was to reconcile its ideals. The French Republic of 1792 to 1799 gave Europe a vocabulary of citizenship, rights, popular sovereignty, and national mobilization that outlived the regimes that first used it. It also gave Europe a record of emergency government, political violence, and military intervention in politics that later movements would cite as warning and as precedent.

    The chapter reveals processes that extended well beyond France. The republic's wars transformed the political map of the Low Countries, the Rhineland, Italy, and Switzerland, and its colonial policies tied the Revolution to Atlantic slavery and to the Haitian Revolution, whose full history lies beyond this chapter. The debates about rights, property, and participation, and the conflict between democratic and restrictive constitutions, were taken up in Chapter 97 and Chapter 98 and in the revolutions of the nineteenth century.

    Several continuities survived the chapter. The reliance on the army, the weakness of public finance, the division between supporters and opponents of the Revolution, and the sale of national lands persisted into the Consulate and the Napoleonic Empire examined in Chapter 90. The administrative centralization of the Year II and the Directory's central commissioners also anticipated the prefectural system that Napoleon would consolidate.

    Within the Volume's larger argument, the chapter shows how the destruction of the old regime did not automatically produce a stable new order. Volume IV follows the transformation of European politics, economy, and empire from the Enlightenment to 1914, and this chapter marks the point at which revolutionary republicanism gave way to a more authoritarian state that nonetheless preserved many revolutionary changes. The transition to the Napoleonic era should therefore be read as a contingent outcome of the republic's unresolved contradictions, not as the predetermined end of the Revolution.
