British and French Overseas Expansion

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

On 4 August 1701, delegates from some three dozen Indigenous nations gathered at Montréal to end decades of war. They signed with the marks of their peoples, and the French governor signed beside them. A European colonial official had presided, but the peace was largely the achievement of Indigenous diplomats. It is a fitting beginning for a chapter on British and French empire, because it shows that the outcome of European expansion depended on negotiation, alliance, and the aims of non-European societies as much as on European ships and armies.

This chapter examines how England, after 1707 Great Britain, and France built overseas empires between roughly 1580 and 1763, with a limited extension to 1765. Both states entered an Atlantic world that Spain and Portugal had opened, examined in Chapters 68 and 69, and that Dutch merchants had commercialized, examined in Chapter 71. Neither could claim the early advantages of its predecessors. Each therefore developed its own methods: English ventures relied on private investors, chartered companies, and the migration of large numbers of settlers, while French ventures relied on fishing and fur trading, royal sponsorship, Indigenous alliances, and smaller settler communities. In the Caribbean, both created plantation economies whose wealth depended on enslaved labor, a subject examined comprehensively in Chapter 70. In South Asia, both East India companies moved from trade toward political intervention.

The chapter is organized into eight divisions. The first examines the foundations of expansion and competing models of empire. The second and third treat English and French North America separately, because their settlement patterns, economies, religious institutions, and relationships with Indigenous peoples differed and cannot be understood as variations on a single model. The fourth turns to the Caribbean, whose sugar islands were often more valuable to European governments than mainland territories. The fifth compares colonial administration and mercantilism. The sixth traces the escalating rivalry that led to the Seven Years' War and the Treaty of Paris of 1763. The seventh follows the British and French East India companies to the formation of early British territorial power in Bengal. The eighth compares the two empires and synthesizes the chapter.

Three themes recur. The first is Indigenous agency. Powhatan, Wampanoag, Haudenosaunee, Wendat, Mi'kmaq, Shawnee, Choctaw, Kalinago, and many other peoples were political authorities with their own interests. European maps and charters claimed territory over which European governments had little practical control, and the gap between claim and control shaped every colonial enterprise. The second theme is the distinction between legislation and enforcement. Navigation Acts, the French colonial exclusive, and royal ordinances expressed ambitions that merchants, officials, and settlers modified, evaded, or ignored. The third is the interdependence of Atlantic and Asian expansion: the same wars, the same finance, and in some cases the same treaties linked North America, the Caribbean, and India.

The evidence for this history is abundant and unevenly distributed. English and French colonial governments produced charters, correspondence, ordinances, land records, court records, and parish registers. Missionary accounts, notably the Jesuit Relations, are rich but written from a particular religious and cultural viewpoint. Merchant ledgers, shipping records, and customs accounts allow economic reconstruction. Indigenous sources include wampum belts, treaty pictographs, oral traditions, and archaeological evidence, and Indigenous voices often reach historians through European translators and scribes. For South Asia, Company records are voluminous, while Persian chronicles and Indian merchant and banking records provide essential alternative perspectives. Students should always ask who produced a source, for whom, and whose experience it omits.

Terminology requires care. The settlements founded before 1707 were English, not British, and the chapter applies "British" only after the Acts of Union of 1707 united the kingdoms of England and Scotland. "New France" was a collection of colonial regions, Acadia, Canada along the St. Lawrence, the Great Lakes and Illinois country, and Louisiana, not a single compact territory. "Indian" appears in primary sources and in some official names, but the chapter uses specific nation names wherever possible. Sources and historians also describe the era's wars in different ways: the French and Indian War began in North America in 1754, while the wider Seven Years' War is conventionally dated 1756 to 1763.

The chapter has firm boundaries. It does not retell Iberian exploration or conquest (Chapters 68 and 69), the comprehensive history of slavery (Chapter 70), or the Portuguese and Dutch commercial empires (Chapter 71). It reserves the cumulative effects of empire on European consumption, finance, and society for Chapter 73, the domestic state building and balance of power diplomacy for Chapters 76 to 80, the Atlantic revolutionary crisis for Chapter 87, and later imperial structures in Asia for Chapter 105. It ends with the changing balance of the 1760s, not with the American Revolution or nineteenth century empire.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Compare English and French overseas expansion with earlier Portuguese, Spanish, and Dutch models.
  2. Explain the economic, religious, and political motives that led to English settlement in North America and to French fur trade and mission colonies.
  3. Analyze how Indigenous societies negotiated, resisted, and shaped colonial settlement, alliance networks, and warfare.
  4. Distinguish the economic and social systems of the Chesapeake, New England, the Middle Colonies, and the southern colonies.
  5. Describe the geography, institutions, and alliances of New France in Acadia, the St. Lawrence, the Great Lakes, and Louisiana.
  6. Assess the Caribbean's role in British and French imperial policy and its connections to North America, with reference to the slavery history of Chapter 70.
  7. Compare British and French colonial administration, including charters, assemblies, intendants, and the Sovereign Council.
  8. Evaluate mercantilism as theory and practice, distinguishing legislation from enforcement.
  9. Trace the sequence of imperial wars from 1689 to 1763 and explain how they altered the colonial balance of power.
  10. Interpret the Treaty of Paris of 1763 and its consequences for Indigenous peoples, colonists, and European states.
  11. Explain how the English and French East India companies moved from commerce to political and military intervention in India, with attention to Indian political actors.
  12. Evaluate historiographical debates about differences between British and French colonial systems and about the consequences of the Seven Years' War.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. Why did the English mainland colonies attract large numbers of settlers, while French North America depended on smaller settler communities, trading relationships, and Indigenous alliances?

  4. In what ways did colonial administration and mercantile regulation connect overseas possessions to European monarchies and merchants, and how effective was enforcement?

Historical Context

By 1580 the Atlantic was a contested space. Spanish colonization of the Caribbean, Mexico, and Peru was well established, Portuguese Brazil was developing a sugar economy, and Portuguese and Spanish ships dominated the African and Asian routes, as Chapters 68 and 69 explained. The papal grants and the Treaty of Tordesillas of 1494 had claimed a division of the non-European world between Spain and Portugal, but those claims were never accepted by the other European monarchies. French and English fishermen sailed to the cod banks off Newfoundland in the sixteenth century, along with Basque and Portuguese crews, and French and English privateers attacked Iberian shipping, treating war and commerce as overlapping activities.

The political situation in Europe shaped overseas ambitions. The Protestant Reformation, examined in Chapters 62 to 66, divided the western church, and in England the Elizabethan settlement and prolonged hostility with Spain made overseas ventures part of a confessional and strategic contest. In France, the Wars of Religion ended with the Edict of Nantes of 1598 under Henry IV, and royal government gradually recovered capacity to sponsor ventures. Chapter 71 described how the Dutch Republic, born in revolt against Spain, developed the chartered joint-stock company and a financial system that made Amsterdam the commercial center of Europe. English and French merchants and officials watched and imitated these methods while also competing with Dutch shipping.

English society in this period had specific features that affected colonization. Population growth in the later sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, combined with enclosure, price inflation, and cyclical unemployment, created a pool of landless or poor workers willing to emigrate or enter servitude. Religious dissent, including Puritanism and later Quakerism, encouraged organized migration of families. England's earlier plantations in Ireland, especially in the Munster plantation of the 1580s and the Ulster plantation after 1609, provided models of settlement, land seizure, and the representation of native populations as obstacles. Historians debate how directly Irish experience shaped American colonization, but many individuals and ideas connected the two.

French society and government offered different conditions. France was the most populous kingdom in western Europe, with a strong agrarian economy and large internal markets, and fewer pressures pushed peasants overseas. Catholic orders took responsibility for missions. The French crown encouraged colonial ventures through monopoly companies, but its main attention was often directed toward European wars. Fishing and the fur trade attracted merchants from Atlantic ports such as Rouen, Dieppe, Saint-Malo, La Rochelle, and Bordeaux, and the royal government periodically took a more active role, most notably under Cardinal Richelieu in the 1620s and under Louis XIV and his minister Jean-Baptiste Colbert after 1661.

The wider world also mattered. India under the Mughal Empire was a major producer of textiles and attracted European merchants, as Chapter 71 showed for the Portuguese and Dutch. The English East India Company, chartered in 1600, and the French company founded in 1664 entered an Asian commercial system in which Indian rulers, bankers, and merchants held the upper hand for most of the period covered in this chapter. Atlantic and Asian expansion therefore developed in parallel, and the wars between Britain and France increasingly connected them.

Division I: The Foundations of British and French Overseas Expansion

Why England and France expanded overseas, and how their strategies developed in an Atlantic world already shaped by Iberian and Dutch expansion.

Section 72.1

72.1 Europe's Changing Atlantic Balance, c. 1580 to 1650

After 1580 Spain, which also ruled Portugal through the Iberian Union, held the largest territorial empire in the Americas and the richest silver flows. The Dutch, as Chapter 71 explained, were meanwhile building the most efficient commercial shipping system in Europe. England and France stood between these poles. Both had maritime populations and ports, but neither had Spanish treasure or Dutch capital, and both were distracted by domestic religious conflict. Their early overseas activity therefore grew from fishing, privateering, and the effort to undermine Iberian power as much as from any plan of territorial empire.

War shaped the opportunity. The Anglo-Spanish War of 1585 to 1604, which included the failure of the Spanish Armada in 1588, legitimized English attacks on Spanish shipping and encouraged investors to back ventures that promised plunder, trade, or strategic bases. The Treaty of London in 1604 ended the war, and the Twelve Years' Truce of 1609 between Spain and the Dutch Republic reduced hostilities further. In these years of relative peace, English and French attention turned from raiding to settlement. France under Henry IV, after the Edict of Nantes in 1598, regained enough stability to sponsor colonial ventures.

Privateering deserves precise definition. A privateer held a commission from a state authorizing attacks on enemy shipping, and the line between privateering and piracy depended on political circumstances. Caribbean and Atlantic waters in this period were crowded with vessels whose owners, captains, and crews moved between legitimate trade, licensed raiding, and illegal seizure. This maritime culture produced both experience of Atlantic navigation and a persistent connection between colonization and violence.

The financial position of the two crowns explains much of the early pattern. Neither the English nor the French monarchy could match the revenues that American silver supplied to Madrid, and both were deeply indebted by the late sixteenth century, the English chiefly through Elizabeth's wars and the French through decades of civil conflict. Royal governments could not underwrite distant settlements, so colonization had to attract private money. London merchants who had profited from the Levant, Muscovy, and Baltic trades, courtiers hoping for patronage, and provincial gentry looking for new outlets for younger sons formed the investing public of the early English ventures. Their expectations were short term. Investors typically hoped for plunder, quick trading profits, or a discovery of precious metals, and they withdrew when returns failed to appear. Antwerp's commercial decline after 1585 and the rise of Amsterdam, examined in Chapter 71, also pushed English and French merchants to seek commercial opportunities that did not depend on Dutch intermediaries.

Religion shaped the strategic setting as well. For Protestant England the Spanish empire represented a Catholic world power, and promoters such as Richard Hakluyt linked colonization to national security and the extension of Reformed Christianity. For France the confessional wars produced a different effect. Huguenot ports such as La Rochelle sponsored privateering and overseas ventures, while the Catholic monarchy after 1598 encouraged expansion partly as a way of directing aristocratic and military energy away from civil strife. The Atlantic setting was therefore not simply a field of competition among nations but also an extension of the religious conflicts examined in Chapters 62 to 66.

Section 72.2

72.2 England's Early Atlantic Ventures and Colonial Ambitions

John Cabot, a Venetian born navigator sailing from Bristol under a patent from Henry VII, reached the North American coast in 1497, and English claims to the region later rested partly on his voyages. For most of the sixteenth century, however, English interest in North America was limited. English ships fished off Newfoundland, and Elizabethan sailors such as Francis Drake attacked Spanish shipping and ports, completing a circumnavigation of the globe between 1577 and 1580. Drake's voyages demonstrated that English seamen could challenge Spain, but they did not create colonies.

Elizabethan promoters, among them the geographer Richard Hakluyt the younger, argued that English colonies would provide raw materials, markets for cloth, bases against Spain, and employment for the poor. Humphrey Gilbert claimed Newfoundland for the crown in 1583 but died on the return voyage. Walter Raleigh, who held a patent for colonization, sponsored settlements on Roanoke Island, off the coast of what is now North Carolina, in 1585 and 1587. The first settlement was withdrawn after relations with local Algonquian peoples deteriorated and supplies failed. The second, which included women and children, was left without relief because the Spanish war disrupted shipping. When John White returned in 1590 the colonists had vanished, leaving a carved word on a post. What happened to them is uncertain, and speculation about their fate has long outrun the evidence.

The failures of the 1580s taught lessons. Settlements needed reliable supply, food production, defensible sites, and relations with local peoples. The Newfoundland fishery remained profitable and permanent English fishing stations developed there, but the private patent system proved too weak to support colonies. The experience pushed promoters toward joint-stock organization, which pooled resources from many investors, a model already used by the Muscovy Company and the East India Company.

The best documented of the Elizabethan efforts was Roanoke. Walter Raleigh received a patent in 1584 modeled on that held earlier by Humphrey Gilbert, and in 1585 a force of about one hundred men under Ralph Lane settled on Roanoke Island. The scientist Thomas Harriot, who accompanied them, studied the Algonquian language and published a promotional account in 1588 that catalogued the region's resources and described Indigenous communities such as the Secotan and Croatoan. The colony depended on Indigenous food and labor, and relations soured when the Roanoke leader Wingina, who had initially sought an alliance, organized resistance. Lane's men killed him in June 1586, and when Francis Drake's fleet appeared that month the colonists left with him. Their experience shows that the first English settlers depended on Indigenous goodwill and that violence could erupt quickly when the English demanded food.

The second Roanoke venture of 1587 under John White was intended to be a settled community, with families, and White's granddaughter Virginia Dare was the first English child born in North America. White returned to England for supplies, but the Spanish crisis of 1588 delayed his return until 1590. He found the settlement dismantled, with a carved clue suggesting the settlers had moved, probably to Croatoan, but no later reliable evidence establishes their fate. Archaeologists continue to examine the possibility that some joined nearby Indigenous communities, but the question remains unresolved. What can be said securely is that Roanoke failed through inadequate supply, poor preparation, and the pressure of war.

Section 72.3

72.3 French Exploration, Fishing, and the Search for Commercial Opportunity

French involvement in the Atlantic predated permanent colonies. Giovanni da Verrazzano, an Italian navigator in French service, explored the North American coast in 1524. Jacques Cartier, from Saint-Malo, made voyages to the Gulf of St. Lawrence and up the river between 1534 and 1536, and again in 1541 to 1542. He encountered St. Lawrence Iroquoian communities at Stadacona and Hochelaga, kidnapped Donnacona and others to take to France, and failed to establish a lasting settlement. The French attempted settlement elsewhere, including Huguenot colonies in Brazil and Florida in the 1550s and 1560s, but the Florida colony at Fort Caroline was destroyed by Spanish forces in 1565.

Fishing, not colonization, provided the base of French activity. Fishermen from Normandy, Brittany, and the Basque coast sailed to the Grand Banks each season, and as they came ashore to dry fish they met Indigenous peoples and began to exchange metal goods for furs. By the 1580s the fur trade, especially for beaver pelts used in making felt hats, had become profitable, and merchants sought monopolies. Tadoussac, at the mouth of the Saguenay River, became a seasonal trading center. When French royal patents gave a single merchant company exclusive rights, the grantees were expected to support settlers in return, an obligation that was usually minimally met.

This pattern shaped French policy. The French economy depended on commerce in furs and fish, and Indigenous hunters and middlemen controlled the supply. Colonists were needed to occupy territory, protect trade, and bring Catholicism, but the profits of the fur trade did not depend on large agricultural settlement, and fur traders often opposed it, since farms displaced hunting grounds.

Cartier's voyages illustrate both French ambition and the central role of Indigenous knowledge. On his second expedition in 1535 he sailed up the St. Lawrence to the settlement of Stadacona, where the leader Donnacona ruled, and then to the larger town of Hochelaga on the island of Montréal. His party wintered near Stadacona in 1535 to 1536, and scurvy struck the Frenchmen badly. According to the traditional account, Indigenous neighbors showed them a remedy made from the bark or foliage of a local tree, which saved many lives. Cartier, however, repaid this help by seizing Donnacona and several others and carrying them to France, where Donnacona and most of the others died. The episode made later relations difficult and shows the risks that Indigenous hosts took in welcoming strangers. The expedition of 1541 to 1542, directed by Cartier and the nobleman Jean-François de La Rocque de Roberval, intended permanent settlement but failed after a hard winter and renewed conflict.

French Protestant ventures were a separate strand. Nicolas Durand de Villegagnon's colony in Guanabara Bay in Brazil in the 1550s collapsed amid religious quarrels and Portuguese attack, and in the 1560s Jean Ribault and René de Laudonnière founded Fort Caroline in Florida, where the Spanish commander Pedro Menéndez de Avilés destroyed the colony in 1565. These events confirmed Spanish determination to exclude rivals from the southeast and demonstrated that Huguenot colonization faced both Spanish hostility and domestic French opposition. After the Wars of Religion, French colonial promoters shifted their attention north, to the St. Lawrence and Acadia, where Spanish power was weak.

Section 72.4

72.4 Chartered Companies, Royal Sponsorship, and Competing Models of Empire

Both monarchies faced a problem: how to encourage colonization without paying for it. The solution was delegation. In England, the crown issued charters granting land and governing authority to private groups. The Virginia charter of 1606 created two companies, one based in London and one in Plymouth, and the London company operated as a joint-stock enterprise whose investors expected profit. Other colonies were established as proprietorships, in which the crown granted territory and powers of government to an individual or small group, as with Maryland in 1632 to the Calvert family, Carolina in 1663 to a group of courtiers, and Pennsylvania in 1681 to William Penn. Some colonies, such as Massachusetts, began with a company charter whose governing body moved to America, creating unusual local autonomy. Over time, the crown converted many of these colonies into royal colonies.

French policy used monopoly companies more consistently. In 1627 Cardinal Richelieu created the Company of One Hundred Associates, which received a monopoly over the fur trade and ownership of New France in exchange for an obligation to bring several thousand settlers over fifteen years. It did not meet that obligation. In 1663 the crown took direct control, and later companies, including the West India Company of 1664, held commercial privileges but not full control. English and French systems therefore differed in degree: both used private capital, but England's colonies were more dispersed and developed more representative institutions, while the French monarchy kept tighter legal control in principle.

Religion also motivated both. English Protestants spoke of spreading the gospel and opposing Catholic Spain, and dissenters sought freedom to worship. French Catholic orders sought converts among Indigenous peoples. These motives coexisted with commercial calculation, and historians generally argue that religion, profit, strategic rivalry, and social pressure all contributed, in varying proportions.

The charters created different relationships among crown, investor, and settler. The Virginia Company of London in its first years was run by a royal council, with shareholders who expected profit. When profits did not appear, the company's leaders turned to land grants, the transfer of ownership to settlers, and tobacco, and the company's reorganization in 1609 and 1612 made it more clearly a joint-stock body governed by its own officials. The Plymouth Company's attempt at Sagadahoc on the Maine coast in 1607 to 1608 failed after a harsh winter, leaving the northern region to later groups, and in 1620 the Council for New England replaced it. The Massachusetts Bay Company's charter of 1629 did not require that its governing body meet in England, a drafting feature that colonists exploited when they carried it to Boston in 1630, and that crown officials later tried to correct by seeking revocation.

Proprietary colonies illustrate a different logic, the use of colonial grants as political patronage. Cecil Calvert received for Maryland powers resembling those of a medieval palatine lord, including the right to establish courts, appoint officials, and grant land, though subject to the requirement of consulting an assembly of freemen. The Carolina proprietors of 1663 were courtiers and supporters of Charles II, and Penn's grant of 1681 settled a royal debt owed to his father, Admiral William Penn. After the Restoration the crown also chartered trading monopolies, among them the Hudson's Bay Company in 1670 and the Royal African Company in 1672, which reflected a view that overseas enterprise could generate revenue for the crown and court supporters while limiting direct state expense.

Historical Vocabulary
Chartered, Proprietary, and Royal Colonies

A chartered company held a grant from the crown to trade and govern; the Virginia Company is an example. A proprietary colony was granted to an individual or group, the proprietors, who held extensive governing powers, as in Maryland and Pennsylvania. A royal colony was governed directly by the crown through a governor, usually with a council and, in most English cases, an elected assembly. These categories were not fixed: many colonies changed status, and even royal colonies depended on local cooperation. The terms matter because they show how English governments delegated, then sometimes reclaimed, authority.

Division II: English and British Settlement in North America

The mainland colonies developed different economies, religious institutions, political arrangements, and relationships with Indigenous peoples. Settlements founded before 1707 were English; after the Acts of Union they were part of Great Britain.

Section 72.5

72.5 Jamestown, the Virginia Company, and the Powhatan World

In May 1607 about one hundred English men and boys landed on a swampy peninsula on the James River and founded Jamestown, named for King James I. The Virginia Company of London, which backed the venture, expected gold, a passage to Asia, or profitable trade. Instead the settlers found a region already organized politically. Wahunsenacawh, known to the English as Powhatan, led a paramount chiefdom of Algonquian speaking communities in tidewater Virginia, which tribute and alliance bound together, and he regarded the English as potential allies and sources of metal goods who could be incorporated into his own system.

The early colony almost failed. Colonists lacked farming skills, suffered from disease and brackish water, quarreled among themselves, and depended on trading with the Powhatan for corn. John Smith, a soldier who became a leader of the colony, enforced discipline and recorded his dealings with Indigenous peoples, though his writings, produced for promotion, must be used cautiously. Relations deteriorated into conflict, the First Anglo-Powhatan War of 1609 to 1614, and the winter of 1609 to 1610, the starving time, reduced the colony from several hundred to a few dozen survivors. The arrival of Lord De La Warr and supplies in 1610 prevented abandonment. A peace in 1614 was associated with the marriage of Pocahontas (Matoaka) to John Rolfe, a union that served diplomatic purposes and whose meaning to Pocahontas is difficult to recover from English sources.

The company reorganized, introduced private land ownership in place of communal labor, and in 1619 summoned a representative assembly, later called the House of Burgesses. In 1622 Opechancanough, Wahunsenacawh's successor in leadership, organized a coordinated attack in which about 347 colonists were killed, according to the 1622 account of Edward Waterhouse, a contemporary English tally and not an independent census, and the colonists retaliated with campaigns of destruction. The company's finances collapsed, and in 1624 the crown revoked its charter and made Virginia a royal colony. Jamestown's survival depended on Indigenous food, on the profits of tobacco, and on the arrival of thousands more colonists.

Understanding Jamestown requires understanding the Powhatan polity. English accounts describe a paramount chiefdom of some thirty districts in the tidewater, each led by a weroance, whose leaders owed tribute and military support to Wahunsenacawh. He had inherited a few districts and extended his authority through alliance, marriage, and coercion in the decades before 1607. His strategy toward the English was to incorporate them as dependents, trading corn for copper, beads, and iron tools, and he sought to bind them through diplomatic gestures such as exchanging young men to learn each other's languages, among them Thomas Savage and Namontack, and through a ceremony in 1608 in which the English crowned him with a gift crown. The English interpreted such events as submission to King James, while the Powhatan may have seen them as exchange between allied leaders. The two sides thus acted under different assumptions.

Instability followed from English dependence. The colony's third supply in 1609 brought hundreds of new settlers, but the flagship Sea Venture was wrecked on Bermuda, and the colony faced winter with insufficient food and with the Powhatan unwilling to supply more. The starving time that winter reduced the population drastically, and the survivors prepared to leave when the new governor, Lord De La Warr, arrived in June 1610 and imposed strict military discipline. The "Lawes Divine, Morall and Martiall" under Thomas Dale after 1611 were harsh. English raids on Powhatan towns continued, and the capture of Pocahontas in 1613 forced negotiation. By 1614 a precarious peace allowed tobacco cultivation to expand. Wahunsenacawh died in 1618, and his brother Opechancanough became the principal leader.

The attack of 22 March 1622 was a response to English land expansion, and its death toll of 347 comes from Edward Waterhouse's 1622 published account, a source written to defend the company and urge vengeance, so it is a contemporary English tally and not an independent census. The English response was a series of campaigns that destroyed Powhatan towns and fields. A second major uprising in 1644 ended in the capture and killing of the aged Opechancanough and a treaty in 1646 that made the Powhatan tributaries and established boundaries between English and Indigenous lands, boundaries that settlers soon crossed. Evidence for this history comes largely from English writings, including John Smith's various accounts, which often served promotional aims. Archaeology at the Jamestown site, where the fort's remains were identified in the 1990s, and Indigenous oral traditions maintained by descendant communities such as the Pamunkey and Mattaponi, supply important corrections.

The First Charter of Virginia, 10 April 1606

Creator: King James I of England, issuing letters patent to investors · Date: 10 April 1606 · Type: A royal charter establishing two colonizing companies and defining territory, governance, and rights

Documentary reference: The First Charter of Virginia (10 April 1606), Yale Law School, Avalon Project. This window is a paraphrase and not a verified literal quotation.

Context: The charter followed years of failed English attempts and was issued soon after England's peace with Spain in 1604. It authorized two companies, one of London and one of Plymouth, to settle separate sections of the North American coast. Its terms shaped the legal assumptions of the colonists and the practical expectations of the investors.

Paraphrase of selected provisions (not a direct translation) The charter grants the two companies rights to settle and hold land on the coast of North America within stated latitudes, and provides that the colony is to be governed by councils whose members are named by the crown, subject to royal instructions. It permits the companies to mine for metals, to coin money for colonial use, and to defend the colonies against attack. It states that colonists and their children born in the colonies will have the same liberties and rights as if they had been born in England. It expresses a hope that the enterprise will spread the Christian religion among peoples living in darkness and ignorance of true worship. This summary reflects the charter's broad content; students should consult a verified edition for exact wording.

Analysis: The charter shows that the crown treated territory in North America as available for grant, with no reference to the political authority of Indigenous peoples, who held and governed the land. It also shows how English law traveled: the guarantee of English liberties later supported colonial claims to representative government. Religious language coexisted with commercial provisions, indicating mixed motives. The document cannot show how the colony actually functioned, how Indigenous societies understood the arrival of the English, or how the colonists interpreted their rights in practice. Those questions require letters, court records, and archaeological evidence, and Indigenous perspectives must be sought in sources beyond English charters.

Section 72.6

72.6 Virginia, Maryland, Tobacco, and the Chesapeake Colonies

Virginia's economic future was set by tobacco. John Rolfe's experiments with a sweeter West Indian variety around 1612 produced a crop that found a market in England. Tobacco required land and labor, and the company introduced the headright system in 1618, which granted fifty acres to anyone who paid for the passage of a settler, including themselves. Wealthy planters accumulated land by importing servants. Most newcomers were young, poor, English men who signed indentures, contracts binding them to labor for a term, commonly four to seven years, in return for passage and maintenance. Mortality was very high, and the gender imbalance, with many more men than women, limited natural population growth for decades.

Maryland, chartered in 1632 to Cecil Calvert, Lord Baltimore, as a proprietary colony, was settled from 1634 and was intended partly as a refuge for English Catholics. Protestants quickly became the majority, and the colony's Toleration Act of 1649 protected Trinitarian Christians, though not others. Maryland's economy resembled Virginia's, with tobacco plantations along the Chesapeake's waterways, and its politics were marked by conflict between proprietary authority and an assembly.

In the later seventeenth century, as English wages rose and fewer servants migrated, planters turned increasingly to enslaved Africans, a shift examined in Chapter 70. Bacon's Rebellion of 1676, in which Virginian frontier settlers, many of them former servants, attacked Indigenous peoples and challenged the governor, alarmed the elite and encouraged a legal order based on race and slavery. The Chesapeake thus developed a plantation society with a small wealthy planter class, a large number of poorer farmers, and a growing enslaved population, a society that was dispersed rather than urban, because tobacco ships loaded at private wharves along navigable rivers.

Tobacco shaped land and labor. A planter needed cleared fields, curing barns, and workers, and the soil lost fertility after a few years, so planters kept moving to new land, which drove expansion up the river valleys and into Indigenous territories. The headright system gave wealthy men a legal basis for accumulating land by importing servants, and the colony's elite emerged from those who survived the early mortality and acquired headrights. Prices for tobacco fluctuated sharply, collapsing in the 1620s and again at intervals in the seventeenth century, so small planters lived with debt and risk. Servants who survived their terms received "freedom dues," often a small amount of corn and clothing, and sometimes land, but good land became scarce, and freed servants became a restless and poor class. This was the social background of Bacon's Rebellion of 1676, which began as a conflict over Indigenous policy and the governor's refusal to authorize attacks on Indigenous neighbors, and became a challenge to the planter elite.

Demography limited family formation. Men outnumbered women by large margins, women often married soon after arrival, and high mortality from disease meant that many marriages were short and children fatherless. Population growth depended on continued immigration until the late seventeenth century, when conditions improved enough for natural increase. The legal order also changed in these years. Virginia statutes of the 1660s, including a law of 1662 providing that a child's status followed that of the mother, defined slavery as inheritable and created a legal basis for a racial caste, a development Chapter 70 treats in the context of Atlantic slavery. By the early eighteenth century Africans and their descendants formed a large part of the Chesapeake labor force, and planters had built a society of large estates and dependent workers.

Section 72.7

72.7 Plymouth, Massachusetts Bay, and Puritan New England

New England's settlement began with religious dissent. In 1620 a group of English Separatists and others, later called the Pilgrims, arrived at Plymouth aboard the Mayflower, and drafted the Mayflower Compact, an agreement to form a civil government. Plymouth survived in part because epidemics between about 1616 and 1619 had devastated coastal Indigenous peoples, leaving cleared land available. Their relations with the Wampanoag leader Massasoit, an alliance made in 1621 and assisted by Tisquantum (Squanto), gave the colony protection and food, while serving Wampanoag interests in a period of demographic catastrophe and rivalry with the Narragansett.

The Massachusetts Bay Company, chartered in 1629, was a larger enterprise. Puritans, who wished to reform the Church of England, transferred the company's charter to New England in 1630 under John Winthrop, thereby gaining unusual political independence. Between 1630 and 1640, in what is called the Great Migration, thousands of English men, women, and children, mostly families, crossed the Atlantic, motivated by religion, economic hardship, and political pressure in England. Estimates of the number of arrivals vary, with a range of about 13,000 to 20,000 often cited, and the figure refers to arrivals, since some migrants later returned to England.

Puritan New England organized itself around towns, each with a church, land allotments, and local government by town meeting, though only male church members could vote in colony elections at first. Education was emphasized: Harvard College was founded in 1636, and a Massachusetts law of 1647 required towns to provide schools. The colony did not practice religious toleration. Roger Williams, who criticized the colony's treatment of Indigenous land and the mixing of church and state, was banished and founded Providence in 1636. Anne Hutchinson, tried for her religious teachings in 1637, was expelled. Quakers were persecuted, and four were executed in Boston between 1659 and 1661. New England's economy combined subsistence farming, fishing, shipbuilding, and trade, notably with the West Indies.

The Puritan commonwealth was a society organized by covenant. Church members entered into a covenant with God and one another, and the New England churches, governed by their congregations, claimed independence from bishops. Civil government was intertwined with church membership: for some years only church members could be freemen entitled to vote for colony officials, though ordinary male householders participated in town meetings. The General Court, including elected deputies from each town, made laws, and magistrates enforced a moral code that regulated Sabbath observance, sexuality, and speech. Leaders such as John Winthrop understood their experiment as a godly society that would serve as an example to the world, though historians caution that the sermon commonly cited for this vision was not widely known in its time and has taken on greater significance in later American memory. The religious model produced striking features: high literacy, a dense network of schools and churches, and a political culture accustomed to discussion in town meetings.

Dissent tested the system. Roger Williams, who argued that the king's charter had no right to Indigenous land and that civil authorities should not enforce religious obligations, founded Providence on land purchased from the Narragansett, and Rhode Island became a refuge for dissenters. Anne Hutchinson, a woman who led religious discussion groups, challenged the clergy's authority and was tried by the General Court in 1637 and banished, a case that shows both the female religious leadership present in early New England and the limits placed upon it. Other settlers migrated to the Connecticut River valley, where the Fundamental Orders of 1639 framed a government. The Quakers, who rejected ministerial authority, were treated as a threat, and four were executed between 1659 and 1661, a repression that the crown later forced the colony to end.

Section 72.8

72.8 Indigenous Resistance and Colonial Warfare in New England

Expansion generated war. The Pequot, a powerful nation in southeastern Connecticut, controlled trade and tribute networks that colonists and rival Indigenous groups challenged. In the Pequot War of 1636 to 1637 English forces from Massachusetts and Connecticut, with Narragansett and Mohegan allies, attacked a Pequot fort at Mystic in May 1637 and killed several hundred people, many of them noncombatants. Surviving Pequot were enslaved, dispersed, or incorporated into other nations, and the Treaty of Hartford in 1638 sought to eliminate the Pequot as a political entity.

Several decades of land purchases, jurisdiction disputes, and settler expansion, along with the spread of English livestock and legal pressure on Indigenous communities, produced the largest conflict. In 1675 Metacom, known to the English as King Philip, a leader of the Wampanoag, led a coalition that included Nipmuc, Narragansett, and others against the colonies. Metacom's War, or King Philip's War, of 1675 to 1676, destroyed dozens of towns, killed a significant share of the colonial population and a far larger proportion of Indigenous peoples in the region, and left New England's economy damaged. Colonial authorities interned Christian Indians from the "praying towns" on Deer Island in Boston Harbor, and many captives were enslaved or sold abroad. Metacom was killed in August 1676.

The war's consequences were lasting. Southern New England Indigenous nations lost most of their independent political power, though they continued to exist, and Wabanaki peoples in the north continued to resist and later allied with the French. Historians emphasize that Indigenous participants had their own objectives, including defense of land, sovereignty, and kinship networks, and that the war cannot be reduced to a simple clash of cultures. The sources are overwhelmingly English, with captivity narratives and official records, so Indigenous motives must be reconstructed with care.

The Pequot War grew from competition. The Pequot controlled a tribute network in southern New England that depended in part on trading wampum, shell beads that Dutch and English traders used to purchase furs, and the Pequot's rivals, the Narragansett and the Mohegan under Uncas, saw opportunities in an alliance with the English. After the killing of the trader John Stone in 1634 and John Oldham in 1636, Massachusetts demanded justice, and a punitive expedition set off hostilities. Connecticut's settlers, who were exposed on the river, declared war in 1637. The Mystic attack, in which the English and allies set fire to a palisaded village, shocked even some of the Narragansett allies, according to colonial accounts, and demonstrated a form of warfare, the destruction of noncombatants, that Europeans increasingly applied in North America. The treaty of 1638 prohibited the Pequot name and divided survivors among allies and colonists.

The coming of Metacom's War illustrates accumulated pressures. Metacom's father Massasoit had kept peace with Plymouth, but his sons Wamsutta and Metacom faced growing English demands for land and political submission, and the Wampanoag saw their autonomy eroded by court rulings. In 1675 the death of John Sassamon, a Christian Indian who had warned the English of Metacom's plans, and the trial and execution of three Wampanoag men by a court that included Indigenous jurors but was run by Plymouth, set the conflict in motion. Metacom's coalition attacked towns across the Connecticut valley and eastern Massachusetts, while the English struck at the Narragansett in the Great Swamp fight in December 1675, though the Narragansett had not initially joined. Eventually the colonial forces, supported by Mohegan and by Mohawk attacks on Metacom's camps in the winter of 1676, and weakened by shortages of food and ammunition, broke the coalition.

The costs were severe. Estimates of casualties vary, but several hundred colonists died, and Indigenous deaths from combat, starvation, disease, and enslavement were a far larger share of the Indigenous population. Many captives were sold into slavery in the West Indies and elsewhere. The Wabanaki in the north fought on until the Treaty of Casco in 1678. The war strengthened English claims to southern New England and made Indigenous communities subordinate, but it also left a legacy of fear and a militarized frontier, and it contributed to the crown's attempts to supervise the colonies more directly.

Section 72.9

72.9 New Netherland, New York, Pennsylvania, and the Middle Colonies

The Dutch colony of New Netherland, founded by the West India Company in the 1620s (see Chapter 71), was commercially oriented and ethnically diverse, with a small population of Dutch, Walloons, Africans, and others. Its fur trade with the Haudenosaunee through Fort Orange (Albany) was central. In 1664 an English fleet seized the colony without major resistance, and Charles II granted it to his brother James, Duke of York, renaming it New York. The English takeover left much of the Dutch population in place and preserved Dutch legal and social institutions for decades. New Jersey was separated and granted to proprietors.

Pennsylvania, granted in 1681 to William Penn, a Quaker, was organized as a proprietary colony with principles of religious freedom and broad male political participation. Penn's Frame of Government and the growth of Philadelphia attracted English and Welsh Quakers, German pietists and others, and Scots-Irish settlers. Penn sought land through purchase and diplomacy with the Lenape (Delaware), and the colony enjoyed relatively peaceful relations in its first decades, though Penn's successors and colonists later pressed Indigenous land claims through sharp practice, as in the Walking Purchase of 1737. The Middle Colonies were more religiously and ethnically plural than New England or the Chesapeake, with commercial agriculture, notably wheat and flour, and busy ports.

Indigenous diplomacy in the region was sophisticated. The Haudenosaunee Confederacy, based in what is now upstate New York, maintained trade and diplomatic relations with Dutch, English, and French. The relationship with English officials at Albany, later called the Covenant Chain, was a series of alliance agreements in which the Haudenosaunee retained political independence while exchanging goods and protection. Students should not take English descriptions of the Haudenosaunee as "subjects" literally.

New Netherland combined commerce with ethnic variety. The West India Company established Fort Orange (Albany) around 1624 and New Amsterdam in 1625 to 1626, and Peter Minuit's reported purchase of Manhattan in 1626 reflects Dutch efforts to acquire land through payment, though the Lenape who accepted the goods may have understood the transaction as a grant of shared use. The company's patroonship scheme, which gave landowners large estates if they brought settlers, produced only a few lasting manors, such as Rensselaerswyck. Conflict with Indigenous peoples, as in Kieft's War of 1643 to 1645 and the Esopus wars, strained the colony, and its population stayed small. Under Peter Stuyvesant, governor from 1647, the colony tolerated diversity within limits, as shown by the Flushing Remonstrance of 1657, in which residents of an English town on Long Island asked that Quakers be allowed to worship.

The English conquest of 1664 under Richard Nicolls came with Articles of Capitulation that protected Dutch property, inheritance, and religion, and the Dutch population generally stayed. A Dutch fleet recaptured New York in 1673 during the Third Anglo-Dutch War, but the Treaty of Westminster returned it to England in 1674. The Duke of York's government, as proprietor, introduced English law and a governor with a council, and an assembly was authorized only in 1683 and later suspended, so New York's government remained more centralized than that of other colonies. When the Duke became James II in 1685 New York became a royal colony.

Section 72.10

72.10 The Carolinas, Georgia, and Southern Colonial Expansion

Carolina was granted in 1663 to eight proprietors, and its first lasting settlement, Charles Town, was established in 1670 by colonists including many from Barbados. These planters brought experience of slavery and sugar, and the colony developed an early economy based on deerskins, naval stores, and an Indian slave trade, in which Indigenous captives were sold to the Caribbean and northern colonies. Around the 1690s rice cultivation, introduced with the knowledge of enslaved West Africans who had grown rice in their homelands, transformed the low country, and indigo became a second export crop in the 1740s. By the early eighteenth century South Carolina's population contained a very large enslaved majority, and the Stono Rebellion of 1739 showed the tensions.

Relations with Indigenous nations shaped the colony. Trade with Cherokee, Creek, Catawba, Yamasee, and others was central, and traders extended credit and enslaved captives. Resentment of debts and abuses led the Yamasee, with Creek and other allies, to attack Carolina in 1715. The Yamasee War of 1715 to 1717 nearly destroyed the colony, which survived with help from the Cherokee. After the war South Carolina reorganized its Indian trade under public regulation, and diplomatic relations became more cautious. In 1729 the crown purchased the proprietors' claims, and Carolina was divided into royal colonies of North and South Carolina.

Georgia, chartered in 1732 with James Oglethorpe as a leading trustee, was meant to serve as a buffer against Spanish Florida and to offer a fresh start to poor English people. Its trustees initially prohibited slavery and large landholdings, but those restrictions were repealed by the early 1750s. The southern colonies were thus areas of imperial frontier policy, where Spanish, French (from Louisiana), and English interests, and Indigenous nations such as the Creek and Choctaw, competed in the same space.

Carolina's founders hoped to create a hierarchical society. The Fundamental Constitutions of 1669, drafted with the assistance of John Locke as secretary to the proprietor Lord Ashley, proposed a landed aristocracy and recognized slavery, though the colony's actual institutions evolved from local circumstances and never fully followed this plan. The Barbadian settlers who dominated the early colony brought slave law and an expectation of a plantation economy. In the early decades the economy rested on cattle, timber and naval stores, and a trade in deerskins and Indigenous slaves, in which Carolina traders exchanged European goods for captives taken in raids by allied Indigenous nations, particularly against groups allied with the Spanish in Florida. Estimates of the number enslaved in this trade range into the tens of thousands, and the trade devastated nations of the region while enriching some English and Indigenous participants.

The shift to rice, first established in the 1690s, transformed Carolina. Rice cultivation in tidal swamps required irrigation, labor, and skills that enslaved West Africans from rice growing regions brought, and the task system allowed enslaved workers to complete defined labor assignments each day. By about 1708 the colony's population had a black majority, an unusual structure in mainland North America that shaped fears of revolt. In 1739 the Stono Rebellion, an uprising of enslaved people near Charles Town, was suppressed, and the legislature responded with the Negro Act of 1740, which tightened controls. Indigo, introduced as a crop in the 1740s with the experiments of Eliza Lucas Pinckney and others, added a profitable second export, and Charles Town grew into the wealthiest city in the South.

Section 72.11

72.11 Population, Immigration, Land, and Society in British Mainland Colonies

British mainland colonial population grew rapidly. A figure of roughly 250,000 around 1700 rising to about 1.6 million by 1760 is often cited, including a large and growing enslaved population, though estimates vary. Growth was due to natural increase, which was higher than in Europe because land was relatively available, and to migration. Besides English migrants, large numbers of Scots and Ulster Scots, Irish, Germans, and others came, many as indentured servants or redemptioners, as did several hundred thousand Africans brought involuntarily. Women's roles were shaped by law and custom: under English common law, married women had limited property rights, though widows sometimes managed estates, and household production relied on the labor of wives, children, and servants.

Land acquisition shaped social structure. New England towns distributed land among families, producing communities of small farmers. In the Chesapeake and the low country, large estates concentrated in the hands of planters. In the Middle Colonies, tenant and freehold farms coexisted. Colonists purchased land from Indigenous nations through deeds whose meaning each side understood differently, and settlers also simply squatted on land beyond legal boundaries. By the mid eighteenth century pressure for western land produced tension among colonists, imperial officials who sought to manage frontiers, and Indigenous peoples who resisted encroachment.

These societies shared English law and language but differed in economic, religious, and political structure. The statement that the colonies were a single "society" is therefore misleading. Merchants dominated Boston, Newport, New York, and Philadelphia, planters dominated the southern colonies, and frontier farmers of many backgrounds lived in the backcountry. The tensions among colonists, imperial officials, and Indigenous peoples would intensify in the 1750s and 1760s.

Immigration changed the character of the colonies in the eighteenth century. From about 1717, large numbers of Scots and Ulster Scots, many of them Presbyterians, left northern Ireland because of rent increases and economic distress, and they settled mainly in the Pennsylvania backcountry and along the Appalachian valley to the southern colonies. German speaking migrants, from the Rhineland and southwest Germany, began arriving in large numbers from the 1720s and 1730s, usually through Philadelphia, and they included Lutherans, Reformed, Mennonites, Moravians, and others. Many came as redemptioners, who owed the cost of their passage and negotiated their service on arrival. Britain also transported tens of thousands of convicts to the colonies in the eighteenth century, who worked as servants mainly in Maryland and Virginia. Africans, who arrived involuntarily in rising numbers, were, by 1760, approximately one fifth of the mainland population by common estimates.

Households were the basic units of production, and their structure reflected law and gender. Under the doctrine of coverture a married woman's legal identity was subsumed in her husband's, restricting her ability to hold property or sue, though widows often managed estates and women controlled domestic production, dairying, and marketing. Children contributed labor early. Wills, probate inventories, and tax lists allow historians to reconstruct wealth, and they show wide inequality, greatest in the southern plantation regions and in the commercial port cities, and less in the northern farming areas. Mobility in British America was high, and a family might migrate from a settled coastal region to the frontier within a generation.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living in a New England Town, New France, and a Caribbean Colony

In a New England town of the later seventeenth century, a household typically consisted of a married couple, children, and sometimes servants or apprentices, living in a timber frame house, farming a mixed holding of grain, livestock, and orchard, and attending a meetinghouse where sermons structured the week. Literacy was widespread among men and growing among women, and town records, wills, and court cases reveal disputes over land, debt, and morality. In the Saint Lawrence valley, a habitant family held a long narrow farm in a seigneury, paying modest dues to a seigneur, hearing mass in a parish church, and sometimes working in the fur trade or as boatmen. Winters were long, and families were large; settler women had a relatively high marriage rate and fertility.

In the Caribbean, life differed sharply. On a sugar island such as Barbados or Martinique, a small group of planters and managers presided over a large enslaved majority who labored in cane fields and mills under violent discipline, in a climate of tropical disease that made life expectancy short for newcomers and enslaved people alike. Most evidence about enslaved people comes from planters' records, legal codes, and the testimony of observers, so their experiences must be reconstructed carefully. Chapter 70 treats this subject more fully. These contrasts show why the "colonial experience" varied so much by region, status, and gender.

French expansion must be treated independently, not simply as a smaller or unsuccessful version of British colonization. Indigenous alliances were indispensable, and French claims often exceeded the practical reach of French settlement or administration.

Section 72.12

72.12 Champlain, Québec, and the Beginnings of Permanent French Settlement

Samuel de Champlain, a navigator and mapmaker who had traveled in the Caribbean and Spanish America, took part in the settlement of Port-Royal in Acadia in 1605. In 1608 he founded a trading post at Québec, on a narrows of the St. Lawrence River at the site of the earlier Stadacona. The location was strategic: it commanded the river route into the continent. The first winters were harsh, and the habitation's small population suffered from scurvy and death. Champlain's goal was to secure the fur trade and extend French influence by allying with Indigenous peoples.

Champlain allied with the Innu (Montagnais), Algonquin, and Wendat (Huron) peoples, and in 1609 he joined an Algonquin and Innu war party that fought Mohawk warriors near Lake Champlain, an action that placed France in the middle of Indigenous rivalries with the Haudenosaunee. Settlement grew slowly. Louis Hébert, an apothecary, began farming in the 1610s, but by the late 1620s the population remained only a few hundred. In 1629 English privateers under the Kirke brothers captured Québec, and the colony was returned to France by the Treaty of Saint-Germain-en-Laye in 1632. Champlain returned, and died in Québec in 1635. Montréal was founded in 1642 as a mission settlement, Ville-Marie, by Paul de Chomedey de Maisonneuve.

The colony's slow growth was a result of the structure of the fur trade, the monopoly companies' failure to bring settlers, the cold climate, the difficult journey, and the French crown's limited interest. Historians note that French migration to New France was small compared with English migration to the mainland colonies, but those who stayed had high survival rates and grew by natural increase.

French efforts at Port-Royal and Québec were linked. Pierre Dugua de Monts, who held a trading monopoly from 1603, brought Champlain to the Bay of Fundy, and the settlement on Île Sainte-Croix in 1604 suffered badly from scurvy and cold, leading to the move to Port-Royal in 1605. When de Monts's monopoly lapsed, he and Champlain concentrated on the St. Lawrence, where the site at Québec had a defensible position and good access to inland trade. The "habitation" at Québec, a wooden fort and storehouses, housed a few dozen people in the early winters, and many died. Champlain built relationships with Indigenous partners who supplied food and furs, and in 1615 he went with a Wendat war party to attack an Onondaga fort, in an unsuccessful expedition that reinforced the French alliance with the Wendat. His maps and writings document early French knowledge, though they also reflect his own interests.

Growth was slow. A major reason was that the monopoly companies, including the One Hundred Associates, did not meet their obligations. In 1628 the English privateering fleet of the Kirke brothers captured the company's supply ships, and in 1629, after a winter of near famine, Champlain surrendered Québec. After the colony was restored in 1632, the Jesuits, and the devout laymen who founded Montréal in 1642, were the leading figures of a mission oriented settlement. The census of 1666, the first systematic count, enumerated about 3,200 non-Indigenous inhabitants, which suggests how slowly the colony had grown before the reforms of the 1660s.

Section 72.13

72.13 The Fur Trade and French-Indigenous Commercial Alliances

The fur trade was the economic foundation of Canada. Beaver pelts were exported to France for felt hats, and Indigenous trappers and traders exchanged them for metal tools, kettles, cloth, firearms, and other goods. The trade was organized through alliances. The Wendat acted as middlemen, trading with nations to the north and west and bringing furs to Québec in annual canoe fleets, and the Innu and Algonquin served as partners and guides. French merchants and officials gave gifts and participated in ceremonies of exchange that were central to Indigenous diplomacy. Trade was not simply commercial: it created kinship ties, obligations, and political alliances.

French traders extended into the interior. Young men called coureurs de bois traveled to Indigenous communities to trade, often without license, and later licensed voyageurs paddled large canoes carrying goods. Many married Indigenous women, producing Métis families that linked French and Indigenous worlds. The crown tried to regulate the trade, issuing licenses (congés) and trading permits, but control was weak, and the supply of furs sometimes exceeded demand, leading to price falls.

Epidemics reshaped the system. In the 1630s smallpox and other diseases struck Wendat communities in the Great Lakes region, with very high mortality, and in 1649 the Haudenosaunee attacked and destroyed the principal Wendat towns, scattering the survivors. This disrupted the trade system and increased French reliance on other nations, including the Odawa and Anishinaabe peoples of the upper Great Lakes, who became the new intermediaries. French political authority thus rested on managing a network of Indigenous alliances.

The trade had specific rhythms. Each summer Indigenous traders, often in canoe flotillas, brought furs to Montréal or earlier to Trois-Rivières and Québec, where the trade fair lasted weeks and combined bargaining with diplomacy, speeches, feasting, and gift giving. The exchange rate between furs and goods was negotiated, and French merchants had limited ability to dictate terms, since Indigenous suppliers could take furs to Dutch or English competitors at Albany, who often offered more goods or better cloth. Indigenous consumers were discerning. They demanded specific styles of kettles, cloth, blankets, and guns, and French suppliers adapted. The goods did not simply replace Indigenous material culture but were incorporated into it, as copper kettles were cut up for ornaments and metal tools replaced stone ones.

Intermediaries held power. The Kichesipirini Algonquin controlled the Ottawa River route at Allumette Island and charged tolls on passing traders, and the Wendat, in a densely populated region of southern Georgian Bay, brought corn and goods to the northern nations in return for furs. After Wendat dispersal in 1649 the Odawa, Anishinaabe peoples of the upper Great Lakes, became the leading middlemen, and French traders increasingly went directly to the interior. The beaver trade fluctuated, and a glut in the 1690s, when French warehouses held more pelts than the hat makers could use, led the crown in 1696 to order the closing of western posts, a policy that was reversed after Indigenous allies complained and the French feared that the English would take over the trade. Women played important roles as processors of pelts, as interpreters, and as marriage partners who connected French traders to Indigenous kinship networks.

Section 72.14

72.14 Missions, Conversion, and Indigenous Religious Responses

Catholic missions formed a second pillar of New France. Recollect friars arrived in 1615, and Jesuits came from 1625. The Jesuits, who wrote annual reports published in France as the Jesuit Relations between 1632 and 1673, built missions among the Wendat, Innu, Algonquin, and other peoples, and established a mission headquarters, Sainte-Marie among the Hurons, in 1639. Women's orders, notably the Ursulines under Marie de l'Incarnation and the Hospital Sisters, founded schools and hospitals in the 1630s and 1640s.

Indigenous peoples responded in different ways. Some communities adopted elements of Christianity, sometimes combining them with existing beliefs, while others resisted, especially when epidemics were blamed on missionaries and conversions caused factional divisions. Mission villages near Montréal, such as Kahnawake, became centers of Catholic Haudenosaunee communities, and Kateri Tekakwitha, a Mohawk woman, became venerated and was later canonized by the Catholic Church. Missionaries also learned languages and recorded ethnographic detail, producing evidence that is valuable and difficult.

The evidence requires caution. The Jesuit Relations were written for European patrons and readers, to demonstrate progress and to raise funds, and they reflect missionary assumptions and selective emphasis. They record Indigenous speech and practices through a European filter. Historians use them alongside archaeological evidence and Indigenous oral traditions to understand religion as a site of negotiation and cultural interaction, not simply as one-way conversion.

The Jesuit Relations are a rich but difficult source. Published annually in France between 1632 and 1673, they described mission work in vivid detail, with accounts of Indigenous customs, language, and religion. The authors, trained in classical learning, interpreted what they saw through Catholic theology, and they wrote partly to maintain support from donors, so they emphasized hardship, martyrdom, and conversion. They also describe Indigenous responses with considerable attention, and often recorded debates in which Wendat leaders questioned Christian claims. Modern historians combine them with archaeology of Wendat villages, studies of Indigenous languages, and later oral histories.

Section 72.15

72.15 The Haudenosaunee, Colonial Conflict, and the Great Peace of Montréal

The Haudenosaunee Confederacy, a league of five nations (Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca, joined by the Tuscarora in the early eighteenth century), lay between French Canada and English and Dutch settlements. In the seventeenth century the confederacy fought a series of wars, often called the Beaver Wars, against neighbors including the Wendat, Neutral, Erie, and nations further west, partly to secure captives, hunting territory, and trade. They obtained firearms from Dutch and English traders at Albany and conducted "mourning wars" to replace population lost to epidemics. The 1640s and 1650s saw the destruction of Wendake and attacks on French settlements.

Conflict with the French persisted. In 1687 the governor Denonville seized Haudenosaunee people at Fort Frontenac, some of whom were sent to France as galley slaves, and invaded Seneca country. In 1689 Haudenosaunee warriors attacked the settlement of Lachine, near Montréal, killing many. By the 1690s all sides were exhausted: French trade was disrupted, and Haudenosaunee communities had suffered heavy losses. The Haudenosaunee also faced English pressure for land and the loss of their position as middlemen as French allies traded directly with English.

The Great Peace of Montréal, concluded on 4 August 1701, ended the wars. Louis-Hector de Callière, the governor, hosted delegates from some thirty-eight or thirty-nine Indigenous nations, depending on how nations are counted, with about 1,300 delegates reported, and the Wendat-Petun (Tionontati) leader Kondiaronk played a decisive role in the negotiations until his death on 2 August 1701, after which a major funeral was held. The agreement committed the nations to peace, to the return of prisoners, and to consultation with French governors in disputes, and the Haudenosaunee adopted a policy of neutrality between the French and English, a position they maintained for much of the next half century. The peace was an Indigenous diplomatic achievement as much as a French one, and the signers marked the document with pictographic signs identifying their nations.

The causes of the long Haudenosaunee wars lay partly in demography. Epidemics in the 1630s and 1640s reduced Haudenosaunee populations, and a custom of mourning war, in which war parties captured prisoners to replace the dead through adoption, intensified conflict. The competition for beaver, as hunting grounds near the five nations were depleted, and for access to European goods, added to this. French invasions of Mohawk country in 1666 under Alexandre de Prouville de Tracy and the peace of 1667 produced a period of relative calm, during which Jesuit missions operated in Haudenosaunee towns. But the pressure of the western fur trade, the rise of French allies, and the interests of English governors at New York brought renewed conflict in the 1680s.

The Great Peace was an Indigenous achievement for several reasons. Peace delegations came to Montréal during the summer of 1701, and the councils involved the exchange of wampum belts, the smoking of the calumet, and ceremonies of condolence that had meaning in Indigenous diplomacy. Kondiaronk, an orator widely respected, who had long been a leading opponent of Haudenosaunee expansion, argued for a general peace, and his death during the proceedings, after an illness, produced a moving public funeral with French military honors. The Haudenosaunee concluded the treaty but also, in the same summer, negotiated with English officials at Albany, where they placed their beaver hunting grounds under English "protection," in what is called the Nanfan Treaty. This dual diplomacy was the essence of the neutrality policy. It allowed the Haudenosaunee to avoid being drawn into French and English wars while retaining trading relationships with both, and it kept their independence. The treaty's design, with the French governor as mediator for disputes among allies, also meant that France was bound to Indigenous expectations about peacekeeping and gifts, obligations that proved costly.

Section 72.16

72.16 Acadia, Atlantic Fisheries, and the French Maritime Frontier

Acadia, settled from the early seventeenth century after the failed attempt at Île Sainte-Croix in 1604 and the foundation of Port-Royal in 1605, developed differently from the St. Lawrence. The region, in what are now the Maritime provinces, was the homeland of the Mi'kmaq and the Wabanaki peoples. French settlers, most from western France, farmed the tidal marshes of the Bay of Fundy by building dikes called aboiteaux to reclaim fertile land, and they traded with the Mi'kmaq. Acadian communities, by the early eighteenth century, were distinct from those of Canada, with a growing population of farming families, relative autonomy, and ties to both New England traders and Mi'kmaq neighbors.

The region's political status was unstable. Port-Royal changed hands several times between French and English forces, and Britain gained permanent possession of mainland Nova Scotia (Acadia) by the Treaty of Utrecht in 1713. France retained Île Royale (Cape Breton Island) and the Île Saint-Jean (Prince Edward Island), and began building the fortified town of Louisbourg on Île Royale in 1719, which became a major fishing port, a naval base, and a strategic fortress guarding the Gulf of St. Lawrence. The Mi'kmaq maintained alliance with France and a sense of independent authority, and treaties signed with British authorities in the 1720s and later were interpreted differently by each side.

Acadia illustrates that French settlement was not confined to fur trading. Acadian agriculture and the Atlantic cod fishery supported substantial populations, and the maritime region linked French and Indigenous societies, New England merchants, and Caribbean markets.

Acadia's settlers developed a distinct society. Settlement grew slowly from the 1630s, when Isaac de Razilly brought families to La Hève and Port-Royal, and by about 1700 the Acadian population was perhaps one and a half thousand, rising to over ten thousand by the 1750s through high natural increase. They built dikes and sluice gates to reclaim salt marsh, creating productive meadows that grew grain, kept livestock, and supported orchards. Trade with Boston and with Mi'kmaq neighbors supplemented subsistence. After British conquest, many Acadians sought neutrality, and some governors accepted a conditional oath, but others demanded unconditional allegiance, creating the dilemma that would lead to the deportation of 1755.

Section 72.17

72.17 The Great Lakes, Illinois Country, and the French Interior Network

French power in the continental interior was a network of posts and alliances. From Québec and Montréal, canoe routes led up the Ottawa River and through the Great Lakes to Michilimackinac, Green Bay, and beyond. Forts such as Frontenac (1673), Niagara, Detroit (1701), and posts in the Illinois country, notably Kaskaskia and Cahokia, served as trade centers, military bases, and diplomatic meeting places. The region, known as the pays d'en haut, was home to Anishinaabe (Odawa, Ojibwe, Potawatomi), Miami, Illinois, Meskwaki (Fox), Sauk, and other nations.

The relationship between French and Indigenous peoples in this region has been described by historians as a "middle ground," a space in which neither side could impose its will, and both adapted each other's customs to build workable relationships. French officials provided gifts, mediated conflicts, and gave presents to allied leaders, while Indigenous nations used French goods and alliances to pursue their own aims. The system was not stable. The Fox Wars of the 1710s to 1730s, in which the Meskwaki resisted French efforts, show that French influence faced limits. The Illinois country developed agricultural settlements along the Mississippi, producing grain that supplied Louisiana.

French claims to the interior vastly exceeded French control. A few thousand French people lived across a territory of millions of square kilometers, and the French relied on Indigenous partners who could and did withdraw support. The fort and the trading post were instruments of diplomacy as much as of military control.

The French posts of the interior functioned as meeting places more than as garrisons. Detroit, established in 1701 by Antoine de Lamothe Cadillac to concentrate allied nations near French protection and block the English, drew Wendat, Odawa, Potawatomi, and Miami communities around the fort, producing a multicultural town. Michilimackinac, at the straits between Lake Michigan and Lake Huron, hosted a mission and a trading post, and each summer brought traders, interpreters, and Indigenous visitors together. Commanders of the posts operated mostly through diplomacy, giving gifts and arbitrating disputes, and an unpopular commander could find himself isolated. The Métis families that grew up around the posts, with Indigenous mothers and French fathers, formed a link between worlds.

Conflict showed the limits. The Meskwaki (Fox) quarrelled with the French and their allies over access to the western trade and control of the route to the Mississippi, and in 1712 they besieged Detroit. The wars that followed, until the 1730s, saw French expeditions in alliance with Illinois, Potawatomi, Ojibwe, and other nations that nearly destroyed the Meskwaki, who survived only by incorporating into other communities. The Illinois country was a site of French agricultural settlement, with villages around Kaskaskia, Cahokia, and Fort de Chartres producing wheat and flour for Louisiana, and enslaved Indigenous and African laborers worked there, evidence that French settlement in the interior was not wholly a world of free trade and cooperation.

Section 72.18

72.18 The Mississippi Valley, Louisiana, and the Expansion of New France

In 1682 René-Robert Cavelier de La Salle traveled down the Mississippi to its mouth and claimed the valley for Louis XIV, naming it Louisiana. His later attempt to found a colony on the Gulf coast in 1684 failed, and he was killed by his own men in 1687. The French crown, motivated by the desire to block Spanish and English expansion, sent Pierre Le Moyne d'Iberville and his brother Jean-Baptiste Le Moyne de Bienville, who founded settlements at Biloxi in 1699 and Mobile in 1702. In 1718 Bienville founded New Orleans on the lower Mississippi, a site chosen for its control of river access to the Gulf.

Louisiana's early population was small, disease ridden, and dependent on Indigenous alliances, particularly with the Choctaw, who supplied food and acted as a counterweight to the Chickasaw, who traded with the English. French settlement involved soldiers, a few farmers, convicts, and, after 1719, increasing numbers of enslaved Africans brought for plantation agriculture. A speculative scheme, the Company of the Indies and the financial system of John Law, promoted colonization in the early 1720s but collapsed in 1720, bringing losses in France. The Natchez rebelled in 1729, attacking the French post at Fort Rosalie, and the French and their Choctaw allies destroyed the Natchez as a political community in the war that followed.

By mid century Louisiana was a society of river plantations, small farms, and an Indigenous trading frontier, with ties to Saint-Domingue and the Caribbean. It completed a continental arc of French claims from the St. Lawrence to the Gulf, although the effective French presence remained thin.

The settlement of Louisiana changed in scale after 1717, when the crown handed the colony to a monopoly company led by John Law, which merged into the Company of the Indies in 1719. Promotional campaigns in France and Germany attracted settlers, including German families who founded the "German Coast" on the Mississippi, and the company shipped convicts and enslaved Africans. Law's financial system collapsed in 1720, and the colony passed to direct royal control in 1731. The economy relied on tobacco, indigo, timber, and later sugar, and on trade in deerskins with Indigenous nations, and New Orleans served as an administrative and commercial center despite its vulnerability to floods and hurricanes.

Indigenous diplomacy shaped Louisiana's survival. The Choctaw, the largest nation in the region, traded deerskins and slaves with the French, and their alliance helped counterbalance the Chickasaw, who sold to English traders from Carolina. When the French failed to supply goods the Choctaw, who were divided into factions, turned toward the English, and French governors devoted enormous effort to gifts. The Natchez war of 1729 to 1731 began as a reaction to French demands for land at Natchez, and in the war the French, with Choctaw allies, destroyed the Natchez as an independent people, with many captives sold into slavery in Saint-Domingue. The French campaigns against the Chickasaw in 1736 and 1739 to 1740 failed, which shows the limits of French military power. Louisiana's society included Europeans, enslaved Africans and their descendants, free people of color, and Indigenous peoples in close contact, and it remained thinly settled, with French authority limited largely to the river and the coast.

Section 72.19

72.19 British Caribbean Expansion: Barbados, Jamaica, and the Lesser Antilles

English colonization in the Caribbean began in the 1620s with settlements on Saint Christopher (St. Kitts), shared with the French from 1625, and Barbados, settled in 1627. Early settlers grew tobacco, cotton, and indigo, using indentured servants. In the 1640s Barbados shifted toward sugar, supported substantially by English investment and merchant networks alongside Dutch and other Atlantic commercial connections. Historians dispute the older claim that Dutch finance predominated. The transition to large plantations and enslaved labor made Barbados one of the wealthiest English colonies by the mid seventeenth century. Other islands, including Nevis, Antigua, and Montserrat, followed, forming the Leeward Islands.

Jamaica was seized from Spain in 1655 by an expedition sent by Oliver Cromwell's government as part of the Western Design, which had failed to capture Hispaniola. The island, Spain's loosely held possession, became English and was formally recognized in the Treaty of Madrid of 1670. Jamaica developed first as a base for privateers and buccaneers, notably at Port Royal, and then as a large sugar producer in the later seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.

Colonial government in the English Caribbean included governors and councils, and assemblies controlled by planters, who held considerable influence in London through lobbying and absentee ownership. Planters were among the richest people in the empire. The strategic importance of the islands led governments to protect them with garrisons and, later, naval squadrons.

Barbados illustrates the dynamics of a settler colony converting to plantation agriculture. Early English settlers worked small farms with servants, and after the fall in tobacco prices the island's planters experimented with cotton, indigo, and sugar. Sugar's profitability led to consolidation of land into larger estates, a decline in the number of small farmers, and the departure of many whites, some for Jamaica, South Carolina, and Virginia. The Barbadian assembly, established in 1639, controlled local taxation, and planters were politically influential in England. The civil wars of the 1640s sent Royalist exiles, whose networks added to the island's elite. The planters' wealth made Barbados the model of the plantation colony for English observers.

Jamaica came to England by conquest. The Western Design, a plan authorized by Cromwell's government in 1654, aimed to seize Spanish territory in the Caribbean, but the expedition's attack on Santo Domingo failed, and the English commanders took Jamaica as a consolation in May 1655. The Spanish resisted for several years, and some formerly enslaved people who had fled the Spanish joined the interior as the first Maroon communities, which remained a political presence thereafter. Port Royal became a hub of privateers, and the government of Thomas Modyford encouraged raids on Spanish settlements, including Henry Morgan's attack on Panama in 1671, though the Treaty of Madrid in 1670 recognized English possession in return for peace. Sugar planting expanded in the late seventeenth century and especially in the early eighteenth century, when Jamaica became the empire's largest sugar producer, with large estates and a mostly absentee planter class.

Section 72.20

72.20 French Caribbean Expansion: Martinique, Guadeloupe, and Saint-Domingue

French colonization began on Saint-Christophe in 1625 and expanded to Martinique and Guadeloupe in 1635, under the Company of the American Islands. The company was bought out by proprietors in the 1640s, and the French West India Company, founded in 1664 by Colbert, took control and was dissolved in 1674, after which the islands came under direct royal administration. Sugar production grew, and with it the use of enslaved Africans.

Saint-Domingue, the western third of Hispaniola, began as a haven for French buccaneers and settlers on Tortuga and the north coast. Spain formally ceded the western part of the island to France in the Treaty of Ryswick of 1697. In the eighteenth century Saint-Domingue developed into the most productive plantation colony in the Atlantic world, with sugar and coffee exports of great value, though its explosive growth and the revolution that followed belong largely to later chapters. French governments treated the Caribbean colonies as commercially more valuable than Canada, a judgment confirmed in 1763.

Commercial links tied the islands to Bordeaux, Nantes, La Rochelle, and Marseille, and the sugar trade made these ports wealthy. The Caribbean colonies were administered by governors and intendants, as in Canada, but planters held significant local power.

The French islands had a more complex early history. The Company of the American Islands, formed in 1635, planted Martinique and Guadeloupe, and proprietors who acquired the islands in the 1640s sold land to planters. Early French planters also grew tobacco and then sugar, a transition in the 1640s that was helped by Dutch merchants who provided equipment and credit, though planters later resented Dutch commercial competition. Colbert's West India Company took control in 1664, and its failure to supply the colonies led the crown to buy it out in 1674 and to place the islands under royal governors and intendants. In practice French planters held substantial local power, through militia commands and councils.

Saint-Domingue began with buccaneers. French hunters and pirates settled on Tortuga and the north coast of Hispaniola, and in the 1660s and 1670s governors such as Bertrand d'Ogeron encouraged planting, the importing of women, and the creation of settled communities. After 1697 the colony gained recognized status, and in the eighteenth century it expanded into plains in the north and west, with sugar, indigo, and, from the 1720s, coffee, the latter spreading in the Caribbean after plants reached Martinique. Its society contained a small group of wealthy plantation owners, a larger group of less wealthy white colonists, free people of color who held property and sometimes enslaved others, and a large enslaved majority. The scale of its later growth, and the Revolution that followed in the 1790s, belong to later chapters, but by the 1750s it was already the most valuable French overseas colony.

Section 72.21

72.21 Indigenous Caribbean Peoples, Colonial Conquest, and Territorial Dispossession

The Lesser Antilles were home to Kalinago (Island Carib) and related peoples, who resisted colonization more effectively than Spanish accounts of the Greater Antilles might suggest. French and English settlers, who initially coexisted and traded with them, increasingly seized land, and wars occurred in the 1630s to 1650s. The Kalinago retreated to Dominica, St. Vincent, and other islands, where the 1660 agreement between French, English, and Kalinago representatives recognized those two islands as Kalinago territory. The agreement was not permanent: European settlement continued, and in the eighteenth century Dominica, St. Vincent, Saint Lucia, and Tobago were declared neutral in the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle of 1748, without Indigenous consent.

Indigenous peoples did not disappear, though their numbers declined from disease, warfare, and enslavement. On St. Vincent, a community formed from Kalinago and people of African descent, later known as the Garifuna, resisted both British and French claims into the late eighteenth century. The Caribbean pattern differed from North America: sugar economies demanded large amounts of land and labor, and Indigenous peoples were not the principal labor force by the time English and French planters arrived. Differences between English and French practices, the more extensive French marriage and trade relationships with Indigenous communities in some islands for example, were real but should not be exaggerated.

Kalinago resistance shaped the pace of colonization. In the 1630s and 1640s the Kalinago of Dominica, St. Vincent, Saint Lucia, and Grenada attacked French and English settlements, and the settlers retaliated with expeditions that destroyed villages and crops and took captives. Raymond Breton, a French missionary who lived with the Kalinago in Dominica, compiled dictionaries that preserve their language. The agreement of 1660 between French and English commissioners and Kalinago leaders reserved Dominica and St. Vincent for the Kalinago, though both Europeans soon tried to settle there, and in practice the arrangement reflected a stalemate in which neither side could control the islands.

On St. Vincent, Kalinago communities and people of African origin, who arrived by shipwreck and escape, mixed to create what later became the Garifuna, a population that combined language, kinship, and agriculture, and resisted European authority. The declaration of neutrality in 1748 recognized that no European power could settle Dominica, St. Vincent, Saint Lucia, and Tobago without provoking others, and in 1763 Britain acquired them and began selling land, which provoked the first Carib War from 1769, a development beyond this chapter. The island Indigenous peoples did not disappear but were drawn into a plantation order that depended on enslaved Africans, and their continued presence, language, and culture challenge accounts that treat the Caribbean as emptied by the Spanish conquest.

Section 72.22

72.22 Plantation Colonies, Commercial Wealth, and Colonial Administration

Sugar plantations required capital, land, and labor on a scale far larger than tobacco farms. Planters invested in mills, boiling houses, and enslaved workers, and relied on credit from merchants in London, Bristol, Bordeaux, and Nantes. Plantation production was organized in gangs under overseers, and the work was dangerous and brutal. Because mortality was high and birth rates low, planters kept buying new slaves, tying the Caribbean to the Atlantic slave trade, which Chapter 70 examines in detail. Sugar, rum, and molasses went to Europe and North America, and the islands imported food, timber, livestock, and manufactured goods.

Legislation reflected the dependence on enslaved labor. Barbados enacted a slave code in 1661, which influenced later English colonial laws. The French Crown’s 1685 ordinance, subsequently known as the Code Noir, originally regulated slavery in Martinique, Guadeloupe, and French Saint-Christophe; it was extended to French Saint-Domingue in 1687. Colonial assemblies passed laws on land, taxation, and labor, and governors and councils administered justice, but in practice planters had considerable power over the people they enslaved.

Caribbean planters, merchants, and officials influenced imperial policy. English sugar interests lobbied Parliament, and French planters were represented in the Council of Commerce and in trading company circles. The wealth of the islands was a central consideration in imperial diplomacy and warfare, as the peace negotiations of 1763 would show.

The economics of sugar demanded scale. A mid eighteenth century estate in Jamaica or Saint-Domingue often included hundreds of acres of cane, a mill driven by animals, wind, or water, a boiling house, a distillery, and quarters for well over one hundred enslaved workers. Investors needed credit to purchase land, equipment, and enslaved laborers, and plantations were often mortgaged to merchant houses in London, Bristol, Bordeaux, and Nantes, which gained leverage over planters. Many planters lived in Europe as absentee owners, relying on attorneys and managers, and formed lobbying groups, the "West India interest," that influenced Parliament and ministers. Sugar and its by-products supplied the European market for sweeteners, which expanded in the eighteenth century, as Chapter 73 examines, and the French and British government valued the duties that sugar generated.

It was a statement of legal intention, and historians have shown that enforcement varied: planters often disregarded provisions protecting enslaved people. The code does not tell historians what enslaved people thought, how they resisted, or how they built families and communities, which require other evidence such as court records, runaway notices, and archaeology.

Section 72.23

72.23 Caribbean Ports, Atlantic Shipping, and the Strategic Geography of Empire

Caribbean ports linked the islands to Europe, Africa, and North America. Port Royal in Jamaica, Bridgetown in Barbados, Fort-Royal in Martinique, Cap-Français in Saint-Domingue, and later Kingston became busy harbors, with warehouses, merchants, and shipyards. Port Royal was devastated by an earthquake in 1692. Ships crossed the Atlantic by the trade winds and returned by the northern route using the Gulf Stream, a pattern that made certain islands and straits strategic. Spanish Havana, the assembly point of the treasure fleets, remained the key to the region.

Commerce was regulated but not controlled. Customs officers, who tried to collect duties and enforce monopoly rules, faced smuggling, particularly with the Dutch at St. Eustatius and Curaçao, and with Spanish colonies hungry for goods. Privateers and buccaneers, who held commissions or operated without them, raided Spanish shipping and settlements, and officials sometimes tolerated them in wartime. The asiento, a license to supply enslaved Africans to Spanish America, granted to Britain in 1713, brought opportunities for legal and illegal commerce.

Naval strength became central. Fortifications protected harbors, and navies, French and British, convoyed merchant ships and fought in the Caribbean in each of the great wars. Islands changed hands in wars and returned in treaties, since both sides valued sugar territory highly. The geography of empire, in other words, was a geography of ports, sea lanes, and winds, and not merely of territory.

Ports, naval bases, and shipping routes determined who could hold the islands. Port Royal in Jamaica, a major entrepôt and privateering base before the earthquake of 1692, was replaced as a commercial center by Kingston, while the Royal Navy kept a dockyard at Port Royal. In the Leeward Islands, English Harbour in Antigua, with deep, sheltered water, was developed as a base in the eighteenth century. The French relied on Martinique's harbor at Fort-Royal and on Cap-Français in Saint-Domingue, and fortifications and garrisons were costly. Spanish Havana, with its shipyard and fortifications, remained the strategic prize, and the English assault on Cartagena in 1741, a failure under Admiral Edward Vernon, shows how difficult operations against fortified ports were and how disease destroyed expeditions.

Section 72.24

72.24 Chartered, Proprietary, and Royal Colonies in the English Empire

English colonial government developed unevenly, without a single design. Virginia began as a company colony and became royal in 1624. Massachusetts, Connecticut, and Rhode Island governed themselves under charters, with elected governors and assemblies. Maryland, Pennsylvania, and Carolina were proprietary. Jurisdictional variety was a defining feature, and it complicated metropolitan oversight.

After the Restoration of 1660, the crown created committees to supervise colonies, which in 1696 evolved into the Board of Trade and Plantations. In the 1680s James II, as king, attempted to impose greater control by merging the northern colonies into the Dominion of New England (1686 to 1689) under Edmund Andros, which removed assemblies. The Glorious Revolution of 1688 to 1689 produced uprisings in Boston, New York, and Maryland against the dominion's officials, and the new monarchs William and Mary restored assemblies. Massachusetts received a new charter in 1691 that made it a royal colony with a crown-appointed governor.

By the early eighteenth century, most colonies were royal or proprietary under crown supervision, and the number of chartered self-governing colonies had shrunk. Distance, communication delays, and local resistance meant that the crown's authority was always negotiated. Officials in London relied on governors, customs officers, and naval officers to implement policy.

The variety of English colonial charters reflected improvisation. Virginia's transformation into a royal colony after the dissolution of the company in 1624 gave it a governor, council, and assembly, and Massachusetts's company charter, which created a government with elected officials, drew the attention of the crown, which in the 1630s considered revoking it, an effort cut short by the English civil wars. Maryland's palatine powers allowed the proprietor to appoint officials, while Pennsylvania's Frame of Government created a council and assembly. Rhode Island and Connecticut received charters from Charles II in 1663 and 1662 that confirmed local election of governors and remained effectively self governing until the Revolution. The Carolinas, after the 1720s, and Georgia, in 1752, became royal colonies, while Maryland and Pennsylvania stayed in the hands of proprietary families.

After 1660 the crown attempted greater coordination. Councils for trade and plantations, followed by the Lords of Trade in 1675, collected information and advised on policy, and in 1684 the court of Chancery vacated the charter of Massachusetts Bay. James II's Dominion of New England, headed by Edmund Andros, combined Massachusetts, Plymouth, Connecticut, Rhode Island, New York, and New Jersey into a single administration without assemblies, and imposed new taxes and land rules. News of the Glorious Revolution in England sparked uprisings in Boston, where Andros was arrested in April 1689, in New York under Jacob Leisler, and in Maryland, and the new monarchs restored most existing institutions. The Board of Trade, established in 1696, and the vice-admiralty courts created the same year to enforce trade laws, show the crown's growing interest in oversight. But the board was an advisory body without executive power, and appointments of governors, usually by patronage, shaped the quality of colonial administration.

Section 72.25

72.25 Colonial Assemblies, Law, and British Imperial Authority

Representative assemblies were a defining feature of English colonial government. The Virginia House of Burgesses (1619) was an early model, and by the eighteenth century every mainland colony had an elected lower house, a governor, and a council. The assemblies controlled taxation and often the salaries of governors, giving them leverage. Property qualifications limited the franchise to a minority, though that minority was larger than in England, and the assemblies' claims to authority rested on English constitutional traditions and the practical need to govern local affairs.

Conflicts arose over authority. Governors, appointed by the crown or proprietors, held veto powers and could dissolve assemblies, and the Privy Council could disallow colonial laws. Assemblies resisted, and over the first half of the eighteenth century colonial legislatures gained practical power. Parliament's role was evolving. After the Glorious Revolution it asserted sovereignty over the empire, passing laws regulating trade, such as the Wool Act of 1699, the Molasses Act of 1733, and the Iron Act of 1750, but it exercised little direct taxation of the colonies before 1763. The imperial constitution was unsettled, and its tensions would become central after the Seven Years' War, a subject for Chapter 87.

Colonial law followed English common law with local adaptation, and colonial courts handled disputes over land, debt, and morals. Legal culture contributed to a sense of rights that colonists later invoked.

Assemblies became the main forum for colonial politics. They controlled taxation, which gave them influence over the governor's salary, military spending, and the colony's finances. The practical working of colonial government rested on bargaining. Governors, who held formal authority to summon, prorogue, and dissolve assemblies and to veto legislation, often depended on assemblies for revenue, so they made concessions. Assemblies also used the "power of the purse" to gain control of appointments, particularly of treasurers, and over time they expanded their rights in matters such as electoral procedure and judicial structure. Colonial agents, representing assemblies in London, lobbied the Board of Trade and Parliament, a pattern that shows the interaction of local and imperial politics.

The franchise varied. In most colonies men who owned a modest amount of property could vote, which meant a high proportion of adult white men compared with Britain, though less than universal, and women, enslaved people, free Black men in many places, Indigenous people, and in some cases Catholics and Jews were excluded. Elections were often public, with oral voting, and local elites usually dominated office. Parliament legislated for the empire through trade acts, such as the Wool Act of 1699, which limited exports of colonial woolens, the Hat Act of 1732, and the Iron Act of 1750, which restricted certain manufactures, and the Currency Act of 1751 limiting paper money in New England. These measures were irritants but rarely enforced to the letter, and Parliament did not attempt to tax the colonies directly for revenue before 1764. The legal basis of Parliament's authority over the colonies, however, remained undefined, a question that became central after the Seven Years' War.

Section 72.26

72.26 French Colonial Government, Royal Administration, and the Intendant System

In 1663 Louis XIV's government, guided by Colbert, took New France from the Company of One Hundred Associates and placed it under royal administration. The structure resembled provincial government in France: a governor, responsible for military affairs and relations with Indigenous peoples; an intendant, responsible for justice, finance, and civil administration; and the Sovereign Council, a body that registered ordinances and acted as a court of appeal. The bishop, François de Laval, held significant influence as head of the colonial church. Jean Talon, the first effective intendant (1665 to 1668 and 1670 to 1672), promoted population growth, agriculture, and local industry.

Royal policy encouraged settlement. The crown sent regiments, notably the Carignan-Salières regiment in 1665, which fought the Haudenosaunee and left many soldiers as settlers. It sponsored the passage of about 800 young women, the filles du roi, between 1663 and 1673 to marry settlers, and it encouraged early marriage and large families. Land was granted through the seigneurial system, in which seigneurs held land from the crown and granted plots to habitants who paid fixed dues and owed limited services. Law followed the Custom of Paris.

The system was more centralized in principle than the English one but was constrained in practice by distance, the importance of Indigenous alliances, and the autonomy of fur traders. There was no elected assembly, though local militia captains and parish structures gave habitants some role. Historians stress that the frequently repeated contrast between a "free" English and an "authoritarian" French empire oversimplifies: company privileges persisted after 1663, and the governor and intendant often quarreled, which gave colonists room to maneuver.

The reform of 1663 followed from the crisis of the Company of One Hundred Associates and from the assertion of royal power after Louis XIV took personal control in 1661. In 1665 the crown sent the Marquis de Tracy as lieutenant general with a force of soldiers, and the governor Daniel de Rémy de Courcelles and the first intendant Jean Talon arrived to implement a program of settlement, defense, and economic development. The Sovereign Council, which sat at Québec and which included the governor, the bishop, the intendant, and a small group of appointed councillors, registered ordinances, judged civil and criminal cases, and regulated commerce. In 1703 it became the Superior Council, with more members. Below the council, lower courts and the militia, organized by parish, linked local communities to the government.

Authority was divided in ways that produced conflict. The governor commanded troops and handled Indigenous relations, the intendant oversaw justice and finance, and the bishop led the church, so disputes among them over precedence, trade, and the sale of alcohol to Indigenous people were frequent. Count Frontenac, governor in the 1670s and again from 1689, quarreled with his intendants and with clergy. The crown, which preferred divided authority as a check, often sided with whichever official seemed to serve royal interests best. From the early eighteenth century the Ministry of the Marine managed colonial affairs, and the intendant's finances relied on grants from France, supplemented by the card money first issued in 1685 to pay soldiers, an improvisation that shows how the colony's economy lacked coin. The Domaine d'Occident, a tax farm created in 1674, collected duties on beaver and other goods, reflecting the crown's interest in revenue. French officials also tried to attract settlers and enforce colonial rules through ordinances, but Louis XIV and his successors often restricted emigration, fearing depopulation, and so the population policy that Talon championed was never fully realized.

Section 72.27

72.27 Mercantilism, the Navigation Acts, and the French Colonial Commercial System

Mercantilism, the dominant economic thinking of the period, held that national wealth depended on exports exceeding imports, the accumulation of bullion, and the encouragement of domestic shipping and manufacturing. Colonies, in this view, supplied raw materials and bought manufactured goods, enriching the metropolis. The theory was never a single doctrine, and officials applied it pragmatically.

England's Navigation Ordinance of 1651, passed by the Commonwealth and aimed at Dutch carriers, was followed by the Navigation Act of 1660 and the Staple Act of 1663, and subsequent legislation. The core principles required that colonial trade be carried in English (later British) ships with predominantly English crews, that certain enumerated goods, including sugar, tobacco, cotton, and indigo, be shipped only to England or other English colonies, and that European goods bound for the colonies pass through England. The Plantation Duty Act of 1673 imposed duties on enumerated goods moved between colonies, and further acts followed. The acts benefited English shipping and merchants and were broadly accepted by colonists in the seventeenth century, because they also protected colonial products, such as tobacco, in the English market.

France developed its own system. Colbert promoted state supported manufacturing, trading companies, and tariff policy, and sought to ensure that colonial trade benefited French merchants. The principle later called the exclusif required that colonies trade only with the metropole, and French colonial products were to be shipped to France and manufactured goods supplied from France. Enforcement was uneven, and French planters complained of high prices and shipping shortages.

The Navigation Acts evolved by accumulation. The ordinance of 1651, aimed at Dutch carriers who dominated the colonial trade, contributed to the outbreak of the First Anglo-Dutch War the next year. The 1660 act added the enumerated list, the Staple Act of 1663 required that European goods be shipped to the colonies through England, and the Plantation Duty Act of 1673 placed duties on enumerated goods shipped between colonies, to prevent colonists from avoiding the English market. A new act in 1696 strengthened enforcement by requiring colonial governors to take oaths, establishing vice-admiralty courts, and tightening rules on shipping documents. The effect on shipping was real: the requirement that vessels be English or colonial built encouraged colonial shipbuilding, so that by the mid eighteenth century a large share of English-flagged shipping was built in New England. Chesapeake tobacco re-exported to Europe through England produced customs revenue and employment for English merchants. But the acts also raised prices for colonial consumers and limited markets for sugar, and planters disliked limitations on trade with the French and Dutch.

French policy under Colbert had a different emphasis. Colbert sought to increase manufacturing in France, protect domestic industry with tariffs, build a navy, and found monopoly companies, in an effort to reduce reliance on Dutch intermediaries. Colonial policy sought to ensure that colonial products flowed to France and that French goods supplied colonies, and the principle, commonly called the exclusif, was reaffirmed in the letters patent of 1717, which opened trade with the colonies to a designated list of French ports. French sugar refiners in France, protected by tariffs, resisted colonial refining, and the crown banned the import of colonial rum into France to protect brandy, which meant French planters sold molasses cheaply to New England. Such details show that a mercantilist regime produced contradictory effects and created opportunities for the trade it sought to restrict.

Section 72.28

72.28 Merchants, Credit, Shipping, Smuggling, and Colonial Commerce

Atlantic commerce depended on merchants and credit. In England, London dominated, with Bristol, Liverpool, and later Glasgow, whose "tobacco lords" prospered after the Union of 1707. In France, Bordeaux, Nantes, La Rochelle, Le Havre, and Saint-Malo handled colonial trade. Merchants advanced credit to planters and farmers, shipped goods on consignment, and used bills of exchange to settle accounts across the ocean. Insurance, developed in London coffeehouses and in Amsterdam, spread the risks of shipwreck and capture.

Commerce moved in patterns that connected regions. New England exported fish, timber, and provisions to the Caribbean in return for molasses, which was distilled into rum, and sometimes to Africa for the slave trade. The Chesapeake exported tobacco to Britain and re-exports reached Europe. French Canada exported furs, with Caribbean and fishing links. These trades overlapped and made the empires interdependent.

The limits of mercantilist enforcement were clear. Customs officers were few and often corrupt, and colonial merchants traded with Dutch, French, and Spanish partners when profitable. The Molasses Act of 1733, which imposed high duties on foreign molasses imported to British North America, was widely evaded. The phrase "salutary neglect," attributed to Edmund Burke in 1775, is a later characterization of a policy of loose enforcement, and historians debate whether it describes deliberate policy or administrative weakness. What is clear is that regulation and practice diverged.

The merchants who operated the system formed networks that crossed boundaries. London, which handled the greater part of English colonial trade, was home to families with colonial branches, including Quaker, Huguenot, and Jewish merchants, and from the early eighteenth century Glasgow merchants built a large business buying tobacco from Chesapeake planters, supplying them with goods on credit through stores in the backcountry. The merchant acting as a factor for planters accepted consignments, sold tobacco or sugar in London, and supplied clothing, tools, and furniture. This credit structure left many planters indebted, and some colonial laws favored debtors in ways that worried merchants. Because British colonies lacked sufficient coin, merchants used bills of exchange, and colonial governments issued paper currency, which merchants viewed with suspicion. In France, the great ports of Bordeaux, Nantes, La Rochelle, and Le Havre developed complex businesses linking colonial produce, the slave trade, and the export of French wine, textiles, and provisions.

Commercial law and practice also shaped enforcement. Customs officers, who were often poorly paid, relied on informers and cooperation from local officials, and juries in colonial courts often declined to convict smugglers. In the Caribbean, the Dutch free ports supplied slaves and goods at lower prices than French companies, and governors tolerated them when the legal channels failed. The Molasses Act of 1733 did not change the volume of the molasses trade much because enforcement officers were bribed or avoided, and a trade with the French islands became a routine of New England commerce, even in wartime, which prompted complaints from British officials that colonists were supplying the enemy. The emphasis on enforcement failure should not obscure that mercantilist rules often served the interests of the merchants and shipowners they protected, and that many colonists accepted them. The history of the trade is thus one of negotiated compliance.

Section 72.29

72.29 Land, Religion, Population, and the Social Foundations of Colonial Rule

Land systems shaped society. In English colonies, land grants and purchases created individual freeholds, while in New France seigneuries created layered dues, and in the Caribbean plantations concentrated land in a few hands. Indigenous peoples had their own systems of land use and authority, which colonial law often failed to recognize or deliberately overrode. Disputes over deeds, boundaries, and jurisdiction recur throughout the period.

Religion organized communities. The Church of England was established in Virginia and the southern colonies, Congregationalism in most of New England, and a mixture of denominations, Quaker, Lutheran, Reformed, Baptist, and Presbyterian, characterized the Middle Colonies. Catholic communities existed in Maryland. In New France the Catholic Church was dominant, and Protestants were generally excluded after 1627, though some Huguenots entered. The Great Awakening of the 1730s and 1740s, a transatlantic revival, spread across the British colonies and affected religious life.

Population and governance varied. English colonies had large populations of free families and a significant minority of enslaved people, with most free men owning land. New France had a smaller population concentrated in a few settlements, with a more hierarchical structure, and Louisiana and the Caribbean had enslaved majorities in plantation zones. Colonial rule rested on local elites, churches, militias, and Indigenous alliances, not only on metropolitan authority. Indigenous sovereignty remained a fact in much of the continent, and imperial officials were aware that disputes over it could cause war.

Land and settlement structures varied sharply. New England towns received land grants as a whole, and the town allocated plots to families according to rank, a practice that favored compact villages and communal oversight. In the Chesapeake, headrights and patents produced dispersed plantations on rivers. In Canada the seigneurial system made seigneurs the intermediaries between the crown and the habitants, who paid fixed annual dues and sometimes owed labor service, and who were required to grind grain at the seigneur's mill. The system's obligations were light compared with European feudal burdens, and habitants enjoyed considerable independence, though some seigneurs also lived modestly. In the Caribbean, large grants favored substantial planters. Indigenous understanding of land involved use rights, kinship, and spiritual connection, and a deed of sale, which Europeans considered a permanent transfer, could be understood as an agreement for shared access, so land purchases produced misunderstandings that sometimes erupted into conflict.

Section 72.30

72.30 The Origins of British-French Colonial Rivalry

Rivalry developed from overlapping claims and commercial interests. English and French fishermen competed for the Newfoundland cod banks. The fur trade placed French traders in competition with English and Dutch traders at Albany and, after the founding of the Hudson's Bay Company in 1670, with English traders on Hudson Bay, a venture inspired by the French Canadian traders Radisson and Groseilliers. Borderlands such as Acadia, the Great Lakes, and the Ohio Valley were claimed by both sides. Competition in the Caribbean over sugar territories and trade routes added further friction.

The colonies were connected to European politics. After 1689, wars in Europe between Louis XIV's France and coalitions that included England and the Dutch Republic automatically spilled into the colonies. Colonial leaders, however, also had local motives: New England merchants sought fishing grounds and an end to Wabanaki raids, French officials sought to protect the fur trade and allies, and Indigenous nations sought to use European rivalry to their advantage.

Indigenous alliance networks structured the conflict. The French drew on alliances with Anishinaabe, Wabanaki, and mission communities, while the English maintained the Covenant Chain with the Haudenosaunee and traded with southern nations such as the Cherokee and Chickasaw. These networks were not fixed, and the nations in them acted on their own interests.

Seen from the colonies, rivalry had different roots than in European courts. The English and French competed for fisheries, but also for the fur trade of Hudson Bay, which the Hudson's Bay Company claimed through its charter of 1670 and which French Canadian traders had opened. In the 1680s French expeditions from Canada attacked English posts on the bay, and the control of its forts changed hands several times in the following decades, until Utrecht gave them to Britain. Along the northeastern frontier, Wabanaki communities in Maine and Acadia, with French missionary and trade ties, resisted New England expansion, and raids on isolated settlements and the English response, which included scalp bounties, set a pattern of frontier violence. French authorities in turn saw English expansion as a threat to their Indigenous alliances and to the St. Lawrence trade.

The practice of war shaped the rivalry. Colonial warfare in northeastern America involved the "petite guerre," small raiding parties that struck villages, burned crops, and took prisoners, with Indigenous allies playing central roles, because Indigenous warriors knew the terrain and the techniques. Captives were adopted, held for ransom, or enslaved, and such captures created bonds that complicated later relations, as in the case of New England children raised in Catholic Indigenous communities. The geography of the frontier, with rivers and portages connecting Canada to the English settlements through the Lake Champlain and Hudson route, and the Mohawk valley, made some places repeated targets. Outposts such as Fort Oswego, built by New York in 1727 on Lake Ontario to attract western traders, and the French response of strengthening Niagara, show that commercial competition and military planning were interwoven. Mercantilist thinking treated trade as a zero sum contest, which gave governments reasons to fight for control of fisheries and fur routes.

Section 72.31

72.31 King William's War and Queen Anne's War in the Atlantic World

King William's War (1689 to 1697), the colonial dimension of the Nine Years' War, saw raids on frontier settlements. French and Indigenous forces attacked Schenectady in 1690, and English colonial forces under William Phips captured Port-Royal in 1690 but failed to take Québec. The war ended with the Treaty of Ryswick in 1697, which restored most territory.

Queen Anne's War (1702 to 1713), part of the War of the Spanish Succession (see Chapter 80), saw a new round of raids, including the French and Indigenous attack on Deerfield, Massachusetts, in 1704, in which captives were taken to Canada. Carolina colonists and their Indigenous allies attacked Spanish Florida, burning St. Augustine's town in 1702 and raiding Apalachee communities in 1704. British forces captured Port-Royal in 1710, and an attempt to take Québec in 1711 failed when a fleet was wrecked. Fighting in the Caribbean included attacks on St. Kitts. In these wars, frontier warfare was characterized by raids on civilians, captive taking, and destruction of crops, and Indigenous allies pursued their own aims.

The wars tested colonial resources. Colonial legislatures voted taxes and raised militia, and New France mobilized a large proportion of its male population. Neither side could achieve a decisive victory in North America before 1713, and outcomes were determined largely at the negotiating table in Europe.

King William's War began in 1689 and shaped a pattern of terror. In early 1690, French and Indigenous war parties attacked Schenectady, Salmon Falls, and Casco Bay, and a New England expedition under William Phips took Port-Royal in May 1690, but his attempt to capture Québec in October failed against Count Frontenac's defense. The Haudenosaunee, who suffered heavily in the French campaigns of the 1690s, were disillusioned with English support and moved toward peace. In the north Pierre Le Moyne d'Iberville led a campaign that captured the English posts on Hudson Bay and in 1697 defeated English ships, and after the Treaty of Ryswick returned things largely to the previous status.

Queen Anne's War extended the geography. The attack on Deerfield on 29 February 1704, in which French and Indigenous forces killed dozens and marched more than a hundred captives to Canada, including the minister's daughter Eunice Williams, who later stayed with her Kahnawake family, became part of New England's memory of the war. In the south, James Moore of Carolina led raids on Spanish missions in 1704, destroying Apalachee communities and enslaving many of their people, and the English and Spanish fought for control of the Gulf. In the Caribbean the French and Spanish captured Nevis and part of St. Kitts, and the British navy contested control. In 1710 a British and colonial force took Port-Royal, renaming it Annapolis Royal, but a major expedition against Québec in 1711 under Hovenden Walker ended when ships ran aground in the St. Lawrence. The war's decisive results came from the diplomacy of Utrecht, after European armies had shifted the balance in Europe, as examined in Chapter 80.

Section 72.32

72.32 The Treaty of Utrecht, 1713, and the Reorganization of Atlantic Claims

The Treaty of Utrecht reshaped the Atlantic balance. France ceded to Britain its claims to Hudson Bay, Newfoundland (while retaining fishing rights on part of the coast), peninsular Acadia (Nova Scotia), and the island of St. Kitts, and kept Île Royale and Île Saint-Jean. Spain ceded Gibraltar and Minorca to Britain and granted the British South Sea Company the asiento, the monopoly on supplying enslaved Africans to Spanish America for thirty years, along with permission for one annual trading ship, a concession that brought legal and illegal trade into the Spanish Caribbean.

The treaty also included a clause in which France recognized the Haudenosaunee as subject to British authority. The Haudenosaunee did not accept this characterization, and the article illustrates how Europeans negotiated over Indigenous peoples without their participation. It also shows the persistent gap between treaty language and political reality.

Strategically, Utrecht gave Britain footholds around French Canada but left New France intact. France responded by building Louisbourg and by strengthening the interior network. Britain, with Nova Scotia, had to govern an Acadian population that had not chosen British rule. The settlement created frontiers that would become sites of conflict.

The treaty settled questions with ambiguous language. France gave up Acadia "with its ancient boundaries," a formula that left uncertain whether this meant the peninsula alone or also the territory north of the Bay of Fundy, and the disputed boundary became a source of friction until 1750s commissions failed. France retained fishing rights on a stretch of the Newfoundland coast, the French Shore, which prevented British settlement there and became a long term irritant. The cession of Placentia and French withdrawal from Newfoundland gave Britain a base in the fishery. France kept Île Royale and began to fortify it, so the loss of Acadia was partly offset by Louisbourg.

For Britain the commercial concessions mattered. The asiento and annual ship gave the South Sea Company a share of the Spanish American trade, and the acquisition of Gibraltar and Minorca added Mediterranean bases that later proved valuable. Article 15 provided that France recognized the Five Nations as under British dominion, but the Haudenosaunee did not consider themselves British subjects and told French and British officials so, and Indigenous peoples more generally rejected treaty clauses that transferred their land or allegiance. After 1713 a period of Anglo-French cooperation in Europe, under the regency of Philippe d'Orléans and the ministry of Robert Walpole, held the peace, and meanwhile colonial populations and commerce grew. The Acadians, now under British rule, remained largely neutral, and the Mi'kmaq continued to assert sovereignty, so Utrecht set up conditions for later conflict without resolving them.

Section 72.33

72.33 King George's War, Louisbourg, and the Intensification of Colonial Competition

King George's War (1744 to 1748), the North American extension of the War of the Austrian Succession, began with a French attack on a British post in Nova Scotia. In June 1745 a force of New England militia under William Pepperrell, supported by a Royal Navy squadron, besieged and captured Louisbourg, a victory that astonished contemporaries, since the fortress was considered strong. Raids on frontier communities, such as the French and Indigenous attack on Saratoga in 1745, continued.

The peace of Aix-la-Chapelle in 1748 returned Louisbourg to France, in exchange for the return of Madras in India, which a French force had captured in 1746. New Englanders, who had borne the cost and the casualties, felt betrayed. The exchange shows the global linkages of the period: Atlantic and Indian positions were traded against each other at the peace table.

The war left the balance unresolved. Britain built the naval base of Halifax in Nova Scotia in 1749 to counter Louisbourg and brought settlers, which provoked Mi'kmaq resistance and further tension with Acadians. France reinforced its network of forts. Both sides understood that the next war would determine the continent.

King George's War began as part of the European War of the Austrian Succession and entered the colonies in 1744, when a French force from Louisbourg captured the British post at Canso, and then besieged Annapolis Royal. Governor William Shirley of Massachusetts proposed an attack on Louisbourg, and the colonies raised about four thousand volunteers under William Pepperrell, with a Royal Navy squadron under Commodore Peter Warren. The siege lasted from April to June 1745, and the fortress surrendered on 17 June after supply failures and bombardment. The victory brought celebration across the colonies and was followed by a failed French relief expedition under the duc d'Anville in 1746, which lost many men to disease and storms. In the Hudson valley, French and Indigenous raiders destroyed Saratoga in November 1745, and New York built defenses.

The return of Louisbourg in 1748, following the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle, in exchange for Madras, angered New Englanders who had paid heavily, though Parliament reimbursed the colonies for part of their costs. The treaty restored prewar conditions and left boundaries unsettled. Britain founded Halifax in 1749, settled with military families and colonists, partly to counter Louisbourg, and French forces built forts at Beauséjour and on the St. John River to defend the Acadian isthmus. The Mi'kmaq, who viewed Halifax as an intrusion, resumed raiding. In the interior the war had increased the Indigenous understanding that the two empires could be played against each other, and that trade and presents depended on rivalry. The conflict did not resolve the issues of Acadia, Ohio, or the balance of power, and both governments prepared for the next war.

Section 72.34

72.34 The Ohio Valley Crisis and the Beginning of the French and Indian War, 1754 to 1756

The Ohio Valley, between the Appalachians and the Mississippi, became the flashpoint. Virginian land speculators formed the Ohio Company in 1747 to settle the region, and Pennsylvania traders had built relationships with Shawnee, Lenape, Mingo, and Miami communities. France, which wished to maintain a link between Canada and Louisiana, sent an expedition under Pierre-Joseph Céloron in 1749 to assert claims, and later built forts including Fort Duquesne at the forks of the Ohio, the site of present-day Pittsburgh.

In 1754 Virginia sent a force under the young George Washington. In May his men and Seneca allies under Tanaghrisson attacked a French party at Jumonville Glen, killing the French commander Jumonville, and in July Washington surrendered Fort Necessity to a larger French and Indigenous force. The Albany Congress of 1754 attempted to unify colonial defense and renew the Covenant Chain, but its plans were rejected by colonial governments. In 1755 Major General Edward Braddock led a British and colonial army toward Fort Duquesne and was defeated at the Monongahela in July, with Braddock killed.

Braddock's defeat left the frontier open. Many Ohio Indigenous nations, who had been uncertain, allied with France and attacked settlements in Pennsylvania and Virginia, motivated by fear of British expansion and by French military success. In 1755 British authorities in Nova Scotia began deporting Acadians, a policy that continued through the war and scattered thousands. Britain declared war on France formally in May 1756. The French and Indian War, which began in North America in 1754, thus merged into the wider Seven Years' War.

The Ohio country had become a center of Indigenous migration. Shawnee, Lenape, and Mingo communities, displaced by British expansion in the east and by Haudenosaunee claims, settled in towns along the Ohio River, such as Logstown, where they traded with Pennsylvania merchants like George Croghan. They considered themselves independent of the Haudenosaunee, though the Confederacy claimed overlordship, and they sought trade without domination. When the Miami leader known to the English as Old Briton moved his town of Pickawillany into trade with the English, French and allied forces under Charles Langlade destroyed it in 1752, which showed France's willingness to enforce its claims. The new governor Marquis Duquesne ordered a chain of forts south from Lake Erie in 1753, and Virginia, whose governor Robert Dinwiddie and Ohio Company investors had interests, sent the young Washington with a message demanding French withdrawal.

The incidents of 1754 started the war. When Washington's party, with the Seneca leader Tanaghrisson, attacked Jumonville's detachment on 28 May, Jumonville was killed, and the French regarded this as an assassination of an envoy, while Washington and Tanaghrisson interpreted the party as hostile. French forces under Louis Coulon de Villiers forced Washington to surrender Fort Necessity on 3 July, and Washington signed a capitulation that used language implying responsibility for Jumonville's death. At the Albany Congress in June and July, delegates from several colonies met Haudenosaunee leaders, who complained about land fraud and colonial neglect, and discussed a plan of union drafted by Benjamin Franklin that no colonial assembly accepted. In Britain the cabinet decided to send regular troops, and Braddock's march in 1755 was the first major commitment. In July 1755 his column was ambushed near the Monongahela by French, Canadian, and Indigenous forces, and he was mortally wounded.

The consequences were immediate. Shawnee and Lenape warriors, who judged the British unable to defend them, attacked the Pennsylvania and Virginia frontier, killing and capturing hundreds. In September 1755 William Johnson, with Mohawk allies, defeated a French force under Baron Dieskau at Lake George. In Nova Scotia, Governor Charles Lawrence and the council, after the capture of Fort Beauséjour in June 1755, ordered the removal of Acadians who refused an unconditional oath, and thousands were forcibly deported that autumn to the colonies and later to Britain and France, with approximately six thousand removed from Nova Scotia during 1755 and more than ten thousand Acadians deported during the wider expulsion period, with totals varying by geography and timeframe. Families were separated, villages burned, and many died of disease. The deportation, termed by some historians an ethnic cleansing, and by others a military measure with brutal consequences, is a case in which terminology is itself contested.

Section 72.35

72.35 The Seven Years' War and the Treaty of Paris, 1763

The Seven Years' War, fought from 1756 to 1763, involved Britain and Prussia against France, Austria, Russia, and others, with fighting in Europe, North America, the Caribbean, West Africa, India, and the Philippines. Early British setbacks, including the loss of Minorca in 1756, gave way to success after William Pitt the Elder took leadership of the war effort in 1757. Pitt directed resources to naval power and colonial operations, subsidizing Prussia to occupy French armies in Europe.

In North America British forces captured Louisbourg in 1758 and Fort Duquesne late that year, helped by the Treaty of Easton of 1758, in which Pennsylvania and Ohio Indigenous nations were persuaded to withdraw support from France. In September 1759 General James Wolfe's army defeated the Marquis de Montcalm's forces on the Plains of Abraham outside Québec, and both commanders died. Montréal capitulated in September 1760. British naval victories at Lagos and Quiberon Bay in 1759 limited French ability to reinforce, and British forces captured Guadeloupe in 1759, Martinique in 1762, and Havana in 1762, while Manila fell in the same year. Spain, which entered the war on France's side in 1762, lost both.

The Treaty of Paris, signed on 10 February 1763, redrew the colonial map. France ceded Canada and the territory east of the Mississippi to Britain, with the exception of New Orleans, and ceded Louisiana west of the river to Spain by a separate arrangement. Britain received Florida from Spain, which got Havana back. France kept Guadeloupe, Martinique, and Saint Lucia, and fishing rights near Newfoundland with the islands of Saint-Pierre and Miquelon, while Britain gained Grenada, Dominica, St. Vincent, and Tobago, and Senegal in West Africa. In India, France recovered its trading posts but agreed to restrictions on fortification. The war's costs were great: British national debt rose from roughly 75 million pounds to over 130 million pounds, according to standard estimates, creating pressure for new revenue.

The settlement ignored Indigenous nations. In May 1763 Odawa leader Pontiac and a coalition of nations attacked British posts in the Great Lakes and Ohio Valley, in what is called Pontiac's War, protesting British policies, including the reduction of gifts and the occupation of former French forts. In October 1763 the British government issued the Royal Proclamation, which reserved lands west of the Appalachian watershed for Indigenous nations and required that land purchases go through the crown. Colonists, who wanted western land, resented it.

The global war reflected shifts in European diplomacy. The Diplomatic Revolution of 1756 produced the alliance of France and Austria and the Anglo-Prussian Convention of Westminster, and Britain declared war on France in May 1756, shortly after France's attack on Minorca. France's early victories in North America, under Montcalm, who took Oswego in August 1756 and Fort William Henry in August 1757, were followed by atrocities when Indigenous allies attacked the surrendering garrison, which the French commander could not prevent, and led to a reputation that affected later British treatment of French and Indigenous forces. French victory at Ticonderoga in July 1758 did not prevent the fall of Louisbourg that month and Fort Frontenac in August. The French position collapsed through the combination of British command of the sea, which prevented reinforcements from reaching Canada, and the withdrawal of Indigenous allies who judged French power waning. In October 1758 the Treaty of Easton, negotiated by Pennsylvania officials with Ohio Indigenous nations, promised no settlement west of the Alleghenies and removed many from the French side, helping the British to take Fort Duquesne in November 1758.

The British ministry under Pitt gave priority to naval and colonial operations while subsidizing Prussia. Quebec was besieged for much of the summer of 1759, and after Wolfe's forces ascended to the Plains of Abraham on 13 September and won a short battle, the city surrendered on 18 September. The French recovered with a victory at Sainte-Foy in April 1760, but when British forces converged on Montréal, Governor Vaudreuil capitulated on 8 September 1760. The Haudenosaunee, who had remained largely neutral, and Canadian Indigenous allies, some of whom made separate arrangements with Johnson, were given assurances, and the terms preserved the property and religious freedom of the Canadiens. Beyond North America, Britain took Gorée and Senegal in 1758, defeated the French fleet at Quiberon Bay in November 1759, and captured Guadeloupe in 1759 and Martinique in 1762. The French and Spanish alliance, formed by the Family Compact of 1761 and Spain's entry in January 1762, brought British expeditions to capture Havana in August and Manila in October 1762. Meanwhile, secret negotiations at Fontainebleau in November 1762 transferred Louisiana west of the Mississippi to Spain, to compensate for its losses.

The consequences differed by region. In North America, Britain gained Canada and a continent east of the Mississippi, but faced the cost of garrisoning and administering it, and the Indigenous response was swift. In May 1763 Odawa, Ojibwe, Potawatomi, Wendat, Shawnee, Lenape, Seneca, and others, inspired in part by the prophet Neolin and led by Pontiac and other leaders, attacked British forts, taking most and besieging Detroit and Fort Pitt, and the conflict continued until 1764 to 1766. British officials, including Henry Bouquet, used coercion and diplomacy, and the war showed that Britain could not simply inherit French alliances. In December 1763, a Pennsylvania vigilante group, the Paxton Boys, massacred Indigenous people at Conestoga, revealing the hatred that frontier warfare engendered among settlers. In the Caribbean, the settlement enlarged British sugar territories while returning the most productive French islands. In India it ended French political ambition. In Europe, the Peace of Hubertusburg in February 1763 restored the prewar situation between Prussia and Austria. In Africa, Britain held Senegal while France retained Gorée. For Britain, the war's costs doubled the national debt, and ministers sought new sources of revenue and a standing army in America, the beginning of the dispute examined in Chapter 87.

Historical Debate

Did the Seven Years' War Establish British Global Predominance, or Create New Vulnerabilities?

The question: Britain emerged in 1763 with the largest empire in the Atlantic world, yet within twenty years it lost its thirteen mainland colonies. Historians ask whether the victory laid the foundations of imperial dominance or sowed the seeds of crisis.

Interpretation A: Victory consolidated British power: Some historians stress Britain's naval superiority, its financial system, its gains in Canada, the Caribbean, India, and West Africa, and the removal of French military power from North America. In this view, the war made Britain the leading commercial and naval power, and the loss of the American colonies was a separate and later development.
Interpretation B: Victory created strains: Others argue that the war left Britain with heavy debt, a larger frontier to defend, new Catholic and Indigenous populations to govern, and colonists who no longer needed British protection against France. Attempts to raise revenue and regulate the frontier after 1763 provoked resistance, so the victory contained the seeds of crisis. They also note that France retained commercial strength and sought revenge.

Evidence: The debate draws on fiscal records, parliamentary debates, colonial petitions, Indigenous diplomacy, and naval and commercial statistics.

Section 72.36

72.36 The English and French East India Companies

The English East India Company received its charter from Elizabeth I on 31 December 1600. After early voyages to Southeast Asia, where it was pushed out by the Dutch, it concentrated on India. It established a factory at Surat in 1608 to 1612 with the permission of the Mughal Emperor Jahangir, whom the English envoy Thomas Roe visited. The Company built fortified settlements: Madras (Fort St George) in 1639 to 1640, Bombay, acquired by the crown from Portugal as part of the dowry of Catherine of Braganza in 1661 and transferred to the Company in 1668, and the Company settlement at Sutanuti associated with Job Charnock in 1690, which contributed to the later growth of Calcutta (Kolkata) from several preexisting Bengali settlements, with Fort William built by about 1700. In 1709 rival English companies merged into the United Company.

The French East India Company was founded by Colbert in 1664. Its early years were difficult, but the settlement at Pondicherry, acquired in 1674 under François Martin, and Chandernagore in Bengal, established in 1690, became its principal bases. The French company, closely tied to the crown, was reorganized several times and merged into the Company of the Indies in 1719.

Both companies were monopoly trading corporations with shareholders, directors, and permission to build forts, but they were small and dependent on Indian rulers' goodwill. Their commerce centered on Indian textiles, which became fashionable in Europe, along with pepper, saltpeter, and later tea. The companies were also political bodies in the sense that they negotiated, paid tribute, and fought to protect their trade, and their charter powers made them quasi-sovereign.

The corporate structure of the English East India Company influenced its behavior. Its charter of 1600 created the "Governor and Company of Merchants of London trading into the East Indies," with a monopoly on English trade east of the Cape. Early voyages were financed separately, but a permanent joint stock was created in 1657, and shareholders in the General Court elected a Court of Directors. Competition from interlopers, English merchants who traded without a license, led to political struggle, and in 1698 a rival "New Company" received a charter after lending money to the government. The two companies merged in 1708 to 1709 as the United Company, a settlement brokered by Parliament that bound the company's privileges to its loans to the state. Parliament thus became involved in company affairs, and the company's charter was periodically renewed in exchange for payments.

Section 72.37

72.37 Indian States, Merchants, and European Trading Settlements

The India of the early modern period was dominated by the Mughal Empire, which under Akbar and his successors controlled most of the subcontinent and had a sophisticated bureaucracy and army. European traders operated under imperial permission, called farmans, which granted trade privileges. Indian merchants, bankers, and producers were not subordinate: Gujarati and Bengali bankers financed trade, Armenian and Indian merchants moved goods across Asia, and weavers worked through brokers who advanced funds. The Europeans bought textiles through contracts and intermediaries, and paid largely in silver.

After the death of Aurangzeb in 1707, the Mughal center weakened. Regional rulers and governors established autonomous states: the Nizam of Hyderabad (from 1724), the nawabs of Bengal, Awadh, and the Carnatic, and the Marathas, whose confederacy expanded across western and central India. Nadir Shah of Persia sacked Delhi in 1739, showing the empire's vulnerability. These successor states competed with each other, and the Europeans, who held ports and brought military training and finance, became potential allies.

Historians debate the extent of Mughal "decline," a term that some replace with regionalization or transformation, as regional states often thrived economically. The key point for this chapter is that European companies in 1700 were minor players, and their later power grew from the opportunities created by Indian political fragmentation, not from technological superiority alone.

The Indian commercial world was dominated by Indian capital. Bankers such as the Jagat Seth house of Murshidabad dealt in bills of exchange, financed the nawab's revenue, and loaned to merchants, and Gujarati and Parsi merchants in Surat and Bombay, Chettiar and other southern merchants, Armenians, and Marwari bankers formed networks across the subcontinent. The Company bought textiles through Indian brokers who advanced funds to weavers, and the system of advances meant that weaving was embedded in credit relations. The companies employed local intermediaries, called dubashes in Madras and banians in Calcutta, who combined trade, finance, and diplomacy, and who acquired influence within settlements. The Company's imports of silver, which paid for textiles, formed a significant share of its European trade, though Indian goods also sold widely within Asia. In 1717 the Mughal emperor Farrukhsiyar granted the Company a farman exempting its imports and exports in Bengal from customs duties in return for an annual payment, a privilege that the Company later used to protect private trade by its employees, and a source of conflict with the nawabs.

Political change opened opportunities. In Bengal, Murshid Quli Khan, who was appointed diwan in 1700 and nawab by about 1717, ruled with substantial autonomy and sent revenue to Delhi. Alivardi Khan, nawab from 1740, defended Bengal against Maratha raids in the 1740s, and the Company fortified Calcutta with a ditch in 1742 for protection. In the south the Nizam-ul-Mulk, Asaf Jah, founded the Hyderabad state around 1724, and the nawabs of the Carnatic ruled at Arcot with nominal subordination to Hyderabad. Further west, the Maratha confederacy, directed by the Peshwa, expanded across central and northern India, and the Persian invasion of Nadir Shah in 1739 exposed the Mughal weakness. Scholars debate whether to describe this as decline or as a shift of power to regional states that often governed effectively, but for the Company it meant a world of competing rulers with whom alliances were possible and sometimes necessary.

Section 72.38

72.38 The Carnatic Wars and the Militarization of Company Competition

The wars of the Carnatic, on the southeastern coast, began as extensions of the European War of the Austrian Succession. In 1746 a French fleet under Mahé de La Bourdonnais captured Madras, which was restored to the English in 1748 in the exchange for Louisbourg. The French governor Joseph-François Dupleix, based at Pondicherry, saw that European trained Indian soldiers, called sepoys, could be hired and trained to fight in the European manner, and that alliances with Indian rulers could bring influence and revenue.

In the Second Carnatic War (1749 to 1754), Dupleix and the English supported rival claimants in succession disputes in Hyderabad and the Carnatic. The French backed Chanda Sahib, and the English backed Muhammad Ali Wallajah. Robert Clive, a young Company clerk turned officer, helped seize and hold Arcot in 1751, raising English fortunes. The companies fought not as direct state armies but as allies of Indian claimants, and the Indian rulers had their own aims and used the Europeans as mercenaries and financial partners. The French company's directors, disturbed by costs, recalled Dupleix, and the Treaty of Pondicherry of 1754 ended the phase.

The wars changed the nature of the competition. Commercial rivalry became military and political, and the Europeans learned that armies could secure revenues. The practice of hiring Indian troops and intervening in succession disputes became a template.

The French success in the first Carnatic War showed what disciplined infantry could achieve. After La Bourdonnais captured Madras in September 1746, Dupleix repudiated his arrangement to ransom the town, and the nawab of the Carnatic, Anwaruddin Khan, who regarded the area as his own, sent an army to enforce his authority. At the Battle of Adyar in November 1746, a small French force with European and Indian infantry and artillery defeated a much larger Carnatic army, and the lesson was widely noted. Dupleix drew from it the idea that European trained sepoy battalions, paid by revenue assigned from local rulers, could determine the outcome of Indian wars, and he intervened in disputes at Hyderabad and Arcot.

The Second Carnatic War was a succession conflict. After the death of the Nizam Asaf Jah in 1748, his son Nasir Jang and his grandson Muzaffar Jang contested the throne, while in the Carnatic Chanda Sahib challenged Anwaruddin, who was killed at the battle of Ambur in 1749. Dupleix supported Muzaffar Jang and Chanda Sahib, and the English backed Nasir Jang and Muhammad Ali Wallajah, Anwaruddin's son. Charles Bussy, who accompanied Muzaffar Jang's successor Salabat Jang, created a French sphere of influence at Hyderabad that included the Northern Circars. Clive's seizure of Arcot in 1751 and its defense against a siege of about fifty days gave the English a reputation for success, and the war's campaign around Trichinopoly dragged on. The French company's directors, who wanted dividends not conquest, recalled Dupleix in 1754, and the Godeheu treaty signaled a pause. Indian rulers had used the Europeans as mercenaries, and the Europeans, in turn, had learned to use revenue assignments to pay armies, a financial method that later underpinned the Company's rule. The Third Carnatic War, linked to the Seven Years' War, brought the French commander Lally to besiege Madras in 1758 to 1759, but the siege was lifted, and the English prevailed in 1760 to 1761.

Section 72.39

72.39 Plassey, Bengal, and the East India Company's Emergence as a Political Power

Bengal, the richest province, produced textiles, silk, and saltpeter and was governed by a nawab who was nominally subordinate to the Mughal emperor but effectively independent. Powerful Bengali banking houses, such as that of the Jagat Seths, and merchant groups financed trade and politics. When Alivardi Khan died in April 1756, his grandson Siraj ud-Daulah succeeded him. Tensions arose over the English Company's fortification of Calcutta and the abuse of trading privileges, including the misuse of the dastak, trade passes, for private commerce.

In June 1756 Siraj ud-Daulah captured Calcutta. The episode known as the Black Hole of Calcutta, in which prisoners were confined overnight in a small cell, became a staple of British imperial memory, based largely on an account by John Zephaniah Holwell. Historians question the number of deaths he reported and the nawab's responsibility, but agree that many prisoners died. A Company force under Clive and Admiral Charles Watson retook Calcutta in January 1757 and captured the French post of Chandernagore in March.

The Battle of Plassey, on 23 June 1757, was won less by fighting than by conspiracy. Clive and the Company's agents made a secret arrangement with Mir Jafar, a commander in the nawab's army, and with leading bankers and courtiers, who wished to replace Siraj ud-Daulah. Mir Jafar's troops did not fully engage, the nawab's army broke, and Siraj ud-Daulah was captured and killed. Mir Jafar became nawab, paying the Company large sums and granting concessions. The Company thus became a kingmaker, not a ruler, and Indian elites, who expected to control a dependent European ally, had helped create the situation.

The Calcutta crisis had multiple causes. Siraj ud-Daulah, who succeeded his grandfather Alivardi Khan in April 1756, faced rivals within his own family, including his aunt Ghasiti Begum and cousin Shaukat Jang, and doubts among military leaders and bankers. He objected to the Company's fortification of Calcutta without permission during the approaching war with France, to its abuse of the dastak privilege, and to its sheltering of a fugitive official, Krishna Das. After seizing the Company factory at Kasimbazar, he took Calcutta in June 1756. The episode of the "Black Hole," in which prisoners were confined overnight, and which Holwell reported as causing 123 deaths out of 146, has been questioned by historians who consider his numbers inflated, though they accept that many prisoners died. The tale entered British imperial memory as a symbol of Indian "tyranny," which shows how the story served later propaganda.

The Company's response came from Madras, where Clive and Admiral Watson sailed in October 1756, recaptured Calcutta in January 1757, forced the Treaty of Alinagar in February, and took Chandernagore from the French in March. The plot against Siraj ud-Daulah united diverse interests: Mir Jafar, the army's paymaster, who had family ties to the late nawab, the banker Jagat Seth, the Hindu official Rai Durlabh, and other courtiers who feared the young ruler's unpredictability. The Company's agent William Watts negotiated at court, and the merchant Omichand tried to extract a large payment by threatening to reveal the plan, a threat answered, according to Clive's own account, by deceiving him with a forged version of the treaty. At Plassey on 23 June 1757, the nawab's much larger army, perhaps tens of thousands, was held by a Company force of roughly three thousand, and heavy rain, the death of the loyal commander Mir Madan, and the inaction of Mir Jafar and others led to a rout. The battle was shorter and less bloody than its fame suggests, but the political result was transformative: Mir Jafar became nawab, paid large sums to the Company and its officials, with Clive receiving a gift variously reported at around £230,000, and the Company obtained the zamindari of the 24 Parganas.

Section 72.40

72.40 The Seven Years' War in India and the Transition toward Territorial Empire

The Seven Years' War in India was decided in the south. In January 1760 Sir Eyre Coote defeated the French commander Lally at Wandiwash, and in January 1761, after a long siege, Pondicherry fell and was largely destroyed. The Treaty of Paris of 1763 returned French trading posts but with restrictions on fortification, ending French political ambitions in India while leaving French commerce.

In Bengal, the Company found Mir Jafar's payments burdensome and replaced him with his son-in-law Mir Qasim in 1760, who tried to reform finances and assert independence. Disputes over private trade and customs led to war in 1763. At the Battle of Buxar in October 1764, a Company army under Hector Munro defeated a coalition of Mir Qasim, Shuja ud-Daula, nawab of Awadh, and the Mughal emperor Shah Alam II. In the settlement of 1765, negotiated by Clive in the Treaty of Allahabad, the emperor granted the Company the diwani, the right to collect revenue, of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa.

The grant was a decisive moment, though its meaning was ambiguous. The Company became the revenue authority of a rich province, while a puppet nawab retained judicial functions. A commercial corporation now governed millions, funded by taxation, a role for which it was institutionally unprepared and which led to corruption, famine in 1770, and later parliamentary regulation. The transition from commercial enterprise to colonial government had begun, though the Company did not rule the whole subcontinent, and the Marathas, Mysore, and others remained powerful. The later development of British India belongs to Chapter 105.

The war's end in southern India depended on resources from Bengal and naval superiority. At Wandiwash on 22 January 1760, Eyre Coote defeated Lally, and after a long blockade Pondicherry capitulated in January 1761. Its walls were demolished on British orders. In Bengal, the Company's officials found that Mir Jafar could not meet their demands and replaced him in 1760 with Mir Qasim, who reorganized finances, moved his capital to Monghyr, built a larger army with European style training, and in 1763 abolished internal customs duties to counter the unfair competition from Company servants' private trade. This led to war. Mir Qasim's forces massacred Company prisoners at Patna in October 1763, and Mir Jafar was restored. Mir Qasim fled to Awadh, where he joined the nawab Shuja ud-Daula and the emperor Shah Alam II, who had his own reasons for seeking a position in the east.

At Buxar on 22 October 1764, a Company army under Hector Munro defeated this coalition, and the military victory, with the weakness of their allies' coordination, opened the way for a settlement. After Mir Jafar's death in early 1765 and Clive's return as governor, the Treaty of Allahabad of August 1765 restored most of Awadh to Shuja ud-Daula, gave the emperor the districts of Allahabad and Kora and an annual payment, and in return the emperor granted the Company the diwani of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa. The Company collected revenue through the Indian official Muhammad Reza Khan, leaving the nawab with judicial and policing functions, an arrangement later called dual government that left responsibility ambiguous. The consequences included heavy extraction of revenue, a cycle of corruption, and, in 1769 to 1770, a catastrophic famine in Bengal, which is beyond this chapter. The Company in 1765 remained a trading corporation with a territorial base and a large army, not the ruler of India: the Marathas, Mysore, Hyderabad, and Awadh remained independent powers, and the extension of Company rule across the subcontinent was gradual and contingent, as Chapter 105 explains.

Section 72.41

72.41 Comparing British and French Empires: Settlement, Commerce, Governance, and Power

Comparison clarifies both empires. In migration, Britain's mainland colonies received far larger numbers of settlers, producing a population of about 1.6 million by 1760, against about 70,000 in New France, though French settlers had high birth rates. In Indigenous relations, the French relied on alliances and trade over a vast interior, with intermarriage and mission networks, while English colonists in the coastal colonies acquired land through purchase, pressure, and war, though they also depended on Indigenous diplomacy, especially through the Covenant Chain. Neither approach was benevolent, and both involved dispossession and violence.

In governance, New France was administered by royal officials without an assembly, while the British colonies had elected assemblies and varied charters. In commerce, both pursued mercantilist policies, with Navigation Acts and the exclusif, but enforcement was imperfect on both sides. In the Caribbean, both depended on sugar and enslaved labor and treated these islands as priorities. In India, both companies were small trading bodies that became entangled in Indian politics, with the English company succeeding.

The settlement of 1763 strengthened Britain's position: it gained Canada, Florida, and several islands, and the removal of French power in North America. Yet French commercial and colonial influence continued. France retained Saint-Domingue, Martinique, Guadeloupe, Saint Lucia, Indian trading posts, and a flourishing Atlantic trade, and sought to rebuild its navy. French language, law, and religion persisted in Canada, and Louisiana passed to Spain while remaining French in culture. Britain's victory was therefore substantial but not total, and the empires continued to compete.

Settlement and demography offer the clearest contrast. The British mainland colonies had approximately 251,000 inhabitants around 1700 and about 1.59 million by 1760 according to historical census reconstructions, a figure that includes enslaved people but generally excludes Indigenous populations and refers only to the thirteen colonies, not to Britain's Caribbean or Nova Scotian settlements. Estimates for French Canada, which means the settler communities of the St. Lawrence valley, are about 15,000 around 1700 and some 70,000 by 1760, and a broader count including Acadia, Île Royale, the Illinois country, and Louisiana would add to those totals but not change the order of magnitude. The difference does not mean that the French empire was merely a trading empire: the habitants were farmers, and their fertility was high. It means that England's religious and economic pressures, the availability of servants and later of enslaved labor, the openness to non-English migrants, and a land policy that encouraged families produced a settler society far larger than France's. The French crown restricted Protestant emigration and was reluctant to depopulate France, and the fur trade did not require many settlers.

Relations with Indigenous peoples differed but were not opposites. French officials depended on alliances because their numbers were small, and they used gift exchange, marriage, and mission networks to sustain them, but they also took part in wars that enslaved Indigenous people and in attacks on nations that resisted, as in the Fox Wars. English colonists on the Atlantic seaboard negotiated treaties, purchased land, and relied on Indigenous allies such as the Mohegan and the Haudenosaunee, but their growing numbers, farming economies, and demand for land produced greater dispossession. The Covenant Chain and the Great Peace both show that Indigenous nations used European rivalry to maintain their independence, and the change of power in 1763 removed one of the means of doing so. Institutions also contrasted: the English colonies' assemblies and the French intendant system both combined local elites with imperial authority, though the French monarchy's attempt to control settlement and religion more directly was matched in practice by informal bargaining. Their mercantile regimes were similar in intent and in the gap between rules and enforcement.

The decisive differences in 1763 involved war finance and sea power. Britain, with a financial system that allowed it to borrow at low rates, a navy that could blockade French ports and move expeditions, and a political decision under Pitt to concentrate on colonies, could outspend France, which prioritized its European land war and its army. France's Atlantic trade, however, had grown strongly in the eighteenth century, with Bordeaux and Nantes profiting from Saint-Domingue, and after 1763 the French navy was rebuilt and French commerce prospered. The settlement thus strengthened Britain without ending France's role, and it gave Britain new responsibilities: governing French Catholic Canadians, managing Indigenous relations over a vast interior, financing a larger debt, and supervising a company that was becoming a territorial power in India. Chapter 73 examines how the commerce of both empires affected European society, and Chapter 87 how the strains of 1763 reached the Atlantic revolutions.

What Changed

  • Colonial governments continued to depend on private capital, local elites, and Indigenous or Asian partners

  • Slavery and coerced labor remained central to Caribbean and southern mainland economies
  • Indigenous sovereignty persisted in much of the continent, despite European claims

Who experienced change most directly: Indigenous nations on the coast, Acadians, and enslaved people faced the most severe disruption. Who experienced greater continuity: Interior Indigenous nations, many farming families, and Atlantic merchants saw more continuity until the 1750s. Why the pattern matters: It shows that empire consisted of uneven processes and not a single, uniform transformation.

Quantitative History Study
Comparing Colonial Populations and Scale

Historians often use population estimates to compare empires. Around 1700 the British mainland colonies are usually estimated at roughly a quarter of a million inhabitants, rising to about 1.6 million by 1760, of whom several hundred thousand were enslaved Africans and their descendants. Estimates for the French-settler population of Canada, chiefly the St. Lawrence communities, are roughly 14,000 to 15,000 around 1700 and on the order of 60,000 to 70,000 by 1760; figures vary by source and whether adjacent French settlements are included. On that general comparison the British mainland colonial population was more than twenty times larger by about 1760. These categories refer to colonial settler populations, with enslaved residents included where applicable in the British figures, not the Indigenous populations of these regions. Caribbean populations were dominated by enslaved people in the sugar colonies, and Saint-Domingue and Jamaica held far more enslaved people than free settlers.

Cautions: Early modern counts are uneven. Colonial censuses were incomplete, undercounted enslaved people, women, and children, and generally excluded Indigenous peoples, whose populations were not counted in the same systems. The figures above are modern scholarly estimates, rounded for comparison, and different historians give different totals. The raw contrast should also not be read as a measure of political or military capacity: French forces, mobilizing a larger share of their population and relying on Indigenous allies, held their own for a long time. Shipping and trade volumes are less securely documented, and students should be wary of comparing figures that measure different things, such as ship tonnage, trade value, and number of voyages.

Section 72.42

72.42 Chapter Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has traced a long process in which English and French ventures, initially marginal compared with Iberian and Dutch ones, developed into rival empires. The chronology runs from the failures of the 1580s through the foundations of 1607 and 1608, the growth of settlement and institutions in the seventeenth century, the wars from 1689 to 1763, and the Company's acquisition of revenue rights in 1765.

Colonial geography was varied. British North America comprised four regional societies along the Atlantic seaboard, New France stretched in a thin arc from the Atlantic to the Gulf, the Caribbean produced the empires' sugar wealth, and India offered a new field of commercial and political competition. Institutions reflected different logics: assemblies, charters, and proprietorships in the English case; governors, intendants, and a Sovereign Council in the French case. Mercantilist regulation applied to both, and in both enforcement lagged behind legislation.

The Indigenous relationships were central throughout. Powhatan, Wampanoag, Haudenosaunee, Wendat, Mi'kmaq, Shawnee, Choctaw, and others negotiated, resisted, and shaped European settlement, and the diplomacy of 1701 and the resistance of 1763 show their continuing political agency. In India, Mughal successor states, Bengali bankers, and regional rulers shaped the Company's rise. The evidence on which these conclusions rest includes charters, treaties, missionary reports, merchant records, Persian chronicles, and archaeological findings, and each carries limits that students should keep in mind.

Chapter 73 will take up the wider consequences of this expansion for European consumption, finance, migration, scientific knowledge, and imperial competition, showing how the colonial systems described here reshaped European societies themselves.

The integrating question for the chapter is how these developments fit together. Settlement of the Chesapeake and New England, New France, the Caribbean, and India occurred in different environments, but they shared a reliance on delegated authority, on private capital, and on negotiated relationships with local powers. The same diplomatic conferences, notably Utrecht, Aix-la-Chapelle, and Paris, bundled territories from different oceans, and commercial regulation in London and Paris treated the empires as unified systems. Students should therefore read the sections not as separate regional narratives but as parts of a connected history, in which events in one region, such as the capture of Madras or Louisbourg, shaped outcomes elsewhere.

Evidence supports these conclusions unevenly. Charters, legislation, treaties, and merchant and company records document the European side in great detail, while Indigenous and South Asian perspectives survive through wampum protocols, pictographic signatures, oral traditions, Persian chronicles, and banking archives, and through the reading of European sources against the grain. The chapter's source windows, which are paraphrases and not verified editions, should be supplemented by consulting published scholarly editions. The transition to Chapter 73 follows from the argument: the commodities and capital of these empires, from sugar and tobacco to textiles and furs, entered European households, markets, and finance, and the next chapter examines how empire changed Europe itself.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how England, later Great Britain, and France built overseas empires between about 1580 and 1763, and how the East India companies began to acquire political power in India by 1765. The central argument is that these empires were competing systems whose development depended on settlement, commerce, state policy, military force, and negotiation with non-European societies, and that none of these elements alone explains their growth.

Both states entered an Atlantic world already shaped by Spanish, Portuguese, and Dutch expansion. Their early ventures grew from fishing, privateering, and war with Spain, and the failures of the 1580s, notably Roanoke, taught that colonization required organized capital, supply, and relations with local peoples. Chartered companies, proprietary grants, and royal sponsorship allowed monarchies to delegate the costs and risks of colonization to private investors, and the English and French monarchies used these tools differently.

English settlement in North America produced four distinct regional societies. The Chesapeake combined tobacco, headrights, indentured labor, and eventually slavery. New England was settled by Puritan families in towns organized around churches and schools. The Middle Colonies were commercial, plural, and ethnically diverse, and the Carolinas and Georgia were shaped by rice, indigo, slavery, and frontier diplomacy. Population grew rapidly through natural increase and migration. Settlement brought conflict with Indigenous peoples, including the Anglo-Powhatan wars, the Pequot War, Metacom's War, and the Yamasee War, and these conflicts reflected Indigenous political objectives as well as settler expansion.

New France developed on different foundations. The fur trade, which required Indigenous hunters and middlemen, shaped alliances with the Innu, Algonquin, Wendat, and later Anishinaabe peoples. The St. Lawrence valley supported a settled farming population, and Acadia and Île Royale supported fishing and agriculture. The interior network of forts and trading posts in the pays d'en haut and the Illinois country, extended to Louisiana, produced claims far larger than French settlement. Missions added a religious dimension whose evidence, notably the Jesuit Relations, must be read critically. The Great Peace of Montréal in 1701 showed the strength of Indigenous diplomacy and the role of Kondiaronk and the Haudenosaunee in shaping the settlement.

The Caribbean was often more valuable than the mainland. Barbados, Jamaica, Martinique, Guadeloupe, and Saint-Domingue produced sugar through plantation systems that depended on enslaved Africans, as Chapter 70 explains, and their wealth drove imperial policy, naval strategy, and diplomacy. Kalinago and other Indigenous peoples were displaced or resisted, and Caribbean ports, shipping lanes, and smuggling networks linked the islands to European and North American markets.

Colonial administration and mercantilism connected the colonies to the monarchies. English colonies moved from company and proprietary rule toward royal government, with assemblies that gained practical authority and with the Board of Trade and Parliament exercising growing oversight. New France was reorganized in 1663 under a governor, an intendant, and a Sovereign Council, and the French crown sponsored settlement through regiments and the filles du roi. The Navigation Acts and the French exclusif sought to channel colonial trade, but smuggling, merchant networks, and local interests limited enforcement.

Rivalry produced repeated wars. King William's War and Queen Anne's War, King George's War, and the French and Indian War connected colonial conflicts to European ones. The Treaty of Utrecht in 1713 and the Treaty of Paris in 1763 redrew the map, with Britain gaining Canada, Florida, and several Caribbean islands, and France retaining valuable sugar colonies. Indigenous nations, who were excluded from the treaties, resisted the new order in Pontiac's War, and the Royal Proclamation of 1763 attempted to manage the frontier. The Acadian deportation illustrated the human costs of imperial war.

In India, the English and French East India companies entered a commercial world dominated by Mughal and successor states and by Indian merchants and bankers. The Carnatic Wars militarized the rivalry, and the intervention in Bengal, culminating in Plassey in 1757 and Buxar in 1764, led to the grant of the diwani in 1765. This gave a commercial corporation revenue authority over a rich province. The process was gradual and contingent, depended on Indian political alliances, and did not yet constitute British rule of India.

Comparing the two empires shows that differences in settlement, institutions, and Indigenous diplomacy were substantial but should not be reduced to a contrast of free and authoritarian systems or of settlers and traders. Britain emerged strengthened in 1763, with naval and commercial advantages, but also with heavy debt, a larger frontier, and new populations to govern. France lost Canada but retained its Caribbean colonies, Indian posts, and commercial power. The historical debate about whether 1763 established British predominance or created vulnerabilities remains open.

The chapter's lessons are that European expansion was contingent, that Indigenous and Asian actors shaped outcomes, and that the gap between legal claims and practical control was central. Chapter 73 will examine how the products, capital, and people of these empires transformed European societies themselves.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how European states, through private companies, settlers, merchants, soldiers, and diplomats, built overseas systems that were not simply extensions of metropolitan power. English and French empires were negotiated, contested, and shaped by Indigenous nations, Asian rulers, enslaved populations, and colonial elites. Understanding them requires attention to the gap between claim and control, between law and practice, and between European treaties and the societies they affected.

The chapter reveals larger processes. The competition between Britain and France turned local disputes into global wars, and treaties exchanged territories across oceans, so that Louisbourg could be traded for Madras and Canada weighed against Guadeloupe. Sugar, furs, tobacco, and textiles tied together regions that had little previous contact, and the institutions that managed these connections, companies, assemblies, intendants, and customs houses, became part of the political history of Europe itself.

Later developments grew from this period. The settlement of 1763 created the fiscal and territorial problems that will be examined in Chapter 87 as part of the Atlantic revolutionary crisis. The Company's diwani opened a new phase of British involvement in India, examined in Chapter 105, and the plantation systems of the Caribbean continued to shape the economies and societies of the Atlantic world. Indigenous nations continued to defend their lands, and Indigenous diplomacy remained a force into the next era.

For the argument of Volume III, this chapter shows that early modern European states were not only dynastic and confessional powers but also participants in an expanding, interconnected world economy, and that their rivalry reshaped the global balance of power. Chapter 73 now turns to the consequences of these empires for European consumers, finance, migration, and knowledge, to ask how empire changed Europe as well as the societies it touched overseas.
