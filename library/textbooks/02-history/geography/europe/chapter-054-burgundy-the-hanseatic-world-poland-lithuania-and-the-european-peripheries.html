Burgundy, the Hanseatic World, Poland-Lithuania, and the European Peripheries

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

During the later fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, some of the most inventive experiments in political and economic power in Europe took place in regions that older textbooks treated as the margins: the Low Countries, the Baltic, the lands between the Oder and the Dnieper, and the northern kingdoms.

Imagine a merchant from Lübeck arriving in Bruges with a cargo of Baltic wax and furs. He would deal in a city ruled, after 1384, by the dukes of Burgundy, a dynasty whose lands were scattered from the North Sea to the Jura. Another merchant, bound for Novgorod, would pass through towns governed by councils, under the authority of princes, bishops, or the Teutonic Order, all of whom he had to satisfy. Neither man lived in a centralized kingdom, and neither belonged to a state in the later national sense. Both worked inside networks of law, privilege, and obligation that crossed what modern maps would show as borders.

Chapters 51 to 53 followed war, social unrest, and religious crisis, mostly through England, France, and Italy. This chapter, the geographic re-centering of Part VIII, asks what happens when the narrative moves north and east. It examines four ways of building power: the Valois dukes of Burgundy assembled a composite principality; Hanseatic merchants coordinated towns without founding a state; Poland and Lithuania joined distinct political communities through a dynasty; and the Kalmar Union tried a dynastic union of three Scandinavian kingdoms.

The central question is how dynastic states, urban leagues, commercial networks, and political unions reshaped northern, central, and eastern Europe in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, and what these regions reveal about the limits of describing medieval Europe as a western "center" surrounded by distant "peripheries." The word "periphery" is used here only for specific relationships, never as a ranking of importance or sophistication.

The evidence is as varied as the regions: ducal accounts and ordinances, city council records, Hanseatic recesses and customs accounts, merchant letters, Teutonic chronicles, Polish and Lithuanian charters, and dynastic acts. Each tradition has its own biases, and historians must read them against one another. The chapter connects to Part VIII by showing that late medieval crisis did not produce one western path toward centralized states. It also prepares Chapter 55, on printing, gunpowder, and Europe around 1500.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why England, France, and Italy cannot stand for late medieval Europe as a whole.
  2. Trace the growth of Valois Burgundy through inheritance, marriage, purchase, diplomacy, and war.
  3. Analyze why the wealth of the Low Countries made them both an asset and a problem for rulers.
  4. Describe the commercial geography of the North Sea and Baltic.
  5. Distinguish the Hansa from a centralized state and explain how it coordinated collective action.
  6. Explain the Polish-Lithuanian dynastic union, including the roles of Krewo and Jadwiga's reign.
  7. Assess the significance of Grunwald/Tannenberg without exaggerating its immediate effects.
  8. Compare Burgundy, the Hansa, Poland-Lithuania, and the Kalmar Union as forms of political organization.
  9. Interpret primary and administrative sources from regions with differing record traditions.
  10. Evaluate the usefulness and limits of "center" and "periphery" as historical categories.

Historical Context

The commercial revival of the High Middle Ages, examined in Chapter 43, had already tied the Low Countries, northern Germany, and the Baltic into regular exchange. Chapter 46 explained the loose structure of the Holy Roman Empire, in which many of the towns discussed here held their status. Chapter 45 and Chapter 51 traced the stronger monarchies of England and France, and the Hundred Years' War created the opening in which Burgundy grew: French royal power was weakened, yet Burgundy's rulers remained princes of the French royal house.

The fourteenth century's famine and plague (Chapter 50) and the social conflicts of Chapter 52 shaped these regions too, though unevenly. Labor scarcity affected cities and rural estates differently across the Baltic and the Low Countries. Chapter 53 covered the schism and the Hussite movement, whose theology lies outside this chapter. Meanwhile the Ottoman advance in the Balkans, treated mainly in Chapter 41, formed the southeastern background for Hungary. Overseas expansion lay in the future; Chapter 68 treats it.

Historians reconstruct these worlds from sources of striking variety. Ducal account books survive in rich series; Hanseatic towns kept council records and customs registers; Poland and Lithuania are known through royal and princely charters and chronicles written by outsiders as well as insiders. Because the Teutonic Order's and its opponents' chronicles served political ends, no single source tradition can be treated as neutral.

Section 54.1

Re-centering Late Medieval Europe: Multiple Centers and Connected Regions

England, France, and Italy dominate many accounts of the late Middle Ages because their records are abundant and their later national histories are well known. They cannot stand for the whole. Burgundy and the Low Countries, the Baltic ports, Poland-Lithuania, Bohemia, Hungary, and the northern kingdoms were linked by trade routes, dynastic marriages, and religious networks into interconnected political and commercial zones.

The Master Series rule applies: "periphery" is an analytical problem, not a verdict. A region can be peripheral to a particular trade (Baltic grain supplying western cities) while being central to others (Baltic shipping, Lithuanian steppe diplomacy). These places should not be called backward because their institutions differed from those of France or England.

Section 54.2

Valois Burgundy: From French Appanage to Composite Principality

In 1361 the death of the last Capetian duke of Burgundy returned the duchy to the French crown. In 1363 King John II granted it to his youngest son, Philip the Bold, as an appanage, a territory assigned to a royal prince to maintain his rank while keeping it in the royal family. The practice served the monarchy: it rewarded kin, anchored a loyal prince in an important region, and avoided dividing the royal domain among sons. Its long-term cost was that appanage princes could build bases of power that rivaled the crown, especially when the crown was weakened by war and by the illness of Charles VI.

Philip's fortunes changed in 1369, when he married Margaret, heiress of Count Louis II of Flanders. When Louis died in 1384, Philip and Margaret inherited Flanders, Artois, the county of Burgundy (Franche-Comté), and associated lands. Nothing in 1363 suggests that Philip planned an independent realm; he acted as a French prince, a royal councillor, and a rival for influence at court. The accumulation was dynastic, the product of marriage and inheritance, not of a program of territorial or national consolidation.

The resulting dominion was awkward. The duchy of Burgundy and Flanders were held from the French crown, but the county of Burgundy was an imperial fief, and part of Flanders lay east of the Scheldt within the Empire. The dukes therefore owed obligations to two overlords, which gave them room for maneuver and sources of conflict. The heartland around Dijon lay far from the northern Low Countries, so that the dukes' rule depended on roads, rivers, and travel between disconnected territories.

Each territory had its own law, courts, estates, and fiscal customs. French was dominant at the ducal court, Dutch in much of Flanders, Brabant, and Holland, and several languages were spoken in the towns and countryside. Cities guarded charters and privileges, and rulers entered each land by swearing to respect its customs. This is why historians call Burgundy a composite principality: the duke was a different kind of prince in each of his lands.

John the Fearless (duke 1404–1419) pushed the dynasty further into French politics. His murder of Louis, duke of Orléans, in 1407 opened the Armagnac-Burgundian conflict, a civil struggle over control of the government of the ill King Charles VI and its revenues, interwoven with the Hundred Years' War (Chapter 51). John himself was assassinated in 1419 by followers of the Dauphin at Montereau. That act pushed his son Philip toward England and framed the politics Philip the Good inherited.

Section 54.3

Flanders, Brabant, Holland, and the Wealth of the Low Countries

The Low Countries were among the most urbanized regions of Europe. In Flanders and Brabant a large share of the population lived in towns, each with its own economic character. Ghent was a large manufacturing city with powerful guilds and a long record of resisting princes (see Chapter 52). Bruges was a commercial and financial center, and Ypres had a smaller but long-established cloth industry. Brussels and Leuven in Brabant combined administration, university life, and crafts, and Antwerp rose as a port and fair town. The smaller towns and the countryside produced textiles, linen, tapestry, and agricultural goods for market.

The old Flemish drapery depended on imported English wool, and the great cities struggled in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries as English cloth exports grew and cheaper cloth from smaller towns and rural districts competed with the luxury product. The textile economy therefore changed rather than simply declined: new products, regions, and organizations of production appeared even as the old guild cities lost ground.

Bruges served as an international entrepôt where Italian, English, Iberian, German, and Baltic merchants met. Foreign communities, including the Hanseatic Kontor, kept houses, warehouses, and privileges there. Brokers, money changers, and merchant bankers arranged credit and exchange, and the city's commercial institutions helped make it a center of European finance. Italian firms and brokers kept contact with Mediterranean markets.

Antwerp's expansion in the fifteenth century owed much to the Brabant fairs held there and at Bergen op Zoom, which drew merchants from England, Germany, and elsewhere. Bruges' relative decline had several causes: changing commercial networks and merchant preferences, political troubles, competition from other markets, and the deteriorating navigability of the Zwin, the channel linking it to the sea, which forced reliance on outports such as Sluis and Damme. Antwerp did not rise simply because Bruges silted up, and Bruges remained important well into the century.

In the north, Holland and Zeeland developed shipping, fishing, commercial agriculture, and brewing. Herring fisheries and a growing carrying trade into the Baltic made Dutch shipping a competitor to the Hansa, a theme touched on in section 54.10 and developed in later volumes.

For the dukes the Low Countries were their main source of money. Taxes (aides) had to be granted by the estates of each province, and the great towns, guilds, and provincial institutions were powerful bargaining partners. Rulers who wanted revenue from wealthy cities generally had to negotiate, offering confirmation of privileges, judicial concessions, or political favor. Urban resistance was not simply opposition to progress; towns defended legal rights and economic interests that they regarded as legitimate.

Section 54.4

Philip the Good and the Government of the Burgundian Lands

Philip the Good succeeded in 1419 and sought vengeance for his father's murder. He allied with England in the early 1420s, then reversed course at the Treaty of Arras (1435), reconciling with Charles VII in exchange for substantial territorial and political concessions. The wider French civil war matters here as background, not as a separate narrative.

Philip's expansion in the Low Countries came through several different methods. He purchased the rights to Namur from John III in 1421, taking possession after John's death in 1429; Brabant and Limburg passed to him by inheritance in 1430; Holland, Zeeland, and Hainaut came in 1433 after he pressured Jacqueline of Bavaria to yield; and Luxembourg followed in the 1440s through a combination of legal claims, purchase, and pressure. These were different legal acquisitions, not a single conquest, and each territory kept its own institutions.

Governing such a dominion required coordination without uniformity. The dukes used a council, a chancellery, financial officials, chambers of accounts at Lille and Dijon, and judicial institutions, while local officials served in the provinces. Provincial estates continued to bargain over taxation. From 1464 a common assembly of delegates from the provinces, conventionally called the States General, met under Philip, and it later met regularly. It did not abolish or replace the provincial estates, and it was not a national parliament; it was a tool for coordinating consent across separate lands. Philip also attempted to coordinate coinage and economic policy, with partial success.

The court was itinerant. Brussels, Bruges, Lille, Dijon, Ghent, and other centers hosted it at different times, and there was no single fixed capital. Ceremony was political: in 1430 Philip founded the Order of the Golden Fleece, which bound high nobles from many lands to him personally. Tournaments, banquets, music, art, and manuscript patronage displayed the dynasty's wealth and tied elites together. These practices produced administrative integration, not constitutional uniformity, and nothing shows that Philip aimed at a centralized modern state.

Historical Vocabulary
Composite State

A composite state is a political structure made of several territories, each keeping its own laws, privileges, and institutions, joined through a ruler or dynasty. Burgundy, the Habsburg lands (Chapter 75), and Poland-Lithuania are examples. The term matters because it warns against assuming that medieval and early modern rulers aimed at, or could achieve, uniform national states.

Section 54.5

Charles the Bold and the Limits of Burgundian State-Building

Charles succeeded in 1467 with a larger and richer dominion than any earlier duke, but one with gaps. He wanted to link the northern and southern lands, and he made stronger claims to sovereignty than his father. This meant heavier taxation and closer supervision of towns and estates. At Liège, which resisted Burgundian influence, he crushed a revolt in 1468. In 1473 he acquired Guelders, and in that year he reached Trier to discuss with Emperor Frederick III a possible royal title for himself. The negotiations ended without a coronation, and their exact terms remain uncertain.

Charles also tried to create a corridor through Lorraine, which he occupied in 1475. His institutional reforms included the Ordinance of Thionville of 8 December 1473, which created a fixed high court of appeal at Mechelen (Malines), first sitting in January 1474, meant to draw legal authority toward the center and encountering resistance from provincial bodies. From 1471 he organized permanent Companies of Ordinance, revised in 1472 and 1473, combining cavalry with artillery and infantry, which required costly, regular payment.

Diplomacy with Louis XI, the Empire, Lorraine, and the Swiss Confederation turned hostile. In the Burgundian Wars, Charles was beaten at Grandson on 2 March 1476 and at Morat (Murten) on 22 June 1476, and he was killed at Nancy on 5 January 1477. The Swiss victories should not be told as a simple triumph of infantry over cavalry. Terrain, command, cohesion, finance, and the political isolation of Charles all played a part, and Charles's resources were strained by an army that was expensive to maintain.

His death left his daughter Mary as heiress. Louis XI seized the duchy of Burgundy proper and other lands, and the estates of the Netherlands used the crisis to extract the Grand Privilege of 1477, which restored provincial and urban rights and reversed some of Charles's reforms. Mary's marriage to Maximilian of Habsburg in 1477 shifted the northern inheritance toward the Habsburg dynasty, though it did not create the later Habsburg Empire at once.

The ending needs three distinctions: Charles's personal territorial project collapsed; many Burgundian institutions survived in the Low Countries, including councils, accounts, and the States General; and the territories entered Habsburg dynastic politics. Burgundy did not simply collapse in 1477.

Cause and Effect

The Failure of Charles the Bold's Burgundian Project

Long-term causes

Legally separate territories; powerful towns and estates with entrenched privileges; a territorial gap between northern and southern lands; rivalry with the French crown.

Short-term causes

Charles's costly campaigns, pressure for taxes and troops, and conflict with Louis XI, Lorraine, and the Swiss.

Event

Defeats at Grandson and Morat (1476) and Charles's death at Nancy (1477).

Immediate effects

French occupation of Burgundian lands; urban demands for restored privileges; Mary's marriage to Maximilian.

Long-term consequences

The Low Countries pass into the Habsburg orbit, with consequences for Volume III's dynastic politics.

Historical complexity

Charles's failure was not simply personal rashness: it also reflects the structural difficulty of governing a composite state. Defeat did not erase the institutions the dukes had built.
Historical Debate

Was Burgundy Becoming a State, and Was the Hansa a "League"?

The question: Did the Burgundian dukes' institutions point toward a unified state, and does "league" fit the Hanseatic network?

Interpretation A: Shared councils, financial offices, and the court show real movement toward a state, interrupted by Charles's defeat. For the Hansa, the Hansetage and collective embargoes suggest a quasi-political association.
Interpretation B: The dukes ruled each territory by its own law and bargaining, so Burgundy remained a composite union. The Hansa had no standing government, fixed membership, or army, so "league" misleads; "network" or "community of merchants and towns" is closer.

Evidence: Ducal ordinances and accounts, estates' records, and Hanseatic recesses and town correspondence. Why it matters: The answer shapes whether late medieval political development is read as a single path toward states. Student task: Identify one piece of evidence for each interpretation and say what additional evidence would help.

Section 54.6

The North Sea and Baltic as an Economic World

The North Sea and Baltic formed linked maritime zones, not separate regional economies. Access between them ran through the Danish straits, the Sound and the Belts, so that whoever controlled or taxed passage held real leverage; a Danish toll at the Sound was introduced in 1429. Lübeck's position between the Baltic and the North Sea also drew goods overland to Hamburg and shortened voyages. From Novgorod and Livonia, goods moved through Baltic ports to northern Germany, the Low Countries, and England. This chapter treats the mature fourteenth and fifteenth century system; the origins belong to Chapter 43.

Commodities came from distinct regions. Novgorod and the east supplied furs and wax; Prussia, Poland, and Livonia timber, pitch, tar, flax, hemp, ash, and amber; Sweden metals; Norway stockfish; the Baltic and Scania herring. Salt, notably from Lüneburg, was needed to preserve the herring caught at the Scania fisheries, and it traveled by way of Lübeck. Western exports included cloth from the Low Countries and England, beer, and manufactured goods. Rivers such as the Vistula carried inland produce to ports like Danzig/Gdańsk.

Grain became more important over time. Prussian and Polish exports through Danzig grew in the fifteenth century, and Dutch shipping increasingly carried them west. Western regions did not depend on Baltic grain everywhere or in every period; dependence varied by place, harvest, and year. Shipping used cogs and later hulks, navigation was seasonal and largely halted in winter, and storms, piracy, wrecking, and tolls all added risk. Merchants spread risk through shares in ships and cargoes.

These flows show mutual dependence, not an advanced west and a backward east. Western urban manufacture required eastern raw materials and markets, while eastern merchants, nobles, and rulers earned income from exports and imported cloth and salt. The Baltic was a commercial world in its own right, with towns, elites, and political authorities of many kinds.

Section 54.7

What Was the Hanseatic League?

The Hansa grew from associations of merchants, called Hansen, traveling abroad together and seeking privileges from foreign rulers. During the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, towns whose merchants shared these privileges began to cooperate, and the word came to describe a wider community of towns. Lübeck was the leading town, and often convened and led meetings, but it was not a capital, and no town ruled the rest.

Towns met at assemblies called Hansetage, where delegates debated common action. Decisions were written as recesses, records of what the delegates agreed, though towns could decline to follow them. The Hansa had no permanent executive, standing army, or navy, and no fixed list of members: towns joined, left, were expelled, or simply stopped taking part. Regional groupings, such as the Wendish, Prussian, and Livonian towns, organized much of the practical work.

Collective action took the form of embargoes, negotiation, temporary fleets, war, and sanctions against towns that broke ranks. Success depended on shared interest, so cooperation might be strong in one dispute and weak in the next. A town with grain interests in Danzig might see an English quarrel differently from Cologne or Lübeck. The key assets were privileges won from foreign rulers.

Historians disagree over the best label. "League" suggests a formal alliance, "federation" implies more constitutional structure than existed, and "network" or "community" better captures its fluidity. The Hansa was not a nation, a centralized federation, a German empire, or a state with borders, and it should not be read as an ancestor of modern European integration.

Historical Vocabulary
Hanse and Kontor

Hanse originally meant an association, especially of merchants. A Kontor was a major foreign trading station of the Hansa, with its own rules and privileges. The terms matter because modern phrases like "Hanseatic League" suggest a formal alliance the sources do not clearly describe.

Section 54.8

The Kontors: Bruges, London, Bergen, and Novgorod

A Kontor differed from an ordinary merchant colony in having its own statutes, elected officers, and privileges recognized by the host authority. Each was a privileged foreign merchant institution, not a colonial possession: it existed because rulers and towns granted rights, and it could lose them. Each had warehouses and lodging, rules of conduct, and procedures for disciplining members and settling disputes among them, while serious cases often involved local courts. Yet the four were not interchangeable.

At Bruges the Hanseatic merchants were an embedded community inside a great international market (see section 54.3). Elected aldermen represented merchants organized in regional groupings, and the Kontor negotiated with the city and the count or duke over privileges, tolls, and jurisdiction. Disputes with local merchants and rulers were frequent, and the Kontor sometimes used the threat of moving trade elsewhere as leverage.

In London the Steelyard (Stalhof), beside the Thames, gave German merchants secure quarters and royal privileges. Their exports of cloth and imports of Baltic goods mattered to the crown, but English merchants resented their advantages, and the dispute became political, leading to the Anglo-Hanseatic war of 1469–1474 and the Peace of Utrecht (28 February 1474), which confirmed their position and property.

At Bergen the Bryggen wharf quarter organized the stockfish trade. Stockfish from northern Norway reached Bergen through producers, Northfarers, and other intermediaries. Hanseatic firms exchanged imported grain and other necessities for fish and operated an extensive credit system in which goods and equipment could be advanced against future deliveries. Hanseatic privilege clashed at times with Norwegian royal officials and local interests, and the quarter was run under strict rules, with young merchants serving apprenticeships there.

At Novgorod the Peterhof, under its own statutes, handled furs, wax, and other eastern goods. Its seasonal rhythm shaped residence, with merchants traveling in summer and winter, and it depended on Novgorodian authority and on routes between Novgorod and the Baltic, often through Livonian towns. Language, translation, and local mediators were essential at every Kontor.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Merchant at a Kontor

The following is reconstructed from regulations, not from one person's biography. A young merchant sent to a Kontor often lived in shared quarters under strict house rules, learning commercial practice from senior members. Letters and account books traveled between partners in distant towns, and success depended on trust, credit, and information. Such a life was not typical of all merchants: it describes a pattern inferred from regulations and surviving records, not one person's documented experience. The Kontors were mostly male communities, and the labor of local workers, sailors, and household servants rarely appears in the sources.

Section 54.9

Hanseatic Towns, Merchants, and Urban Government

Hanseatic towns were politically very different. Lübeck had wide autonomy, Prussian towns lay under the Teutonic Order, Livonian towns under the Order and bishops, some towns were imperial (see Chapter 46), and others stood under territorial princes. This is why the Hansa depended on negotiation, not command. Danzig/Gdańsk, Elbing/Elbląg, Riga, Reval/Tallinn, Rostock, Stralsund, Hamburg, and Bremen each had distinct interests.

Councils were generally dominated by merchant oligarchies, but guilds and crafts challenged them for a voice, sometimes in disruptive conflicts that reshaped town governments. Merchants worked through family firms, partnerships, and factors or agents in distant towns, who exchanged commercial letters reporting prices, shipping, and risk. Credit and delayed settlement were routine. Reputation, kinship, citizenship, and legal enforcement made trust possible, and merchants shared maritime risk by holding shares in ships and cargoes.

These networks depended on a larger urban labor system: shipmasters and sailors, dock laborers and carriers, artisans such as coopers, rope makers, and shipwrights, and servants. Women took part in household businesses, and widows sometimes continued firms, although the sources record them unevenly. "Hanseatic merchant" was therefore not a socially uniform category.

Section 54.10

The Hansa, Denmark, Piracy, and Political Power

Denmark controlled the Sound and Belts, so Danish royal policy mattered enormously. Valdemar IV Atterdag strengthened the monarchy, and his capture of Visby in 1361 alarmed the towns. In 1367 many towns, including Prussian and Dutch ones, formed the Cologne Confederation against him. The war ended with the Peace of Stralsund in 1370, which confirmed Hanseatic privileges, gave the towns control of key Scanian fortresses for a limited period, and gave them a voice in confirming a Danish successor. This did not make them sovereign over Denmark; the clause's practical force was limited.

Scania remained central because of its herring fairs. Relations with Margaret I and the Kalmar Union (section 54.17) were mixed. During the Scandinavian succession struggles, the Victual Brothers, privateers who supplied besieged Stockholm and later raided shipping, show how privateering and piracy overlapped; the Teutonic Order took Gotland from them in 1398. Legitimate naval warfare, privateering, and piracy were not always cleanly separated.

The fifteenth century brought Dutch and English competition, stronger princes, and shifting trade routes, and the towns often disagreed among themselves. Yet the Hansa did not decline in a simple line, and Dutch growth did not displace it at once.

The Hansa possessed commercial influence, diplomatic leverage, and at times temporary military power. It did not possess territorial sovereignty.

Primary Source Window

The Peace of Stralsund, 1370 (paraphrase)

Context: Negotiated at Stralsund in 1370 between the Danish crown and the Hanseatic towns after the war of the Cologne Confederation, to settle the dispute over Danish control of the straits and the towns' privileges. Source type: A treaty, preserved in town and royal archives.

Paraphrase: The treaty confirmed commercial privileges for the Hanseatic towns and gave them certain rights in the Scania herring trade and influence over the Danish succession. This is a summary, not a translation.

Analysis: The treaty shows merchants bargaining with a king as political partners over trading privileges, Scanian interests, and a limited voice in the Danish succession. It is a negotiated document, reflecting the towns' leverage at one moment and the winning side's priorities. It does not prove permanent Hanseatic dominance, and the Hansa did not control Denmark after 1370. It tells little about ordinary fishermen or workers, or about Danish perspectives beyond what the text admits.

Section 54.11

Poland and Lithuania before the Dynastic Union

Poland and Lithuania were already substantial and internally complex political communities before 1385. The Polish kingdom emerged from a long period of political fragmentation and was restored in the early fourteenth century. Casimir III (1333–1370) consolidated it through royal administration, law, castles, and towns, and from the 1340s he extended Polish rule into Red Ruthenia (the Galician-Ruthenian lands), bringing a large Orthodox population under the Polish crown. He managed relations with Bohemia, Hungary, and the Teutonic Order through diplomacy and territorial settlements.

Casimir died in 1370 without a legitimate son, and the crown passed to his nephew Louis of Hungary. To secure the succession of his daughters, Louis granted the Privilege of Koszyce (Košice) in 1374, which limited noble taxation and obligations. It shows the nobility bargaining with a dynasty over succession.

Lithuania grew far beyond its Lithuanian-speaking core under the Gediminid dynasty. Gediminas (died about 1341) and later Algirdas and Kęstutis built a state that incorporated or dominated extensive Ruthenian, largely Orthodox territories, such as Polotsk, Vitebsk, and, by the 1360s, areas around Kyiv. Lithuania dealt with the Rus' principalities, the steppe, Moscow, Poland, and the Teutonic Order. The dynasty and heartland remained largely pagan while many subjects in the east and south were Orthodox Christians. Lithuania was a major power in its own right, not an undeveloped state that needed Poland to matter.

Section 54.12

Jadwiga, Jogaila, Krewo, and the Christianization of Lithuania

When Louis of Hungary died in 1382, a succession crisis followed in Poland. His younger daughter Jadwiga was crowned in 1384 as rex, king of Poland, a title that marked her as a ruler in her own right, not merely a royal bride. Polish elites sought a husband who could secure the realm against the Teutonic Order. Jogaila, Grand Duke of Lithuania, was in a weakened position: he had faced dynastic conflict, in which Kęstutis was captured, imprisoned at Kreva in 1382, and died there in Jogaila's custody (sources traditionally say he was strangled, and they are politically charged), and Kęstutis's son Vytautas fled to the Order. A match with Poland offered security and prestige to him, and a powerful partner to Poland.

The Act of Krewo (Krėva), of 14 August 1385, set out promises linked to the proposed marriage: Jogaila's baptism and the conversion of his subjects, the release of prisoners, and territorial claims. Historians dispute the precise legal sense of its key verb, applicare, which may suggest joining or attaching lands. It did not incorporate Lithuania into Poland or create the later Commonwealth; it became foundational to a developing dynastic relationship.

In 1386 Jogaila was accepted as ruler, baptized as Władysław, married Jadwiga, and was crowned as Władysław II Jagiełło. In 1387 the formal Christianization of Lithuania began, with a bishopric at Vilnius and privileges for Catholic elites. The baptism of the ruler did not convert every inhabitant: many subjects were already Orthodox, pagan practices persisted, and Samogitia followed a later chronology. Christianization was both religious and political, and political calculation need not exclude sincere belief.

Section 54.13

The Teutonic Order and the Baltic Frontier

The Teutonic Order conquered Prussia in the thirteenth century and by 1309 had its headquarters at Marienburg (Malbork). Its state rested on castles, commanderies, administration, taxation, agriculture, and settlement of German-speaking colonists, alongside the Prussian population, whom conquest transformed and in part displaced. The region was not ethnically homogeneous. Towns such as Danzig/Gdańsk, Elbing/Elbląg, and Thorn/Toruń prospered on trade connected to the Hanseatic world of sections 54.6–54.10, and they and the Prussian nobility became estates with their own interests.

Samogitia lay between Prussia and Livonia and was a strategic problem for the Order. The conversion of Lithuania weakened the claim that war against it was a crusade against pagans, and its legitimacy was contested in diplomacy. Conflict continued, however, over territory and power, so the end of paganism in the ruler's house did not at once end the Northern Crusades.

At the Council of Constance (1415) the Polish side presented its case through Paulus Vladimiri (Paweł Włodkowic), a Kraków jurist. He argued that non-Christian peoples held rights, including to property and political rule, and could not be deprived of them simply through crusade or forced conversion. His argument targeted the Order's wars; the council's wider history belongs to Chapter 53.

Section 54.14

Grunwald/Tannenberg and the Transformation of the Baltic Balance

Vytautas had ceded Samogitia to the Order in the Treaty of Salynas (1398), but Teutonic control remained contested and Samogitian resistance continued. An uprising there in 1409, supported by Lithuania, led to open war. In summer 1410 Jogaila and Vytautas united their forces and marched on Marienburg. The Polish-Lithuanian army included Poles, Lithuanians, Ruthenians, Tatars, and Czech and other contingents, and the Order's army included guest crusaders from western Europe as well as local troops. Figures for both armies are uncertain.

On 15 July 1410, near Grunwald (Tannenberg), the Order was defeated. Grand Master Ulrich von Jungingen was killed, with much of the senior leadership. The exact sequence of the battle is uncertain because the sources are partisan. Yet the victory did not destroy the Teutonic state: the Order held Marienburg, which the besiegers could not take, and Heinrich von Plauen organized its defense. The First Peace of Thorn in 1411 imposed an indemnity but left territorial change limited, with Samogitia held only for the lives of Jogaila and Vytautas.

The longer effects were financial strain on the Order and growing tension with Prussian towns and estates, who formed the Prussian Confederation in 1440. In 1454 the Confederation appealed to Casimir IV, who accepted its incorporation, and the Thirteen Years' War began. Both sides struggled to pay for war, relying on towns and mercenaries. The Second Peace of Thorn (1466) gave Poland Royal Prussia, which kept significant local institutions, while the Order retained eastern Prussia, with its grand master required to recognize the Polish king's overlordship. The Order did not disappear in 1466, and Grunwald was never a simple Polish-German national battle.

Section 54.15

The Jagiellonian Political World

After 1386 Poland and the Grand Duchy remained separate, each with its own offices, councils, elites, and interests. Jogaila held a dual position, and Vytautas fought for authority in Lithuania. Ostrów (1392), Vilnius-Radom (1401), and Horodło (1413) were stages in negotiation, not a straight path toward merger. At Ostrów Vytautas was restored as ruler under Jogaila. At Vilnius-Radom he was recognized as grand duke while Jogaila's superior dynastic position was preserved. At Horodło, 47 Catholic Lithuanian boyar families were adopted into 47 Polish heraldic clans and offices were created in Lithuania on Polish models.

Horodło favored Catholic elites over Orthodox nobles, who were politically important but had fewer privileges. Lithuanian magnates guarded the Grand Duchy's autonomy, and Polish and Lithuanian interests often diverged. The relationship was neither a simple annexation nor a modern federation.

In Poland the nobility bargained with kings through privileges tied to royal succession and taxation: Koszyce (1374), Czerwińsk (1422), Jedlnia and Kraków (1430–1433), and Nieszawa (1454). These document negotiated monarchy, with kings trading concessions for support, not a march toward noble democracy. Casimir IV became grand duke of Lithuania in 1440 and king of Poland in 1447, holding both positions. The dynasty's reach widened when his son Vladislaus II became king of Bohemia in 1471 and was elected king of Hungary in 1490, though no regional empire was inevitable. The Commonwealth of 1569 was not the necessary result.

Section 54.16

Religion, Language, and Peoples in Poland-Lithuania

The Jagiellonian realm held Latin Catholics under the Polish church hierarchy, Orthodox Christians under their own metropolitan and clergy, recently Christianized Lithuanians among whom pre-Christian practices lingered, Jews, Muslim Tatars, and Poles, Lithuanians, Ruthenians, Germans, and Armenians in towns. These categories overlapped and were not modern national identities.

Language followed function. Latin served the church and some government, Polish was widespread in Poland, Lithuanian in the Lithuanian heartland, Ruthenian (a chancery Slavonic) in the Grand Duchy's administration, German in many towns and commercial communities, and Hebrew and Yiddish in Jewish communities. No single standard language united the realm.

Jewish communities held royal privileges and worked in trade, finance, and crafts, though they also faced restrictions, hostility, and sometimes violence, with variation by locality. Tatars settled in Lithuania, served in the military, and kept their Islamic faith. Armenian merchants lived in towns such as Lwów. Orthodox nobles shared in politics but, after Horodło, lacked some privileges Catholic nobles gained.

This was not a modern multicultural paradise, nor a society of constant persecution. Diversity was governed through overlapping jurisdictions, privileges, churches, communities, and estates, a composite system for populations with different laws and languages.

Section 54.17

Scandinavia and the Kalmar Union

Late fourteenth century Scandinavia was a region of three kingdoms with powerful aristocracies and councils. Margaret, daughter of Valdemar IV of Denmark and wife of Haakon VI of Norway, gained effective authority in Denmark and Norway as regent for her son Olaf, who died in 1387. In Sweden, Albert of Mecklenburg ruled but faced aristocratic opposition. His defeat in 1389 made Margaret ruler of Sweden as well. Many Scandinavian nobles supported union because it promised stability, a shared defense against Mecklenburg and the Hanseatic towns, and favors from a strong monarch.

At Kalmar in 1397 Erik of Pomerania, Margaret's grandnephew, was crowned king of all three realms. The union meant a shared monarch, not a merged kingdom: each country kept its own laws, council, aristocracy, and traditions. The legal status of the documents associated with the arrangement is ambiguous, which left the constitutional meaning of the union open. Margaret remained the effective ruler until her death in 1412.

Erik then tried to exercise stronger royal authority. His taxation, centralizing policies, and use of foreign-born fief-holders and royal servants provoked resentment, especially in Sweden; patterns differed between the kingdoms, and foreign fief-holders were particularly prominent in Norway. A conflict with the counts of Holstein over Schleswig drew in the Hanseatic towns, which feared for their trade (see section 54.10). In 1429 he introduced the Sound toll, a commercial policy with political consequences. The Engelbrekt rebellion began in 1434 in the mining region of Bergslagen, drawing on Swedish noble and miner grievances. Erik lost support and was deposed in Sweden and Denmark in 1439 and in Norway in 1442.

Later kings, including Christopher of Bavaria and Christian I, preserved or revived the union with differing success, and Sweden contested it, notably at Brunkeberg in 1471. The union ended in Sweden only in 1523. It was neither a modern federation nor a unified Scandinavian state, nor simply Danish conquest. It depended on bargaining among monarchs, aristocracies, councils, towns, and regions, and separate political communities persisted under shared kingship.

Section 54.18

Bohemia, Hungary, Novgorod, Muscovy, and the Eastern Political Frontier

These four cases show additional political forms in central and northeastern Europe. In Bohemia, after the Hussite conflicts treated in Chapter 53, the estates were central and the kingdom remained religiously divided between Utraquists and Catholics. In 1458 George of Poděbrady, a native nobleman, was elected king and tried to govern across the divide. The papacy and foreign opponents resisted him, and Matthias Corvinus intervened and claimed the Bohemian crown, with a settlement negotiated in 1478 and formally confirmed at Olomouc in 1479 recognizing both rulers' claims: Vladislaus II retained Bohemia proper, while Matthias held Moravia, Silesia, and Lusatia, and both continued to use the title king of Bohemia.

Hungary was a large composite kingdom with a powerful nobility, facing Ottoman pressure (see Chapter 41). After John Hunyadi, Matthias Corvinus was elected in 1458 and strengthened royal government through taxation, administration, patronage, and a largely professional force often called the Black Army. He captured Vienna in 1485. Much of his centralizing project did not survive his death in 1490, when nobles elected Vladislaus II, the Jagiellonian king of Bohemia, as king of Hungary (see section 54.15).

Novgorod was a wealthy northern commercial polity governed by a veche (assembly), an archbishop, a boyar elite, and a prince with limited office. "Republic" is a useful label only if it does not imply modern democracy. Its tribute networks reached far north, and the Peterhof tied it to the Hansa. Muscovy under Ivan III expanded through diplomacy, inheritance, pressure, and war. After defeating Novgorod at the Shelon in 1471, Ivan annexed it in 1478, abolishing its autonomous veche and posadnik government and imposing Muscovite authority, though urban life and trade continued. Trade did not end: the Hanseatic Peterhof was closed in 1494. Muscovy also annexed Tver (1485) and faced Khan Ahmad's Great Horde in a standoff on the Ugra River in 1480, an episode whose significance historians interpret more cautiously than the older story of the end of the "Mongol yoke." Muscovite expansion was not inevitable and should not be read from the later Russian Empire.

The comparison shows political diversity: elective and estate politics in Bohemia, royal consolidation in Hungary, an oligarchic commercial government in Novgorod, and dynastic territorial expansion in Muscovy. It is not a ranking from backward to advanced.

Section 54.19

Europe from Its "Peripheries": Networks, Composite States, and Political Diversity

The chapter's political forms were varied: Burgundian composite monarchy, powerful chartered towns in the Low Countries, Hanseatic urban and merchant networks, the Polish-Lithuanian dynastic union, Teutonic crusading territorial government, the Scandinavian dynastic union, Bohemian estate politics, Hungarian monarchic consolidation, Novgorodian commercial oligarchy, and Muscovite territorial expansion. Late medieval Europeans built power from combinations of dynasty, lordship, law, privilege, urban government, estates, commercial networks, religious institutions, war, and negotiated taxation. No single western path toward the nation-state describes them.

Comparison clarifies the differences. A composite monarchy such as Burgundy joined territories through a ruler, while a dynastic union such as Poland-Lithuania or Kalmar joined whole kingdoms without merging them. Hanseatic cooperation was a merchant network and an urban oligarchy's alliance, not a state, while the Teutonic Order combined crusade and territorial rule. Bohemia relied on estates and elections, Hungary under Matthias on stronger monarchy, and Muscovy on territorial expansion.

This returns to the question of center and periphery. Different maps of Europe produce different centers: a map of royal courts centers France and England, a map of Baltic commerce centers Lübeck, Danzig, and Novgorod, and a map of eastern diplomacy centers Vilnius and Kraków. A place could be peripheral to one system but central to another. Baltic ports were not marginal to Baltic trade, and the Low Countries were not economically peripheral because they lay outside the French royal core. Political difference does not equal developmental backwardness, and "periphery" should identify a relationship, not rank a society.

Chapter 55 turns to gunpowder, printing, stronger fiscal and military institutions, widening maritime connections, and Europe around 1500. Those developments did not make modernity inevitable.

Historical Evidence

Burgundian accounts and ordinances show administration but reflect ducal priorities. Hanseatic recesses and merchant records show negotiation among towns, mostly from elite merchants' view. Polish-Lithuanian union documents and privileges are open to disputed readings, as with Krewo, while Teutonic chronicles serve the Order's case. Scandinavian union documents have an uncertain legal status. Bohemian and Hungarian political records come largely from estates and courts, and Novgorodian chronicles and Muscovite sources reflect the later victors' perspective. State and elite records systematically underrepresent peasants, workers, women, non-elite townspeople, and minority communities.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Centers and Peripheries

What changed? The expansion and dynastic transfer of Burgundian power, the failure of Charles the Bold's territorial project, the Teutonic Order's weakening, the Polish-Lithuanian union, the Kalmar Union's repeated crises, and Novgorod's loss of independence. What remained continuous? Baltic and North Sea exchange, urban autonomy, the survival of many Burgundian institutions under later dynasties, and noble and urban bargaining with rulers. Who experienced change most directly? Subjects of the Order, residents of annexed Novgorod, and Burgundian townspeople. Who experienced more continuity? Many rural communities, whose lives are less visible in the sources. Why does it matter? It shows that "center" and "periphery" depend on the relationship studied, not on a fixed ranking.

Chapter Summary

This chapter re-centered the late medieval narrative on regions that older accounts treated as margins. Its argument is that England, France, and Italy cannot stand for Europe, and that power was built in several different ways across the north and east.

Valois Burgundy grew by dynastic accumulation, through the appanage of 1363, the Flemish marriage of 1369, and later inheritances, purchases, and wars. It was a composite principality whose territories kept separate laws and privileges, and its institutions, including councils, chambers of accounts, the Golden Fleece, and the States General from 1464, coordinated rather than unified it.

The Low Countries supplied much of the dukes' wealth. Their cities, cloth industries, and markets made them powerful bargaining partners, and Antwerp's rise and Bruges' relative decline had several causes. Charles the Bold's attempt to tie his lands together provoked resistance and ended at Nancy in 1477. His territorial project failed, yet many institutions survived and the northern lands passed to the Habsburgs.

The North Sea and Baltic formed an integrated economy in which eastern commodities and western manufactures were mutually dependent. The Hansa organized this trade through privileges, assemblies, Kontors, and collective action, without a permanent government, fixed membership, or territorial sovereignty. The Peace of Stralsund showed its leverage, and Dutch and English competition showed its limits without producing a simple decline.

Poland and Lithuania were already complex states when Jadwiga's reign and Jogaila's marriage joined them dynastically. Krewo began a long, negotiated relationship, not a merger, and Lithuania's Christianization from 1387 was gradual in a realm with many Orthodox subjects. The union later developed through Ostrów, Vilnius-Radom, and Horodło.

The Teutonic Order was both a crusading regime and a territorial state dependent on towns and agriculture. Grunwald in 1410 was a major victory but did not destroy it. Prussian opposition, the Thirteen Years' War, and the Second Peace of Thorn in 1466 produced Royal Prussia and a reduced Order under Polish overlordship.

Poland-Lithuania was religiously, linguistically, and legally plural, with Catholics, Orthodox, Jews, Tatars, and others living under different privileges. It was not a modern tolerant state, and its diversity was managed by a composite political system.

The Kalmar Union joined Denmark, Norway, and Sweden under Margaret I and Erik of Pomerania. It survived through bargaining among aristocracies and councils, and it was strained by taxation, trade policy, and the Engelbrekt rebellion. Separate communities persisted under shared kingship.

Bohemia, Hungary, Novgorod, and Muscovy offered further forms of authority: estate politics, royal consolidation under Matthias Corvinus, commercial oligarchy, and Muscovite expansion. Novgorod lost its independence in 1478, yet the Hanseatic Peterhof closed only in 1494.

Together these cases dismantle the idea of one western path toward centralized nation-states. "Periphery" is useful only for a particular relationship, since places peripheral to one system were central to another. Chapter 55 turns to the changes around 1500.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows late medieval Europe as a field of several centers, each building power differently. Burgundy, the Hansa, Poland-Lithuania, and the Kalmar Union reveal that composite and networked forms of authority were as important as the centralizing monarchies of the west.

Its outcomes shaped what followed: Charles's territorial project failed, but Burgundian institutions and the northern inheritance entered Habsburg politics, Baltic commerce kept its importance into the early modern period, and the Jagiellonian world remained a major power. None of these outcomes was predetermined. Chapter 55 now turns to the changes around 1500.
