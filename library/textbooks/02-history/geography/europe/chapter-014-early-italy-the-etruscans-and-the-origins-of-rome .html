Early Italy, the Etruscans, and the Origins of Rome

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

Rome did not begin as Rome. It emerged from a crowded, interconnected Italian landscape in which Latins, Sabines, Etruscans, Greeks, Phoenicians, and many other communities traded, intermarried, fought, and borrowed from one another, and it was only much later that Romans compressed that long process into a story of one founder, one day, and one city.

Stand in the low ground between the Palatine and Capitoline hills, at the place later Romans called the Forum, and the ground itself tells a story that the ancient literary tradition does not. Beneath the paving of later centuries, excavators have found early Iron Age burials, traces of huts on the surrounding heights, and, above them, successive layers of drainage, gravel surfaces, and eventually stone pavements and public buildings. This is a landscape that was occupied, abandoned, reorganized, and rebuilt over many generations. It is not the landscape of a city struck into existence on a single spring day. Yet ancient Romans, writing centuries after these layers were laid down, told of a foundation with a named founder, a fixed year, and a sequence of seven kings, and they told it with such confidence that it shaped how their descendants, and much of Europe after them, imagined the beginnings of Rome.

This chapter opens Part III by asking how Rome emerged from the world around it. It begins not with Rome but with Italy, a peninsula whose peoples spoke many languages, worshipped many gods, buried their dead in many ways, and lived in communities ranging from small villages to fortified cities. Chapters 7 through 13 traced the Aegean and Greek worlds and their Mediterranean connections; this chapter shows that Italy was already part of those networks. Greek settlers had established themselves on the Bay of Naples and in Sicily, Phoenician traders moved along the western Mediterranean, and the cities of Etruria on the Tyrrhenian coast were among the wealthiest and most cosmopolitan communities in the central Mediterranean. Rome grew up beside them, in a region where these currents of trade, technique, religion, and political practice met.

The chapter's central argument is that Rome was formed through interaction rather than created in isolation. Latin speakers of the lower Tiber, Sabine and other neighbors of the interior, Etruscan cities to the north, and Greek and Phoenician merchants along the coast all contributed to the material, religious, and political world in which Rome became a city. This does not diminish what was distinctive about Rome. It locates that distinctiveness in a process of adaptation and combination rather than in a mythic moment of origin. It also requires a disciplined approach to evidence, because the sources for early Rome are unusually uneven. Archaeology provides real information about settlement, burial, trade, building, and urban change, but it does not name kings. The Roman literary tradition names kings and narrates their deeds in detail, but it was written down long after the events it describes, by authors who reworked older memory, family tradition, and invention into a coherent national story.

Students should therefore approach this chapter as an exercise in historical method as much as in narrative. Two bodies of evidence, the archaeological record and the Roman annalistic tradition, are examined side by side, and the chapter repeatedly asks what each can and cannot establish. The date 753 BCE for the foundation of Rome, and the date 509 BCE for the expulsion of the last king, are treated as traditional dates that Romans themselves later fixed, not as independently verified events. The Etruscans, too, require careful treatment. Older textbooks often presented them as a mysterious people who arrived from the east and conquered their neighbors. Modern linguistic, archaeological, and genetic evidence points instead to a population deeply embedded in central Italy, speaking a language unlike its Indo-European neighbors, and participating in a shared Mediterranean world of exchange.

The chapter follows a broad path. It surveys the geography and peoples of Italy, the Bronze Age and early Iron Age cultures of central Italy, and the rise of the Etruscan cities. It then narrows to Latium and the site of Rome, examines the transformation of hill settlements into an urban community, and considers the foundation stories Romans later told about themselves. It treats the traditional monarchy as a problem of evidence rather than a list of biographies, and it examines the sixth century, when Rome acquired monumental public architecture and strong Etruscan connections. It ends with the traditional end of kingship and the earliest phase of republican government, stopping deliberately before the mature political system that Chapter 15 will examine.

Within Part III, this chapter provides the starting conditions. Chapter 15 will explain the magistracies, assemblies, Senate, and social conflicts of the Republic, Chapter 16 will trace the conquest of Italy and the struggle with Carthage, and Chapter 17 will follow Roman power across the Mediterranean. None of that was foreordained by anything described here. In the early fifth century BCE, Rome was one significant city among many in central Italy, and its later Mediterranean dominance was the product of centuries of contingent political, military, and social development.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the geography of the Italian peninsula and identify the principal peoples and language communities of early Italy without projecting a unified Italian identity backward.
  2. Distinguish archaeological culture labels such as Latial and Villanovan from ethnic or political identities.
  3. Explain the rise of the Etruscan cities as autonomous urban communities rather than a centralized Etruscan state or empire.
  4. Evaluate competing explanations of Etruscan origins, separating questions of language, ancestry, and cultural identity.
  5. Analyze Etruscan participation in Mediterranean trade and the multidirectional cultural exchange among Etruscans, Greeks, Phoenicians, and Latins.
  6. Assess the probable and attested Etruscan influences on Roman religion, political symbolism, and architecture without overstating them.
  7. Trace the transformation of scattered settlements on the Tiber into an urban community, using archaeological evidence.
  8. Interpret the Romulus, Remus, and Aeneas traditions as evidence for Roman memory and identity rather than as literal reports.
  9. Distinguish the traditional monarchy described by later Roman authors from what can reasonably be recovered about early Roman kingship.
  10. Explain why the traditional dates 753 BCE and 509 BCE are later conventions and why the Republic did not appear fully formed.
  11. Compare the kinds of questions archaeology and literary tradition can answer about early Rome.
  12. Connect the emergence of Rome to the wider Greek, Phoenician, and Mediterranean networks examined in earlier chapters.

Historical Context

By the eighth century BCE, the Mediterranean was a world of intensifying contact. Chapter 7 traced the palace societies of the Aegean Bronze Age and their connections with Anatolia, Egypt, and the Levant, and Chapter 8 followed the collapse of that order and the emergence of the Greek polis. Chapter 9 examined the Greek colonizing movements that, from the eighth century, planted communities across the central and western Mediterranean, including southern Italy and Sicily. Phoenician cities of the Levantine coast had meanwhile developed their own trading networks and settlements, reaching Sardinia, Sicily, North Africa, and Iberia. Italy stood at the center of these movements, and its inhabitants were participants in them, not merely observers.

Chapter 6 introduced the Iron Age societies of Europe and cautioned against projecting later national identities onto them. That caution applies with special force to Italy. The peninsula in the early first millennium BCE contained speakers of Italic languages such as Latin, Faliscan, Umbrian, and Oscan, as well as speakers of Etruscan, Venetic, Messapic, and other languages, alongside Greek speakers in colonies of the south and Sicily. Sicily and Sardinia had their own peoples and Phoenician presences. Communities differed in their material culture, burial customs, and political organization, and they did not think of themselves as belonging to a single "Italy." The word Italia itself originally referred to a much smaller region in the far south and was extended to the whole peninsula only gradually, a development that lies largely beyond this chapter.

The evidence for this period is unusually mixed. Archaeology provides the foundation: excavated settlements, cemeteries, sanctuaries, and workshops, together with imported and locally produced pottery, metalwork, and building remains. Writing appears in Italy from the eighth century, adapted from Greek models, and inscriptions in Etruscan, Latin, Faliscan, and other languages survive, though most early texts are short and concern ownership, dedication, or burial. Greek authors, including Herodotus, and later Roman writers, above all Livy and Dionysius of Halicarnassus in the late first century BCE, provide continuous narratives of early Rome, but they wrote some seven centuries after the traditional foundation. Their accounts drew on earlier annalists, priestly records, family memory, and Greek writing about the west, and they rationalized and dramatized what they found. This chapter treats those accounts as evidence for what Romans later believed and wanted to remember, and as possible, though never automatic, guides to earlier realities.

The geography described in Chapter 2 also matters. Italy is dominated by the Apennines, which run down its length and separate the Tyrrhenian and Adriatic sides, and by river valleys and coastal plains that supported farming and settlement. The Tiber, flowing from the Apennines to the Tyrrhenian Sea, formed a boundary and a corridor between Etruscan-speaking communities to the north and Latin-speaking communities to the south. The volcanic soils of Etruria, Latium, and Campania were productive, and central Italy contained metal ores, above all the copper, iron, and other deposits of Etruria and the island of Elba. These conditions favored the growth of substantial communities long before Rome mattered outside its neighborhood.

Section 14.1

Italy before Rome: Geography, Peoples, and Networks

The Italian peninsula extends some 1,200 kilometers from the Alps into the central Mediterranean. Its spine is the Apennine range, which divides a narrow Adriatic slope from the broader western side, where river valleys such as those of the Arno, Tiber, and Volturno open onto fertile coastal plains. The Po Valley in the north forms a great lowland of its own, while the south, with its long coastlines and volcanic soils, looks toward Sicily and the Ionian and Tyrrhenian seas. Chapter 2 stressed that such geography creates possibilities rather than destinies, and Italy illustrates the point. Its mountains divided communities but did not prevent contact, and its coasts invited seaborne exchange at exactly the moment when Mediterranean shipping was intensifying.

The peoples of early Italy are known mainly through language, archaeology, and later reports by Greek and Roman authors, and these sources do not map neatly onto one another. Speakers of Italic languages, a branch of Indo-European, included the Latins of Latium, the Faliscans just north of the Tiber, the Umbrians of the northern Apennines, and speakers of Sabellic languages who occupied much of central and southern Italy, including Umbrian, Sabine, and, farther south, Oscan-speaking communities such as the Samnites. The Etruscans spoke a language outside the Indo-European family. In the northeast, the Veneti spoke a language of their own, and in the southeast, Messapic and related languages were spoken. Greek settlers occupied parts of the south and Sicily, which later writers called Magna Graecia, "Great Greece," and Sicily also held indigenous peoples such as the Sicels and Elymians alongside Phoenician settlements. Ligurians, Celtic-speaking groups in the north, and the peoples of Sardinia belong to the wider picture.

Students should resist two temptations. The first is to treat these names as fixed ethnic nations equivalent to later peoples. Ancient names such as Sabine or Umbrian often described regional groupings, linguistic communities, or the categories Greek and Roman observers used, and individual communities might identify with a village, a city, a cult, a kin group, or a broader language community depending on circumstances. The second temptation is to read Italy's later political unity backward. Nothing in the early first millennium BCE pointed toward a single Italian state. Communities were autonomous, alliances shifted, and warfare between neighbors was common.

What united these communities, at least loosely, was participation in overlapping networks of exchange. Metals moved from the ore districts of Etruria and Elba, salt from coastal pans, grain and livestock products within regional economies, and prestige goods, including pottery, bronzes, and jewelry, along coastal and river routes. Greek and Phoenician merchants brought new products and techniques, and Greek settlers also transmitted alphabetic writing in forms ultimately derived from Phoenician script, while Italian communities produced goods that circulated in return. The later rise of Rome must be read against this background of dense, multidirectional contact.

Historical Vocabulary
Latial and Villanovan: Archaeological Culture Labels

Archaeologists group sites that share similar pottery, burial customs, and metalwork into "cultures," and name them after a region or type site. The Latial culture is the label for the early Iron Age material culture of Latium, and the Villanovan culture, named after a site near Bologna, is the label for related early Iron Age material culture in Etruria and parts of northern Italy, characterized in many places by cremation burials in biconical urns.

These are modern classifications of objects, not names that ancient people gave themselves. A Villanovan cemetery is not evidence of a separate "Villanovan people" who were later replaced by Etruscans. In core Etruria, strong archaeological continuity connects Villanovan communities with the later Etruscan cities, but the Villanovan label should not itself be treated as an ethnic identity. Treating culture labels as ethnic groups is one of the commonest errors in older accounts of early Italy, and the chapter uses these labels only to describe archaeological patterns.

Section 14.2

Bronze Age and Early Iron Age Central Italy

Bronze Age communities in central Italy practiced mixed farming, keeping cattle, sheep, goats, and pigs and growing cereals and legumes, and they worked bronze produced from local and imported metals. Settlements ranged from small villages to larger fortified sites on defensible hills, and material connections with the Aegean, evident in some imported pottery of the later second millennium, show that Italy was not cut off from the eastern Mediterranean even before the Iron Age. Chapter 5 traced the wider Bronze Age patterns of metallurgy, hierarchy, and exchange, and central Italy fits within them. Settlement activity is documented on and around the site of Rome in this period, though it consists of dispersed occupation rather than anything resembling a city.

Around the beginning of the first millennium BCE, the archaeological record of central Italy changes. Communities in Etruria and Latium, as elsewhere in Italy, adopted iron working gradually, and settlement patterns shifted toward larger, often plateau-based communities. Burial practices become one of the richest sources of evidence. In many Villanovan contexts, the ashes of the dead were placed in distinctive biconical urns, sometimes covered by a helmet-shaped lid, and buried in cemeteries with modest grave goods. In Latium, both cremation and inhumation appear, and the choice varied by place and period. Cemeteries allow archaeologists to study age, sex, and apparent status, and they show growing differentiation. Some graves contain weapons, ornaments, or fine metalwork, while others contain little, indicating emerging elites.

Latial settlements such as those in the Alban Hills, and communities like Gabii, have been extensively studied, and excavations at cemeteries like Osteria dell'Osa near Gabii have shown how households and kin groups marked identity and status in burial. On the site of Rome, the early Iron Age is documented by hut foundations on the Palatine and by burials in the low ground of the later Forum, which was, in this period, a burial area rather than a public space. These finds show that the future site of Rome hosted communities on its hills from at least the tenth and ninth centuries BCE, and that during the eighth century BCE settlement became denser and more organized. Their exact relationship, whether a single community or several allied ones, is not something the archaeological evidence can settle unaided.

The seventh century BCE brought further change. Elite burials in Etruria and Latium became richer, with imported and locally produced luxury goods, in what scholars call the orientalizing period, because of the prominence of styles and objects influenced by Near Eastern and eastern Mediterranean models. Chamber tombs, chariots, bronze vessels, gold jewelry, and imported ivory and faience appear in the wealthiest graves. Such display implies aristocratic groups competing for prestige and using foreign connections to underline it. Warfare, kinship, and inherited status were central to these societies, and the growth of cities followed in part from the consolidation of such elites.

Section 14.3

The Rise of the Etruscan Cities

Etruria, the region between the Arno and Tiber rivers and the Tyrrhenian coast, developed some of the earliest and most impressive urban centers of central Italy. Tarquinia, Caere (modern Cerveteri), Vulci, Veii, Volsinii, Volterra, Populonia, Vetulonia, Chiusi, and other communities grew from Villanovan settlements into cities with extensive urban territories, organized cemeteries, sanctuaries, and, by the sixth century, planned street layouts at sites such as Marzabotto in the Po Valley region. Veii, on the northern side of the Tiber only a short distance from Rome, was among the largest, and its history is intertwined with Rome's in later centuries.

The economic base of these cities combined agriculture with the exploitation of metals and maritime trade. Etruria's fertile volcanic plains supported grain, olives, vines, and livestock, while the metal deposits of the Colline Metallifere, Elba, and adjacent areas supplied copper, iron, and other metals. Populonia in particular became associated with iron working, and archaeologists have found extensive traces of smelting. Coastal cities such as Caere and Tarquinia had ports, and their access to the sea connected them to Greek, Phoenician, and other traders. Wealth is visible in cemeteries. The Banditaccia necropolis at Caere, with its cemetery of tumuli and streets of tombs, and the tomb known as the Regolini-Galassi tomb, dating to the seventh century BCE and containing rich gold and bronze objects, illustrate the display of aristocratic families in this era. Tarquinia is renowned for painted tombs, whose frescoes depict banquets, dances, athletic contests, and hunting scenes.

Politically, Etruscan cities were independent communities, each with its own territory, institutions, and ruling elite. Kingship appears to have existed in early Etruscan cities, followed, in many, by magistracies held by aristocratic officeholders, though the evidence is thin and the political arrangements varied by city and period. Later Greek and Roman writers spoke of a group of twelve Etruscan cities that met at a sanctuary of Voltumna, a tradition suggesting a religious and perhaps diplomatic association. It is important not to inflate this into a state. The tradition speaks of shared cult and occasional cooperation, not of a central government, a common army, or an empire. Etruscan cities competed with one another, sometimes fought each other, and allied with or opposed outside powers according to their own interests.

Etruscan influence and settlement also extended beyond Etruria proper, into the Po Valley in the north and into Campania in the south, where cities like Capua were closely tied to Etruscan culture. This expansion should be understood in terms of trade, settlement, and cultural connection, and not as conquest by a single Etruscan power. The picture that emerges is of a network of prosperous, autonomous cities with shared language and religious traditions, integrated into wider Mediterranean commerce and vulnerable, in later centuries, to pressure from Greek, Latin, Samnite, and Celtic neighbors.

Section 14.4

Etruscan Language, Origins, and Identity

The Etruscans, whom the Romans called Tusci or Etrusci and the Greeks Tyrrhenoi, spoke a language that belongs to no Indo-European branch. Etruscan is known from thousands of inscriptions, most of them short, written in an alphabet adapted from a Greek model, probably that of Euboean Greeks in the west, from around 700 BCE. Because the inscriptions are mostly funerary, dedicatory, or concerned with ownership, and because few long texts survive, scholars can read the script and understand much of its vocabulary and grammar but cannot translate every text with confidence. Etruscan is generally classified with the small Tyrsenian family, which also appears to include the language of a stele from the Aegean island of Lemnos and the Raetic language of the Alps. How this family relates to the wider linguistic history of Europe remains debated.

The question of where the Etruscans came from is one of the most persistent in ancient history, and it illustrates how easily different questions can be confused. The fifth-century Greek historian Herodotus reported that the Etruscans were descendants of Lydians who, driven by famine, migrated from Anatolia under a leader named Tyrrhenus. Another Greek writer, Dionysius of Halicarnassus, argued centuries later that the Etruscans were indigenous to Italy, and that the differences between their language and customs and those of other peoples supported this view. Modern scholars long debated these positions, and the older "eastern origin" model was often presented in popular literature as an established fact, with the Etruscans portrayed as mysterious eastern newcomers.

Current evidence does not support that picture. Archaeologists have found continuity between Villanovan material culture and the later Etruscan world, with no sign of a sudden break of the kind that a large migration might produce. Recent studies of ancient DNA from individuals buried in Etruscan and other central Italian contexts, notably a large genomic study published in 2021, found that sampled Iron Age Etruscans were genetically very similar to their Latin-speaking neighbors and showed no evidence of a recent mass migration from Anatolia. They shared the mixed ancestry typical of Iron Age central Italy. These results do not mean the question is closed in every respect, since sampled individuals cannot represent an entire population, and the origins of the Tyrsenian languages remain unexplained. But the genomic evidence strongly supports substantial local population continuity in Iron Age central Italy and does not support a recent mass migration from Anatolia as the origin of the sampled Etruscan-associated populations.

The general lesson is that language, ancestry, and cultural identity are separate matters. A community may speak a language unrelated to its neighbors while sharing much of their biological ancestry, and communities with different ancestry may share language and culture. The Etruscan case reminds students that the ancient sources, including Herodotus, offered explanations shaped by Greek assumptions about migration and by the tendency to explain unfamiliar customs through foreign origins, and that modern evidence must be assessed on its own terms.

Historical Debate

Where Did the Etruscans Come From?

The question: Did the Etruscans arrive in Italy from elsewhere, as Herodotus's Lydian story suggests, or did their distinctive culture and language develop within Italy? And do the answers for language, ancestry, and culture necessarily coincide?

Interpretation A — Eastern origin or significant eastern influence: Supporters of migration theories pointed to Herodotus, to the orientalizing character of seventh-century Etruscan display, and to the non-Indo-European character of the language, arguing that a distinct population must have come from the eastern Mediterranean or Anatolia.
Interpretation B — Local development within Italy: Others, following Dionysius of Halicarnassus in antiquity and archaeological continuity from Villanovan contexts in modern research, argue that Etruscan culture developed in place, drawing on Mediterranean contacts, and that oriental elements reflect trade and imitation, not settlement. Recent ancient-DNA evidence does not support a recent mass migration from Anatolia as the origin of the sampled Iron Age Etruscan populations, though it cannot by itself resolve the prehistory of the Etruscan language.

Evidence: Both positions draw on ancient texts, archaeological sequences, linguistics, and, increasingly, genetics. They differ in how much weight they give to the ancient literary explanations compared with material continuity and genetic data.

Why it matters: How the question is framed affects whether Etruscans are pictured as foreign to Italy or as a society that developed largely through local continuity within central Italy, and whether cultural difference is confused with biological origin. The current evidence favors local development, but the linguistic puzzle remains.

Student task: Explain why a non-Indo-European language does not by itself prove a migration, and identify one further type of evidence that would help evaluate the origins of the Tyrsenian languages.

Section 14.5

Etruscans in the Mediterranean World

Chapter 13 closed by following Greek objects and forms into wider Mediterranean circulation, and Etruria was among their principal destinations. Greek painted pottery reached Etruscan cities in very large quantities from the eighth century onward, first from Euboean and Corinthian workshops and later, in the sixth century, from Athens. Some of the finest surviving Athenian vases were found in Etruscan tombs, and much of what modern museums display of Greek vase painting was recovered from cemeteries at Vulci, Caere, and elsewhere. This pattern reflects Etruscan demand, and probably also the tastes of elite families who used imported vessels in banquets and funerary ritual. It is not evidence that Etruscans passively received a superior culture.

The exchange ran in several directions. Etruscan cities exported metals and metalwork, and Etruscan bronzes, including vessels and other fine objects, circulated widely in Italy, across the Alps, and in the Mediterranean. Etruscan and Greek merchants shared or contested trade routes, and Etruscan seafaring was reputed strongly enough that Greek writers called the western sea the Tyrrhenian. Etruscan craftsmen also reworked Greek models. Local workshops produced imitations and variations of Greek pottery, adapted Greek mythological scenes to Etruscan tastes, and developed distinctive traditions in terracotta sculpture, bronze casting, and tomb painting. The mythological stories that appear on Etruscan objects were often selected and combined in ways that differ from Greek practice, suggesting active reinterpretation.

Etruscan cities also dealt with Phoenician and, later, Carthaginian traders and settlers. The best surviving evidence is a set of three thin gold tablets from Pyrgi, one of the harbors of Caere, dated to around 500 BCE. Two are in Etruscan and one is in Phoenician, and they record a dedication to a goddess, identified in the Etruscan text with Uni and in the Phoenician with Astarte, by a ruler of Caere. The tablets show a ruler of Caere expressing a cultic and political dedication in parallel Etruscan and Phoenician texts, strong evidence of close Etruscan-Phoenician and Punic connections. Later tradition also records cooperation between Etruscan cities and Carthage, as well as conflict with Greek colonies, notably at the naval battle off Alalia in the sixth century, in which Etruscan and Carthaginian forces are said to have opposed Greek settlers from Phocaea. The details rest on later Greek sources and should be treated with caution.

Etruria's Mediterranean position also drew upon its writing. As noted above, the Etruscan alphabet came from a western Greek model, and Etruscan literacy, in turn, became a source for other Italian alphabets, including that of Latin. Writing therefore entered Latium through the mediation of Greek settlers and Etruscan cities together. The lesson for the wider chapter is that Rome's early contacts were not with a single "civilizing" neighbor. They ran through a web of cities and merchants, each adapting what it received.

Section 14.6

Religion, Authority, and Etruscan Influence

Etruscan religion is known largely from archaeology, from inscriptions, from Etruscan objects, and from Greek and Roman writers, who often treated it as a body of specialized knowledge. Romans later credited the Etruscans with skill in interpreting signs of divine will. The practice of haruspicy, the examination of the entrails of sacrificed animals, especially the liver, and the interpretation of lightning and other portents, were associated with Etruscan specialists, and a bronze model of a sheep's liver from Piacenza, divided into regions labeled with the names of deities, is a famous, though much later, object connected to these practices. Augury, the interpretation of birds, was practiced widely in Italy and was not exclusively Etruscan, but the Etruscan tradition of a formal "discipline" of divination left a strong mark on Roman religious life, and the Romans regularly consulted Etruscan haruspices in times of crisis.

Etruscan temples were also distinctive. Many Etruscan temples used a high podium, a deep frontal porch, a timber and mud-brick superstructure, and richly decorated terracotta roofs. Vitruvius later described a Tuscan type with three rear chambers, and some excavated temples fit that pattern, but Etruscan temple plans varied considerably. Etruscan building traditions influenced Roman temple architecture, and the Roman Capitoline temple, discussed in 14.11, followed this tradition. Public ritual, sanctuaries, and the display of votive offerings likewise show shared practices across Etruria and Latium. Etruscan deities took names and forms that often overlapped with Greek and Italic ones, and cults could be adopted and adapted across cultural lines, including the goddess honored at Pyrgi.

Some symbols of authority that Rome later used are probably or possibly of Etruscan origin. The fasces, a bundle of rods with an axe carried by attendants of magistrates, is traditionally said by Roman writers to have come from Etruria, and bundle-and-axe forms appear in some Etruscan contexts, such as a find at Vetulonia. The curule chair, a folding seat of office, and certain forms of elite dress, such as the toga's ancestry and the purple-bordered garment worn by magistrates, are similarly linked in the ancient tradition to Etruscan models. Roman writers were, however, inclined to attribute borrowings to Etruria as an explanatory device, and modern scholars separate attested parallels from tradition. What can be said is that elite display, ritual expertise, and architecture at Rome show clear Etruscan and wider Mediterranean connections, and that Rome's aristocrats participated in a shared central Italian symbolic vocabulary.

It would be a mistake to claim that Roman religion or politics were Etruscan in origin. Roman cult included Latin and Sabine elements, and Roman priestly colleges and calendars had features that cannot be traced to Etruria. Etruscan influence needs to be weighed practice by practice, not assumed wholesale.

Section 14.7

Latium and the Communities of the Lower Tiber

Latium, the lowland region south of the Tiber and west of the Apennines, was the homeland of the Latin-speaking communities. Its volcanic Alban Hills gave the region fertile soils, while the coastal plain and river valleys offered pasture, farmland, and routes of communication. Settlements such as Alba Longa, Lavinium, Ardea, Praeneste, Tusculum, Gabii, and Rome, along with numerous smaller communities, developed in the early Iron Age. They shared a language, common cults, festivals, and a network of relationships that Roman tradition later organized into a Latin League. Latin communities were politically independent but connected by kinship, religion, and periodic cooperation and rivalry.

Rome's location gave it advantages that were significant but not decisive. It lay on the Tiber some twenty kilometers from the sea, at a point where the river could be crossed at a ford by the Tiber Island, and where routes running north to south, notably those between Etruria and Campania, intersected with the river. Later tradition associated the route known as the Via Salaria with the movement of salt inland from the coastal salterns. Whatever the precise chronology, Rome's location connected the Tiber crossing with routes between the coast, interior, Etruria, and Latium. The seven hills gave defensible ground, and the marshy ground between them could be drained. Yet many Latin communities had comparable resources, and Rome initially had no clear superiority over Gabii, Veii, or the Alban Hills communities.

Ancient tradition presented Rome as an offshoot of Alba Longa and the Latin world, and also as a mixed community incorporating Sabines and later Etruscans. Archaeology cannot confirm this genealogy, but it does show a settlement in constant interaction with its neighbors, and the later Roman practice of incorporating outsiders into citizenship, which Chapter 15 will examine, fits a pattern of mixed origins. Above all, this section reminds students that Rome should appear here as one settlement within a crowded regional landscape, not as Italy's predetermined capital.

Section 14.8

From Hill Settlements to an Urban Rome

The site of Rome comprises a group of hills, including the Palatine, Capitoline, Quirinal, Esquiline, Caelian, Aventine, and Viminal, separated by low ground and streams. On the Palatine and some neighboring heights, excavators have found traces of early Iron Age huts and other structures. In the low ground that would become the Forum, they have found a cemetery of the early Iron Age, followed by a change of use. The burials cease, the area is drained and raised, and by the end of the seventh century BCE it is given paved surfaces that mark its transformation into an open public space. This shift, from burial ground to communal center, is one of the clearest archaeological indicators that the separate hill communities were coming to act as a single community.

Scholars often use the word synoecism, a Greek term for the merging of separate settlements into one polity, to describe this process. In Rome's case the details are uncertain. Later Romans remembered a festival of the "Septimontium," a gathering associated with a number of hills or districts, which some historians take as a memory of early groupings. What the archaeology suggests is a long transition rather than an abrupt union: communities living on separate hills grew, shared cemeteries and cult places, and developed a common civic space.

By the sixth century, the evidence for urban development is substantial. Building remains in the Forum area, including the structure later known as the Regia, associated by tradition with the king and later with the chief priest, appear in this period, and there is evidence of temples, houses with stone foundations and tiled roofs, and drainage works. Terracotta roof decorations of the kind found on Etruscan and Latin buildings adorned public and religious structures. The Capitoline hill received a monumental temple, discussed in 14.11. These developments imply the mobilization of labor and resources on a scale that requires organized authority, whether royal, aristocratic, or both, and they show Rome participating in the same urbanizing trend as its neighbors.

Students should draw a methodological lesson from this section. A city can form gradually even when later tradition assigns it a single birthday. Archaeology shows occupation extending far earlier than the traditional foundation date and monumental urban development coming later. Neither fits the tidy narrative of a foundation in 753 BCE, though, as Section 14.9 explains, some scholars think elements of the tradition preserve a memory of real eighth-century change.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Hill Settlements to a Monumental City

What Changed

  • Previously separate or semi-independent hill settlements became increasingly integrated around shared public spaces
  • The Forum valley shifted from cemetery and marshy ground to drained, paved civic center
  • Simple huts gave way to stone-founded buildings, tiled roofs, temples, and monumental architecture
  • Elite differentiation intensified, and public authority became able to command large building projects

What Stayed the Same

  • The economy remained based on farming, herding, craft production, and exchange
  • Kinship groups and aristocratic households continued to organize social life
  • Latin remained the community's language, and local cults continued to shape ritual
  • Rome remained one of several comparable communities in Latium and central Italy

Who experienced change most directly: Families whose burial grounds or dwellings were displaced by drainage and building, and laborers drawn into public works, experienced change acutely, while many farming households outside the built-up area may have noticed less. Why the pattern matters: It shows urbanization as a process with uneven effects, not a founding act.

Source Method
Archaeology versus the Roman Annalistic Tradition

Historians of early Rome work with two kinds of evidence that do not translate directly into one another. Archaeology provides huts, burials, pottery, drainage channels, temple foundations, and settlement stratigraphy, the layered sequence of deposits that allows relative dating. Material archaeology reveals settlement, economy, urbanization, and material change. Epigraphic evidence can sometimes provide names, titles, and formulas, but motives and narrative sequences usually remain inaccessible without other evidence. The Roman literary tradition, as preserved in Livy, Dionysius of Halicarnassus, and others, supplies kings, battles, laws, and speeches, but it was composed centuries after the events described and shaped by later political concerns.

The guiding principle is that later Roman tradition tells us what Romans came to believe about their origins, while archaeology tells us about the material history of the site. Neither automatically confirms or refutes the other. A Palatine hut does not prove Romulus, and the absence of a documented Romulus does not disprove that eighth-century Rome underwent real change. Students should ask of each claim: is it based on excavated evidence, on a contemporary inscription, or on a tradition written down many generations later?

Everyday Life and Social History

Households and Work in Early Central Italy

Most inhabitants of early Latium and Etruria lived in villages and farmsteads, or in the dwellings of growing towns, working the land. Grain such as emmer and other wheats, legumes, and, later, vines and olives provided a base, supplemented by cattle, sheep, goats, and pigs. Seasonal labor, plowing, sowing, harvest, and pasturing, structured the year, and animal products and textiles supplemented crops. Spinning and weaving, done largely by women, are among the best-attested crafts, since spindle whorls and loom weights are common finds in houses and graves.

Houses were at first simple, with timber frames, wattle-and-daub walls, and thatch, and later, in the sixth century, more substantial, with stone footings and tile roofs in some towns. Households were organized around families, extended kin, and dependents, and probably included unfree laborers, though evidence for early dependency is limited and later Roman writers describe it in retrospective terms. Burials reveal family groups and marked differences in wealth, and grave goods, such as spindle whorls in female graves and weapons in some male graves, hint at gender roles while remaining difficult to interpret. Elite women in Etruscan tomb paintings and inscriptions are visible in banquets and public ritual, which Greek and Roman writers noted, but such elite visibility should not be turned into a claim that women had equal status with men across society. Most evidence, whether written or archaeological, was produced by or for elites, and the daily lives of farmers, laborers, and dependents must be inferred from what remains.

Section 14.9

Romulus, Remus, Aeneas, and Roman Foundation Traditions

The Romans developed several overlapping stories about their beginnings. The best known tells of twin brothers, Romulus and Remus, born to a Vestal princess of Alba Longa and the god Mars, exposed on the Tiber, suckled by a she-wolf, and raised by a shepherd. As adults they founded a city, quarreled over the site and the auspices, and Romulus killed Remus, or was involved in his death in some versions, and became the first king. Romulus was said to have populated the city by offering asylum to outsiders and then to have arranged the seizure of Sabine women for wives, which led to war with the Sabines and eventually to a joint rule with the Sabine king Titus Tatius. This story, which centers on an act of sexual violence, is a Roman narrative that explains how the community absorbed neighbors. It should be read as a memory device about mixed origins, not as a record of events.

The second major tradition linked Rome to Aeneas, a Trojan hero who fled the fall of Troy and, after wandering, settled in Latium, where his descendants founded Alba Longa. Greek writers had connected Aeneas with the west, and the story appears in Italian contexts, including terracotta representations from Veii, dating around the late sixth or early fifth century BCE and interpreted as Aeneas carrying Anchises, which suggests it circulated in Etruscan and Latin worlds before Romans adopted it as their own. Roman writers faced a chronological problem: the Trojan War was placed in the Greek tradition several centuries before 753 BCE. They solved it by inserting a line of Alban kings between Aeneas and Romulus, a good example of scholarly reconstruction in antiquity. The story later served Augustan-era literature powerfully, including Virgil's Aeneid, though that development belongs to a later chapter.

Even the date of foundation was contested. The date 753 BCE became canonical through the work of the scholar Varro in the first century BCE, but earlier writers, including Greek authors and early Roman annalists, proposed other dates, some falling a few years earlier or later, and some tied to Greek chronology. Traditions about the she-wolf were also material. The famous Capitoline Wolf was long regarded as an Etruscan work, but radiocarbon dating of casting residues strongly supports an eleventh- or twelfth-century CE date for the wolf itself, and the figures of the twins were added during the Renaissance. Nothing in these objects, or in the archaeology of the Palatine, verifies the tradition, and one should avoid treating labels such as "Wall of Romulus" as proof of the story.

Why, then, do the traditions matter? They are evidence for Roman identity. They show a community that thought of itself as formed by immigrants and outsiders, as descended from divine and Trojan ancestors, as founded in violence and fratricide, and as a place of asylum. These themes recur in later Roman political thought, and they were used, argued over, and reshaped by writers for centuries. They also illustrate how Greek stories about the western Mediterranean shaped Roman self-understanding, reminding students that Rome's memory of itself was interwoven with Greek narratives.

Historical Vocabulary
The Annalistic Tradition

The annalistic tradition refers to the Roman historical writing that organized the past year by year, listing consuls and other magistrates and recording events, in the manner of priestly records called annals. Early Roman historians, beginning with Fabius Pictor in the late third century BCE, wrote accounts of Rome's origins that later writers, including Livy and Dionysius, elaborated. Because most surviving early records were lost or unreliable, and because authors were writing centuries after the events, the tradition for the regal period and early Republic is heavily shaped by later concerns, family pride, and literary composition.

This does not mean the tradition contains nothing useful. Some elements, such as lists of magistrates, religious institutions, and ancient place names, may preserve genuine memory. But historians must test each element against other evidence, and avoid treating the tradition as a continuous documentary record.

Historical Debate

Was Rome Founded or Did Rome Form?

The question: Should the emergence of Rome be understood as a foundational event that later tradition remembered in mythic form, or as a long process of settlement aggregation and urbanization that tradition compressed into a single act?

Interpretation A — A real eighth-century transformation lies behind the tradition: Some scholars emphasize that archaeology shows significant changes in settlement on the Palatine and surrounding areas around the eighth century, and they argue that the tradition may preserve a memory of a real moment of political organization or cult foundation, even if the details are legendary.
Interpretation B — A gradual process, with monumental urbanization in the sixth century: Others stress long occupation from about 1000 BCE, slow aggregation of settlements, and the clearest evidence for urban organization only in the late seventh and sixth centuries, and they regard the foundation date and founder as later constructions that cannot be verified.

Evidence: Both positions rely on the same excavations of huts, burials, and Forum stratigraphy, and on the same literary accounts. They differ in how they date and interpret change in settlement and in how much they credit the tradition.

Why it matters: The debate concerns different kinds of historical questions. One asks whether an identifiable moment of organizational change occurred, while the other asks how urban communities normally form. Students need not choose between "Romulus existed" and "Romulus was fake," but should distinguish what archaeology can show from what tradition asserts.

Student task: Identify one kind of new evidence that would strengthen Interpretation A and one that would strengthen Interpretation B, and explain what each would still leave unanswered.

Section 14.10

The Roman Monarchy and the Problem of the Seven Kings

Roman tradition described seven kings who ruled successively: Romulus, Numa Pompilius, Tullus Hostilius, Ancus Marcius, Tarquinius Priscus, Servius Tullius, and Tarquinius Superbus. Together they were assigned some 244 years, from 753 to 509 BCE, an average reign of about thirty-five years, which is implausibly long and tidy for a sequence of rulers in an early society, especially as several are said to have died violently. Modern historians therefore doubt that the list is an exact succession with secure reign dates. Some of the kings may be historical, and the Tarquins in particular are widely thought to reflect real elite families of the sixth century, but the details of their reigns, and the attribution to each of specific institutional inventions, were shaped by later Roman interest in explaining how the city's institutions arose.

Rather than treat the kings as seven biographies, it is more useful to ask what the tradition attributes to monarchy and what can be inferred from other evidence. First, kingship combined military and political leadership. The rex led armies and judged disputes, and the later Roman word for the office carried associations of arbitrary rule that made it politically toxic. The tradition assigned military expansion and incorporation of neighboring communities to several kings. Second, kings held religious authority. The tradition credited Numa with organizing priesthoods and the calendar, and the continued existence in the Republic of a priest called the rex sacrorum, the "king of sacred rites," suggests that religious functions once belonging to the king were preserved after monarchy ended. The Regia in the Forum is associated with these functions.

Third, the tradition describes a council of elders, the Senate, advising the kings, and assemblies in which the people acclaimed or confirmed decisions. Whether these bodies existed in anything like their later form is uncertain, but some form of aristocratic council and gatherings of armed citizens is plausible for a community of this size. Fourth, kings were credited with urban works, including drainage of the Forum, construction of the Capitoline temple, and later monumental walls and sewers. Archaeology confirms substantial building in the relevant period but does not attach it to individual rulers. Fifth, the tradition stresses incorporation of outsiders: Sabines under Titus Tatius and Numa, Albans under Tullus Hostilius, and Etruscans under the Tarquins. This theme fits a Rome whose community was open to newcomers.

Students should be cautious about attributing every institution to a named king. The so-called Servian constitution, an organization of citizens by property class and military service later associated with Servius Tullius, appears in the tradition in a form closely resembling later republican arrangements and cannot be treated as securely documented sixth-century legislation. The Cloaca Maxima, the great drain of Rome, was traditionally associated with Tarquinius Priscus, but its surviving monumental form belongs to later centuries, and the earliest drainage works were probably more modest and cumulative. The Servian Wall, similarly, is named after Servius Tullius, but the major surviving circuit of masonry dates to the fourth century BCE. Individual royal stories can nevertheless illustrate how later Romans explained institutions, and are valuable as evidence for that reason.

The Lapis Niger inscription provides important evidence that a figure styled rex existed at archaic Rome. It is a fragmentary inscription in archaic Latin, found beneath a black pavement in the Forum and dated to around the sixth century BCE. Because the damaged text does not establish whether this rex was a political ruler or held another religious or magisterial office, however, it does not by itself prove the later literary model of Roman monarchy. The tradition's broad outline, kings followed by elected officeholders, is plausible, but its details cannot be trusted.

Section 14.11

Sixth-Century Rome and Etruscan Connections

The sixth century BCE is the archaeological high point of early Rome. The Forum was paved, drainage was extended, and the area's buildings were monumentalized. Above the Forum, on the Capitoline hill, a massive temple to Jupiter, with Juno and Minerva, was built on a huge podium, larger than most Etruscan temples, and decorated with terracotta sculpture. Roman tradition credited the vow and beginning of the temple to the Tarquins and dated its dedication to the first year of the Republic. Its size, plan, and decoration reflect the broader central Italian tradition of temple building, and later sources say that Etruscan craftsmen from Veii contributed to its decoration. Even if such details are traditional, the evidence for Etruscan and Latin craftsmen working across regional lines is strong.

Other signs point to connections with Etruria. Etruscan names appear in Rome, imported goods and styles circulated, and the Tarquins bore a family name resembling that of the city of Tarquinia, while Servius Tullius appears in a tradition, preserved by the emperor Claudius in a speech of the first century CE, as an Etruscan adventurer under another name. Inscriptions and pottery from Rome show mixed Latin and Etruscan elements. Roman elites clearly moved within a wider network of aristocratic families and cities.

What is not established is the traditional formula that the Etruscans conquered Rome and installed foreign kings. The mechanism of Etruscan influence is debated. It could have involved movement of elite families and craftsmen, marriage alliances, military adventurers, trade, or shared aristocratic fashion, and the presence of an Etruscan name does not prove an Etruscan national conquest. Etruscan cities were not a single power, so there was no Etruscan state to conquer Rome. Rome in this period is best seen as a substantial central Italian city that belonged to the same urbanizing world as Etruscan and Latin centers, and whose ruling groups drew on Etruscan and Latin resources alike.

Rome's growing role is visible in other evidence. A treaty between Rome and Carthage, which the Greek historian Polybius later reported having read on a bronze tablet and dated to the beginning of the Republic, shows Rome treated as a significant Latin power by a Mediterranean state, though scholars debate details of its date and wording. Tradition also speaks of Roman dominance over neighboring Latin communities, a claim that should be treated cautiously. Rome was a major city, but it was still one power among many in a competitive landscape, and later chapters show that its dominance was neither immediate nor certain.

Multiple Perspectives

Living in Early Italy: Perspectives on a Changing Rome

From the perspective of a Latin farmer near the Alban Hills: Rome is a large and growing neighbor whose markets, cults, and military demands affect family life. Farming, the seasonal calendar, and local kin ties matter more than any distant city, though obligations to a stronger community may include labor or service in war.

From the perspective of an elite woman as represented in Etruscan or Latin funerary evidence: Rich tombs and inscriptions display her family's status, and she appears in banquets and ritual, but such evidence reflects elite self-presentation and says little about most women.
From the perspective of a laborer or dependent in the growing settlement of Rome: Drainage, paving, and building create heavy demands for labor, while the benefits of monumental architecture accrue mostly to ruling families. Few sources record this experience directly.
Section 14.12

The End of Kingship and the Earliest Republic

According to Roman tradition, the last king, Tarquinius Superbus, "Tarquin the Proud," ruled harshly, and the end of his rule began with an outrage. His son Sextus Tarquinius raped Lucretia, a Roman noblewoman, who told her family and then killed herself. Her kinsmen, including Lucius Junius Brutus, swore to end monarchy, roused the people, and drove out the royal family. The community then elected two annual magistrates, later called consuls, the first of whom were Brutus and Lucretia's husband Collatinus, according to the usual account. Tradition dated these events to 509 BCE and treated them as the founding of the Republic, a "free" state in which no single person held permanent power.

This narrative bears the marks of later composition. Lucretia's story serves as an explanation of political change through personal outrage, a familiar pattern in ancient history, and the neat pairing of a virtuous woman's death and a political revolution suits later Roman moral concerns. The traditional date is one of the anchor points of Roman chronology, supported by the list of annual magistrates known as the Fasti, but the earliest portions of that list were reconstructed and are less reliable. The tradition about the Capitoline dedication and the treaty with Carthage reported by Polybius are compatible with major political change around the turn of the century, but they do not independently verify that monarchy ended in exactly 509 BCE. It is prudent to say that a transition away from kingship toward a republican system of aristocratic officeholding occurred around the end of the sixth century, while the precise form and chronology of the earliest offices remain debated. The date 509 BCE is the traditional one.

Historians also debate how sudden the change was. Some see a real political revolution led by aristocratic families who resented royal power. Others suggest a more gradual evolution, perhaps involving external pressures, the fall of Etruscan-linked dynasties in a shifting regional order, and struggles among elites. Roman tradition mentions Lars Porsenna, an Etruscan ruler of Clusium, who is said to have attacked Rome after the expulsion, and some modern scholars suspect that Rome may have suffered a setback in this period, though the evidence is poor. Whatever the process, political power appears to have become more broadly distributed among aristocratic office-holders. The annual and collegial arrangements familiar from the later Republic may have developed through stages rather than appearing fully formed at once.

Continuity was as important as change. Religious institutions persisted, including the rex sacrorum, the priestly colleges, and the public cults. Aristocratic families, who had dominated the monarchy, continued to dominate the new order, and the community that had grown up in the sixth century continued to live in the same city, worship in the same temples, and farm the same land. The earliest republican offices may have had different titles and structures from those later described, and the Roman tradition of consuls in the first years may be a projection of later arrangements onto an evolving system. The Republic did not appear in 509 BCE fully formed with the mature constitution described by later writers. Struggles between patricians, who dominated early office, and other citizens, the development of assemblies, and the growth of new magistracies belong to the following centuries and to Chapter 15.

Primary Source Window

Livy, From the Foundation of the City, Books 1 and 2, on the End of Kingship

Creator: Titus Livius (Livy), Roman historian from Patavium (Padua) · Date: written in the late first century BCE and early first century CE, some five hundred years after the traditional expulsion of the kings · Type: Narrative history in Latin, organized in books covering Rome's history from its foundation

Context: Livy wrote under Augustus, at a time when Romans were reflecting on the origins of their political order after the civil wars examined in Chapters 19 and 20. He drew on earlier historians and antiquarian traditions and did not have contemporary records of the sixth century. His account of the fall of the monarchy ends Book 1 with the story of Lucretia and the expulsion of the Tarquins, and Book 2 begins with the creation of annual magistrates.

Paraphrase of Livy, Books 1 and 2 Livy presents the end of monarchy as a moral and political turning point. After Lucretia's death, Brutus leads the people in swearing to end royal rule, and the city then turns from the rule of kings to that of annually elected officials. Livy emphasizes that the beginnings of Roman liberty lay in the annual limitation of office and the authority of law, more than in any reduction of the power that individual officials held. This is a summary of Livy's argument, not a translation of a continuous passage.

Analysis: The passage is valuable as evidence for how educated Romans of Augustus's time understood the origin of their political order: as a founding act of liberty against arbitrary rule, driven by aristocratic virtue. It is much less reliable as evidence for what happened around 509 BCE. Livy wrote for a Roman audience with contemporary concerns, and his account of the early Republic likely projects later institutions backward. Perspectives missing include those of non-elite Romans, women other than Lucretia as a symbol, and neighboring communities.

  1. What does Livy's account say about how the monarchy ended and what replaced it?
  2. Who created this account, when, and for what possible purposes?
  3. What can historians learn from it about Roman self-understanding in the age of Augustus?
  4. What can historians not establish from this source alone about the sixth century BCE?
Cause and Effect

The End of Monarchy at Rome

Long-term causes

The growth of a wealthy, competitive aristocracy, the urbanization of Rome, and its involvement in a network of Etruscan, Latin, and Mediterranean elites created, historians have proposed, a ruling class able to limit any one ruler's power.

Short-term causes

Roman tradition points to the tyranny of Tarquinius Superbus and the outrage against Lucretia, while modern scholars also consider elite rivalries, regional political change, and possibly military pressure from neighbors.

Event or transformation

Around the end of the sixth century, traditionally 509 BCE, kingship gave way to a republican system of aristocratic officeholding, whose earliest structure and tenure remain uncertain.

Immediate effects

Political authority passed to aristocratic office-holders, while priestly and cultic institutions continued and the community's urban life carried on.

Long-term consequences

The rejection of kingship became a foundation of Roman political ideology and a reference point for later debates about liberty, power, and monarchy, including under Augustus.

Historical complexity

The change cannot be reduced to one scandal or one leader. It involved social structure, elite competition, regional politics, and the later shaping of memory.

By the early fifth century BCE, Rome had become an urban community whose institutions, population, religion, and material culture reflected centuries of interaction among Latin, Etruscan, Greek, and other Italian worlds. Kingship had disappeared according to Roman tradition, but the political order that replaced it was still evolving. Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Rome emerged through interaction among the diverse societies of early Italy and the wider Mediterranean, not through the act of a single founder. Italy in the early first millennium BCE was a mosaic of Latin, Faliscan, Sabellic, Etruscan, Venetic, Greek, and other communities, linked by exchange but politically independent, and nothing in this landscape pointed to eventual unity under Rome.

Bronze Age and early Iron Age central Italy saw the development of larger settlements, iron working, increasingly differentiated burials, and, in the seventh century, the aristocratic display of the orientalizing period. Archaeological culture labels, especially Latial and Villanovan, describe patterns in objects and burials and should not be treated as ethnic identities, although strong continuity connects Villanovan Etruria with the later Etruscan cities.

The Etruscan cities, including Tarquinia, Caere, Veii, Vulci, and Populonia, were independent urban communities with wealth based on agriculture, metals, and trade. The tradition of twelve cities reflects shared cult and occasional cooperation rather than a centralized state. Etruscan language belongs to the non-Indo-European Tyrsenian family, and ancient-DNA evidence does not support the older theory of a recent mass migration from Anatolia, though the language's ultimate prehistory remains unresolved. The chapter emphasized that language, ancestry, and cultural identity are different questions.

Etruscan cities participated actively in Mediterranean exchange with Greeks and Phoenicians, importing Greek pottery, exporting metals and metalwork, adapting alphabetic writing from western Greek models, and reinterpreting Greek imagery. They influenced Roman religion, symbols of office, and temple architecture, though many claims of Etruscan origins for Roman institutions come from ancient tradition and must be weighed carefully.

Latium was a region of many Latin communities, and Rome was one settlement among them, favored by a river crossing and trade routes but not predestined for leadership. Archaeology shows hill settlements from about 1000 BCE, a Forum valley that shifted from cemetery to drained, paved civic space by the late seventh century, and substantial urban development in the sixth century. A city can form gradually even when later tradition assigns it a single birthday.

Roman foundation traditions, including the stories of Romulus and Remus and of Aeneas, are valuable evidence for Roman identity, emphasizing mixed origins, asylum, divine ancestry, and violence, but they cannot be treated as reports of events. The date 753 BCE became canonical through later scholarship, and competing dates existed in antiquity.

The tradition of seven kings is not reliable as an exact succession, and institutions should not be automatically attributed to named rulers. Nevertheless, evidence such as the Lapis Niger, the continuing rex sacrorum, and the sixth-century monumentalization is compatible with a real monarchic phase of Rome, though its precise character remains uncertain. The idea that Etruscans conquered Rome and imposed kings is a simplification. Etruscan connections were real, but their mechanism is debated, and Rome should be seen as part of an urbanizing central Italian world.

The traditional end of kingship in 509 BCE, associated with Lucretia and Brutus, is a later Roman narrative around a traditional date. Kingship gave way to a developing republican system of aristocratic officeholding, while the mature annual and collegial arrangements emerged over time. Continuity in religion, elite power, and urban life was strong, and the mature Republic developed over centuries.

Methodologically, the chapter has taught students to compare archaeology with the annalistic tradition, asking what each can show. Later Roman tradition reveals what Romans came to believe about their origins, and archaeology reveals settlement, economy, and material change, and neither automatically translates into the other.

The chapter's historical significance lies in showing Rome as a product of Italian and Mediterranean connection at the end of the period covered by Volume I's early chapters and the start of Part III. Its later Mediterranean dominance, examined in Chapters 16 and 17, was not inevitable, and its political order, explained in Chapter 15, was still taking shape.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it changes the question. Instead of asking who founded Rome and when, it asks how a city forms, and it shows that the answer lies in settlement, exchange, and elite competition within a wider Italian and Mediterranean world. That shift is important for the whole of Volume I, which has moved from prehistoric Europe through the Aegean and Greek worlds. Italy's Etruscan, Latin, and Greek communities show that the ancient Mediterranean was a connected space, and that Rome grew within it.

The chapter also demonstrates a method that recurs throughout this textbook. Later tradition and material evidence answer different questions, and historians must resist letting one stand in for the other. The habit of separating what a community believed about its past from what can be reconstructed of that past is as necessary for the Carolingians, the crusaders, and the nation-builders of later volumes as it is for Romulus.

Finally, the chapter shows that the political forms Romans later prized, republic over monarchy, were constructed gradually from existing aristocratic, religious, and civic materials. Chapter 15 examines the institutions that resulted, and Chapters 16 and 17 follow the expansion that transformed a Tiber city into a Mediterranean power. None of that was foreordained. The Rome of the early fifth century BCE was one city among many, and understanding its contingent beginnings is the necessary foundation for understanding the empire that followed.
