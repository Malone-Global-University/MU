Chapter Opening

A government that kept the vocabulary of the Republic while concentrating authority in a single office, a legal code that confirmed equality among male property holders while subordinating wives to husbands, and an empire whose administrators carried French law across much of the continent while its recruiting officers and tax collectors made that empire hated: Napoleonic rule was all of these at once, and the chapter ahead asks how such a system was built and why its strengths and its weaknesses grew from the same roots.

In February 1800, a law adopted in Paris provided that every department of France would be administered by a prefect appointed by the central government. The law of 28 Pluviôse of Year VIII was a dry administrative text, and few contemporaries would have recognized it as a turning point. Yet it embodied the method that would define the next fifteen years. Departments had been created by the Revolution in 1790, as units of an elected and decentralized order. The Consulate kept the departments and inverted their purpose. Officials named in the capital, answerable to ministers, would now collect taxes, report on public opinion, find conscripts, and carry out decisions made far away. The framework of the Revolution survived, but the principle that animated it had changed.

This chapter examines that transformation between the coup of Brumaire in November 1799 and the threshold of the Russian campaign in 1812. Napoleon Bonaparte, a general who had risen through the wars of the Republic, took power in a country exhausted by a decade of upheaval, financial improvisation, political violence, and war. Within five years he had become Emperor of the French. Within ten, he had placed relatives on thrones from Naples to Westphalia, annexed Belgium, Piedmont, Rome, Holland, and the north German coast to France, dissolved the Holy Roman Empire, and drawn much of continental Europe into a hierarchy of annexed departments, allied monarchies, and dependent states. An official-style tabulation for 1812 counted 42,390,718 inhabitants in 130 French departments, excluding dependent kingdoms and separately administered provinces; broader figures for territories under French influence require a different and explicitly defined denominator.

Three things are often conflated in discussions of this period, and keeping them apart is the chapter's principal analytical task. The first is the personal authority of Napoleon, which rested on military prestige, on his skill at self-presentation, and on his willingness to concentrate decisions in his own hands. The second is the set of governmental institutions developed under the Consulate and Empire: the prefectural system, the Council of State, the Civil Code, the Banque de France, the lycées, the Concordat, and the apparatus of police and censorship. Many of these institutions outlived Napoleon by generations. The third is the larger system of annexed territories, client kingdoms, and allied states dominated by France, which depended on armies and diplomacy and did not outlast his fall. A student who treats these three as one will conclude either that Napoleon was a tyrant whose every act was ephemeral or a modernizer whose every act was progressive. The evidence supports neither conclusion.

The chapter gives particular weight to the contradictions of the regime. The Napoleonic Civil Code, enacted on 21 March 1804, abolished legal privilege by birth and protected property and contract, yet it strengthened the authority of husbands and fathers. The Concordat of 1801 ended the religious conflicts that had divided the country since the Civil Constitution of the Clergy, yet it placed the Church under state supervision. The regime consolidated the abolition of feudal dues, yet it also restored slavery in several colonies and, in Saint-Domingue, fought a war that ended in Haitian independence in 1804. The Empire spoke the language of merit and offered careers to talent, yet it created a new hereditary nobility. These were not accidents or exceptions to Napoleonic government. They were its characteristic combination.

Beyond France, the effects of the system varied enormously. In the Kingdom of Italy, in the Confederation of the Rhine, in the Kingdom of Westphalia, and in the Duchy of Warsaw, French law and administrative practice interacted with local dynasties, social orders, churches, and elites in very different ways. In the Duchy of Warsaw, personal servitude was abolished on paper in 1807 while landowners retained their economic power. In Spain, the installation of Joseph Bonaparte in 1808 created a crisis of legitimacy that no administrative reform could solve. The chapter therefore treats Napoleonic Europe as a hierarchy of territories and governments, not as an enlarged France.

Historians reconstruct this period from an unusually rich body of evidence. Statutes, decrees, constitutions, and treaties survive in official bulletins and modern collections. Prefects' reports, police bulletins, and the correspondence of ministers reveal how the state saw its subjects, and how local officials interpreted central instructions. Plebiscite registers record the regime's claims to popular consent, though they must be read with skepticism. Tax and conscription records offer quantitative evidence. Memoirs, letters, and diaries give individual testimony, though many of the most famous memoirs, including Napoleon's own dictated reflections at Saint Helena, were written to shape reputation. Paintings, medals, buildings, and ceremonies show how the regime wished to be seen. Each of these sources reflects a particular viewpoint, and the chapter returns repeatedly to the question of whose experience a given source preserves and whose it omits.

Within Part XIV, this chapter follows Chapter 89, which examined the Terror, Thermidor, the Directory, and the coup of Brumaire itself, and it precedes Chapter 91, which narrates the campaigns, popular resistance, nationalist mobilization, and collapse of the imperial system. The chronology of Chapters 90 and 91 deliberately overlaps. This chapter explains how Napoleon's system was constructed and governed, and stops at its apparent height around 1811 and 1812. Chapter 91 explains how that system was fought over and destroyed, and Chapter 92 examines how the powers of Europe attempted to rebuild an order afterward. Within Volume IV, the chapter marks the point at which the Revolution's political and legal experiments were consolidated, narrowed, and exported, setting terms of debate about liberty, authority, equality, and nationhood that the nineteenth century chapters will take up.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Napoleon and his collaborators converted the political crisis of the Directory into a constitutional order based on executive supremacy.
  2. Distinguish the Consulate of Year VIII, the life consulate of 1802, and the hereditary Empire of May 1804 as successive constitutional stages.
  3. Analyze the prefectural system, the ministries, and the police as mechanisms of administrative centralization, and distinguish administrative effectiveness from political freedom.
  4. Assess the Concordat and Organic Articles as a settlement that reconciled the state with organized religion while preserving state supervision.
  5. Evaluate the Civil Code as a legal settlement that combined equality before the law among men with patriarchal authority within the household.
  6. Interpret the fiscal and monetary reforms of the Consulate, distinguishing currency stabilization, banking, fiscal solvency, and the wider economy.
  7. Explain the restoration of slavery in certain colonies, the Saint-Domingue expedition, and the role of formerly enslaved people in producing Haitian independence.
  8. Compare the political cultures, educational institutions, honours, and censorship of the Empire and assess how they produced loyalty and hierarchy.
  9. Distinguish annexed departments, client monarchies, allied states, and occupied territories within the Napoleonic imperial system.
  10. Contextualize the end of the Holy Roman Empire, the Confederation of the Rhine, the Duchy of Warsaw, and the Italian states within local political and social structures.
  11. Analyze the Continental System and the burdens of conscription, taxation, and occupation as sources of the empire's structural vulnerability.
  12. Evaluate competing interpretations of Napoleon's legacy using documentary evidence and appropriate historical caution.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did many political and administrative elites accept, and in some cases welcome, a strong executive after 1799, and what did they hope to gain?
  2. In what sense was Napoleonic government a continuation of the Revolution, and in what sense was it a reversal of it?
  3. How can historians separate the personal authority of Napoleon from the institutions that outlived him?
  4. What does the Civil Code reveal about the relationship between legal equality and social hierarchy?
  5. Why was reconciliation with the Church possible after 1801, and why did it not restore the Church of 1789?
  6. How should historians weigh the Napoleonic restoration of slavery and the Haitian Revolution against claims that the regime modernized France and Europe?
  7. Why did French law and administration take such different forms in annexed departments, client monarchies, and allied states?
  8. How did the same institutions that strengthened Napoleonic power produce the burdens and resentments that threatened it?
  9. What kinds of evidence are most reliable for measuring consent, coercion, and everyday experience under Napoleonic rule, and what are their limits?

Historical Context

The Revolution left Napoleon an inheritance of unresolved questions as well as institutions. Between 1789 and 1799, French governments had abolished feudal dues and privileges, redefined property and citizenship, replaced ancient provinces with eighty-three departments, nationalized and sold Church property, reorganized the clergy through the Civil Constitution of 1790, declared a republic, abolished slavery in the colonies in February 1794, and fought war against much of Europe from 1792 onward. They had also produced the Terror, repeated purges, emergency government, and a series of constitutions. Chapter 89 examined that experience. For this chapter, what matters is the legacy it created: a legal and social order that large numbers of French property holders, purchasers of Church lands, and officials wished to protect, and a political order that almost nobody trusted to endure.

The Directory, established by the Constitution of Year III, had divided power among five Directors and two councils precisely to prevent the concentration of authority that had marked the Terror. In practice, the system produced repeated crises. The government annulled elections that returned unwelcome majorities in 1797 and 1798, relied on the army to survive coups, struggled with inflation and a collapsed assignat currency, and faced persistent royalist insurrection in the west, brigandage in the countryside, and a hostile coalition abroad. By 1799, the Directory had lost the confidence of many of the very politicians who had created it. Some, such as the former abbé Emmanuel-Joseph Sieyès, were seeking a means to strengthen the executive while preserving the principal gains of the Revolution. Napoleon Bonaparte, returning from the Egyptian expedition in October 1799 with a reputation built on his Italian campaigns of 1796 and 1797, became the instrument they chose.

Students should be aware of three conventions in this chapter. First, the French Republic used a revolutionary calendar from October 1793 until the end of 1805. The Consulate's key legal texts bear dates such as 22 Frimaire Year VIII or 28 Pluviôse Year VIII. Napoleon's government restored the Gregorian calendar effective 1 January 1806. This chapter gives dates in the Gregorian system, with the revolutionary date supplied where it helps students locate documents. Second, the name Bonaparte was used for the general and First Consul, and the regime increasingly used Napoleon as a first name of sovereignty after 1804. This chapter uses Napoleon throughout for consistency. Third, "France" itself changed during these years. The France of 1800 included Belgium and the left bank of the Rhine, annexed during the Revolution. By 1811, the "French Empire" included about 130 departments reaching from the Netherlands and the Hanseatic coast to Rome and the Illyrian provinces. When a number is quoted, students must ask which France is being measured.

The chapter draws on kinds of evidence that differ from those of earlier chapters in this volume. Because the regime governed through written rules, a very large body of legislation survives: the constitutions of Year VIII and the imperial constitutional acts, the Concordat and Organic Articles, the Civil Code and its sister codes, financial laws, educational statutes, and decrees on the press. Because it governed through officials, there are prefects' reports, police bulletins, and ministerial correspondence. Because it claimed popular legitimacy, there are plebiscite registers. Because it was a regime of propaganda, there are official newspapers, army bulletins, medals, monuments, and paintings. Because it affected millions of lives, there are tax rolls, conscription lists, memoirs, petitions, and private letters. Historians treat each class of evidence differently. Legislation shows intention, not execution. Prefects' reports show what officials wished their superiors to know. Plebiscite returns are the product of managed procedures. The Napoleonic archive is vast, but it was largely created by the state that is its subject.

Finally, the chapter must be read alongside the historiographical argument that has accompanied Napoleon since 1815. Admirers have praised him as the consolidator of revolutionary equality, the founder of modern French administration, and a precursor of European integration. Critics have condemned him as a despot who confiscated the political liberty the Revolution had promised, restored slavery, subordinated women, and sacrificed hundreds of thousands of lives to personal ambition. Both traditions shaped nineteenth and twentieth century political memory, in France and beyond, and both continue to influence how the period is taught. This chapter does not adjudicate between memory and history by choosing a verdict in advance. It asks what the institutions did, who they served, and who bore their costs.

Division I · Sections 90.1 to 90.4 · 1799 to 1802

The Consulate and the Reconstruction of Centralized Government

This division explains how Napoleon converted the instability of the Directory into a political system that combined constitutional forms with concentrated executive authority.

Section 90.1

From Brumaire to the Consulate: Napoleon's Consolidation of Political Authority

The coup of 18 and 19 Brumaire, 9 and 10 November 1799, belongs to Chapter 89. What concerns this chapter is what followed. The coup overthrew the Directory, but it did not by itself create a government. On the night of 19 Brumaire, a rump of the two legislative councils, meeting under the eyes of soldiers after the chaotic scenes at Saint-Cloud, voted to replace the Directory with a provisional executive of three Consuls, Napoleon Bonaparte, Emmanuel-Joseph Sieyès, and Roger Ducos, and to adjourn the councils while two commissions prepared a new constitution. The arrangement was explicitly temporary. Most of the men who supported it expected that the general would provide force while Sieyès provided design.

Sieyès had long been one of the Revolution's leading constitutional theorists and had concluded that the Directory's divided and unstable structure could not hold. His plan, as it emerged in the weeks after Brumaire, envisaged a system in which authority would be separated into functions, with lists of eligible notables replacing direct election, and with a grand elector at the apex whose role would be largely symbolic. Napoleon rejected the figurehead. The surviving accounts of the discussions, which come largely from participants' memoirs and later reconstruction, agree that he insisted on a real executive, that Sieyès and Ducos had little leverage once the army supported the general, and that the final text was shaped heavily by Napoleon's priorities. Sieyès left the Consulate with a large estate and a seat in the Senate. Napoleon became First Consul, with the powers that the Constitution of Year VIII would define.

It is important not to read this outcome back into the first days after Brumaire. The consolidation of Napoleon's authority was a process, and the people who participated in it had varied and sometimes conflicting aims. Sieyès and his allies wanted a government that would prevent both royalist restoration and a return of Jacobin radicalism. Many deputies and administrators, including figures who had been prominent in the Revolution, hoped for a more efficient republic. Army officers sought pay, supplies, and a victorious war. Financiers and merchants, who had been hurt by the collapse of paper money and the insecurity of contracts, wanted predictable administration and respect for property. Napoleon, who understood these hopes better than most of his contemporaries, presented himself as the man who could satisfy all of them, and he appointed Jean-Jacques-Régis de Cambacérès, a jurist and former member of the Convention, and Charles-François Lebrun, a former official of the old monarchy who had served in revolutionary assemblies, as his second and third Consuls. The pairing signaled conciliation between the revolutionary and the monarchical past.

Why did a substantial portion of the political and administrative elite accept this development? The answer lies in the experience of the preceding decade. Between 1789 and 1799, France had passed through several constitutional systems, each overturned by force or manipulated by those holding power. The Directory had annulled elections twice. Provincial administration had alternated between elected bodies and emissaries from Paris. Revolutionary governments had confiscated and sold Church and émigré property, and the purchasers of those properties, along with those who had acquired offices, contracts, or standing through the Revolution, feared two possible reversals: a royalist restoration that might reclaim their holdings, and a radical regime that might return to the Terror. Napoleon's early declarations, and the actions of his government, addressed those fears. He promised to protect property, including the sale of national lands, to end factional warfare, and to defend the Republic's territorial gains. The language of national reconciliation was a political strategy, but it also corresponded to what many property holders wanted.

The early measures of the Consulate reinforced this appeal. The government repealed the law of hostages passed under the Directory, which had permitted the detention of relatives of royalists and émigrés. It lifted some of the penalties imposed on priests who had refused the oath to the Civil Constitution, and it permitted a number of individuals who had been exiled or proscribed after the coups of the Directory to return. At the same time, it purged the councils and administration of men considered unreliable, closed newspapers, and began to compile lists of eligible notables from whom officials would be selected. The first weeks of the Consulate thus showed the mixture that would persist: clemency toward those who could be reconciled, and firmness toward those who could not.

Students should distinguish the political settlement imposed after Brumaire from the functioning governmental system that developed in the following two years. The settlement was a consensus among a limited set of elites, ratified under conditions that restricted dissent. The system was something else: an apparatus of councils, ministries, prefects, and courts, gradually staffed by trained personnel and accustomed to routine. Napoleon's authority depended on both. In the first months, it rested above all on the expectation that he would deliver peace and order. As the second chapter of the Consulate showed, it came to rest on the institutional structures that now made his personal decisions the center of government, a development examined next.

Section 90.2

The Constitution of Year VIII: Executive Supremacy, Representation, and the Limits of Republican Government

The Constitution of Year VIII, promulgated on 13 December 1799 (22 Frimaire), is one of the most important and least understood texts of the period. It was drafted quickly, and it retained the vocabulary of the Republic, including references to the French people and to the rights acquired during the Revolution. But it did not include a declaration of rights, and its structure deliberately dispersed legislative functions while concentrating executive initiative. The French National Assembly's historical documentation identifies it as establishing executive predominance rather than restoring the legislative primacy associated with earlier revolutionary constitutions, and the interpretation is widely shared among historians.

At the apex stood three Consuls, appointed for ten years and eligible for reelection. The text named Napoleon as First Consul, and Cambacérès and Lebrun as Second and Third. The distinction between them was fundamental. The First Consul alone promulgated laws, appointed ministers, officials, judges (apart from certain lower magistrates), and military officers, and conducted foreign policy and the direction of the armed forces. The second and third Consuls had a consultative voice only. The constitution declared the executive to be answerable in theory to the law, but it provided no effective mechanism by which any other body could require an account from the First Consul.

Beneath the Consuls, the constitution created four bodies, each with a restricted function. The Council of State, composed of jurists, administrators, and specialists appointed by the First Consul, was given the task of drafting bills and regulations and, increasingly, of resolving administrative disputes. Because the First Consul often presided over its sessions and participated in debate, the Council became the place where Napoleon's government actually worked, and it attracted capable personnel from across the political spectrum. The Tribunate, with one hundred members, could discuss bills drafted by the Council of State and recommend their adoption or rejection, but it could not vote on them. The Legislative Body, with three hundred members, could vote on bills, but it could not discuss them. It listened to speakers from the Council of State and the Tribunate and then voted by secret ballot. The Conservative Senate, with eighty members who served for life, had the power to select the members of the Tribunate and Legislative Body from lists of candidates, to name the Consuls, and to annul acts considered unconstitutional, which in practice meant acts the executive found inconvenient.

The deliberate separation of legislative discussion from legislative voting, and of executive initiative from either, is the most distinctive feature of the system. Sieyès had designed the arrangement on the principle that deliberation, decision, and execution should reside in different hands, so that no single body could dominate. The result in practice was that the executive alone could propose laws, that those who debated could not decide, and that those who decided could not debate. The structure left the legislative bodies with little capacity for independent political action. The Tribunate in particular, which contained some outspoken former revolutionaries, came into conflict with Napoleon over bills in 1801 and 1802, and in 1802 a purge removed many of its more independent members. The Tribunate was reduced in size and eventually abolished in 1807.

The constitution also altered the principle of election. Instead of direct or two-stage voting that selected legislators, it established a system of lists. Adult male citizens in each commune selected a tenth of their number to form a communal list of notables, those lists selected a tenth to form departmental lists, and those lists selected a tenth to form a national list from which the Senate would choose officials and legislators. The theory, associated with Sieyès, was that confidence should come from below and authority from above. In practice, the system operated slowly and did not result in popular control, because the lists did not determine outcomes and because the Senate, which controlled appointments, was itself initially chosen by cooptation from a group named by the provisional Consuls. The effect was that universal male suffrage existed in name, but the meaningful decisions about who held power rested with the executive and the Senate.

The constitution was submitted to a plebiscite in early 1800, and the official returns reported 3,011,007 affirmative votes and 1,562 negatives. These figures need careful interpretation. The voting was not secret in many places, and registers were opened in communes where officials supervised signature or declaration. There was no alternative text, no organized opposition campaign, and no independent supervision. The government had already begun to implement the constitution before the vote was counted. Claude Langlois's examination of the returns found that the official 1800 affirmative total was knowingly inflated, with approximately 1.55 million affirmative votes actually recorded rather than the claimed three million; army and navy returns were an especially problematic category. This finding is more specific than the general observation that voting was public or influenced by officials. The vote therefore functions as evidence of the regime's ability to organize an expression of assent and of the willingness of many citizens to accept a settlement they did not oppose, not as a reliable measurement of enthusiasm. Later plebiscites on the life consulate in 1802 and hereditary empire in 1804 were also administered under constrained political conditions, but they should not automatically be described as having the identical level or mechanism of falsification as the 1800 count; Section 90.9 identifies their distinct reported returns.

The term "republican" retained a double meaning in this period. Napoleon continued to refer to the Republic in official acts, coins, and documents until 1804 and, in a formal sense, until 1808. For many French citizens, the Republic still meant the end of privilege and feudal dues, the sale of national lands, and the equality of citizens before the law. These were the achievements that the Consulate promised to protect. What the Constitution of Year VIII removed was the principle that political power should be answerable to elected representatives. Students should avoid two common errors. One is to treat the constitution as merely a facade without legal effect. The institutions it created, particularly the Council of State and the Senate, shaped actual governance. The other is to treat it as a continuation of republican government as the Revolution's earlier constitutions had understood it. By concentrating initiative in a single office, it marked a decisive break from that tradition.

Historical Vocabulary
Consulate, First Consul, Sénatus-consulte, Plebiscite, and Executive Authority

The Consulate (1799 to 1804) was the form of government established by the Constitution of Year VIII, headed by three Consuls of whom the First Consul, Napoleon, held decisive authority. A sénatus-consulte was a decision of the Conservative Senate that, under the constitution, could interpret or alter constitutional provisions; Napoleon used it repeatedly to amend the constitution without involving the legislative bodies. A plebiscite was a direct popular vote on a government proposal, and under the Consulate and Empire it served to ratify decisions already taken rather than to offer a real choice. Executive authority refers to the power to initiate laws, appoint officials, and direct administration, which the constitution concentrated in the First Consul.

Modern readers may take the word "plebiscite" to mean a free referendum, and the word "republic" to mean elected, accountable government. Neither assumption holds for this period. The terms matter because the regime used the language of popular sovereignty to legitimate a government in which popular participation was limited.

Section 90.3

Pacification, Security, Diplomacy, and the Search for Stability, 1800 to 1802

The Consulate's first task was to stabilize a country still living with the consequences of civil conflict. Large areas of western France, including the Vendée and the Chouannerie in Brittany and Normandy, had experienced royalist and Catholic insurrection since 1793. Banditry, desertion, and the breakdown of rural policing had made roads unsafe in many regions. Public finances were disordered. Napoleon addressed these problems through a combination of military pressure, negotiation, and amnesty. In western France, the government dispatched troops, offered terms to insurgent leaders, and in 1800 secured the surrender or settlement of most of the armed bands. Priests who accepted the new regime were permitted to resume public worship in many places. The policy did not eliminate royalist activity, but it reduced it to conspiracy rather than mass insurrection.

Political opposition remained. On 24 December 1800, a bomb exploded in the rue Saint-Nicaise in Paris as Napoleon's carriage passed, killing several bystanders. The First Consul and his police initially blamed former Jacobins, and the government deported a large number of known republican radicals without trial, using a senatorial decision to give the act a legal appearance. The police later established that the plot had been organized by royalists, and several of those responsible were executed. The episode is revealing. It shows how security concerns could be used to remove political opponents who were not responsible, and it shows the readiness of the government to bypass ordinary judicial process. It also confirmed to many property holders that the regime was acting against threats they feared.

The Consulate also pursued reconciliation with royalists and émigrés who were prepared to accept the new order. A decision of April 1802 granted an amnesty to the great majority of the émigrés, with exceptions for the most prominent leaders, on condition that they return and swear loyalty. Thousands came back, and many of them recovered unsold property or were integrated into administration, the army, and eventually the imperial nobility. This policy of "fusion" was central to Napoleon's governing method, and it served two purposes. It reduced the base of royalist opposition, and it gave the regime a pool of experienced administrators and social prestige. It also alienated some republicans who saw in it a betrayal of revolutionary principle.

Foreign policy and military success supported domestic consolidation. After taking power, Napoleon offered peace to Britain and Austria, partly as propaganda, since neither accepted. In the spring of 1800 he led an army across the Alps into Italy, and on 14 June 1800 the French defeated the Austrians at Marengo, narrowly and after a day of reversals. The victory had political value out of proportion to its military decisiveness. It strengthened Napoleon's reputation and secured the position of the Consulate. French victories in Germany, including Hohenlinden in December 1800 under General Moreau, brought Austria to terms in the Treaty of Lunéville of 9 February 1801, which confirmed French control of the left bank of the Rhine and French domination of northern Italy. The full military narrative belongs to Chapter 91, and the point here is political. Success against Austria turned a coup regime into a government associated with victory.

The Peace of Amiens, signed with Britain on 25 March 1802, ended nearly a decade of intermittent maritime and colonial war. Britain returned most of the colonies it had captured, retaining Ceylon and Trinidad, and France agreed to evacuate certain positions. The treaty was greeted with relief in both countries, and for a short period travelers crossed the Channel in large numbers. It proved fragile. Disputes over Malta, French activity in Switzerland, Italy, and the Netherlands, commercial restrictions, and mutual distrust led to the resumption of war in May 1803. But in 1802, the peace helped confirm popular confidence in the government.

Peace also altered the political dynamics of the regime. During the winter of 1801 and 1802, the Tribunate and Legislative Body had criticized or delayed some of the government's bills, including parts of the Civil Code, and Napoleon had responded with exclusions and threats. The success of the peace diminished the argument that the regime's concentrated authority was an emergency measure, and it also reduced the pressure for accountability. A leader who could claim to have ended the Revolution's wars and religious divisions found it easy to ask for longer and fuller authority, as the life consulate, examined in Section 90.9, demonstrates.

How far did popular acceptance of the Consulate reflect genuine support? The question cannot be answered with a single figure. Exhaustion with upheaval was certainly widespread, and a government that offered stability had a real appeal. Many peasants who had bought or retained land, merchants who wanted dependable money, and officials who wanted a defined chain of authority had practical reasons to support the regime. Official propaganda, the press restrictions imposed in January 1800, and the policing of expression shaped what could be said in public. And practical calculation played a role: a family that depended on an official's favor or hoped to place a son in the army had reasons to profess loyalty. Prefects' reports in the early years often describe a population more concerned with order, grain prices, and religious freedom than with constitutional theory. Historians generally conclude that the regime enjoyed substantial passive support combined with limited organized opposition, while recognizing that both the reports and the plebiscite returns were produced by officials with an interest in showing success.

Section 90.4

Prefects, Ministries, Police, and the Administrative Centralization of France

The prefectural law of 28 Pluviôse Year VIII, 17 February 1800, is the most enduring administrative creation of the Consulate. A common error should be avoided at the outset: Napoleon did not invent the French departments. They were created by the Revolution in 1789 and 1790, replacing the provinces of the old monarchy with units of roughly comparable size administered by elected councils and directories. The Consulate's contribution was to replace elected departmental administration with an official named by the central government and responsible for the entire department.

Under the law, each department received a prefect, a prefectural council to hear administrative disputes, and a general council whose members were appointed from lists of notables and whose function was to allocate taxes among the arrondissements and express wishes. Each arrondissement, a subdivision of the department, was headed by a subprefect. Each commune had a mayor and municipal council. In smaller communes, mayors and councillors were appointed by the prefect, and in larger towns by the First Consul. The result was a chain of command running from the minister of the interior and the First Consul through the prefect and subprefect to the mayor. Orders descended along it, and information ascended. Prefects were given responsibility for tax assessment and collection, public works, conscription, policing and public order, relations with the clergy, and the compilation of statistics and reports.

Prefects were drawn from diverse backgrounds: former revolutionaries, deputies, former nobles, military officers, jurists, and administrators from the old regime. Napoleon deliberately chose men from different political origins, partly to balance factions and partly because he valued competence. Many served for years. They were expected to reside in their departments, to tour them, to know the leading families, and to report regularly to Paris on public opinion, harvests, prices, health, disturbances, and the conduct of subordinate officials. The prefects' reports are among the best sources for the social history of the period, though historians must keep in mind that they were written by men whose careers depended on presenting their departments as orderly and loyal.

The central ministries gave the system its coherence. The ministry of the interior supervised the prefects and, through them, public works, education, commerce, and local administration. The ministry of finance, under Martin-Michel-Charles Gaudin, directed the collection of direct taxes through a new tax administration, discussed in Section 90.7. The ministry of war handled recruitment and supply, and the ministry of police, from December 1799, brought together intelligence, surveillance, and the control of political opposition. Joseph Fouché, a former Jacobin and member of the Convention who had held the police ministry under the Directory, was reappointed. Fouché built a network of agents and informants, compiled files on political figures, monitored the press and the theaters, and used the threat of arrest to manage opposition. Napoleon suspended the ministry in 1802, replacing it with a different arrangement, and then reestablished it in 1804 with Fouché at its head until 1810. Police authority overlapped with that of the prefects, and Napoleon, who distrusted concentration in any other hands, encouraged competition among his officials so that each would report on the others.

The capacity of the state to gather information about its population grew significantly. The regime compiled statistics on agriculture, industry, population, and public health, and it systematized registers of civil status, which since 1792 had replaced parish registers as the official record of births, marriages, and deaths. Conscription lists, tax rolls, and property records, along with the cadastre begun in the following years, made individuals more visible to the state. Historians sometimes describe this as a movement toward the "legibility" of society, a development that served the regime's military and fiscal needs and also provided the basis for later public administration.

Students should distinguish administrative effectiveness from political freedom. The prefectural system helped standardize administration, ensured that laws were applied more uniformly, and made it more difficult for local notables to obstruct central policy. It also reduced the opportunities for independent local decision-making that the Revolution's elected bodies had provided. Mayors and councillors owed their positions to superiors rather than to voters. In practice, the prefects often relied on the cooperation of established local notables, since a prefect who alienated the leading landowners of his department could not govern effectively. The system therefore produced a partnership between the state and a class of property owners, whom it enabled to participate in local government on terms set by the center. That partnership, more than any appeal to ideology, was the foundation of Napoleonic administration, and it survived, with modifications, through the political changes of the nineteenth century.

Section 90.5

The Concordat, Religious Reconciliation, and the Reorganization of Church–State Relations

The religious divisions created by the Revolution were among the most intractable problems the Consulate inherited. The Civil Constitution of the Clergy of 1790 had made bishops and parish priests elected officials paid by the state, and the requirement of an oath had split the French clergy between "constitutional" priests who swore and "refractory" priests who refused. Under the Convention, the state had gone further, closing churches, promoting de-Christianization campaigns in some places, and, in 1795, formally separating Church and state. The Directory had alternated between toleration and persecution. By 1799, many French communities, especially in the west and south, were divided by conflict over the clergy, while a significant part of the population wanted regular worship restored. The Church itself was in disarray, with bishops in exile, parishes without priests, and the property that had supported it sold.

Napoleon approached the problem with calculation rather than personal piety. He believed that religion was socially useful, that organized Catholicism could strengthen social order and the legitimacy of his government, and that the continued religious conflict was a source of royalist support. He also recognized that any settlement had to protect the purchasers of former Church property. Negotiations with the Papacy began after the election of Pope Pius VII in March 1800, and they were conducted, on the French side, by Joseph Bonaparte, the priest Étienne-Alexandre Bernier, and Cardinal Ercole Consalvi for the Holy See. The Concordat was signed on 15 July 1801.

Its principal terms were these. The Catholic religion was recognized as that of the great majority of the French, though not as the state religion. The government would nominate bishops, and the Pope would grant canonical institution. The Pope accepted the new circumscription of dioceses, which meant reducing their number to sixty and requiring the resignation of all existing bishops, including those who had emigrated. Parish priests would be appointed by the bishops with the approval of the government. Clergy would swear loyalty to the government and would be paid salaries by the state, in recognition of the loss of Church property. The Pope accepted the sale of Church property confiscated during the Revolution, and the Church renounced claims to it. The text provided that public worship would be free, subject to police regulations judged necessary for public order.

The government published the Concordat in April 1802 together with a set of Organic Articles that had not been agreed with the Holy See. The Articles established additional state controls over Catholic institutions. They required government authorization before the publication of papal bulls or the holding of councils or synods, prohibited bishops from leaving their dioceses without permission, regulated seminaries and their teaching, required a single catechism, and stipulated that civil marriage must precede religious marriage. Pius VII protested against the Articles but did not repudiate the Concordat. The Articles also contained provisions for Protestant churches, organizing the Lutheran and Reformed communions under consistories with state-appointed or approved pastors who, like Catholic clergy, were paid by the state. Jewish communities were not included in the 1802 arrangements. Their institutional position was addressed later, after the assembly of Jewish notables in 1806 and the "Sanhedrin" of 1807, in a series of decrees in 1808 that organized consistories for the Jewish communities of the Empire and, in a separate decree widely resented by Jews and later called the "infamous decree," imposed restrictions on moneylending that were initially to last ten years. The regime thus extended recognition to Catholic, Protestant, and Jewish communities while subjecting each to supervision.

Students should be careful not to interpret the Concordat as a simple restoration of the Church of 1789. The Church had lost its landed wealth, its tithes, its independent corporate status, its control over civil registration and much of education and poor relief, and its monopoly of religious life. Bishops were appointed through a process dominated by the government, and many of the new bishops came from the ranks of those who had accepted the Revolution. The state paid the clergy, which gave it leverage. At the same time, the settlement gave the Church a secure legal position, allowed the reconstruction of dioceses and parishes, and ended the schism between constitutional and refractory clergy, though not without bitterness. Some former refractory bishops, who refused to resign, formed a dissident group known as the Petite Église. For many Catholics, the reconciliation was a relief. For some republicans, it was a betrayal, and for some royalists, a surrender. The solemn Te Deum at Notre-Dame on Easter Sunday, 18 April 1802, publicly celebrated the agreement.

The Concordat remained in force in France until 1905. Its longevity reflects its character as a compromise that gave neither the Papacy nor the state everything it wanted, but allowed each to obtain what it needed. It also shows the way Napoleonic government worked: by reconciling groups through institutions that bound them to the state. The relationship was never free of tension. As Section 90.19 shows, the conflict between Napoleon and Pius VII over the Papal States, the Continental System, and ecclesiastical independence led to the Pope's arrest in 1809 and to years of detention.

The reception of the settlement varied by region, and the evidence for it comes largely from the reports of prefects and police, who were concerned with the loyalty of the clergy and the tranquillity of the parishes. In many western and southern departments, the reopening of churches and the return of priests were greeted with relief, and officials reported crowds at the first Masses held in years. In parts of the west, however, some of the faithful distrusted priests who had sworn the revolutionary oath and had now been confirmed by the Concordat, and some departments saw tension between returning refractory priests and constitutional clergy who had served in their absence. In regions with large Protestant populations, such as Alsace, the Cévennes, and parts of the south, the Lutheran and Reformed consistories gained a secure legal footing for the first time since the revocation of the Edict of Nantes in 1685 had driven Protestantism underground and the Edict of Toleration of 1787 had only partly relaxed it. Bishops appointed after 1802 were frequently men with administrative experience, and many served as partners of the prefects in maintaining order, though some, such as those who resisted government instructions during the quarrel with the Pope, found themselves in conflict with the state. The Concordat thus functioned as an instrument of governance as well as a religious settlement, and historians read it both as a restoration of religious peace and as an extension of state supervision into a sphere that had earlier resisted it.

Section 90.6

The Civil Code of 1804: Property, Legal Equality, Marriage, and Family Authority

The Code civil des Français, enacted on 21 March 1804 (30 Ventôse Year XII) and renamed the Code Napoléon in 1807, was the most influential legal text produced under the regime. Its creation reflected a long ambition. The cahiers of 1789 had called for uniform law, and revolutionary assemblies had repeatedly debated codification. In the old monarchy, France had been divided between regions governed largely by Roman law in the south and by a patchwork of customary law in the north, with different rules on property, inheritance, and marriage. Cambacérès had presented drafts to the Convention and the Directory, but political turmoil prevented their adoption.

After Brumaire, Napoleon appointed a commission in August 1800 composed of four jurists: François Tronchet, a Parisian lawyer versed in customary law who had defended Louis XVI at his trial; Jean-Étienne-Marie Portalis, from Provence, with experience of the Roman law tradition of the south; Félix Bigot de Préameneu, a jurist from Brittany, a region of customary law; and Jacques de Maleville, from southwestern France, where written Roman law prevailed. The commission prepared a draft within a few months. The text was then discussed in the Council of State, where Napoleon frequently presided, intervened, and sometimes shaped the outcome, particularly on divorce, adoption, and paternal authority. Cambacérès remained a central figure. The code that emerged, then, was the work of jurists, Council of State debate, and political direction. It should not be attributed to Napoleon personally, who did not write its articles, though his preferences influenced its content. The text passed through the legislative bodies in a series of separate laws in 1803 and 1804 and was then consolidated into a single code with 2,281 articles.

The Code's most important achievement was to provide a single body of civil law for the entire country. It confirmed the abolition of feudal rights and personal servitude, the equality of citizens before the civil law, the freedom of conscience in civil matters, and the freedom of work. It defined property in strong terms as the right to enjoy and dispose of things in the most absolute manner, within limits fixed by law, and it protected contracts. It abolished primogeniture, requiring that estates be divided among children in largely equal shares, with a portion that testators could dispose of freely. It established civil status as a matter for state registers, with marriage as a civil contract, and it provided a system for the transfer of property that gave security to the purchasers of national lands. These provisions were of fundamental importance to the rural and urban property holders who formed the social base of the regime.

The Code also illustrates sharp inequalities. In marriage, the wife owed obedience to her husband, and the husband owed protection to his wife. A married woman could not, without her husband's authorization, give, sell, mortgage, or acquire property, appear in court, or enter contracts, with limited exceptions. The husband administered the couple's property, including, under the default regime, the wife's. A wife's adultery could lead to divorce or imprisonment under more severe terms than a husband's, whose adultery counted as grounds only if he kept a concubine in the family home. Fathers held authority over children, including the power to request their detention for a limited period. The Code limited the rights of illegitimate children, who were excluded from inheritance except in restricted circumstances, and it prohibited the search for paternity. Divorce, which the Revolution had made easy in 1792, remained legal but more restricted, available by mutual consent under strict conditions and on specified grounds. It was abolished after the Bourbon Restoration in 1816, and restored only in 1884.

Other provisions confirmed the unequal position of workers. Under the Code, in disputes about wages, the employer's word was to be believed, a provision that remained in force until 1868. The law of 1803 requiring workers to carry a work booklet (livret) stamped by employers, and the continuing ban on workers' associations under the revolutionary Le Chapelier law of 1791, gave employers significant advantages. Thus the Code's guarantees of equality before the law did not eliminate inequality of condition.

A central analytical distinction follows. Equality before civil law coexisted with substantial gender inequality within the household, and with class inequality in the workplace. The Code did not simply restore the patriarchal family of the old regime. It preserved certain revolutionary principles in property, inheritance, and civil status, and it confirmed that legal privilege by birth was abolished. But it also shaped a particular vision of the family, one in which the household was a legal unit under male authority, and the state supported that authority. Many of the jurists who defended these provisions saw them as necessary for social order after the Revolution. Women's participation in revolutionary politics, including the clubs and petitions of the 1790s, and the radicalism that some of them represented, had alarmed many of the men who wrote the Code.

The Civil Code was followed by a series of related codes: the Code of Civil Procedure (1806), the Commercial Code (1807), the Code of Criminal Procedure, called the Code d'instruction criminelle (1808), and the Penal Code (1810). The Commercial Code gave businesses standardized rules for partnerships, bills of exchange, bankruptcy, and maritime commerce. The Penal Code defined offenses and penalties, including severe punishment for political crimes, and retained branding and mutilation for certain offenses, which were not abolished until later. Together, the codes created a structured legal system that made administration of law more predictable and provided templates for other jurisdictions. Their influence extended across the territories directly annexed to France and through the client states, and, through later adoption and adaptation, to Latin America, parts of the Middle East and Africa, and other regions. The pattern of adaptation was not uniform, as Section 90.16 shows, and the Code's reception depended on local conditions and elites.

Historical Debate 2

Was the Civil Code a Major Expansion of Legal Equality or a Selective Reconstruction of Traditional Authority?

The question: How should historians weigh the Code's confirmation of civil equality and property rights against its strengthening of paternal and marital authority and its treatment of workers and illegitimate children?

Interpretation A: A durable expansion of legal equality. Historians sympathetic to this view emphasize that the Code ended legal privilege by birth, created uniform law, guaranteed property and contract, abolished feudal burdens and primogeniture, and established a rational and accessible system. They point out that it provided a model for reform across Europe and beyond and that its basic structures of civil equality among men survived Napoleon.
Interpretation B: A selective reconstruction of hierarchy. Other historians, many working from gender history and labor history, emphasize that the Code reversed some revolutionary advances for women, including relatively easy divorce and equal inheritance, tied women's legal capacity to their husbands, discriminated against illegitimate children, and gave employers legal advantages over workers. They argue that the Code consolidated the interests of male property holders and treated the household as the foundation of social order.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the code's text, the Council of State debates, and later court practice. Historians of women's history also use marriage contracts, divorce petitions, and notarial records to examine how women used or evaded its rules, and legal historians compare the Code with the pre-revolutionary customary law, the revolutionary legislation, and later reforms.

Why it matters: The debate shapes how the Napoleonic legacy is understood. If the Code principally expanded equality, Napoleon appears as a consolidator of revolutionary achievement. If it principally reconstructed hierarchy, the regime appears as a settlement among property-owning men at the expense of women and workers. Many historians conclude that the Code did both, and the question is which element should be given priority in assessing its long-term effects.

Section 90.7

The Napoleonic Fiscal Settlement: Taxation, Banking, Currency, and Economic Administration

The Revolution had left French finances in a state of near collapse. The assignats, paper currency backed by confiscated national lands, had lost almost all value by 1796, and the Directory's attempts at replacement had produced bankruptcy for state creditors in 1797, when the government repudiated two thirds of the public debt. Tax collection had been disrupted by the dismantling of the old fiscal institutions and the unreliability of elected local administrations. Contractors who supplied the armies were often unpaid, and the government borrowed at ruinous rates. The Consulate's financial program, associated above all with the minister of finance Martin-Michel-Charles Gaudin, Nicolas-François Mollien at the treasury, and Napoleon's own insistence on a balanced and predictable system, addressed these problems through several distinct measures.

The first was the reconstruction of tax collection. In November 1799, the government established a central body of tax officials. Direct tax collection was placed in the hands of salaried collectors appointed by the government, working under prefects, with receivers in each department and arrondissement. The government retained the four main direct taxes established by the Revolution: the land tax, the personal and movable tax, the tax on doors and windows, and the license tax on trades and professions. Collection improved because the lists of taxpayers were updated and because collectors were required to provide security for the sums they handled. In 1804, the government reintroduced indirect taxes, notably the "droits réunis" on beverages, tobacco, and other goods, which proved unpopular because they recalled the hated taxes of the old regime. A cadastre law of 1807 launched a survey of all landed property to provide a basis for a fairer land tax, though the project was slow and incomplete.

The second was the creation of the Banque de France, founded in January 1800 by a group of Parisian bankers and financiers with the encouragement of Napoleon and the support of government deposits. It began as a private company that discounted commercial paper and issued banknotes. In April 1803, a law granted it the exclusive privilege of issuing banknotes in Paris, and it later expanded its role, with the government increasing its supervision, particularly after a liquidity crisis in 1805 led to reforms and a greater role for state appointees in its governance in 1806. The Bank gave merchants a reliable source of credit and the government a financial partner, and it laid the foundation of a central banking institution.

The third measure was the monetary law of 28 March 1803 (7 Germinal Year XI), which created the franc germinal. The law defined the franc as a silver coin containing five grams of silver at nine-tenths fineness and established a bimetallic standard in which gold coins, notably the twenty-franc piece, were fixed at a ratio of about fifteen and a half to one with silver. The decimal system had been adopted during the Revolution, and the new currency was integrated into it. The franc germinal proved remarkably stable and remained the basis of French currency until 1914. Its significance lay in the restoration of confidence: merchants, farmers, and lenders could contract in a currency whose value was predictable. The March 1803 law provides a useful case study of the relationship between the state, money, and commercial confidence.

Students should distinguish carefully among currency stabilization, banking development, fiscal solvency, and the broader economy. Stabilizing the currency did not by itself make the government solvent, and the Consulate's budgets were balanced in large part because war indemnities, requisitions, and contributions from conquered territories supplemented regular taxation. As the wars continued after 1803, spending on the army and navy rose, and the Empire financed itself increasingly through levies on occupied and allied territories and through special funds, such as the "extraordinary domain" that Napoleon controlled personally. The relationship between currency stability and the economy as a whole was also uneven. French agriculture, which employed most of the population, recovered from the dislocations of the 1790s, and a number of industries, including cotton textiles, benefited from state contracts and from protection from British competition. Maritime commerce, in contrast, was damaged by war and by the British blockade, and the ports of the Atlantic coast, such as Bordeaux and Nantes, suffered substantially, as Section 90.17 shows.

The government also engaged in public works, building roads across the Alps, including the Simplon and Mont Cenis routes, canals, bridges, and urban improvements in Paris. It encouraged industrial exhibitions, supported technical education, and created chambers of commerce and a council of manufactures, which gave businessmen an institutional voice. Government procurement, particularly of military supplies, shaped the economy, and contractors with access to officials could prosper. Thus the economic policy of the Consulate and Empire was neither laissez-faire liberalism nor simple mercantilism. It combined stable money, careful tax administration, state spending, protection, and the use of conquered resources.

The fragility of the arrangement was exposed in the autumn of 1805, when Napoleon was campaigning in Germany and a financial crisis struck Paris. The speculator Gabriel-Julien Ouvrard, who had entered into large contracts with the French and Spanish governments, found himself unable to meet obligations, and the Banque de France, which had advanced funds against the government's paper, faced a run on its notes and a shortage of specie. The crisis forced the government to reorganize the bank's governance, and it forced Napoleon, on his return from Austerlitz, to impose stricter control over contractors and the Treasury. The episode shows that the stability of the franc and the credit of the state depended on the reliability of military success and on the avoidance of overextension. It also illustrates a recurrent feature of Napoleonic finance: the regime distrusted public borrowing, preferring to fund war from taxes, indemnities, and levies on conquered states, and thus tied its solvency to the continued success of its armies.

Section 90.8

Napoleon, Colonial Slavery, the Saint-Domingue Expedition, and the Limits of Revolutionary Universalism

A chapter on Napoleonic modernization cannot overlook the government's restoration of slavery in several colonies and its attempt to reassert colonial domination by force. This section is indispensable to any assessment of the regime. In February 1794, the Convention had decreed the abolition of slavery in all French colonies and the equality of rights for all men of whatever color, a decision that followed the slave uprising in Saint-Domingue in 1791 and the emancipation proclaimed in 1793 by the commissioners Léger-Félicité Sonthonax and Étienne Polverel. The decree was applied unevenly. In Saint-Domingue, Guadeloupe, and French Guiana, abolition took effect, while in Martinique, which was under British occupation, and in the Mascarene Islands, where colonists resisted, slavery continued. Formerly enslaved people in Saint-Domingue, led by Toussaint Louverture, had become soldiers, officials, and political leaders. Toussaint, himself a former slave, joined the French Republic in 1794, defeated the Spanish and British forces, and by 1801 was effectively governing the entire island of Hispaniola. His constitution of July 1801 named him governor for life and declared that slavery was forever abolished in the colony, while affirming its connection to France and also attempting to maintain plantation production through coerced labor regulations.

Napoleon's policy toward the colonies was shaped by several factors. The sugar and coffee economy of Saint-Domingue had been the most valuable colonial economy in the world before 1791, and many merchants, planters, and officials in France wished to restore it. Planter exiles, including those connected to Joséphine's family in Martinique, pressed their case. The peace with Britain in 1802 offered an opportunity to send a large force across the Atlantic. Napoleon also viewed Toussaint's constitution as a challenge to French sovereignty, and he regarded Black political and military power in the Caribbean with hostility. His thinking on race and colonial hierarchy was shaped by the prejudices of his time and milieu, and it is documented in his instructions and in the policies his government adopted.

The expedition to Saint-Domingue, commanded by Napoleon's brother-in-law General Charles Leclerc, set sail in December 1801 and arrived in early 1802 with an initial force of about twenty thousand men, later reinforced. Leclerc's instructions and public proclamations promised to respect freedom, but the intention of the government was to restore French authority and, in the long run, plantation labor on terms that would resemble the prior system. Toussaint and his generals, including Jean-Jacques Dessalines and Henri Christophe, resisted at first, then some submitted. In June 1802, the French arrested Toussaint by deception and deported him to France, where he was imprisoned in the Fort de Joux in the Jura mountains and died in April 1803. The arrest did not end resistance. When news arrived that slavery had been restored in Guadeloupe, and as French commanders engaged in brutal repression, including massacres and drowning of prisoners, former officers of Toussaint's army who had joined the French turned against them.

The legislative basis for slavery's restoration is often misunderstood, and precision matters. The law of 20 May 1802 (30 Floréal Year X) declared that, in the colonies returned to France under the Treaty of Amiens, such as Martinique, slavery would be maintained according to prior laws and regulations. Article II extended the same rule specifically to other French colonies beyond the Cape of Good Hope, notably the Mascarene islands (Réunion and Mauritius), where abolition had not been enforced; Article III permitted the trade and importation of enslaved people in the colonies covered by the preceding provisions. The statute did not use an unqualified formula covering every French colony. The law did not by itself abolish the Convention's decree of 1794 nor expressly reinstate slavery in every colony. It did not mention Saint-Domingue. The wider restoration nevertheless proceeded through distinct military and administrative actions. In Guadeloupe, General Antoine Richepanse arrived in May 1802, crushed resistance led by officers including Louis Delgrès, whose defenders died at Matouba on 28 May, and imposed coercive colonial control; a separate consular order signed by Bonaparte on 16 July 1802 formally directed the colony to be governed under the laws operating in 1789. That order was not the same instrument as the May statute, and its publication and enforcement had their own chronology. In French Guiana, coercive reenslavement began under Victor Hugues in November 1802; the consular act of 7 December and subsequent measures carried the process into 1804. In Saint-Domingue, slavery was not formally reintroduced by statute, but French commanders and officials moved toward restoring forced labor and racial hierarchy, and the intent was understood by the population. The measure therefore did not automatically reimpose slavery everywhere the Convention had abolished it, but it opened the way to a general reversal. Separate measures in 1802 and 1803 also restricted the entry of Black and mixed-race persons into metropolitan France and the contracting of interracial marriages there.

The failure of the Saint-Domingue expedition cannot be explained solely by disease or French military reversals. Yellow fever killed a large number of French soldiers, including Leclerc himself in November 1802, and this was an important factor. But the political and military agency of formerly enslaved people was decisive. The armies of Dessalines, Christophe, and other leaders combined disciplined units and guerrilla tactics, and the revived French campaign under General Donatien de Rochambeau in 1803 was marked by atrocities that strengthened Haitian determination. The resumption of war between Britain and France in May 1803 enabled the British navy to blockade the island and cut French reinforcements. The final French defeat at Vertières on 18 November 1803 led to the evacuation of the remaining troops. On 1 January 1804, Dessalines proclaimed the independence of the colony under its indigenous name, Haiti. It became the first state in the modern era founded by formerly enslaved people, and the second independent republic in the Americas after the United States.

The consequences were far reaching. As the Saint-Domingue expedition deteriorated and war with Britain threatened to resume, Napoleon abandoned his North American imperial plans and agreed to sell Louisiana to the United States by a treaty signed on 30 April 1803. The transaction preceded the final French defeat at Vertières in November 1803; the faltering colonial project contributed to the decision, but the colony's loss had not yet been conclusively completed. Slavery remained in the French colonies until the Second Republic abolished it in 1848. The French slave trade, which Napoleon's government had allowed to resume, was outlawed in 1815 under pressure from Britain, though it continued illegally. In France itself, the colonial economy had shaped the commercial prosperity of Atlantic ports and the fortunes of many merchants, as Section 90.18 and the Atlantic chapters of Volume III show. Haiti's independence also reshaped political thought across the Atlantic world, inspiring enslaved and free Black communities, alarming slaveholders, and exposing the limits of revolutionary universalism. A regime that claimed to preserve the principle of equality before the law had, in the colonies, reimposed racial subjection. It is a contradiction that any assessment of Napoleon's legacy must hold in view.

Section 90.9

The Life Consulate, the Imperial Constitution, and the Creation of Hereditary Monarchy

By the spring of 1802, Napoleon had accumulated formidable prestige: peace on the continent and with Britain, the Concordat, and a program of legal and administrative reform. His term as First Consul was ten years. Leading supporters, seeking to secure both their own positions and the stability of the regime, proposed that his tenure be extended. In May 1802, the Tribunate and the Senate moved to propose a prolongation of ten years. Napoleon, preferring a more decisive step, arranged that a plebiscite be held on the question of whether he should be consul for life. The results announced in early August were officially reported in the Bulletin des lois as 3,653,600 affirmative votes and 8,272 negative votes; other contemporary summaries sometimes report different totals. As with the vote of 1800, political competition was constrained, the administration supervised the process, and no organized alternative was permitted. The demonstrated wholesale inflation of the 1800 return should not be assumed to establish exactly the same falsification for the 1802 return. Historians recognize that many French voters probably did support the extension, since it seemed to promise continued peace. They also recognize that the figures cannot be treated as an unrestricted measure of consent.

A sénatus-consulte of 4 August 1802, often called the Constitution of Year X, proclaimed Napoleon consul for life and gave him the right to designate his successor. It also reorganized the representative institutions in ways that reduced their independence further. The electoral colleges, with members serving for life and chosen from the highest taxpayers, replaced the lists of notables as the basis for selecting candidates. The Tribunate was reduced from one hundred to fifty members. The Senate gained the power to dissolve the legislative bodies and the Tribunate and to suspend the constitution in certain areas, and the First Consul acquired the right to grant pardons. The regime had moved from a republic with a ten-year executive toward a personal monarchy in everything but name. The Constitution of Year X also changed the basis of political life by emphasizing property qualifications and notability. Wealthy landowners and officials became the principal political class.

The renewal of war with Britain in May 1803 intensified the argument for dynastic stability. Royalist conspiracies, supported in part by British money, targeted the First Consul. In 1804, police uncovered a plot involving Georges Cadoudal, the Breton royalist leader, and the former general Jean-Charles Pichegru, with possible links to General Jean Victor Moreau, Napoleon's rival. Cadoudal was arrested and executed, Pichegru was found dead in prison, and Moreau was convicted and exiled. In the aftermath, Napoleon ordered the abduction of Louis Antoine Henri de Bourbon, Duke of Enghien, a young Bourbon prince living in the German state of Baden, whom he suspected of involvement. Troops crossed the border, seized Enghien, brought him to Vincennes, and a military commission tried and executed him on 21 March 1804. The act violated the sovereignty of Baden and outraged European courts, and the evidence available later suggests that Enghien had no significant role in the conspiracy. For Napoleon, the execution signaled to the royalists that he would not be intimidated, and, in the eyes of republicans, that the regime had decisively broken with the Bourbons. The affair also strengthened arguments among Napoleon's supporters that an hereditary succession would protect the regime from the danger of assassination.

On 18 May 1804 (28 Floréal Year XII), the Senate passed a sénatus-consulte, approved by the government, which provided that the government of the Republic was entrusted to an Emperor of the French, that this dignity was hereditary in the Napoleonic family in the male line, and that Napoleon Bonaparte was Emperor. The act provided for succession by direct male descendants and, in the absence of such an heir, for adoption of nephews, and it established an imperial household, grand dignitaries, and a revised constitutional structure. A plebiscite held in the following months officially recorded 3,521,675 affirmative and 2,579 negative votes in the Bulletin des lois tabulation, although other published tallies vary; like the earlier plebiscites, it was conducted without unrestricted political competition. The form was careful. The text still referred to the Republic, and French coins and public documents continued for a time to carry the phrase "French Republic" alongside Napoleon's name, until the republican designation was dropped from coinage in 1808.

A historical safeguard requires emphasis: Napoleon became Emperor through the constitutional act of 18 May 1804, not through the coronation in December. The coronation was a ceremony of religious and political legitimation, not the source of his legal title. The Fondation Napoléon and other historical collections document the legislative and political process by which the Empire was proclaimed.

Why did a Republic that had executed a king in 1793 accept an emperor in 1804? Explanations have emphasized several elements. The experience of political instability and assassination plots encouraged many to value continuity. Napoleon's supporters, including many former revolutionaries, believed that heredity would prevent the recurrence of a struggle for power. The regime wished to give the new order a status equal to that of the monarchies of Europe, with which it negotiated, and the title of emperor, which recalled Charlemagne and ancient Rome rather than the Bourbons, suggested a claim to a different kind of authority. Many former republicans accepted the change because they saw that the legal and social achievements of the Revolution, including the sale of national lands and the abolition of privilege, were now protected by a dynastic regime that depended on them. Others, including a number of generals and republican intellectuals, opposed or resented it. Ludwig van Beethoven's removal of Napoleon's name from the dedication of the Third Symphony is a frequently told story of disillusion, though its details rest on later testimony and should be treated with some caution.

Section 90.10

Coronation, Imperial Court, Dynastic Legitimacy, and the Reinvention of Political Hierarchy

The coronation at Notre-Dame on 2 December 1804 was designed to give the new monarchy a ceremonial foundation. Napoleon wanted Pope Pius VII to attend, as a sign of the Church's recognition and as a means of linking the regime to Catholic tradition, and he obtained the Pope's agreement after protracted negotiations. The ceremony combined diverse elements. The setting was the cathedral of Paris, a Christian and French national shrine, though one that had suffered during the Revolution. The symbols included the regalia of Charlemagne, some brought from Aachen or reproduced, and imperial emblems such as the laurel wreath and the eagle, which evoked ancient Rome. The anointing by the Pope recalled the consecration of Frankish and French kings. Napoleon, however, placed the crown on his own head, and then crowned Joséphine, and the Pope, who had come to Paris to bless the ceremony, was left in a secondary position. The symbolic message was that the Emperor's authority came from the people and from his own achievements, with religion as a confirming element rather than a source.

The painter Jacques-Louis David depicted the ceremony in a monumental canvas, completed in 1807, which is examined in the Visual Source Study below. Other artists, architects, and designers contributed to the development of a distinctive imperial style, drawing on Roman, Egyptian, and neoclassical models and used in furniture, decorative arts, public buildings, and uniforms. Paris was embellished with monuments such as the Arc de Triomphe du Carrousel, the Vendôme Column, which was modeled on Trajan's Column, and the facade of the Madeleine, though some of these were completed only after Napoleon's fall.

The imperial court reinvented political hierarchy in a society that had abolished legal privilege. Napoleon established an imperial household with great officers: the grand elector, the arch-chancellor (Cambacérès), the arch-treasurer (Lebrun), the constable, the grand admiral, and others, along with chamberlains, ladies in waiting, and ceremonial officials. In May 1804 he named eighteen generals marshals of the Empire, a title that recognized military achievement and created a group of senior commanders tied to the dynasty. Court etiquette, partly modeled on that of the old monarchy, was designed to demonstrate the standing of the new regime and to discipline elites. The court at the Tuileries, Saint-Cloud, and Fontainebleau attracted both former revolutionaries and members of the old nobility, who were encouraged to rejoin public life.

In 1808, the regime established a new imperial nobility. By decrees of March 1808, Napoleon created titles of prince, duke, count, baron, and knight, awarded to individuals for service to the state, and typically linked to endowments called "majorats," inherited estates in conquered territory that supported the titles. By 1814, approximately three thousand titles had been conferred, and a majority went to military officers, though administrators, jurists, scientists, and some members of the old nobility also received them. The new nobility did not restore legal privileges comparable to those of the old regime. Its members had no exemption from taxation and no special jurisdiction, but they enjoyed social prestige and the possibility of transmitting wealth and rank to their descendants. This created a contradiction. The regime claimed to reward merit and to uphold equality before the law, while building a privileged hereditary elite.

Dynastic marriage and family appointment served political purposes. Napoleon arranged marriages for relatives and officials with German princely families, installed brothers and sisters as monarchs and grand dukes, and sought, after 1809, an heir. The divorce from Joséphine, who had not borne him a child, and the marriage to Marie-Louise of Austria in 1810, examined in Section 90.19, followed from this logic. The imperial family was large, and its members had very different capacities and ambitions, a source of recurring tension in the regime. The distribution of honours and offices ensured loyalty from supporters, but it also entangled the regime in the interests of individuals and families whose performance varied widely.

Contemporaries differed in their judgments of the court. Memoirs by participants, notably those of women who served in Joséphine's household, such as Claire de Rémusat, describe a court in which etiquette was strict, Napoleon's moods shaped the atmosphere, and ambitious men and women competed for favor. These sources must be handled with care: many were written or revised after 1815, when authors had reasons to distance themselves from the regime or to present their own conduct favorably. Joséphine herself played a significant social role, presiding over receptions, supporting artists and gardeners, and cultivating the links between old and new elites, and her influence did not disappear with her divorce. Royalist critics ridiculed the court as a parody of monarchy, republican critics saw it as a betrayal of equality, and foreign observers, including diplomats at the courts of Europe, reported on both its splendor and its dependence on the Emperor's personal will. The reinvention of hierarchy therefore produced a mixture of acceptance, imitation, and satire that helped shape the regime's image and its enemies' arguments.

Historical Vocabulary
Empire, Hereditary Succession, Legion of Honour, Lycée, and Imperial University

The Empire (1804 to 1814, with a brief return in 1815) was the form of government headed by Napoleon as hereditary Emperor of the French. Hereditary succession meant that the imperial title passed within a defined family line. The Legion of Honour, founded in 1802, was an order of merit open to military and civilian recipients. A lycée was a state secondary school established by the education law of 1802. The Imperial University was the state corporation, organized in 1808, that supervised education across the Empire.

The word "empire" in this context did not mean only a state ruling several peoples. It also recalled ancient Rome and Charlemagne and was chosen to suggest authority beyond that of the old kings of France. Students should be careful not to confuse the "University" of 1808, a national administrative structure, with a single institution of higher learning.

Section 90.11

Education, Merit, the Legion of Honour, and the Formation of an Imperial Elite

The Revolution had produced numerous plans for national education but few durable institutions. The old Catholic colleges had been closed, the revolutionary central schools had been uneven, and primary schooling was patchy and dependent on local initiative and the Church. The Consulate turned to education as a means of training officials, officers, engineers, and jurists, and of shaping loyal citizens. The law of 1 May 1802 (11 Floréal Year X), the General Law on Public Instruction, established the principal structure. It provided for primary schools to be maintained by the communes, secondary schools run by communes or private persons under state supervision, and state lycées, intended to provide the highest secondary education, with a curriculum in Latin, mathematics, and the sciences. Thirty lycées were planned initially, and it was envisaged that scholarship holders would be supported by the state. The 1802 law provides an identifiable legislative source for the organization, curriculum, and financing of the lycées.

The lycées were designed on a quasi-military model, with uniforms, drum signals, strict discipline, and daily routines. Students were organized into companies, taught by teachers who were state employees, and prepared for entry to higher schools and state service. Scholarships, called bourses, were reserved for the sons of officers and officials, and a significant proportion went to children of military families. The curriculum emphasized Latin, mathematics, and physical science, with less emphasis on the humanist and rhetorical emphasis of the old colleges. A law of 10 May 1806 and a decree of 17 March 1808 created the Imperial University, a centralized corporation headed by a Grand Master, Louis de Fontanes, with authority over all public education, including faculties of law, medicine, sciences, letters, and theology. The decree also created the baccalauréat as the qualification for entry to higher studies. Private schools were permitted only with the University's authorization, and the Church's institutions were subject to its control. Higher technical education, notably the École polytechnique, which had been founded in 1794 and was placed under military administration in 1804, supplied engineers and artillery officers.

Students should not conclude that the regime established universal schooling. The lycées served a small minority, mainly boys from families with the money or connections to enroll them or to obtain a bourse, and the number of pupils was modest compared with the population. Primary education remained largely the responsibility of communes, religious teaching orders such as the Brothers of the Christian Schools, which Napoleon allowed to resume work, and local initiative, and literacy rates varied sharply between the north and east, where they were higher, and the south and west, where they were lower. Girls were largely excluded from the state system. The regime's educational priority for women was religious and domestic instruction, and the main state institutions for girls were the schools of the Legion of Honour, beginning with the house at Écouen in 1805, which educated daughters and relatives of members of the Legion for roles as wives and mothers. Social and gender inequalities in access were therefore built into the system.

The Legion of Honour, created by law on 19 May 1802 (29 Floréal Year X), was a new kind of distinction. It was open to soldiers and civilians, was organized in cohorts with endowments and salaries, and was intended to replace the abolished orders of the old monarchy with an award for merit. The Legion encountered skepticism in the Tribunate and Legislative Body, where it was seen as a revival of aristocratic distinction, and it passed by narrow margins. Napoleon argued that human beings respond to honours and that the republic needed a means to reward and attach public servants. The first large distribution of the decoration took place in 1804. Its recipients included numerous military officers, but also civilians, and the order became a symbol of the regime's claim to reward service rather than birth, as well as an instrument for binding elites to the state.

The education system and the Legion jointly contributed to the formation of an imperial elite. Officials, jurists, officers, engineers, and scholars who passed through the institutions of the regime acquired shared training, loyalty, and social standing. The regime's claim to career open to talent had substance, since many individuals of modest origin rose in the army and the administration. But those who benefited were disproportionately drawn from property-owning families, and the system also favored sons of the existing elite. The new structures of education and honour thus embodied a tension that recurred throughout Napoleonic government: openness to merit within the limits of a hierarchical social order.

Evidence for the working of the education system survives in the registers of the lycées, the reports of inspectors, the records of scholarship awards, and the memoirs of former pupils, and historians use them to ask who actually benefited. They show that many of the first lycées struggled to attract students, that families of the provincial notability and the middle ranks of officialdom supplied a large share of pupils, and that few sons of peasants or urban workers reached secondary schooling. They also show that the military and disciplinary character of the schools attracted some families and repelled others, and that Catholic families often preferred the private and clerical schools that the regime tolerated but regulated. The state's attempt to shape the loyalty of a generation succeeded unevenly, and the educational structure that mattered most for the long run was less the individual lycée than the centralized framework of examinations, inspection, and state diplomas that outlasted the Empire.

Section 90.12

Censorship, Surveillance, Propaganda, and the Management of Imperial Public Opinion

The regime combined appeals to public opinion with strict control over its expression. From its earliest weeks, the Consulate limited the press. In January 1800, a decree suppressed most of the political newspapers of Paris, leaving about a dozen, and imposed the threat of closure on any that criticized the government or the army. Napoleon regarded the press as a dangerous instrument, and he expressed to his ministers the conviction that the government could not endure if newspapers were free. Under the Empire, restrictions tightened. A decree of 1805 authorized the government to appoint censors to newspapers, and in 1810 and 1811, the number of papers allowed to publish political news was reduced, with a single official paper in each department, under the supervision of the prefect. In Paris, the four principal papers were left, with the semi-official Moniteur universel as the main source of government communication.

The decree of 5 February 1810 regulating printing and bookselling embedded censorship in the institutional structure of imperial government. It created a Directorate General of Printing and Bookselling under the ministry of the interior, limited the number of printers, required that printers hold licenses, imposed a system of prior review for books, and subjected the circulation of imperially unwelcome works to the discretion of the authorities. A separate decree of March 1810 re-established state prisons for the detention, without trial, of individuals considered dangerous to the state, an institutional counterpart to the control of expression. Theaters were similarly restricted: in 1807, the number of theaters in Paris was reduced, and their repertories were supervised.

The ministry of police, the prefects, the censorship authorities, informants, and the imperial court formed overlapping systems of surveillance. After Fouché's dismissal in 1810, the ministry passed to General Anne Jean Marie René Savary, duke of Rovigo, who pursued a more openly authoritarian policy. The police monitored the mail, followed individuals judged suspicious, and compiled reports on public sentiment, including rumors, songs, and gatherings. Prefects submitted periodic reports on public opinion in their departments, and the regime used these reports to adjust policy, to identify sources of discontent, and to apply pressure on individuals. Napoleon also created a network of informal informants and used private agents. Surveillance was not total. Resources were limited, officials differed in zeal, and censors were sometimes inconsistent. But it was effective enough to inhibit the open expression of political opposition in print and in public.

Propaganda complemented control. The bulletins of the Grande Armée, written under Napoleon's supervision and published in the official papers, announced victories, and, as contemporaries and later historians noted, sometimes concealed or minimized defeats and losses. Public ceremonies celebrated victories, anniversaries, and the Emperor's birthday, which in 1806 was fixed as a feast day, the Saint-Napoléon of 15 August. Monuments, medals, coins, engravings, paintings, and theatrical performances reinforced the image of the Emperor as military genius, lawgiver, and benefactor. Artists such as David, Antoine-Jean Gros, and Anne-Louis Girodet were commissioned to portray Napoleon and his achievements, and the Salon was used as a stage for imperial themes. Official history, textbooks, and catechisms, including the Imperial Catechism of 1806, which taught that Christians owed love, obedience, fidelity, and military service to the Emperor, shaped the presentation of authority to schoolchildren.

The regime thus combined public claims to equality, merit, national glory, and stability with restrictions on political opposition. The combination was not accidental. It reflected Napoleon's belief that the state should provide order and prosperity in exchange for obedience, and that open political debate had caused the instability of the Revolution. It also reflected the limits of the regime's confidence. A government secure in popular support would not have needed so elaborate an apparatus of control. Students should also recognize that the effectiveness of propaganda was uneven. Rural audiences often had little access to newspapers, and many people relied on rumor, sermons, and word of mouth. Unofficial communication, including songs, jokes, and clandestine pamphlets, carried criticism, as police reports reveal.

Public opinion was also shaped through the arts and entertainment. Painters and sculptors competed for commissions at the Salon, where large canvases celebrating battles and the Emperor's visits to the wounded were displayed, and the authorities supervised the selection of themes. Theaters staged plays on Roman and national subjects that could be read as praise of the regime, and the police monitored performances for allusions that might be interpreted as criticism. Writers faced pressure: Germaine de Staël, who had publicly opposed Napoleon's concentration of power, was exiled from Paris and later from France, and in 1810 the authorities ordered the pulping of the first printing of her book on Germany. Chateaubriand, whose defense of Christianity had been welcomed in 1802, later ran into conflict with the censors. The regime's cultural policy was not simply repressive. It supported scientific expeditions, museums, and learned societies, and it enriched the Louvre, renamed the Musée Napoléon, with works taken from conquered territories, a practice that tied the cultural prestige of the capital to conquest. Historians therefore treat the cultural life of the Empire as the product of patronage, supervision, and constraint working together, and they caution against treating either official art or suppressed dissent as representative of the whole.

Historical Debate 1

Did Napoleon Preserve the Principal Achievements of the French Revolution, or Replace Revolutionary Liberty with Authoritarian Government?

The question: To what extent should the Consulate and Empire be understood as consolidating the Revolution's gains and to what extent as reversing its political promise?

Interpretation A: Consolidation of revolutionary achievements. Historians who take this view point to the Civil Code, the abolition of feudal dues and privileges, the protection of the purchasers of national lands, the unified administration, and the institutions of merit and education. They argue that the Revolution could not have survived without a stable institutional framework, and that Napoleon provided one while preserving the social settlement that most French citizens valued.
Interpretation B: Authoritarian replacement of liberty. Other historians stress the loss of representative government, press freedom, and political participation, the use of arbitrary detention and police surveillance, the creation of a hereditary monarchy and nobility, the restoration of slavery in some colonies, the subordination of women, and the militarization of society. They argue that the regime preserved equality before the law for men in civil matters while abandoning the Revolution's commitment to popular sovereignty and political liberty.

Evidence: The interpretations depend on the same sources: constitutional texts, legislation, plebiscite returns, prefects' reports, police files, memoirs, and the comparison between revolutionary and Napoleonic law. They differ on which of the Revolution's goals should be treated as essential and on how to weigh the regime's achievements against its repression.

Why it matters: The question shapes interpretations of nineteenth century French politics, which was divided between those who claimed the legacy of 1789 and those who claimed the legacy of the Empire, and it shapes the comparison between Napoleonic government and later authoritarian regimes, which historians treat with caution.

The Organization of Napoleonic Europe: Territories, Client States, and Administrative Transformation

This division examines the Empire as a European system of government, not merely as the territory of France enlarged by conquest.

Section 90.13

The French Imperial System: Annexed Departments, Allied Monarchies, and Dependent States

Napoleon exercised power beyond metropolitan France in several distinct ways, and a student who treats them as equivalent will misunderstand the system. At its core lay France itself, which by 1799 already included territories annexed during the revolutionary wars. Around that core lay lands directly annexed and organized as departments. Beyond these lay states that were nominally independent but ruled by Napoleon himself, by members of his family, or by allies, and states that retained their dynasties but acted within the limits of French power. Finally, there were territories governed through military occupation, with administration varying with the progress of the war. The categories overlapped and changed over time, and the Empire did not follow a single master plan. It grew through a series of wars, treaties, and improvised arrangements.

Directly annexed territories formed part of France in law. Belgium and the left bank of the Rhine had been annexed during the Revolution and were organized as departments by 1797 and formally ceded at Lunéville in 1801. Piedmont was annexed in 1802, Genoa and Liguria in 1805, Parma and Tuscany in 1808, the Papal States in 1809 (with Rome declared the second city of the Empire), Holland in July 1810, and in December 1810 a long coast stretching from the Dutch border to Lübeck, including Hamburg, Bremen, and the former Hanseatic cities, along with Oldenburg and other northwest German territories. The Illyrian Provinces, taken from Austria in 1809 and extending along the Adriatic coast from Carinthia and Carniola to Dalmatia and Ragusa, were administered as a distinctive territorial unit. Around 1811–1812, the imperial departmental system approached 130 departments; the Illyrian Provinces were a separate administrative grouping, not extra departments in that count. Published 1812 tabulations give 42,390,718 inhabitants for the 130 departments, excluding the populations of dependent kingdoms and other separately governed territories. These areas received the Civil Code, the prefectural system, French taxes, conscription, and the Concordat, though implementation varied with local conditions and the time available.

Client states and satellite monarchies formed a second category. In northern Italy, the Cisalpine Republic became the Italian Republic in 1802, with Napoleon as its president, and in March 1805 it became the Kingdom of Italy. Napoleon crowned himself King of Italy with the iron crown of Lombardy in Milan on 26 May 1805, and he appointed his stepson, Eugène de Beauharnais, as viceroy. The Kingdom of Naples was given to Joseph Bonaparte in 1806 and to Joachim Murat, who was married to Napoleon's sister Caroline, in 1808. The Kingdom of Holland was created in 1806 for Louis Bonaparte, and the Kingdom of Westphalia, formed in 1807 from Hessian, Hanoverian, and Prussian territories, was given to Jérôme Bonaparte. Murat became Grand Duke of Berg in 1806. Joseph moved to the throne of Spain in 1808. A third category consisted of the allied states, such as the kingdoms of Bavaria, Württemberg, and Saxony, the grand duchy of Baden, and other members of the Confederation of the Rhine, which were rewarded with territory and royal or grand ducal titles while being obliged to provide troops and adopt administrative policies favorable to France. Switzerland, reorganized under the Act of Mediation of February 1803, remained a confederation under French protection, with Napoleon as mediator.

In addition, defeated great powers became constrained partners. Austria, after Austerlitz and the Treaty of Pressburg in December 1805, lost Venetia, Tyrol, and other territories, and again in 1809 after Wagram, in the Treaty of Schönbrunn of October 1809, it lost further territory. Prussia, after its defeat at Jena and Auerstedt in October 1806, lost approximately half of its territory in the Treaty of Tilsit of July 1807, was occupied, and was restricted in the size of its army. Russia, after Friedland, entered an alliance with France at Tilsit in 1807, becoming a partner rather than a subordinate. These arrangements are the subject of the diplomatic and military narratives in Chapter 91, and they are introduced here only as political context.

The mechanisms by which France exercised control were varied. Annexed territories were administered by French prefects, with local elites incorporated as councillors, mayors, and officials. Client monarchs were Napoleon's relatives or clients, expected to follow imperial policy, supply troops, enforce the Continental System, and pay for French garrisons. Napoleon corresponded regularly with his brothers, instructing them in considerable detail on administration, finance, and law, and he did not hesitate to rebuke or replace them when they displayed independence. Louis in Holland resisted the enforcement of the blockade and was forced to abdicate in 1810, which led to the annexation of the kingdom. French ambassadors, commissioners, and military governors exerted pressure, and French troops were present in most dependent states. Local elites were essential: the system worked through the cooperation of landowners, officials, jurists, and, in some places, former revolutionaries and reformers who saw the French as allies of reform.

Analysis of Napoleonic Europe must therefore distinguish direct French sovereignty from influence exercised over ostensibly separate governments. The distinction matters for assessing the extent of the Empire, for understanding the burdens it imposed, and for interpreting the later resistance. A map that colors all territories under French influence in the same shade obscures the differences between a department of the French Empire governed by a prefect and a kingdom ruled by a relative, or an allied state that retained its own army and dynasty. Students should also remember that the territorial organization was unstable. Boundaries changed frequently, and some entities, such as the Kingdom of Westphalia or the Grand Duchy of Berg, existed for only a few years.

Section 90.14

The German Lands, the Confederation of the Rhine, and the End of the Holy Roman Empire

The Holy Roman Empire, which Chapter 46 and Chapter 78 examined as a decentralized political structure of emperors, princes, ecclesiastical lords, free cities, and numerous smaller jurisdictions, had been transformed even before the proclamation of the French Empire. The French annexation of the left bank of the Rhine, confirmed at Lunéville, deprived many German princes of territory, and the Imperial Recess of 1803, the Reichsdeputationshauptschluss, compensated them by secularizing ecclesiastical principalities and mediatizing, that is, subordinating to other rulers, most of the imperial free cities and many small lordships. Hundreds of political units disappeared, and larger states, including Bavaria, Württemberg, and Baden, gained substantially. The reorganization was driven by negotiations among the German princes, with France and Russia as arbiters, and it shows that the reshaping of Germany was a collaborative process in which German rulers actively sought advantage.

After the French victory over Austria and Russia at Austerlitz in December 1805, the Treaty of Pressburg elevated Bavaria and Württemberg to kingdoms and granted further gains to Baden and others, binding these states to France through dynastic marriages and military obligations. On 12 July 1806, sixteen German princes signed the Act of the Confederation of the Rhine in Paris, withdrawing from the Holy Roman Empire and placing themselves under the protection of Napoleon, who was named Protector. They agreed to provide contingents to a common military force and to coordinate foreign policy with France. The Confederation eventually grew to include most German states except Austria, Prussia, and for a time Swedish Pomerania and the Danish territory of Holstein, and by 1808 had some thirty-six members. Its federal diet at Frankfurt rarely functioned as an effective body, and the real instrument of French influence was Napoleon's personal relationships with individual rulers and the French diplomats at their courts.

On 6 August 1806, Emperor Francis II, responding to a French ultimatum that the dissolution of the Empire was inevitable, declared that the imperial crown had ceased to bind him and that he abdicated the title of Holy Roman Emperor, retaining the style of Francis I, Emperor of Austria, a title he had assumed in 1804 in response to Napoleon's imperial proclamation. The institution that had existed in some form since the coronation of Charlemagne in 800 or Otto I in 962, depending on the convention, ended. Students should recognize that contemporaries did not treat this as the end of "Germany," a concept that did not yet correspond to a state. The end of the Empire was the dissolution of a particular constitutional and legal structure, within which the Imperial Aulic Council and the Imperial Chamber Court had provided venues for disputes among subjects, rulers, and cities. It was also, for some observers, a matter of regret, as it removed an institution that protected smaller states and local rights.

The reorganization of German territories had several consequences. Territorial consolidation produced states of significant size and relative coherence, which were better able to maintain armies, tax systems, and bureaucracies. Many of the former ecclesiastical lands and free cities lost their autonomy. The princes of the Confederation, freed from imperial restraints, claimed full sovereignty, and many undertook administrative modernization under French influence, including the adoption of the Civil Code in the Kingdom of Westphalia and its adaptation in Baden, the reform of taxation, the abolition of guild restrictions, the emancipation of Jews in some states, and the reduction of feudal obligations. In Bavaria, the minister Maximilian von Montgelas directed a program of centralization, secularization, and reform that created a modern bureaucratic state. In Westphalia, Jérôme's constitution of 1807 declared the abolition of serfdom and legal equality, and promised a representative assembly, though in practice the assembly had few powers and the kingdom's finances were burdened by French exactions and by Jérôme's court.

Relations between German rulers and Napoleon were complex. Rulers benefited from the elevation of titles, territorial gains, and French protection against Austria or Prussia, and many collaborated willingly. But they also lost autonomy, were compelled to supply troops that fought in distant wars, and bore the costs of French occupation. Local populations experienced modernization together with new burdens of taxation and conscription. The administrative reforms often met resistance from entrenched elites. The nobility defended its privileges, and ecclesiastical populations resented secularization. Thus, French-backed modernization coexisted with the political interests of dynasties and the loss of autonomy.

The defeat of Prussia in 1806 and its humiliation at Tilsit prompted a distinct response. Prussian reformers, including the ministers Heinrich Friedrich Karl vom Stein and Karl August von Hardenberg, the military reformers Gerhard von Scharnhorst and August von Gneisenau, and the educational reformer Wilhelm von Humboldt, undertook a program of reform from above. The October Edict of 1807 abolished hereditary serfdom and relaxed restrictions on the sale of noble land, municipal government was reorganized in 1808, military reform sought to create a more efficient army, and the University of Berlin was founded in 1810. These measures, implemented unevenly and often limited by noble resistance, were influenced by the French example, but they were also designed to strengthen Prussia against France. They illustrate how Napoleonic power could stimulate reform among its opponents, a theme that Chapter 91 develops in discussing German resistance and nationalism.

Section 90.15

Italy, the Netherlands, Spain, and the Governance of Napoleonic Client Monarchies

The Kingdom of Italy, Naples, Holland, and Spain provide four distinct examples of the imperial system. In each case, a Bonaparte or a close associate ruled, the French model of administration and law was adopted, and local conditions limited what could be achieved. The positions of Joseph Bonaparte, Louis Bonaparte, Joachim Murat, and other members of the family, and the relationship between dynastic appointment, local administration, tax obligations, and political legitimacy, can be compared.

The Kingdom of Italy, created in 1805 from the Italian Republic and expanded in 1806 and 1808 to include Venetia, parts of the Papal States, and other territories, covered much of northern and central Italy outside the annexed departments. Its government was headed by the viceroy, Eugène de Beauharnais, who was capable and trusted, and who worked within limits set by Napoleon, who retained decisive control over legislation and finance. The kingdom adopted the Civil Code, a prefectural administration, a concordat with the Pope, a conscript army, and an educational system modeled on the lycées. It undertook public works, including roads and canals, and promoted the Italian bourgeoisie and landowners, many of whom had supported reform since the 1790s. The kingdom was also heavily taxed and was required to supply large military contingents and to support French troops. The liberal and patriotic hopes of those Italians who had seen in French rule the beginnings of a unified Italy were disappointed, though they found expression in institutions and a political generation that would matter later. Historians still debate whether the Napoleonic period should be seen as a preparation for the later Risorgimento or as an episode of foreign domination that left ambiguous legacies.

The Kingdom of Naples, ruled first by Joseph Bonaparte from 1806 to 1808 and then by Joachim Murat, presented a different set of problems. The southern Italian kingdom had a social order dominated by large landowners and by feudal rights, and it had experienced the brief Parthenopean Republic of 1799, which was overthrown by a Bourbon and popular reaction that led to harsh repression. Joseph and Murat abolished feudalism in 1806, reorganized the administration, and sought to sell church and crown lands, but the benefits went largely to the existing landowning and bourgeois classes, and rural poverty and brigandage persisted, with French forces suppressing resistance in Calabria and elsewhere with violence. Murat, who proved popular in some circles, developed ambitions for greater independence, and he would later break with Napoleon, as Chapter 91 and Chapter 92 show.

The Kingdom of Holland, under Louis Bonaparte from 1806, replaced the Batavian Republic, which had been a French sister republic since 1795. Louis took his role seriously, learned some Dutch, and tried to protect Dutch interests, including trade with Britain, which Napoleon regarded as treason. The conflict between brothers over the enforcement of the Continental System, taxation, and conscription led Napoleon to demand Louis's abdication in July 1810 and to annex Holland. The Dutch economy, historically dependent on maritime commerce, suffered severely, and the country's merchants, ports, and fishing communities experienced the Empire as economic ruin. The episode shows the limits of the autonomy of client monarchs.

Spain presented the clearest case of a failure of imperial government. In 1808, after the French occupation of Spanish territory under the pretext of operations against Portugal, Napoleon forced the abdication of the Bourbon king Charles IV and his son Ferdinand VII at Bayonne and installed his brother Joseph, transferred from Naples, as King of Spain. Joseph issued a constitution, the Statute of Bayonne, drawn up in 1808, that proposed reforms, including the abolition of feudal privileges, a unified legal system, and the end of the Inquisition. But the installation of a foreign king, imposed by force on a dynasty that had been part of the French alliance, provoked a popular uprising in Madrid on 2 May 1808, and a national and religious insurrection across the country. Spanish juntas organized resistance, and British troops landed in Portugal. Joseph's government controlled only part of the territory, depended on French armies, and had little legitimacy. Spanish liberals who accepted reform under Joseph, known as afrancesados, were a minority, while the Cortes of Cadiz, convened in 1810 in the unoccupied city, produced the liberal Constitution of 1812. The Peninsular War that followed belongs primarily to Chapter 91. What is important here is that Spain illustrates the limits of imposed dynastic substitution: institutional reform could not replace legitimacy, and the Spanish crisis became a major drain on French resources.

Students should distinguish the institutional organization of client states from the subsequent military campaigns and resistance movements. In all of them, the system worked when local elites found advantage in cooperation, and failed when the burdens of conscription, taxation, and foreign domination exceeded the benefits of reform. Joseph, Louis, Jérôme, and Murat each experienced the tension between their roles as Napoleon's agents and their wish to rule as legitimate monarchs of their kingdoms.

Section 90.16

Legal Reform and Imperial Rule Beyond France: The Duchy of Warsaw and Comparative European Experience

The extension of French-influenced legal institutions, administrative practices, and social reforms beyond France varied considerably, and the Duchy of Warsaw is a particularly useful example. The Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth had been partitioned among Russia, Prussia, and Austria in 1772, 1793, and 1795, and it had ceased to exist as an independent state. Many Poles, particularly officers and political exiles, had served in French armies, hoping that France would restore Polish independence, and Polish Legions fought in Italy and elsewhere. Napoleon's campaign against Prussia and Russia in 1806 and 1807 brought him into Polish territories, where he was received with enthusiasm. At Tilsit in July 1807, he created the Duchy of Warsaw from the Polish territories that Prussia had acquired in the second and third partitions, and he placed it under Frederick Augustus I, King of Saxony, who became Duke of Warsaw. The creation of a Polish state was welcomed by many Poles, but it was not the restoration of Poland. It was a small, dependent state, constrained by Napoleon's need to avoid a definitive break with Russia.

The Constitution of the Duchy, signed at Dresden on 22 July 1807, established a government with a hereditary duke, a council of ministers, a Senate, a Chamber of Deputies, and a judicial system. It declared the Catholic religion the state religion, while extending toleration to other religions, and it introduced the Napoleonic Code, which came into force in 1808. It proclaimed the abolition of personal servitude and the equality of citizens before the law. The Jagiellonian University's historical legal collections preserve the text. The duchy's army, led by Prince Józef Poniatowski, became an important contingent of the French army. After the war of 1809 against Austria, the Treaty of Schönbrunn expanded the Duchy to include Kraków and territories in Galicia, enlarging its population to over two million.

The formal abolition of serfdom did not result in an immediate transformation of rural society. The peasants were declared personally free, but the constitution and subsequent legislation left the land in the hands of the landlords, who remained the owners of the estates. Peasants who lived on noble land could be evicted, and the peasants' right to the land they cultivated was not guaranteed. A decree issued by King Frederick Augustus on 21 December 1807 clarified that peasants could leave their villages while treating cultivated holdings, buildings, livestock, and much of the associated property as belonging to the estate owner unless a specific agreement provided otherwise; it also introduced a transitional restriction on evictions. This decree must not be confused with the entry into force of the Napoleonic Code in the duchy in 1808. The peasants' new personal freedom could therefore coexist with severe economic dependence. Landowning nobles retained substantial influence in the Senate, in local administration, and through property relations; any claim about continued seigneurial judicial jurisdiction must be evaluated separately against the duchy's reorganized courts. The nobility, whose members dominated the government and the army, welcomed the introduction of French law but resisted measures that would diminish their economic power. The state's financial burdens, including the maintenance of a large army and French garrisons and demands for contributions, were heavy, and the peasantry bore much of the cost.

The case of Jews in the Duchy was similarly mixed. The Civil Code introduced equal civil rights in principle, but in 1808, a decree suspended the political rights of Jews for ten years, on the grounds of social conditions that the government attributed to the Jewish community, a policy that paralleled the decree of 1808 in France. Jewish merchants and financiers nevertheless played important roles in supplying the army and in the economy, and many Jewish communities experienced the period as a combination of opportunity and discrimination.

The Duchy of Warsaw shows the difference between formal legal change and the actual redistribution of social and economic power, a distinction that applies across Napoleonic Europe. In Westphalia, the abolition of serfdom and feudal dues required peasants to pay compensation in many cases, and landowners often retained substantial rights. In the Rhineland departments annexed to France, the abolition of feudal rights was more complete and benefited peasants and purchasers of national lands, as it had in France itself. In the Italian states, the sale of Church lands and the abolition of feudal rights benefited middle-class and noble landowners more than peasants. In Spain and southern Italy, resistance and weak administration limited the impact of reform. In the German states, reform was shaped by the aims of local rulers, with a wide variation in the extent of change. The adoption of the Civil Code was frequently modified: Baden adopted it with local adaptations in 1810, the Rhenish departments retained it long after 1815, and many other territories abandoned it after the fall of Napoleon.

Historians therefore compare the experiences of different regions, and several patterns appear. Reform was most thorough where French law and administration were imposed directly, in the annexed departments, and less thorough where it depended on local rulers and elites. Reform benefited propertied groups, officials, and some urban middle classes, and it often left peasants and workers dependent. The costs of the system, including taxation, conscription, and the burden of French garrisons, were borne unevenly, and the benefits and burdens often fell on different groups. And in nearly every case, the introduction of law and administration under foreign domination raised questions of legitimacy, which affected the capacity of reforms to endure.

Historical Vocabulary
Client State, Confederation of the Rhine, Continental System, Annexation, and Conscription

A client state was a nominally independent state whose government was controlled or heavily influenced by a more powerful state, here France. The Confederation of the Rhine (1806 to 1813) was the league of German states under Napoleon's protection. Annexation meant the formal incorporation of a territory into France, as a department. The Continental System was the set of measures designed to exclude British trade from the continent. Conscription was compulsory military service imposed by law, established in France by the Jourdan law of 1798 and extended through the Empire.

Students should avoid using "satellite" or "puppet" as automatic synonyms for "client." Some client rulers, such as Louis in Holland, tried to assert independence, and some allied rulers, such as those of Bavaria, pursued their own interests within the alliance. The terms describe a relationship of dependence that varied in degree.

Historical Debate 3

Did Napoleon Modernize Europe, or Did French Domination Principally Serve Imperial Exploitation?

The question: How should historians weigh the legal and administrative reforms introduced or encouraged by France against the military, fiscal, and political burdens that Napoleonic rule imposed?

Interpretation A: Modernization. Some historians argue that French rule or influence abolished feudal and ecclesiastical privileges, introduced uniform law, reorganized administrations, secularized property, and prepared the way for liberal and national movements, and that reform in many parts of Europe would have been much slower without it. They sometimes describe a "Napoleonic model" of the modern state.
Interpretation B: Exploitation. Others argue that the Empire was primarily an instrument of French power, that conquered and dependent territories were forced to pay for French wars through taxes, requisitions, and conscription, that their economies were disrupted by the Continental System, that local institutions and cultures were overridden, and that reform was subordinated to military needs. They also point out that many reforms were already underway before the French arrived, as in the Prussian and Bavarian cases.

Evidence: Historians draw on legislation, administrative records, tax and conscription figures, trade statistics, local studies, and memoirs. Evidence varies by region, since the annexed Rhineland, the Kingdom of Italy, Westphalia, Spain, and the Duchy of Warsaw experienced different combinations of reform and burden.

Why it matters: The debate affects how historians interpret the origins of nineteenth century liberalism and nationalism in Europe, the relationship between reform and foreign domination, and the legacy of Napoleonic institutions in the states that survived 1815. Many scholars now suggest that modernization and exploitation were not alternatives but coexisted.

Section 90.17

The Continental System: Britain, Commercial Warfare, Blockade, and Economic Regulation

After the renewal of war in 1803, and especially after the naval victory of Britain at Trafalgar in October 1805, Napoleon could not defeat the Royal Navy or invade the British Isles. He turned to economic warfare. Britain's wealth, he believed, depended on its exports to the continent, and its capacity to finance coalitions depended on that revenue. If continental ports were closed to British goods and ships, the British economy would suffer, public finances would deteriorate, and the government would be forced to seek peace. The policy, later called the Continental System, was announced in the Berlin Decree of 21 November 1806, issued after the defeat of Prussia.

The decree declared the British Isles in a state of blockade, a claim Britain's naval superiority made impossible to enforce at sea, and prohibited all trade and correspondence with Britain. It ordered the confiscation of British goods and of merchandise belonging to British subjects, declared British subjects found in territories occupied by France or its allies to be prisoners of war, and closed continental ports to ships coming from Britain or its colonies. The text of the Berlin Decree is available in documentary editions and provides a suitable basis for evaluating its claims about British maritime policy, which the decree presented as a response to British restrictions on neutral shipping.

Britain responded with Orders in Council of January and November 1807, which required neutral ships to call at British ports and pay duties before trading with continental Europe. Napoleon answered with the Milan Decree of 17 December 1807, declaring that any ship that submitted to British search or called at a British port lost its neutral status and would be treated as British. The result was a spiral of restrictions that squeezed neutral shipping, notably that of the United States, which responded with the Embargo Act of December 1807, a measure that harmed American commerce. The system was extended by later measures, including the Trianon tariff and the Fontainebleau decree of October 1810, which ordered the seizure and burning of British manufactured goods in territories under French control, and by a system of licenses that allowed trade with Britain under strict conditions, particularly for the export of French grain and wine.

Enforcement depended on the control of coastlines, which was one reason for the expansion of the Empire. In 1807, Napoleon demanded that Portugal, Britain's traditional ally, close its ports, and when it refused, French and Spanish forces invaded, leading to the crisis that engulfed the Iberian Peninsula. The annexation of Holland in 1810, of the north German coast in December of the same year, and of the Papal States in 1809 was partly justified by the need to enforce the system. Russia joined at Tilsit in 1807 but began to relax enforcement as its economy suffered, and on 31 December 1810, Tsar Alexander I issued a decree that opened Russian ports to neutral shipping and imposed duties on French luxury goods, an act that Napoleon regarded as a challenge.

The system's official objectives differed from its actual consequences. British exports to the continent fell in some years, and 1808 and 1811 were periods of severe commercial depression in Britain, with unemployment and unrest in manufacturing districts. But Britain found alternative markets in Latin America, the Mediterranean, the Baltic through Sweden, and in smuggling operations conducted from Heligoland, Malta, and Gothenburg. Smuggling was widespread, and many customs officers and local officials were complicit, as they profited from it or sympathized with merchants. The French government itself issued licenses that allowed trade in particular goods, undermining the principle of exclusion. British naval supremacy allowed Britain to continue to import raw materials and to export manufactured goods, while the continental economies were disrupted.

The effects of commercial restrictions were unequal. French manufacturers of cotton goods, iron, and some other products benefited from the protection and the loss of British competition, and regions such as Alsace, Normandy, and Belgium, along with Saxony, experienced industrial growth, with Ghent becoming a center of cotton spinning. Port cities and regions that depended on overseas trade, such as Bordeaux, Nantes, Marseille, Hamburg, Amsterdam, and Trieste, suffered severe decline. Merchants lost their business, shipbuilders and sailors lost employment, and agricultural producers who exported grain, wine, timber, or flax, in Baltic and north German regions, lost markets. Consumers faced shortages and higher prices for colonial goods, such as sugar and coffee, which had to be obtained through smuggling or substitutes, and Napoleon encouraged the development of beet sugar and chicory as substitutes for cane sugar and coffee, with state support for research and factories from 1811. The policy thus transferred economic costs to groups across the Empire in ways that generated resentment among merchants and consumers.

The Continental System also affected Napoleon's relations with allies. Governments forced to enforce it faced the loss of customs revenue and the hostility of their commercial classes. It contributed directly to the conflicts with Portugal, Spain, the Papacy, Holland, and Russia. Historians continue to debate whether the system seriously injured Britain, and most now argue that its effects on Britain were real but limited, and that it did more harm to the continental economies and to the political stability of the Empire than to its target. It is best understood as a policy that sought to turn France's military control of the continent into economic victory, but whose enforcement demanded further conquest and thereby increased the strains on the Empire.

Section 90.18

Conscription, Taxation, Occupation, and the Human Costs of Imperial Government

The institutions that provided the Empire with administrative reach also made possible its demands on the people it governed. Military recruitment, taxation, requisitioning, troop quartering, and occupation shaped everyday life across the Napoleonic domains, and they differed according to place, social position, and the moment at which they were applied.

Conscription was the most direct burden. The Jourdan law of 1798 had established the principle that all unmarried men between the ages of twenty and twenty-five were liable to military service, and the Consulate and Empire developed it into a regular annual levy, with each department assigned a quota. Prefects and mayors compiled lists of eligible young men, and lotteries determined who would serve. The law allowed those drawn to hire a substitute, a practice called remplacement, and the price of a substitute rose as demand increased, which meant that wealthier families could avoid service, while poorer families could not. Exemptions were granted for certain occupations and family circumstances. As the wars grew larger and casualties mounted, the government drew successive classes earlier and lowered the standards, and in 1809 and 1812, it introduced additional levies and the National Guard. Estimates of the total number of men called up in France between 1800 and 1814 vary according to the boundaries and categories used, but they run to well over two million, and the number of French dead in the Napoleonic wars is estimated by modern scholars at several hundred thousand, with the figure depending on the definition of war deaths and on the inclusion of deaths from disease.

Resistance to conscription took many forms. Young men and their families sought exemptions, fraudulent medical certificates, substitutes, or marriages, and some mutilated themselves. Others became refractaires, deserting or failing to report, and by 1810 and 1811 thousands of refractaires and deserters lived in the mountains and forests, supported by their communities. Authorities responded by billeting troops, called garnisaires, on the families of refractaires, fining relatives, and conducting searches. In regions with weaker ties to the regime, including the west, the mountains, the newly annexed territories, and the countries of the Confederation of the Rhine, Italy, and the Low Countries, conscription provoked particular hostility. It was also a source of change. For many peasants, military service meant the first experience of life outside their village, the acquisition of literacy and new skills, and a connection with national and imperial identity. The meaning of conscription varied with the circumstances of each family and community.

Taxation increased with the demands of war. In France, direct taxes were relatively stable, while indirect taxes grew, and the regime relied heavily on extraordinary revenues from conquered territories. In the annexed departments, French tax systems were imposed, and in dependent states, governments were required to pay contributions to France, support French troops, and raise their own armies. The cost of French garrisons was borne by the territories they occupied. In Germany, Prussia after 1807 paid a large indemnity to France, which was a heavy burden, and the Kingdom of Westphalia paid for French troops and contributed to the Emperor's treasury. The Kingdom of Italy paid substantial sums and supplied tens of thousands of soldiers. Requisitions of food, horses, and transport, and the quartering of soldiers in private houses, imposed burdens on rural communities, particularly along the routes of the armies. In Spain, Portugal, and Poland, the movement of armies and the foraging that supported them devastated districts and provoked hostility.

The experiences of different groups varied. Rural households were concerned with conscription, taxes, requisitions, and the loss of labor, but some benefited from the abolition of feudal dues and the opportunity to buy land. Artisans were affected by the guild abolitions and the Continental System, which in some places encouraged new manufactures. Merchants experienced both opportunity, for those who supplied the army or developed new industries, and ruin, for those in maritime trade. Clergy faced new state controls but regained legal status. Local officials and property owners often cooperated in exchange for office and protection, though many felt their authority diminished. In occupied areas, civilians dealt with military authorities whose behavior ranged from disciplined administration to plunder.

Students should understand why some communities welcomed legal reform while resisting conscription, economic regulation, or foreign domination. A Rhineland peasant who gained from the abolition of feudal dues might nevertheless resent a son's conscription. A Dutch merchant who appreciated the Commercial Code might resent the Continental System. A Polish noble who welcomed the Duchy might resent French demands. The benefits of the regime often accrued to particular groups, while its burdens were widely shared. The burdens created tensions that later supported nationalist and anti-French mobilization. Chapter 91 examines the organization, ideology, military development, and political consequences of those resistance movements in depth. The point here is that resistance was not simply the product of ideology but was rooted in the experience of conscription, taxation, and occupation.

Multiple Perspectives

Experiencing Napoleonic Rule: Differing Perspectives on Law, Conscription, and Empire

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified from many sources. They are not quotations from any named individual, and the groups did not have equal power or freedom of action.

A property-owning official or landowner in France: The regime protects the purchase of national lands, defends property by law, and offers careers in the prefectural and legal system. The loss of political liberty and the burdens of war are tolerated as the price of order, and the ability to purchase a substitute for a conscripted son softens the cost of service.
A married woman under the Civil Code: The Code protects her family's property in principle but places her legal capacity under her husband's authorization and makes divorce harder than it had been in the 1790s. The security of the household depends on her husband's conduct, and the law offers her little independent standing.
A formerly enslaved person in Saint-Domingue or Guadeloupe: Emancipation was won through revolution and military service, and the French attempt to restore the old order is met with armed resistance. The meaning of French law is determined by whether it recognizes freedom, and in 1802 it does not.
A peasant conscript or refractaire in a French or Rhineland village: The abolition of feudal dues is welcome, but the annual levy takes sons away to distant wars. Some families purchase substitutes, others hide the young men, and gendarmes and billeted soldiers press the community to comply.
A merchant in Hamburg, Amsterdam, or Bordeaux: The Commercial Code and a stable currency are useful, but the blockade closes the sea on which the business depends. Smuggling, licenses, and the favor of officials become the means of survival, and ruin follows for many.
A Jewish family in the Empire or the Duchy of Warsaw: Equality before the civil law and the organization of consistories confirm legal recognition, but the decrees of 1808 impose restrictions on moneylending and, in the Duchy, suspend political rights. Opportunity and discrimination coexist.
Everyday Life and Social History

Daily Life under the Consulate and Empire

Most inhabitants of the Empire were rural, and for them the regime appeared through the mayor, the tax collector, the gendarme, the priest, the notary, and the conscription board. Civil registration meant that births, marriages, and deaths were recorded by the mayor, and marriage before the civil officer became a legal prerequisite for a religious ceremony. The restoration of public worship allowed the revival of religious festivals, processions, and church bells, which many communities welcomed, and the Concordat's reconstruction of parishes restored a familiar figure to many villages. Local markets, fairs, and the agricultural calendar structured the year, and food prices, harvest quality, and the availability of grain mattered more than constitutional change. The harvest crisis of 1811, with high bread prices in several regions, produced unrest that the authorities monitored closely.

In towns, artisans worked in a legal environment that had abolished guilds but regulated labor through work booklets and prohibited combinations. Workers who left an employer needed the booklet to be signed, and disputes about wages favored employers. Women worked in agriculture, textile production, domestic service, and small trade, though married women's legal capacity was limited. Children's education depended on family means, with a minority attending lycées or private schools, and others receiving religious or limited local instruction. Soldiers' families lived with absence, risk, and uncertainty. Letters from soldiers, bulletins from the army, and local rumor shaped how news from distant campaigns reached homes. The regime's military and fiscal demands therefore reached into households in ways that earlier governments had not, and the social history of the period is incomplete without them.

Section 90.19

The Imperial Apex, Dynastic Succession, European Dependence, and the Threshold of 1812

By the end of 1809, Napoleon had defeated Austria again, at Wagram on 5 and 6 July, and imposed the Treaty of Schönbrunn on 14 October. The war of 1809, which Austria had begun in hope of exploiting the Spanish crisis, showed the strains on the Empire but ended in French victory. In the following years, the regime reached its greatest territorial extent and appeared at its most secure. France's allies and dependents included nearly all the continental states, Britain remained at war with France and continued to fight alongside Portuguese and Spanish allies in the Iberian Peninsula, although it lacked the kind of broad great-power coalition then able to defeat Napoleon in central Europe, and Napoleon's family ruled or influenced a large part of Europe. Yet the foundations of this power were less stable than they appeared.

Dynastic legitimacy was a continuing concern. The marriage with Joséphine had produced no children, and Napoleon, whose rule rested on the hereditary principle, wanted an heir. The separation was announced to an imperial family council on 15 December 1809, and a sénatus-consulte of 16 December dissolved the civil marriage; the distinct ecclesiastical annulment followed in January 1810. Napoleon then sought a bride from a European royal house. After discussions that included the possibility of a Russian grand duchess, he married Archduchess Marie-Louise, daughter of Emperor Francis I of Austria, first by proxy in Vienna on 11 March 1810, with Archduke Charles standing in for Napoleon; the civil marriage took place at Saint-Cloud on 1 April and the religious ceremony in Paris on 2 April. The marriage tied the Empire to the Habsburgs, one of the oldest dynasties in Europe, and symbolized the acceptance of the Napoleonic regime by the courts it had defeated, though it offended Russia and many in France who associated the Habsburgs with the Austrian alliance of the old monarchy. On 20 March 1811, Marie-Louise gave birth to a son, Napoleon Francis Joseph Charles, who was titled King of Rome. The birth gave the regime a hereditary successor and was celebrated by a salute of cannon and public festivities.

Relations with Pope Pius VII had deteriorated. The Pope refused to join the Continental System, resisted French demands that he declare war on Britain, and objected to the occupation of Rome by French troops in 1808. In May 1809, Napoleon decreed the annexation of the Papal States to the Empire, and in June the Pope issued a bull of excommunication directed at those responsible, without naming Napoleon. French troops arrested Pius in July 1809 and held him in Savona, and later, in 1812, at Fontainebleau. Napoleon sought to resolve the conflict by pressing the Pope to accept the loss of temporal power, and he convened a national council of French bishops in 1811, which failed to give him the institutional authority he sought. The conflict caused unrest among Catholics, and it complicated the regime's religious settlement.

The extent of Napoleonic control around 1811 must be described with care. The French Empire at its territorial apex comprised roughly 130 directly administered departments stretching from the North Sea coast to Rome and parts of northern Germany; the Illyrian Provinces were administered separately and were not ordinary French departments. An imperial tabulation for 1812 reports 42,390,718 inhabitants in the 130 departments, so this figure should not be relabeled as an exact 1811 census of all territories ruled or influenced by Napoleon. Around it lay the Kingdom of Italy, the Kingdom of Naples under Murat, the Confederation of the Rhine, the Grand Duchy of Berg and the Kingdom of Westphalia, the Duchy of Warsaw, Switzerland, and the Kingdom of Spain, where Joseph's authority was limited to areas under French control. Denmark and Norway were allied to France, Austria and Prussia were allied under duress, and Russia, formally allied, was increasingly estranged. Sweden, whose crown prince since 1810 was the former French marshal Jean-Baptiste Bernadotte, pursued its own interests. This was not a unified state but a system of different forms of control, and its cohesion depended on military force and diplomatic constraint.

The contradictions of the system were worsening. The Continental System damaged ports and merchants, strained allies, and failed to bring Britain to terms. The financial burdens of occupation, the maintenance of large armies, and the demand for conscripts were creating hardships. A depression in 1810 and 1811, associated with credit crises, poor harvests, and the effects of the blockade, caused bankruptcies and unemployment in France and elsewhere. The government's dependence on coercion increased, with expanded police powers, state prisons, and surveillance. In Spain, the war continued to absorb several hundred thousand French troops. In the Papal States and in Germany, the regime's measures provoked opposition. Franco-Russian relations deteriorated as Russia withdrew from the Continental System, disputes arose over the Duchy of Warsaw and the annexation of Oldenburg, whose duke was related to the Tsar, and both sides prepared for war.

The chapter stops here, at the threshold of 1812. Napoleon was Emperor, with a son and heir, and his institutions seemed firmly established. Yet the structure rested on the continued ability to wage war successfully, to extract resources from the territories under his control, to preserve alliances with rulers who had reason to resent French domination, and to maintain the acquiescence of populations burdened by conscription, taxation, and economic hardship. The invasion of Russia, the destruction of the Grande Armée, and the coalition wars that destroyed the system belong to Chapter 91.

The concentration of decision-making in the Emperor's hands also contributed to the strain. Napoleon worked long hours, dictated large amounts of correspondence, and intervened in details of administration and finance across the Empire. The Council of State continued to draft and debate legislation, but the most consequential choices about war, diplomacy, and dynastic arrangements were taken by Napoleon with a small circle of advisers. Those advisers were not uniformly loyal. In January 1809, as news of difficulties in Spain and Austrian preparations for war reached Paris, Napoleon returned abruptly from the Peninsula and publicly reprimanded Talleyrand, the former foreign minister, whom he suspected of intriguing with Fouché and others about the succession in the event of his death. The episode shows that even within the elite the regime depended on the continued success of one man, and that some of its most capable officials were already considering what would follow him. The administrative apparatus was robust, but the political system gave few means of correcting the Emperor's judgment, a weakness that would matter when the decision for war in Russia was made.

Cause and Effect

The Establishment of the Hereditary Empire in 1804

Long-term causes

A decade of constitutional instability, civil and religious conflict, financial collapse, and war had discredited the republican regimes of the 1790s. Property holders feared both royalist reaction and radical revolution, and many officials preferred a strong executive.

Short-term causes

Military victories and peace in 1800 to 1802, the Concordat, the life consulate, the resumption of war in 1803, royalist conspiracies in 1803 and 1804, and the execution of the Duke of Enghien encouraged proposals for a hereditary government.

Event

The sénatus-consulte of 18 May 1804 made Napoleon Emperor of the French with hereditary succession, and a plebiscite and the coronation of 2 December confirmed the change.

Immediate effects

The regime gained dynastic form, a court, an imperial household, and a status comparable to that of the other European monarchies. Austria assumed an imperial title, and Britain, Russia, and Austria moved toward renewed coalition in 1805.

Long-term consequences

The Empire enabled the creation of a nobility, the dynastic placement of relatives, and the expansion of imperial government across Europe. It also bound the regime to the fortunes of a single family and to continued military success.

Historical complexity

The change cannot be reduced to Napoleon's ambition. It also reflected the interests of elites who sought security, the fears produced by assassination plots, and the unresolved legacy of revolutionary instability. Different groups supported or accepted the Empire for different reasons.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From the Revolution to the Napoleonic Empire

What Changed

  • Elected local administration gave way to appointed prefects and mayors
  • Representative government was replaced by a system in which the executive alone initiated laws
  • The republic became a hereditary empire, with a new nobility and court
  • Revolutionary abolition of slavery was reversed in several colonies, and divorce and women's legal capacity were restricted
  • Press freedom and open political debate were replaced by censorship and surveillance
  • The Church regained legal recognition under state supervision

What Stayed the Same

  • The abolition of feudal dues, legal privilege by birth, and guild monopolies
  • The sale of national lands and the protection of those who acquired them
  • The departmental framework and civil registration
  • Conscription and the mobilization of resources for war
  • Equality before the civil law among men, and the principle of careers open to talent

Who experienced change most directly? Political participants, journalists, and officials in local government experienced the loss of representative institutions most directly. Women and workers experienced legal changes that restricted their capacities. Formerly enslaved people in the colonies experienced the most violent reversal. Who experienced greater continuity? Peasant landholders and purchasers of national lands experienced continuity in property and the end of feudal dues. Why the pattern matters: The mixture of continuity and change helps explain why Napoleonic government attracted support and opposition from different groups.

Section 90.20

Chapter Review and Historical Synthesis

The preceding nineteen sections described a regime built on several layers. At its base was an administrative and legal structure: the prefects and ministries, the Council of State, the Civil Code and its companions, the Banque de France and the franc germinal, the Concordat, the lycées, and the Legion of Honour. Above it was a political structure that concentrated authority in a single office and, after 1804, a hereditary dynasty, surrounded by a court and a new nobility, and protected by police and censorship. Around it was a system of territories extending French law, administration, and demands across much of Europe, and linked to an overseas colonial policy that restored slavery in several colonies and failed in Saint-Domingue.

These layers reinforced one another. Administrative centralization made it possible to collect taxes and conscripts. Legal uniformity protected the property of those who supported the regime. Religious reconciliation removed a source of royalist opposition. Honours and education attached ambitious men to the state. Victory and annexation provided resources for the army and the treasury. But the same structures created vulnerabilities. The regime depended on constant military success, its legitimacy rested heavily on one man, and its demands on dependent territories produced resentment. Closing the continent to British trade required further conquest, and conquest produced further strain.

The chapter closes by distinguishing the long-lasting institutions created or consolidated under Napoleon from the territorial and dynastic empire that would collapse. The prefectural system, the Civil Code, the Concordat, the Banque de France, the departmental framework, and elements of the educational and judicial systems survived the fall of the Empire, and in several cases shaped states far beyond France. The territorial system of annexed departments, client kingdoms, and allied states did not survive, and neither did the imperial dynasty. Chapter 91 now turns to the wars, resistance, and nationalism through which that system was contested and destroyed.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Napoleon Bonaparte and his collaborators transformed the unstable French Republic into a centralized, increasingly authoritarian government, consolidated important revolutionary reforms, established a hereditary empire, and extended French legal, administrative, and political institutions across much of continental Europe. Its central argument is that Napoleonic government combined revolutionary legal reforms, administrative modernization, public order, dynastic monarchy, political repression, colonial coercion, and military expansion, and that these elements reinforced one another while also creating contradictions that threatened the system.

The Consulate began with the coup of Brumaire and the Constitution of Year VIII, which concentrated initiative in the First Consul and divided the legislative function among bodies that could not act independently. Plebiscites supplied claims to popular approval, but their conditions limited their evidential value. Political and administrative elites accepted the arrangement because they feared both royalist reaction and radical upheaval and wished to protect property, including the national lands sold during the Revolution. Pacification in the west, amnesty for émigrés, and the victories of Marengo and Lunéville, followed by the Peace of Amiens, strengthened the regime and reduced pressure for accountability.

The prefectural law of February 1800 created a chain of command that connected the center to every department, commune, and taxpayer. Napoleon did not invent the departments, which dated from the Revolution, but he replaced elected local administration with appointed officials. The ministry of police under Fouché and the growing apparatus of statistics and civil registration increased the state's capacity to observe its population. Administrative effectiveness, however, did not mean political freedom, and the system depended on cooperation between the state and local property owners.

The Concordat of 1801, accompanied by the Organic Articles of 1802, reconciled the state with organized Catholicism, ended the schism between constitutional and refractory clergy, and gave the Church legal security, but it did not restore the Church of 1789. The state nominated bishops, paid clergy, and supervised religious life, and it organized Protestant and, later, Jewish communities under consistories. The Civil Code of 1804, produced by jurists and the Council of State under political direction, gave France a uniform law that confirmed equality before the civil law for men, property rights, and the end of feudal privilege, while strengthening paternal and marital authority, restricting divorce, and giving employers legal advantages.

The fiscal and monetary settlement stabilized the currency through the franc germinal, created the Banque de France, and improved tax collection, though the regime's solvency depended increasingly on war indemnities and contributions from occupied territories. The restoration of slavery in several colonies, through the law of 20 May 1802 and subsequent orders in Guadeloupe and Guiana, and the failure of the Saint-Domingue expedition, which led to Haitian independence in 1804, demonstrated the limits of revolutionary universalism. The agency of formerly enslaved people was decisive in that outcome.

The move from republic to empire proceeded through the life consulate of 1802 and the sénatus-consulte of 18 May 1804, which made Napoleon Emperor before the coronation of December. The coronation, the court, the marshalate, the imperial nobility of 1808, and the Legion of Honour created a new hierarchy that coexisted uneasily with the regime's claims to merit. The lycées and the Imperial University trained an elite for state service, but access was limited by class and gender, and universal schooling was not established. Censorship, police surveillance, and propaganda shaped public opinion, and the decree of February 1810 embedded press control in the structure of government.

Napoleonic Europe was a hierarchy of territories. Annexed departments were governed directly, client monarchies were ruled by Napoleon's relatives, and allied states, such as those of the Confederation of the Rhine, retained their dynasties under French protection. The dissolution of the Holy Roman Empire in 1806 followed the territorial consolidation of the German states, which German princes themselves pursued. The Kingdom of Italy, Naples, Holland, and Spain illustrated the different forms of client government, and Spain showed the failure of imposed dynastic substitution. The Duchy of Warsaw demonstrated that legal reform could abolish personal servitude on paper without redistributing economic power.

The Continental System sought to defeat Britain through economic warfare, but it damaged ports and merchants across the continent, depended on smuggling and licenses, and required further conquests that increased the strain on the Empire. Conscription, taxation, requisitioning, and occupation imposed heavy burdens, and communities that welcomed legal reform often resisted these demands. Those burdens helped provide the basis for later anti-French and nationalist mobilization, which Chapter 91 examines.

By 1811 and 1812, the Empire reached its greatest extent and apparent security, with a hereditary heir, a Habsburg marriage, and about 130 departments. Yet the conflicts with the Papacy and Russia, the continuing war in Spain, economic depression, and increased reliance on coercion revealed vulnerabilities rooted in the same structures that had enabled expansion. The institutions created or consolidated under Napoleon proved more durable than the territorial and dynastic system built on them.

Historians continue to debate Napoleon's legacy. Some emphasize the consolidation of revolutionary achievements, others the replacement of liberty by authoritarian rule, and still others the coexistence of both. The chapter has argued that the evidence supports a mixed assessment, in which legal equality for men, protection of property, and administrative uniformity coexisted with political repression, patriarchal law, colonial slavery, and imperial domination.

Key Terms

Brumaire
The coup of 18 and 19 Brumaire Year VIII (9 and 10 November 1799) that overthrew the Directory and opened the way to the Consulate.
Consulate
The government of France from 1799 to 1804, headed by three Consuls under the Constitution of Year VIII, with decisive power in the First Consul.
First Consul
The title held by Napoleon from 1799, giving him authority to promulgate laws, appoint officials, and direct foreign policy and the army.
Constitution of Year VIII
The constitution of 13 December 1799 that concentrated initiative in the executive and divided the legislative function among the Tribunate, Legislative Body, and Senate.
Council of State
The body of jurists and administrators that drafted laws and regulations and decided administrative disputes; the working center of Napoleonic government.
Tribunate
A body of one hundred members created in 1799 to discuss bills without voting; reduced in 1802 and abolished in 1807.
Conservative Senate
A body of eighty life members with power to select legislators and, by sénatus-consulte, to alter the constitution.
Plebiscite
A direct popular vote on a government measure, used in 1800, 1802, and 1804 to ratify decisions under conditions that limited choice.
Sénatus-consulte
A decision of the Senate with constitutional force, used to establish the life consulate and the Empire.
Prefect
An official appointed to head a department, responsible for taxes, public order, conscription, and reporting.
Law of 28 Pluviôse Year VIII
The law of 17 February 1800 that created the prefectural system.
Ministry of Police
The ministry, led by Fouché for most of 1799 to 1810, that organized surveillance and political control.
Concordat of 1801
The agreement between France and Pope Pius VII that recognized Catholicism as the religion of most French people, reorganized dioceses, and provided for state-paid clergy.
Organic Articles
The regulations of 1802, issued unilaterally by the government, that placed Catholic and Protestant worship under state supervision.
Civil Code
The unified civil law of 1804, later called the Code Napoléon, covering property, inheritance, contracts, marriage, and civil status.
Remplacement
The practice of hiring a substitute to serve in place of a conscript, available to those who could afford it.
Banque de France
The bank founded in 1800, given a monopoly of note issue in Paris in 1803, that became a central financial institution.
Franc germinal
The currency defined by the monetary law of 28 March 1803, on a bimetallic standard of silver and gold.
Law of 20 May 1802
The statute that maintained slavery in colonies returned under the Treaty of Amiens and set the framework for pre-1789 law elsewhere.
Haitian Revolution
The revolution in Saint-Domingue that ended slavery and French rule, culminating in independence on 1 January 1804.
Life consulate
The extension of Napoleon's tenure for life by the sénatus-consulte of August 1802.
Hereditary Empire
The government established by the sénatus-consulte of 18 May 1804, with Napoleon as Emperor and hereditary succession.
Marshals of the Empire
Senior commanders, eighteen of whom were named in May 1804, who formed part of the imperial elite.
Imperial nobility
The titles created from 1808 and rewarded to service, often with endowed estates, which formed a new hereditary elite.
Legion of Honour
An order of merit founded in 1802 for military and civilian service.
Lycée
A state secondary school created by the law of 1 May 1802, with a military style regime and limited scholarships.
Imperial University
The centralized corporation organized in 1808 to supervise education across the Empire.
Client state
A nominally independent state controlled or strongly influenced by France, such as the Kingdom of Italy or Westphalia.
Annexation
The formal incorporation of a territory into the French Empire as a department.
Confederation of the Rhine
The league of German states formed in 1806 under Napoleon's protection, which led to the end of the Holy Roman Empire.
Mediatization
The subordination of formerly sovereign imperial entities, such as free cities and small lordships, to larger states.
Duchy of Warsaw
The Polish state created in 1807 under French protection, with a constitution abolishing personal servitude.
Continental System
The set of decrees beginning with the Berlin Decree of 1806 that sought to exclude British trade from the continent.
Conscription
Compulsory military service by annual levy, regularized in France and extended across the Empire.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a revolutionary society resolved, at least for a time, the tensions it had produced. The Revolution had broken the legal order of the old regime but had not created a stable government. Napoleonic rule offered one answer: secure the social and legal gains that propertied groups valued, concentrate authority in a powerful executive, reconcile with religion, and mobilize the resources of the state for war. That answer shaped the institutions of France and, through conquest, influence, and later imitation, the legal and administrative development of much of Europe and beyond.

The chapter also reveals the costs and contradictions of that answer. A regime that guaranteed equality before the law for men while strengthening patriarchal authority, that ended the Revolution's religious conflicts while supervising the Church, that spoke of merit while creating a new nobility, and that restored slavery in several colonies while claiming to protect revolutionary principles, shows that equality, liberty, and order could be pursued selectively. The Haitian Revolution, in particular, demonstrated that the meaning of revolutionary freedom would be decided not by European governments alone but by those who had been excluded from it.

For Volume IV, the chapter marks the moment when the political and legal experiments of the Revolution were consolidated, narrowed, and exported across Europe. The liberalism, conservatism, nationalism, and socialism of the nineteenth century would develop partly in response to what Napoleon had built and destroyed. Many of the Napoleonic institutions, such as the prefects, the codes, and the centralized educational system, survived into the nineteenth and twentieth centuries and continued to shape European states, while the memory of the Emperor became a resource for political movements of very different kinds, a subject that later chapters will address.

Chapter 91 now turns from the construction of the Napoleonic system to its contest and destruction. The Peninsular War, the Russian campaign, popular resistance, nationalist mobilization, and the coalition wars that ended in 1814 and 1815 would reveal how far the system depended on military success and how deeply its demands had alienated the populations on which it relied. Understanding the institutions and burdens described in this chapter is essential to understanding why that collapse occurred and why so many of the institutions nevertheless endured.
