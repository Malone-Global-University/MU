Catholic Reform and the Council of Trent

European History: Antiquity, Christendom, States, Empires, and Modern Europe

Chapter Opening

When the Council of Trent opened on December 13, 1545, in a prince bishopric of the Holy Roman Empire where Italian and German speech met, only a few dozen prelates were present, no Protestant leader had agreed to attend, and many observers doubted that anything lasting would come of it. When it closed on December 4, 1563, it had defined Catholic teaching on scripture, justification, and the sacraments and had laid down a program for bishops, seminaries, and parishes that Catholic Europe would spend generations trying to realize.

The town of Trent (Latin Tridentum, Italian Trento) was a compromise. Emperor Charles V wanted a council on imperial soil, where German Catholics and, he hoped, Protestants could take part. The papacy, fearful of a council that might discipline popes as the fifteenth century councils had tried to do, wanted a location it could influence. Trent lay on the Adige river and the Alpine route between Italy and the German lands, a borderland of the kind examined in Chapter 2, and it belonged to a prince bishop who was a vassal of the empire. The assembly that gathered there met in three periods across eighteen years, was suspended twice, and was shaped throughout by wars, dynastic rivalries, epidemics, and quarrels among its own members. Its decrees survive in the council's official acts, in the diaries and letters of participants, and in the reports of ambassadors, and from these sources historians reconstruct not a single confident voice but a prolonged negotiation.

This chapter studies the broader movement of which Trent was the institutional center: the renewal of Catholic Christianity in doctrine, discipline, education, devotion, and mission between the late fifteenth and early seventeenth centuries. The movement had two faces that cannot be separated. It reaffirmed and sharpened doctrines that Protestant reformers had attacked, including the authority of tradition and the Church in interpreting scripture, the cooperation of human beings with divine grace, the sacramental system, and the sacrifice of the Mass. It also pursued a long agenda of institutional reform, aimed at absentee bishops, ignorant and undisciplined clergy, neglected parishes, and lax religious houses, that many Catholics had demanded long before Martin Luther's protest of 1517 (Chapters 61 and 62).

That double character is why historians argue over what to call it. The older label Counter-Reformation emphasizes reaction against Protestantism, while Catholic Reformation or Catholic Reform emphasizes renewal with roots in the later Middle Ages. Section 65.1 and the Historical Debate in this chapter examine the labels carefully, because the choice of word changes what students notice. The evidence supports neither label alone. Reforming initiatives in Spain and Italy predate Luther, yet the Council's timing, its anathemas, the Roman Inquisition, and the Index of Prohibited Books were plainly shaped by the Protestant challenge.

The chapter also insists that Trent was not the whole story. Reform depended on bishops such as Charles Borromeo, on new religious orders such as the Jesuits, Capuchins, and Theatines, on older orders that reformed themselves, on women such as Teresa of Ávila and Angela Merici, on lay confraternities, and on the secular rulers whose cooperation could make a decree effective or leave it unread. Between prescription and practice lay a wide gap, and the chapter attends to it throughout: the Council could order bishops to reside in their dioceses and found seminaries, but finance, patronage, local custom, and political conflict determined whether they did so.

Catholic renewal also combined care with coercion. The same period saw expanded charity, preaching, catechesis, and schooling alongside censorship, inquisitorial prosecution, pressure on Jewish communities, and cooperation between church and state in suppressing dissent. Students should neither celebrate this combination nor reduce it to repression. The history of Catholic renewal includes both the founding of schools and hospitals and the restriction of what people could read, say, and believe.

Finally, Catholic renewal was never confined to Europe. Missionaries, many of them Jesuits, Franciscans, and Dominicans, carried Tridentine ideas to the Americas, Africa, and Asia, where they encountered societies with their own religious systems and political authorities. Section 65.24 introduces that story only as far as needed to understand Catholic renewal; the imperial and commercial systems themselves belong to Chapters 68 through 73.

This chapter stands in the middle of Part X. Chapters 61 through 64 examined the late medieval demand for reform, Luther, Reformed and radical Protestantism, and the English Reformation. Chapters 66 and 67 will show how confessional division interacted with dynastic ambition, rebellion, and international war. Nothing in Catholic renewal made those conflicts inevitable, and nothing in it was inevitably triumphant. It was a set of contingent responses, uneven in effect, whose consequences were still unfolding in the seventeenth century and beyond.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  2. Trace reforming movements in Spain and Italy before 1517 and explain how they shaped later Catholic renewal.
  3. Explain why a general council took so long to convene and how papal, imperial, and French politics shaped its three periods.
  4. Analyze the Council's teaching on scripture, tradition, original sin, justification, and the sacraments in relation to Protestant arguments.
  5. Describe the Council's disciplinary program for bishops, clergy, seminaries, marriage, and religious houses.

  7. Evaluate the role of the Society of Jesus, new orders, and older orders in Catholic education, pastoral care, and spirituality.
  8. Assess how the Roman Inquisition, the Index, and cooperation with secular rulers combined coercion with renewal.
  9. Interpret the experience of women religious and the constraints placed on female religious leadership.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. To what extent was Catholic reform a response to Protestantism, and to what extent did it continue older movements for renewal?
  2. Why did it take nearly three decades after 1517 for a general council to meet, and how did the delay shape the outcome?
  3. Did Trent invent new Catholic doctrine, or did it define and defend existing teaching more precisely, and why does the distinction matter?
  4. Why did decrees on bishops, seminaries, and parish life require generations to implement, and what did implementation depend on?
  5. How did the Jesuits and other new orders change Catholic education, preaching, and spiritual life, and who else mattered?
  6. How did renewal and coercion operate together, and who experienced each most directly?
  7. How did Catholic reform differ among Italy, Spain, France, the German lands, the Habsburg territories, and Poland-Lithuania, and what explains the differences?
  8. What evidence allows historians to measure the effects of conciliar decrees on ordinary parishes, clergy, and households?

Historical Context

By 1545 Western Christendom had been divided for a generation. Lutheran reform had been established in many German territories and in parts of Scandinavia (Chapter 62), Reformed churches had taken root in Zurich, Geneva, and elsewhere (Chapter 63), and Henry VIII had separated the English church from papal obedience (Chapter 64). The Peace of Augsburg of 1555 would later give Lutheran and Catholic princes within the Holy Roman Empire a legal framework for coexistence, though not one that included Reformed Christians. At the same time the Ottoman Empire pressed on Habsburg Hungary and the Mediterranean, and the Valois and Habsburg dynasties continued their rivalry, so that every attempt to assemble a council was also a diplomatic problem.

The Latin church that these events challenged was a complex institution. Its head, the pope, was simultaneously a spiritual leader and the Italian ruler of the Papal States. Below him stood bishops who often owed their offices to royal nomination, family connection, or papal favor, and who might hold several benefices at once and live far from their dioceses. Cathedral chapters, monasteries, and many religious orders enjoyed exemptions that limited episcopal authority, and lay patrons controlled appointments to thousands of parishes. Parish clergy varied enormously, from learned preachers in university towns to poorly trained priests who barely understood the liturgy they recited. The late medieval background of these arrangements is treated in Chapters 44, 53, and 61 and need not be repeated here.

Catholic theology in 1545 was not a single fixed system. Scholastic traditions, examined in Chapter 48, included Thomist, Scotist, and Augustinian emphases, and on questions such as grace and free will, the nature of justification, and the authority of councils relative to popes, respected Catholic theologians disagreed. Humanists had also urged a return to scripture and the Church Fathers (Chapters 57 and 59). Some Catholic thinkers, in Italy especially, sympathized with parts of the Protestant emphasis on faith and grace without leaving the church. One reason Trent matters is that it narrowed this range of permitted opinion, though less completely than later polemic suggested.

Historians reconstruct this subject from unusually rich sources: the acts and decrees of the Council, the diaries of secretaries and participants, diplomatic dispatches, papal bulls, diocesan synod records, and above all the records of episcopal visitations, in which inspectors described the condition of churches, clergy, and parishioners. Inquisition trial records, confraternity statutes, catechisms, sermons, the letters of Jesuit missionaries, and surviving parish registers add further evidence. Most of these sources were produced by clergy and officials. Laypeople, women, and dissenters appear mainly as they were described, questioned, or disciplined, and their own voices must be sought carefully.

A note on dating: throughout this chapter, dates before 1582 follow the Julian calendar then used in all of Catholic Europe. Pope Gregory XIII's calendar reform, discussed in Section 65.13, moved Catholic states to the Gregorian calendar in October 1582, while Protestant and Orthodox regions adopted it much later. No date in the Council's proceedings is affected, but students should remember the difference when comparing Catholic and non-Catholic sources from the later sixteenth century.

Section 65.1

Catholic Reform and the Protestant Challenge

Three labels compete in the historiography. Counter-Reformation describes the Catholic effort to resist and reverse Protestantism. The German form Gegenreformation appeared in legal and political writing in the later eighteenth century and was popularized in the nineteenth by Protestant historians who saw Catholic revival chiefly as reaction. Catholic Reformation and Catholic Reform stress renewal that drew on medieval and humanist traditions and would have occurred, in some form, without Luther. In the twentieth century the church historian Hubert Jedin argued that both a Catholic reformation and a Counter-Reformation took place and that the two were intertwined. More recently, many historians prefer early modern Catholicism, a term that avoids defining the whole period by its relation to Protestantism, or they analyze all the major churches together through the concept of confessionalization, associated with Wolfgang Reinhard and Heinz Schilling, which examines how churches and states built confessional identity and discipline in parallel.

Each label is useful and each distorts. Counter-Reformation correctly captures the polemical edge of the period, including the anathemas of Trent, the Index, and the confessional wars. It obscures the reforming bishops, the charitable confraternities, and the mystics for whom Protestantism was a distant concern. Catholic Reformation correctly emphasizes pre-Lutheran initiatives, but it can understate coercion and can imply a unified movement when reform was pursued by competing groups with different aims. This chapter uses Catholic reform for the whole process of renewal, reserves Counter-Reformation for measures plainly aimed at Protestantism, and uses Tridentine to describe what derived from the Council of Trent. The Historical Debate later in the chapter returns to the problem.

The Protestant challenge nonetheless changed the conditions of reform. Before 1517, reform proposals were arguments among Catholics about how to improve a church whose basic structure was not in question. After the Reformation, every proposal could be read as a concession to or a defense against Protestant claims, and church leaders had to decide which practices were negotiable and which were essential. The Council of Trent resolved many of these questions by defining Catholic positions in terms that excluded Protestant ones. That choice produced clarity at the cost of foreclosing compromise, which many contemporaries understood.

Section 65.2

Late Medieval Reform Traditions and the Demand for Renewal

The Catholic church of the fifteenth century was not stagnant. Observant movements within the Franciscan, Dominican, Augustinian, and Benedictine orders sought stricter adherence to rules of poverty, prayer, and common life. Benedictine congregations such as Santa Giustina of Padua and Bursfeld in Germany linked monasteries into networks of mutual inspection. The Devotio Moderna, a lay and clerical movement centered in the Low Countries, promoted interior prayer and practical piety and produced the widely read Imitation of Christ. Confraternities organized lay devotion and charity, and preachers such as Bernardino of Siena and, more controversially, Girolamo Savonarola called city populations to moral reform. Chapter 61 examined these currents and the criticisms of clerical abuses that accompanied them.

Institutional reform proved harder. The conciliar movement of the early fifteenth century, treated in Chapter 53, produced reform decrees at Constance and Basel, but papal resistance and political conflict prevented comprehensive change. The Fifth Lateran Council (1512–1517), summoned by Julius II and concluded under Leo X, issued measures on preaching, censorship of books, and the endowment of charitable lending institutions known as monti di pietà, but it did not seriously reorganize the Curia, curb the sale of offices, or address the pastoral concerns that were soon to become Protestant causes. The reforms that mattered were often local, led by a determined bishop, a reforming prior, or a pious lay association.

Humanism contributed tools and aspirations. Philological scholarship, practiced by Lorenzo Valla and by Erasmus (Chapter 59), made the biblical and patristic texts more accessible and encouraged a more inward, scripture centered piety that some Catholic reformers shared. Erasmus himself stayed within the church but was suspect to both sides, and the fate of his reputation shows the narrowing that followed. Works that circulated freely among Catholic readers in the 1520s were by the 1550s regarded as dangerous in Italy and Spain. Catholic reform was therefore partly a continuation of the late medieval search for renewal and partly a redefinition of which forms of renewal were acceptable.

Section 65.3

Spanish Reform and the Renewal of Religious Institutions

Spain offers the clearest example of reform before Luther and also of its dependence on royal power. After the union of the crowns of Castile and Aragon, Ferdinand and Isabella obtained from the papacy substantial control over ecclesiastical appointments in their realms and in newly conquered territories, and they supported the reform of monasteries and convents through the Observant movements. In 1478 the crown established the Spanish Inquisition, an institution under royal rather than papal control, directed first at conversos suspected of secretly practicing Judaism and later at other forms of heterodoxy. The expulsion of Jews who refused baptism in 1492 and the pressure on Muslims of Granada to convert in the following decade were pursued as acts of religious unification and illustrate the combination of renewal and coercion that characterizes the whole chapter.

Francisco Jiménez de Cisneros (1436–1517) embodied the combination. A Franciscan Observant, he became confessor to Queen Isabella and in 1495 archbishop of Toledo, the senior see of Spain. He reformed convents and monasteries, sometimes over fierce resistance, promoted preaching and the printing of devotional works in Castilian, and in 1499 founded the University of Alcalá de Henares to train clergy in theology, languages, and the arts; teaching began in 1508. Cisneros also served as Inquisitor General from 1507, supported the forced conversion campaign in Granada in 1499–1501 that provoked revolt, and acted as regent of Castile after Ferdinand's death in 1516. His career shows that Spanish reform was inseparable from the state.

His most famous scholarly project was the Complutensian Polyglot Bible, printed at Alcalá between 1514 and 1517 and circulated after papal authorization in the early 1520s. It set the Hebrew, Greek, and Latin Vulgate texts of the Old Testament in parallel columns, with the Aramaic Targum for the Pentateuch, and printed the Greek New Testament alongside the Vulgate. It reflected Catholic humanism: a commitment to textual accuracy and to the original languages, held together with loyalty to the Vulgate as the church's received text. Spanish theologians trained at Alcalá, Salamanca, and elsewhere, including Dominicans such as Domingo de Soto and Melchor Cano, later played important roles at Trent. Spanish reform was thus a major source of the Council's personnel and ideas, but it came with a distinctively royal and inquisitorial character that other Catholic regions did not share to the same degree.

Section 65.4

Italian Reform Movements and New Religious Communities

Italian reform drew on urban lay piety and clerical networks. The Oratory of Divine Love, a confraternity of clergy and laity that began at Genoa in the late 1490s and had a Roman branch in the 1510s, combined prayer, frequent communion, and care for the sick and incurably ill. Its members founded hospitals for the incurables at Genoa and Rome and included men who would later shape the church, among them Gaetano da Thiene, Gian Pietro Carafa, Jacopo Sadoleto, and Gian Matteo Giberti. The dates and membership of the Roman Oratory are less certain than later hagiography suggests, but the pattern is clear: devout laypeople and clergy created charitable and spiritual institutions without waiting for papal direction.

New communities of priests, called regular clerics, appeared in the 1520s and 1530s. The Theatines, founded in 1524 by Gaetano da Thiene and Carafa, who was then bishop of Chieti (Latin Theate), combined strict poverty with a program of improving the clergy by example, preaching, and liturgical care. The Barnabites, begun in Milan around 1530, and the Somaschi, devoted to orphans, followed. The Capuchins, a reform of the Franciscan order that Pope Clement VII approved in 1528, stressed austerity, preaching to the poor, and service during epidemics. Their prominent preacher Bernardino Ochino fled to Protestant lands in 1542, an episode that nearly led to the order's suppression and illustrates how porous the boundary between reform and dissent still was.

Reform in dioceses mattered as much. Giberti, appointed bishop of Verona in 1524, resided in his diocese, held visitations, supervised clergy, and founded a printing press and a school of clerical education, creating a model that Charles Borromeo later admired. Around Viterbo and Rome gathered a circle of reform minded figures later called the Spirituali, including Reginald Pole, Vittoria Colonna, and Giovanni Morone, who valued an inward piety of grace and faith that came close to Protestant formulations. Some of them paid for this later, when the harsher climate of the 1550s led to inquisitorial inquiries, flight, and in the case of Morone imprisonment under Paul IV before his rehabilitation. Italian reform was diverse, and some of its members could be claimed by later Catholic and Protestant narratives alike.

Section 65.5

The Papacy, Failed Reconciliation, and the Road to Trent

The papacy's response to Luther was slow and uneven. Leo X issued a condemnation in 1520 but treated the matter largely as a dispute among clerics. Adrian VI, a Dutch scholar elected in 1522, admitted through his representatives at the Diet of Nuremberg that abuses in Rome had contributed to the crisis, but his reign was short and his reform efforts met resistance from the Curia. Clement VII (1523–1534) feared that a council would revive the conciliar claims of the fifteenth century and place limits on papal authority. His reign was dominated by the Habsburg-Valois conflict and by the Sack of Rome in 1527, when imperial troops devastated the city. Charles V repeatedly demanded a council; popes repeatedly postponed it.

Paul III (1534–1549), a Roman aristocrat who promoted his own family with Renaissance generosity, nonetheless made decisive moves toward reform. He raised Contarini, Pole, Carafa, and Sadoleto to the cardinalate and appointed a commission whose 1537 report, often called the Consilium de emendanda ecclesia, blamed abuses on the Curia, the sale of benefices, the neglect of residence by bishops, lax religious orders, and the careless admission of unqualified men to orders. The report was meant to be confidential. It leaked, and Luther published a German edition with mocking commentary, an early demonstration of the power of print to turn internal reform into a polemical weapon (Chapter 60).

Contarini also represented the hope of reconciliation. At the Colloquy of Regensburg in 1541, sponsored by Charles V, Catholic and Protestant theologians, including Johann Gropper, Philip Melanchthon, and Martin Bucer, drafted an agreement on justification that sought a middle path, emphasizing that believers are made righteous through faith and love together. The colloquy foundered on the Eucharist and on papal authority, and the justification formula was rejected by both Rome and Wittenberg. Contarini died in 1542. In the same year Paul III established the Roman Inquisition, signaling a turn toward definition and enforcement. After years of failed attempts, including aborted councils at Mantua and Vicenza, the pope finally summoned a council to Trent in 1545 after a temporary peace between Charles V and Francis I. By then Protestant movements were so well established that the council would define Catholicism against them rather than reunify Christendom.

Section 65.6

Convening the Council: Papal Authority, Imperial Politics, and Three Phases of Debate

The Council met in three periods. The first (December 1545 to March 1547) opened under Paul III with papal legates, including Cardinal Marcello Cervini and Cardinal Pole, presiding over a small gathering of Italian and Spanish bishops. Its method was a compromise: dogmatic and reform questions would be treated in parallel, which satisfied the pope's wish to define doctrine and the emperor's wish to address abuses. Bishops voted individually rather than by nations, as at Constance, which gave the numerous Italian bishops a decisive advantage. In 1547, citing an outbreak of illness at Trent, the majority voted to move to Bologna, which was within the Papal States. Charles V, who had just defeated the Protestant Schmalkaldic League in the field, objected, and the council effectively stopped. It was suspended in 1549.

The second period (1551–1552) was convened by Julius III and attended by more German bishops, and a few Protestant delegations reached Trent. This phase defined the Eucharist and penance. It ended abruptly in April 1552, when Maurice of Saxony's revolt against Charles V threatened the town. A decade of papal change followed, including the severe pontificate of Paul IV (1555–1559), who distrusted councils and prioritized the Inquisition. Pius IV reconvened the council in January 1562 with the encouragement of Catholic rulers and the pressure of religious war in France.

The third period (1562–1563) was the most consequential for reform. French prelates led by Charles, Cardinal of Lorraine, arrived late in 1562 with Gallican demands, Spanish bishops pressed to define the bishop's duty to reside as a matter of divine law, which threatened papal authority, and the emperor, Ferdinand I, sought concessions on communion under both kinds and clerical marriage. Debate was stalemated until the deaths of two legates in March 1563, and the appointment of Cardinal Morone as president made possible a series of negotiated compromises. The council closed in early December 1563, with about two hundred and fifty prelates signing the final acts. It is important to note what the Council was: an assembly of mostly Italian and Spanish bishops, working through committees, under papal supervision and under pressure from royal ambassadors. It spoke for the Catholic church, but it did not include Protestants, Eastern Christians, or most laypeople.

Section 65.7

Scripture, Tradition, and the Authority of the Church

The fourth session (April 8, 1546) addressed the question that stood at the center of the Protestant critique: where does religious authority lie? Protestant reformers appealed to scripture alone (sola scriptura), though they interpreted it in different ways (Chapters 62 and 63). The Council replied that the Gospel was transmitted in written books and in unwritten apostolic traditions received by the church, and that both were to be received with equal reverence. Historians have long debated one detail: earlier drafts spoke of truth contained "partly" in scripture and "partly" in tradition, while the final text joined them with a simple "and." Whether the change was intended to avoid endorsing two separate sources of revelation has been argued by Catholic theologians and historians ever since, and the decree can support more than one reading.

The decree also fixed the canon of scripture, listing the books of the Old and New Testaments the church received, including the deuterocanonical books that Protestants excluded from the Old Testament canon. It declared the Latin Vulgate "authentic" for use in public lectures, disputations, sermons, and expositions. This did not forbid scholarship on Hebrew and Greek texts, and it did not claim that the Vulgate was free of error, but it meant that Catholic biblical scholarship would be tied to a particular text. The Council further provided that no one should interpret scripture against the sense held by the church or the unanimous consent of the Fathers, and it asked for controls on the printing of religious books. These provisions encouraged later Catholic practices of censorship but also stimulated an enormous literature of commentary and controversy.

The decree on scripture is best understood as an affirmation of existing practice. The medieval church had never taught that scripture was the sole authority, and the Council did not invent the role of tradition. What changed was explicitness. By defining the sources of authority in terms that denied Protestant positions, Trent made the difference between Catholic and Protestant understandings of religious knowledge a matter of dogma rather than of continuing argument.

Section 65.8

Original Sin, Grace, Faith, and Justification

The decree on original sin, issued on June 17, 1546, affirmed that Adam's sin affected all humanity, that baptism removes the guilt of original sin, and that the lingering inclination to sin, called concupiscence, remains after baptism but is not itself sin in the proper sense. The decree avoided taking a position on the Immaculate Conception of the Virgin Mary, a question on which Franciscans and Dominicans disagreed, which reveals how the Council tried to avoid foreclosing legitimate theological disputes among Catholics.

The decree on justification, promulgated on January 13, 1547, after seven months of debate, was the Council's most carefully constructed doctrinal statement. It consisted of sixteen chapters and thirty-three canons. Against Luther and later reformers, who taught that sinners are declared righteous by God on account of Christ through faith alone, it taught that justification is not merely the forgiveness of sins but an inner renewal, in which the believer receives righteousness from God as a gift. The process begins with God's grace, which precedes any human merit, but it requires the free cooperation of the human will, which can reject grace. Faith is the beginning of salvation but is not sufficient without hope and charity, and good works, performed through grace, increase justification. Grace can be lost through mortal sin and recovered through the sacrament of penance. The decree also denied that believers can have absolute certainty of their own election, a position aimed at Reformed doctrines of assurance and predestination.

The text was a product of negotiation. Girolamo Seripando, the Augustinian superior general, argued for a formula of double justification in which the believer's own inherent righteousness is always imperfect and must be completed by reliance on Christ's merits, a view close to the Regensburg compromise. The final decree did not adopt it, though it also avoided condemning those who held related views. Dominican and Jesuit theologians later disagreed about how grace and free will interact, and the dispute reached Rome in the 1590s and 1600s, which shows that the decree left open questions. The Council usually condemned positions without naming the individuals who held them, which has made it difficult to tell exactly which Protestant claims were in view and has fueled disagreement about how far Trent accurately represented Protestant teaching.

Section 65.9

The Eucharist, the Mass, and Catholic Sacramental Theology

At its seventh session (March 3, 1547), the Council affirmed that there are seven sacraments instituted by Christ, baptism, confirmation, the Eucharist, penance, extreme unction, holy orders, and matrimony, and that they confer grace through the rite itself and not through faith alone. Protestant churches generally retained only baptism and the Eucharist, and in some cases penance, so this decree marked one of the sharpest institutional boundaries between confessions. It also defended the principle that the sacraments depend on the minister's intention to do what the church does, not on his personal holiness, which preserved the validity of sacraments performed by unworthy priests.

The thirteenth session (October 11, 1551) defined the Eucharist. It taught that Christ is truly, really, and substantially present under the appearances of bread and wine and that the whole substance of the bread and wine is changed into the body and blood of Christ, a change the church "aptly" calls transubstantiation. The Council did not make the Aristotelian language of substance and accidents the entire doctrine, but it kept the term. The doctrine excluded Lutheran teaching on the real presence in union with bread and wine, Reformed understandings of spiritual presence, and Zwingli's symbolic reading (Chapters 62 and 63). The decree also endorsed eucharistic devotion, including processions on Corpus Christi, adoration of the consecrated host, and the reservation of the sacrament in churches.

Two further decrees shaped Catholic worship. In its twenty-first session (July 1562), the Council taught that Christ is received whole under either form, bread or wine, so that laypeople were not obliged to receive the cup; it left the question of granting the cup in particular lands to the pope. Pius IV did grant it for some German and Habsburg territories in 1564, but the concession proved unpopular with church leaders and was gradually withdrawn. In the twenty-second session (September 1562), it affirmed that the Mass is a true sacrifice, offered for the living and the dead, and defended the use of Latin while recommending that pastors explain its meaning in the vernacular. It condemned those who held that Mass should be celebrated only in the vernacular. These decisions mattered for ordinary people because the Mass was the center of parish life, and later Catholics encountered a liturgy that was more uniform, more clerical, and more distinct from Protestant worship than before.

Section 65.10

Holy Orders, Marriage, Penance, and Christian Life

The fourteenth session (November 1551) defined penance as a sacrament in which a priest, acting as a judge, absolves the penitent who has confessed his or her serious sins and who intends to amend. It insisted on private auricular confession and on the obligation to confess all mortal sins one remembers, a practice that Protestants largely rejected or reduced to voluntary consultation. Confession became the central instrument by which clergy could inform, correct, and discipline the laity, and a large literature of manuals for confessors, often organized by cases of conscience, developed to guide parish priests. The twenty-third session (July 15, 1563) defined holy orders as a sacrament that confers an indelible character and established a distinct priesthood, affirmed the hierarchy of bishops, priests, and deacons, and rejected the Protestant idea of the priesthood of all believers as a substitute for ordained ministry.

The twenty-fourth session (November 11, 1563) addressed marriage. It affirmed that matrimony is a sacrament, that it is indissoluble even in cases of adultery, that clerical celibacy and virginity are superior to the married state, and that clergy in major orders may not marry. Protestant reformers had permitted clerical marriage, and some had allowed divorce, so each of these points drew a boundary. The decree known from its first word as Tametsi also addressed a long standing social problem, clandestine marriage. Medieval canon law had treated the exchange of consent between two people as sufficient to create a valid marriage, even without witnesses, which could leave families, and particularly women, uncertain about their status. Tametsi required that marriages be celebrated before the parish priest and at least two witnesses after the publication of banns, and that marriages be recorded in a register. The Council declined, despite French pressure, to require parental consent for validity, preserving the principle of free consent.

The consequences were practical and uneven. The decree took effect in each parish only after its publication there, so that the date of its application varied across Europe, and in some areas, such as parts of France, it was accepted only gradually or with reservations. Parish registers of baptism and marriage, though not always properly kept, became a foundational source for historians of population and family life. For couples, the rule gave priests greater control over household formation and gave families, particularly propertied ones, a more reliable way to contest unwanted unions, while leaving the principle of consent intact. Social historians still debate how far the decree changed behavior and how far it simply formalized practices already spreading.

Section 65.11

Bishops, Seminaries, Clerical Residence, and Institutional Reform

The reform decrees, issued alongside the doctrinal ones at nearly every session, were aimed at the pastoral failures that critics had identified for a century. Bishops were to reside in their dioceses, preach regularly, conduct visitations of parishes and religious houses, and hold diocesan synods annually and provincial councils every three years. Pluralism, the holding of multiple benefices requiring pastoral care, was restricted, and candidates for bishoprics and parishes were to meet standards of age, character, and learning. Preaching on Sundays and feast days was to be regular, and the sale of ecclesiastical offices was condemned. The Council also reasserted that bishops were pastors of their dioceses with authority over many exempt bodies, though it carefully preserved papal supremacy.

The decree on seminaries, Cum adolescentium aetas, passed on July 15, 1563, is one of the Council's most important reform measures. It required every diocese to found a seminary to train boys, aged at least twelve, from families whose circumstances suggested a vocation, giving priority to the sons of the poor but not excluding others, in grammar, singing, computation of the church calendar, scripture, ecclesiastical books, and moral practice. The seminary was to be funded by a levy on benefices and ecclesiastical revenues, and was placed under the oversight of the bishop with the help of a committee of clergy. Medieval priests had mostly been trained by apprenticeship to a parish priest or by university study, and the idea of a residential institution for forming future priests under the bishop's supervision was a significant innovation. Reform of religious orders, particularly regarding the enclosure of nuns, was reserved for the last session.

The reform program shows how Trent interacted with the structures of power. Many decrees relied on bishops to enforce them, but bishops often lacked authority over the cathedral chapters, exempt orders, and lay patrons that controlled so many appointments. Many prelates were from aristocratic families who regarded a see as an inheritance. The decrees did not abolish patronage, which remained the foundation of the Catholic clergy in many places, and rulers, particularly in Spain and France, defended their rights to nominate and to supervise their churches. Trent thus defined an ideal of the diocesan bishop and parish priest, but it left the means to achieve it largely to local actors, who were not always willing.

Section 65.12

The Reception of Trent and the Problem of Enforcement

Pius IV confirmed the Council's decrees in the bull Benedictus Deus of January 26, 1564. The bull reserved the authority to interpret them to the papacy and forbade unauthorized commentaries. A congregation of cardinals was created to interpret the decrees and monitor their execution. In November 1564 the pope also issued a Profession of Faith, summarizing the Council's teaching, which bishops, beneficed clergy, and later university teachers and converts were required to swear. Trent thus created a program of doctrine and discipline and an instrument for supervising it.

Acceptance by rulers varied. Portugal and Poland accepted the decrees in 1564, and Philip II of Spain ordered their observance in July 1564, though with the reservation of royal rights, such as the crown's control over ecclesiastical appointments and its authority over the Inquisition. Venice and other Italian states accepted with qualifications. Catholic princes in the empire accepted the decrees at the Diet of Augsburg in 1566, although the actual application was left to individual princes. France offered the most conspicuous case of non-reception: the crown, concerned about Gallican liberties and the religious wars then beginning, never formally published the decrees, although the French clergy accepted the disciplinary provisions in 1615 and the doctrinal decrees were never in dispute among French Catholics. Rulers accepted what strengthened their own authority and resisted what limited it.

Enforcement was the larger problem. The Council's decrees assumed bishops with the means, the training, and the freedom to reform. Where such bishops existed, as in Milan under Borromeo, the effects could be dramatic. Elsewhere, bishops were absent, impoverished, politically compromised, or opposed by chapters and local elites. Parish priests who had long lived with concubines or had little instruction could not be replaced rapidly. In large regions, particularly in rural southern Italy, parts of the Iberian peninsula, and mountainous areas, historians who read visitation records find that significant improvement took generations, often not appearing until the seventeenth century. The process was gradual, regionally variable, and dependent on cooperation among bishops, rulers, and religious orders.

Section 65.13

Papal Reform and the Government of the Post-Tridentine Church

The popes of the decades after Trent turned the Council's program into policy. Pius IV (1559–1565) oversaw the end of the Council and the first steps of implementation, including the Roman Seminary of 1565 and the Profession of Faith. Pius V (1566–1572), a Dominican and former inquisitor later canonized, was an austere reformer who enforced residence among bishops, published the Roman Catechism, Breviary, and Missal (Section 65.16), tightened monastic enclosure, and organized a Holy League that won the naval battle of Lepanto against the Ottoman fleet in 1571. Gregory XIII (1572–1585) promoted education by founding colleges in Rome for different nations, including the Collegium Germanicum and the English, Greek, and Maronite colleges, and he sent permanent representatives, called nuncios, to Catholic courts to press for enforcement of the decrees. In 1582 he issued the calendar reform that bears his name, drawing on the calculations of scholars including the Jesuit mathematician Christopher Clavius, in which ten days were dropped so that October 4 was followed by October 15, 1582, in Catholic states.

Sixtus V (1585–1590) reorganized the central government of the church in 1588 into fifteen standing congregations of cardinals with defined responsibilities, such as the Holy Office of the Inquisition, the Congregation of the Index, the Congregation of the Council, and the Congregation of Rites, and he limited the College of Cardinals to seventy. These bodies gave the papacy more efficient tools of supervision and made Rome a bureaucratic center of Catholic Christianity, a development parallel to the growth of fiscal and administrative institutions in secular states, examined in Chapter 74. Rome also became the place where bishops reported on their dioceses through periodic visits and written reports.

The papacy's power remained limited. The popes were also rulers of a central Italian state whose security required diplomacy with Spain, France, and Venice, and they could not impose reform against the will of strong monarchs. Their tools were mostly administrative, financial, and spiritual rather than military. The central direction of the post-Tridentine church was nonetheless a new fact, and it meant that Catholic Christianity developed a more Rome-centered institutional character than in the late Middle Ages.

Section 65.14

Reforming Bishops and Diocesan Government

Charles Borromeo (1538–1584) became the model of the Tridentine bishop. A nephew of Pius IV, he was made a cardinal as a young man and played a role in the closing phase of the Council, and in 1565 he took up residence as archbishop of Milan, a large diocese that included alpine valleys and had not had a resident archbishop for decades. He held provincial councils and diocesan synods whose decrees, collected in the Acta Ecclesiae Mediolanensis, became a handbook for other bishops. He visited parishes, including remote mountain communities, examined clergy, inspected churches and sacred vessels, ordered the rebuilding of altars and confessionals, founded seminaries, and promoted the Confraternities of Christian Doctrine, which taught children on Sundays. During the plague of 1576–1577 he remained in Milan organizing relief. His rigor also created conflicts with the Spanish governor, with cathedral canons, and with a religious order, the Humiliati, one of whose members attempted to murder him in 1569.

Borromeo was not alone. Gabriele Paleotti, archbishop of Bologna, held synods, supported schools, and wrote on sacred images. Bartholomew of the Martyrs, a Portuguese Dominican who took part in the Council, reformed the see of Braga with a close attention to the poor and to the education of clergy. Earlier reformers such as Giberti had provided models, and later bishops, such as Francis de Sales in Geneva's diocese and Julius Echter in Würzburg, adapted them in different political settings. Visitation records, discussed in the vocabulary box below, show these bishops at work: they asked whether the priest resided, whether he kept a concubine, whether parishioners confessed annually, and whether the church building was in good order.

The German lands show the limits of the model. Many bishoprics were held by younger sons of noble families, often several at once, and cathedral chapters elected bishops from their own ranks. In 1583 the archbishop elector of Cologne, Gebhard Truchsess, announced his conversion to Protestantism and his intention to marry, which led to a war that ended with the installation of Ernest of Bavaria, a Wittelsbach who held several sees simultaneously, contrary to Tridentine rules on pluralism. Reform in Germany often depended on territorial princes, such as the Bavarian dukes, rather than on the bishops themselves.

Section 65.15

Seminaries, Parish Priests, Catechisms, and Religious Education

The diocesan seminary was the Council's most ambitious institutional proposal and one of the slowest to be realized. Rome led by example: the Roman Seminary was founded in 1565, and the Collegium Germanicum, established in 1552 by Ignatius of Loyola with the support of Cardinal Morone to train German priests, was reorganized under Gregory XIII. Milan founded seminaries in the 1560s. In most dioceses, however, funds were inadequate, the levy on benefices was resisted by those who paid it, and bishops lacked trained staff. Seminaries became widespread in France only in the seventeenth century, in the wake of the work of Pierre de Bérulle, Jean-Jacques Olier, and others, and in many parts of southern Europe they remained weak into the eighteenth century. The Council's ideal was achieved, in much of Europe, only after generations.

Meanwhile, other means of educating priests and laity multiplied. Printed manuals for confessors, such as the widely used manual by the Spanish canonist Martín de Azpilcueta, trained priests in moral casuistry. Jesuit and other colleges trained both lay and clerical students. The Roman Catechism of 1566, commissioned by the Council and addressed to parish priests, provided a systematic exposition of doctrine, sacraments, commandments, and the Lord's Prayer, and was translated widely. Peter Canisius, a Jesuit active in the German lands, wrote catechisms in several formats between 1555 and 1558 that were reprinted in hundreds of editions, and Robert Bellarmine's brief catechism of 1597 became standard in Italy and was used in missions. The Protestant catechisms of Luther and Calvin served similar purposes, which is one reason historians treat confessional instruction as a parallel process across churches.

Catechesis was delivered not only in books but orally. Parish priests were required to teach children the basic prayers and doctrines on Sundays, often by question and answer, and lay confraternities of Christian doctrine, begun in Milan and spread widely, recruited volunteers to run Sunday schools. Many parishioners were illiterate and learned through hearing, memorizing, images, and processions. Evidence about how much people absorbed is sparse, but visitation records, Inquisition interrogations, and the testimony of missionaries suggest enormous variation: some laypeople knew the prayers and sacramental doctrine reasonably well, while others combined official teaching with older beliefs in magic, healing, and local saints.

Section 65.16

Liturgy, Worship, and the Standardization of Catholic Practice

The Council charged the pope with revising the liturgical books, and the resulting publications transformed Catholic worship. The Roman Catechism appeared in 1566, the Roman Breviary, containing the daily prayers of the clergy, in 1568, and the Roman Missal, containing the texts of the Mass, in 1570. Pius V's bull Quo primum, accompanying the Missal, required its use throughout the Latin church except where a local rite had been in continuous use for at least two hundred years. That exception allowed the Ambrosian rite of Milan, the Mozarabic rite in Toledo, and the liturgies of the Dominicans and certain other orders to survive. Further books followed, including the Pontifical (1596), the Ceremonial of Bishops (1600), and the Roman Ritual (1614). The Vulgate itself was revised under Sixtus V in an edition of 1590 that was quickly withdrawn because of errors and replaced by the Clementine edition of 1592.

Printing made this standardization possible. Rome authorized the Roman books and supervised their publication in cooperation with printers in cities such as Venice, Antwerp, and Paris, and bishops ordered parishes to acquire them. The result was a more uniform Mass than in the Middle Ages, when local variations in texts, ceremonies, and calendars of saints had been widespread. The revised Breviary and Martyrology also reduced the veneration of some legendary saints and tightened requirements for evidence in the cult of new ones. Standardization reinforced the centrality of Rome, but it also produced resistance, and in practice local customs, patron saints, and feast days continued to flourish alongside the official books.

Sacred space and sound changed as well. The Council's decree on images, passed in its last session, defended their veneration, which Reformed Protestants condemned as idolatry, while forbidding images that suggested false doctrine or lascivious figures and requiring bishops to supervise new works. Bishops such as Borromeo issued instructions for church architecture that placed the altar and the tabernacle at the center, installed confessionals, and arranged the nave for preaching. On music, the Council asked bishops to exclude impure or lascivious compositions from the Mass. A later legend that the composer Palestrina "saved" polyphonic music from prohibition at Trent with his Pope Marcellus Mass has no secure basis in contemporary evidence, a good example of how later memory can replace documentation. Polyphonic music continued to flourish in the work of Palestrina, Lassus, and Victoria.

Section 65.17

The Roman Inquisition, the Index, and the Enforcement of Orthodoxy

On July 21, 1542, Paul III's bull Licet ab initio established a commission of cardinals, with Carafa as a leading member, to supervise the investigation and prosecution of heresy, first in Italy and then in the whole church. This Roman Inquisition, or Holy Office, was distinct from the Spanish Inquisition, founded in 1478 and controlled by the Spanish crown, and from the Portuguese Inquisition, established in 1536 and also royally controlled. The Roman tribunal worked through local inquisitors, usually Dominicans and Franciscans, and its authority was limited in states such as Venice, which insisted on the presence of lay officials, and in the kingdoms where national inquisitions already existed. In its early years it prosecuted Italians sympathetic to Protestantism, and several prominent figures fled, including Ochino and the Florentine theologian Peter Martyr Vermigli. Some, such as Pietro Carnesecchi, were executed.

Over time the Roman Inquisition's workload shifted. Most cases did not involve Protestant belief but blasphemy, superstition, sorcery, solicitation in confession, bigamy, and the holding of forbidden books. A large proportion ended with abjuration, penance, or fines rather than execution, and in many courts the procedure involved written records of unusual detail, which are today a rich source for historians of popular belief. Estimates of the numbers of executions by the Inquisitions vary widely and depend on how cases are counted, and students should be cautious about claims of precise totals. The institution's effect should be measured not only by those prosecuted but by the pressure of surveillance on writing, preaching, and conversation.

The regulation of books was linked. Paul IV published the first papal Index of Prohibited Books in 1559, which was so severe that it banned all the works of Erasmus. The Council appointed a commission whose work led, under Pius IV in 1564, to a revised Index with ten general rules governing reading, and a Congregation of the Index was created in 1571. Spain and Portugal published their own indexes. The effect on printing was substantial but incomplete: banned books circulated in secret, were censored by expurgation, or were read with permission by scholars. Censorship fell with particular weight on vernacular Bibles. Religious minorities were affected as well. In 1555 Paul IV's bull Cum nimis absurdum required Jews in the Papal States to live in a walled quarter, creating the Roman ghetto, and restricted their occupations. The burning of copies of the Talmud in Rome in 1553 and later censorship of Hebrew books illustrate how enforcement of orthodoxy touched Jewish communities and illustrate that Catholic renewal had consequences for non-Christians as well. In Spain, the Inquisition continued to prosecute conversos and, after the baptism of Muslims, the Moriscos, whose revolt in the Alpujarras (1568–1570) preceded their expulsion in 1609–1614 (Chapter 75).

Section 65.18

Ignatius of Loyola and the Foundation of the Society of Jesus

Iñigo López de Loyola (c. 1491–1556), the youngest son of a Basque noble family, was a soldier until a cannonball broke his leg at the siege of Pamplona in 1521. During convalescence he read a life of Christ and lives of saints and underwent a religious conversion. After a period of prayer and ascetic practice at Manresa in 1522–1523, he began to develop the exercises that he would later write down as the Spiritual Exercises, a structured course of meditations, examinations of conscience, and decisions designed to be led by a director over about thirty days. He traveled to Jerusalem and then studied at Alcalá, Salamanca, and Paris, where he took a master's degree in 1535. He was investigated by the Inquisition in Spain on suspicion of illuminist sympathies, an experience that did not deter him from conforming to church authority.

In Paris he gathered companions, including Francis Xavier, Peter Faber, Diego Laínez, and Alfonso Salmerón, and on August 15, 1534, they took vows at Montmartre. After the plan of going to Jerusalem proved impossible, they offered their services to the pope, and in September 1540 Paul III approved the Society of Jesus in the bull Regimini militantis ecclesiae. Ignatius was elected the first superior general in 1541. The Society was distinctive in several ways. Members did not sing the office in choir, which left time for preaching, teaching, and pastoral work, they took a special vow of obedience to the pope regarding missions, and they underwent long intellectual and spiritual formation. Authority was centralized in a superior general in Rome, who sent members to wherever the need was greatest. The Constitutions, completed after Ignatius's death, organized this system.

The order grew rapidly, from about ten original companions to roughly a thousand members by 1556, and to many thousands by 1600. It also attracted opposition, which came from the Sorbonne, the Parlement of Paris, Spanish Dominicans, and some bishops who objected to its exemptions from episcopal authority, and from critics who saw its selective admission and political influence as dangers. Later generations attributed to the Jesuits an exaggerated degree of conspiratorial power, which provided material for polemical literature. The historical order was ambitious and influential, but it was also subject to internal tensions and to the control of popes and rulers. Its limitations also included exclusionary policies: in the 1590s the order restricted the admission of men of Jewish or Muslim ancestry, a measure reflecting the Iberian preoccupation with purity of blood that Ignatius's earlier practice had not required.

Section 65.19

Jesuit Education, Colleges, and Religious Networks

Ignatius did not originally intend the Jesuits to be schoolteachers, but within a decade they discovered that education was one of their most effective ministries. A college at Messina in Sicily, opened in 1548, was the first of many for lay students, and in 1551 Ignatius founded the Roman College, later the Gregorian University. By the early seventeenth century the order directed several hundred colleges across Catholic Europe and beyond, from Ingolstadt, Vienna, and Prague to Kraków, Vilnius, and Goa. Instruction was free of charge for students, supported by endowments from rulers, nobles, and towns that valued the schools as instruments of prestige and religious loyalty. Colleges taught grammar, rhetoric, philosophy, and theology within a humanist framework, and they also staged dramatic performances, organized debates, and sponsored Marian sodalities, lay confraternities for devotion and service that spread from the Roman College in the 1560s.

A common curriculum, the Ratio Studiorum, was issued in definitive form in 1599 after drafts in the 1580s and 1590s. It combined the humanist study of classical Latin literature with scholastic philosophy and theology, emphasized repetition, competition, and disputation, and made the order's schools relatively uniform from place to place. Jesuit teachers worked in mathematics and natural philosophy as well: Christopher Clavius, in Rome, trained a generation of mathematicians and contributed to the Gregorian calendar. Robert Bellarmine and Francisco Suárez were among the most influential theologians of the age. Jesuit confessors to rulers, among them Wilhelm Lamormaini, confessor to Emperor Ferdinand II, sometimes gained political influence that their critics found alarming and their admirers found essential.

The effects of Jesuit education were considerable but should be neither exaggerated nor treated as the sole cause of Catholic revival. Schools gave elite families, and in some cases artisan and professional families, access to humanist education and brought many Protestants into contact with Catholic teachers. They shaped the later political and intellectual elites of regions such as Bavaria, Austria, Bohemia, Poland-Lithuania, and parts of France. Yet they trained only a minority of boys and, until much later, almost no girls. Other orders, diocesan seminaries, universities, and parish schools carried much of the educational work, and the Jesuits' pedagogical influence also extended to Protestant schools that borrowed from it. The fortunes of the order in later centuries, including its suppression in 1773, belong to Volume IV.

Section 65.20

New and Reformed Religious Orders

The Jesuits were one of many communities that gave Catholic renewal its character. The Theatines and Barnabites, discussed above, modeled clerical reform. The Capuchins became the order most visible to the poor: they begged, preached in simple language, ministered during outbreaks of plague, and established houses in towns and villages across Italy, France, Spain, and the Habsburg lands. Their popularity stemmed from a combination of austerity and accessibility, and in the seventeenth century they became leading missionaries and confessors. They also illustrate how renewal could proceed within the Franciscan tradition rather than by founding wholly new forms of life.

In Rome, Philip Neri (1515–1595) gathered priests and laymen in informal meetings for prayer, readings, sermons, and music, which became the Congregation of the Oratory, approved in 1575 and based at Santa Maria in Vallicella. The Oratorians avoided formal vows, and their emphasis on lay participation, sacred song, and informal teaching shaped Roman religious culture. Cesare Baronio, a member, wrote the multi volume Ecclesiastical Annals, begun in 1588 as a Catholic answer to the Protestant Magdeburg Centuries, a reminder that Catholic scholarship also responded to Protestant historical polemic. Orders that cared for the sick, such as the Brothers of Saint John of God, founded in Granada in the 1540s, and the Camillians, whose founder Camillus de Lellis began his work in Rome in the 1580s, developed hospital care as a form of religious vocation.

Older orders also reformed. Dominicans and Franciscans produced reforming provincial leaders and theologians of the highest influence at Trent. Benedictine congregations such as that of Saint-Maur in France, founded in 1618, combined renewed discipline with erudite scholarship, while Carmelite and Cistercian reforms sought strict observance. These movements show that Catholic renewal was a plural phenomenon: orders and congregations differed in their emphasis on contemplation, scholarship, preaching, or charity, and they sometimes competed for patrons, recruits, and influence.

Section 65.21

Women, Religious Communities, and Catholic Spiritual Renewal

Teresa of Ávila (1515–1582) entered the Carmelite convent of the Incarnation at Ávila in 1535 and, after a long period of illness and spiritual struggle, experienced visions that she recorded under the direction of her confessors. She came from a family of recent Jewish converts, which exposed her to suspicion in a society concerned with purity of blood. In 1562 she founded the convent of St. Joseph in Ávila as the first house of a reform that returned to a stricter, contemplative observance, and she and her collaborators, including John of the Cross (1542–1591), who helped found the first men's house of the reform in 1568, created the Discalced Carmelites. They faced fierce resistance from the unreformed Carmelites, and John was imprisoned by them in Toledo in 1577–1578. Teresa's Life, Way of Perfection, and Interior Castle, written at the request of confessors and carefully examined for orthodoxy, remain classics of Christian mysticism. The Inquisition's suspicion of women's religious experience, particularly in Spain, meant that she and others wrote under scrutiny.

Angela Merici (c. 1474–1540) founded the Company of St. Ursula at Brescia in 1535, a group of laywomen consecrated to God who lived in their own homes and taught girls the catechism. The idea of unenclosed women performing active ministry challenged ideas of female religious life. The Council, in its decree on religious women in 1563, ordered strict enclosure, forbidding nuns to leave their convents, and Pius V reinforced the rule in the following years. Under the influence of bishops like Borromeo, many Ursuline communities became enclosed orders, though they continued teaching girls within convent walls. The English woman Mary Ward (1585–1645) went further, forming a community of women modeled on the Jesuits who lived without enclosure and educated girls; a papal bull of 1631 suppressed her institute, and she was briefly imprisoned. In France, Vincent de Paul and Louise de Marillac organized the Daughters of Charity in 1633 as a community of women who served the poor outside convent walls by avoiding formal religious vows, an example of how a new canonical form could accommodate active ministry.

Convents were more than places of devotion. In many Italian and Iberian cities, families placed daughters in convents to avoid the cost of dowries for marriage, so that significant numbers of women lived there without strong vocation, and enclosure sometimes provoked protest from nuns, families, and city authorities. Convents also served as centers of music, writing, medicine, and charity, and some nuns exercised significant spiritual authority through their visions and writings. Tridentine reform thus narrowed some opportunities for women while opening others, such as education for girls and the recognition of female mystics. Women's religious experience can be recovered from convent chronicles, autobiographies, Inquisition files, and letters, but these sources were usually mediated by male confessors and superiors.

Section 65.22

Catholic Devotion, Sacred Art, Music, and Everyday Religious Life

For most Catholics, renewal was experienced through devotional practice, not conciliar decrees. Confraternities, associations of laypeople devoted to a saint, the Rosary, the Blessed Sacrament, charity, or Christian doctrine, expanded in many regions, organizing processions, burying the dead, dowering poor girls, and visiting prisoners. The Forty Hours devotion, a period of continuous prayer before the exposed Eucharist, spread from Milan in the mid sixteenth century. Pilgrimage continued to Rome, especially in the jubilee years of 1575 and 1600, and to regional shrines. The Council defended the veneration of saints and relics but required bishops to approve new miracles and relics, which gave church authorities a greater role in regulating popular devotion. Canonizations became more carefully documented, and in 1622 Gregory XV canonized five figures who symbolized the movement: Ignatius, Xavier, Teresa, Neri, and the Spanish farmer Isidore.

Visual culture was also transformed. The Jesuit church of the Gesù in Rome, begun in 1568 to a design by Giacomo Vignola and dedicated in 1584, with its wide nave suitable for preaching, became an influential model. Theorists such as Paleotti in Bologna argued that images should instruct, move, and be accurate. Painters including the Carracci and Caravaggio, and later sculptors and architects such as Bernini, developed a dramatic, emotive, and naturalistic style we call Baroque. The label should be used with care. Baroque art served Catholic purposes in Rome, Spain, and the Habsburg lands, but it also served courts and merchants, appeared in Lutheran and Calvinist regions in different forms, and expressed concerns not reducible to Catholic doctrine. Rubens in Antwerp and Velázquez in Madrid worked for a variety of patrons.

Household religion deserves equal attention. Catholics hung devotional prints and crucifixes, owned rosaries, took part in processions and blessings that protected fields and animals, and sought the help of local saints for sickness and misfortune. Religious books, catechisms, and lives of saints circulated, and sermons, both in churches and in the open air, were a principal form of public communication. Church authorities encouraged some of these practices and attempted to discipline others, particularly those connected with magic, folk healing, and unauthorized cults. The picture that emerges is of a religion that combined official regulation with a lively popular practice in which laypeople often shaped what they received.

Section 65.23

Regional Varieties of Catholic Reform in Europe

Italy was the center of institutional reform and also the site of fragmented political authority. In Milan, ruled by Spain, Borromeo's program of visitations and seminaries was the most thorough. In the Papal States, Rome's direct supervision made reform comparatively intense. Venice defended its autonomy, as shown in the conflict of 1606–1607 when the papacy placed the republic under interdict and Venice refused to yield its right to try clerics and regulate church property. Much of the south, under Spanish rule, retained weaker diocesan structures and widespread popular religion. Spain had a church closely tied to the crown. Philip II saw himself as a defender of the faith and supported reform through the bishops and the Inquisition, but he also jealously guarded the royal patronage and blocked papal interference. The result was strong religious conformity with limited papal control.

France experienced Catholic renewal through the Wars of Religion (Chapter 66). The crown did not formally publish Trent, the Gallican tradition limited papal authority, and the Catholic League of the 1580s and 1590s intertwined reform with political rebellion. After Henry IV's conversion in 1593 and the Edict of Nantes of 1598, which granted Huguenots limited toleration, a remarkable renewal developed: Francis de Sales and Jane de Chantal founded the Visitation order in 1610, Bérulle and Olier promoted clerical formation, and the Capuchins and Jesuits expanded. In Bavaria and the German lands, the Wittelsbach dukes Albert V and William V, with Jesuit support, built a model Catholic territory centered on Munich and the University of Ingolstadt, and prince bishops such as Julius Echter in Würzburg imposed Catholic uniformity in their territories. Local rulers used the principle of the Peace of Augsburg to compel conformity or emigration, a process in which religion and state building were closely connected (Chapter 74).

In the Habsburg territories, the picture was more complex. Ferdinand I and Maximilian II tolerated significant Protestant populations among the nobility and towns, but from the 1580s and 1590s, Archduke Ferdinand of Inner Austria, the future Emperor Ferdinand II, and Jesuit educated prelates such as Péter Pázmány in Hungary promoted systematic Catholic restoration. Bohemia, with its Utraquist and Czech Brethren traditions, and Transylvania, whose diet had recognized several confessions in 1568, remained pluralist for longer. In Poland-Lithuania, Cardinal Stanislaus Hosius brought the Jesuits to the diocese of Warmia in 1564, and later the Jesuits founded the academy at Vilnius in 1579. The Warsaw Confederation of 1573 guaranteed peace among confessions, and Catholic renewal advanced largely through persuasion, education, and noble patronage. The Union of Brest of 1596 brought a part of the Orthodox church in the Commonwealth into communion with Rome while retaining its Byzantine rite, creating a Greek Catholic church whose existence produced conflict within the Orthodox community. These cases show that Catholic renewal worked differently depending on whether it was backed by a strong prince, an inquisitorial state, a divided society, or a tolerant constitution. Chapters 66 and 67 follow how these variations affected the outbreak and course of war.

Section 65.24

Catholic Missions, Global Expansion, and the Limits of Reform

Missionary work depended on the structures of the overseas empires. Under the arrangements known as the padroado (Portugal) and patronato (Spain), the popes granted the crowns the right and duty to support the church in newly acquired territories, including nominating bishops and paying clergy, in exchange for promoting conversion. Missionaries therefore traveled on royal ships, depended on colonial officials, and were often entangled in conflicts over conquest and labor. In the Americas, Franciscans arrived in Mexico in 1524, followed by Dominicans, Augustinians, and, from the 1560s, Jesuits. They established schools, such as the Colegio de Santa Cruz at Tlatelolco in 1536, learned indigenous languages, and wrote grammars and catechisms. The Third Provincial Council of Lima (1582–1583) prepared a catechism in Spanish, Quechua, and Aymara. Missionaries such as Bartolomé de las Casas also criticized colonial violence and debated the legitimacy of conquest (Chapter 69). Baptism was often mass baptism, and indigenous religion was attacked through the destruction of temples and images, yet indigenous people adopted Christianity in ways that blended with local practices, formed confraternities, and acted as catechists and church officers. Indigenous people were exempt from the jurisdiction of the Inquisitions established in Lima and Mexico City in the 1570s.

In Asia and Africa, Catholicism took different forms. Francis Xavier, a Jesuit, reached Goa in 1542, preached in southern India and the Malay world, and in 1549 landed in Japan, where he died off the Chinese coast in 1552. In Japan, Jesuits benefited from the patronage of regional lords, and Christian communities grew to hundreds of thousands by around 1600, although figures are uncertain. Hideyoshi's edict of 1587 and the Tokugawa ban of 1614 led to persecution and the near eradication of open Christian life, while communities of hidden Christians survived. In China, Matteo Ricci reached Macau in 1582 and Beijing in 1601, studied Chinese language and classics, wore the dress of a scholar, and presented Catholicism through European science and Confucian moral language, a method of accommodation later criticized by other missionaries in the "rites controversy" of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. In south India, Roberto de Nobili adopted the lifestyle of a Brahmin ascetic in Madurai from 1606, and in 1599 the Synod of Diamper imposed Latin practices on the ancient Thomas Christians of Kerala. In central Africa, King Afonso I of Kongo (reigned c. 1509–c. 1542) adopted Christianity and corresponded with Rome and Lisbon, while the growth of the Atlantic slave trade (Chapter 70) eroded the political independence of his kingdom. In Ethiopia, Jesuits briefly converted a ruler, Susenyos, in 1622, but the attempt to Latinize the Ethiopian church provoked unrest and its reversal in 1632.

These examples show the limits as well as the reach of reform. Missionaries sometimes relied on coercion or on the protection of conquerors, and sometimes encountered rulers who used Christianity for their own political ends or rejected it. Local converts, catechists, translators, and rulers shaped what Christianity meant in their societies. Rome attempted to coordinate and control the missions, and in 1622 Gregory XV founded the Congregation for the Propagation of the Faith (Propaganda Fide) to reduce dependence on the Iberian crowns. The expansion of Catholicism beyond Europe was thus an outcome of European reform movements, imperial power, and local agency combined, a theme that Chapters 68 through 73 develop.

Historical Vocabulary
Catholic Reform, Catholic Reformation, and Counter-Reformation

Catholic Reform and Catholic Reformation denote the renewal of Catholic institutions, piety, and teaching, including movements that began before 1517. Counter-Reformation denotes measures directed against Protestantism, such as the Index, the Inquisition, and confessional polemic. None of the three was a term used by sixteenth century Catholics to describe themselves, who spoke of reform of the church, of the true faith, or of the Council. The modern labels are historians' tools, and Section 65.1 and the Historical Debate below explain why each both illuminates and distorts.

Historical Vocabulary
Justification

Justification is the theological term for the process by which a sinner is made right with God. For Luther and later Protestants it was chiefly a declaration of righteousness received through faith alone on account of Christ. For the Council of Trent it was both forgiveness and inner renewal in which grace and free human cooperation, expressed in faith, hope, and charity, work together. The difference lay at the center of the sixteenth century conflict, but modern ecumenical dialogue has shown that the two positions were not always as far apart as polemic suggested.

Historical Vocabulary
Visitation

A visitation was a formal inspection of a parish, monastery, or diocese by a bishop or his delegate. The visitor examined the clergy, the fabric of the church, the sacred vessels, the records, and the conduct of parishioners, and he recorded complaints and ordered corrections. Visitation records are among the most valuable sources for the effects of Tridentine reform, but they record what inspectors saw and valued and often reflect their expectations. They must be read as evidence of the reformers' priorities as well as of parish conditions.

Primary Source Window

Council of Trent, Decree on Justification (Session 6), January 13, 1547

Creator: The bishops and theologians of the Council of Trent, under the presidency of the papal legates · Date: January 13, 1547 · Original language: Latin · Type: A conciliar decree of sixteen doctrinal chapters and thirty-three canons, written for the whole church

Context: The decree followed months of debate between theologians of different schools and was written in the shadow of Regensburg's failure and the imminent war between Charles V and the Protestant princes. It addresses, usually without naming them, positions associated with Luther and other reformers.

Paraphrase of the decree's argument The decree teaches that all people are born under sin and that Christ's merit alone makes justification possible. God's grace begins the process by calling the sinner, and the sinner may freely consent or refuse. Those who consent are not merely declared righteous but made so, receiving faith, hope, and charity, and they grow in righteousness through good works that are themselves the fruit of grace. A person may lose this grace through mortal sin, but it can be recovered through penance. The canons condemn anyone who teaches that faith alone justifies, that human beings can merit justification without grace, or that a believer can be certain of being among the elect. This is a paraphrase of the decree's content, not a translation of its wording.

Analysis: The decree is valuable because it shows how the Council sought a position between Pelagian reliance on human effort and the Protestant doctrine of justification by faith alone. It is also a text produced by a specific group of mostly Italian and Spanish bishops, with no Protestant participation, and its canons condemn positions in forms that Protestants themselves might not have recognized. It cannot tell us how ordinary Catholics understood grace, nor how widely it was taught in parishes.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the decree say about the roles of grace and of human will in justification?
  2. Who created it and for what purposes, and who was absent?
  3. What can historians learn from it about the differences and overlaps between Catholic and Protestant teaching?
  4. What can they not establish from the decree alone about belief in parishes?

Council of Trent, Decree Cum adolescentium aetas on Seminaries (Session 23, ch. 18), July 15, 1563

Creator: The Council of Trent · Date: July 15, 1563 · Original language: Latin · Type: A reform decree directed to bishops

Context: The decree responded to a long standing complaint that the clergy were poorly trained. It drew on the example of earlier colleges and on proposals by Cardinal Pole and others. It was approved in the third period, when Cardinal Morone was leading the Council toward conclusions on reform.

Paraphrase of the decree's provisions The decree begins from the observation that young people left unformed are easily drawn to worldly pleasures. It orders each cathedral church to maintain a number of boys, according to the diocese's resources, who will be educated in religion and in the ecclesiastical disciplines. Boys must be at least twelve years old, of legitimate birth, and able to read and write. The poor are to be especially welcomed. The students are to wear clerical dress, learn grammar, singing, computation of the church calendar, scripture, and the practice of the sacraments, and attend Mass daily. The seminary is funded by a levy on ecclesiastical revenues and supervised by the bishop with the advice of a committee of clergy. This is a paraphrase, not a translation.

Analysis: The decree shows the Council's attention to institutions rather than merely to exhortation. It also reveals assumptions: that clerical vocation begins in boyhood, that the Church should control formation, and that the diocese should bear the cost. It cannot show whether any seminary was actually founded; for that, historians use episcopal and municipal records, financial accounts, and later visitation reports.

Historical Debate

Catholic Reform or Counter-Reformation?

The question: Should the renewal of Catholicism in the sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries be understood primarily as a reaction to Protestantism or as an independent movement of reform with deeper roots?

Interpretation A: Counter-Reformation. Historians in this tradition, strongly influenced by nineteenth century Protestant scholarship but also found among Catholic polemicists, emphasize that the decisive institutions and policies, including the Council's anathemas, the Roman Inquisition, the Index, the Jesuits' educational program, and the alliance of church and state, arose in response to the Protestant threat and were aimed at reclaiming lost territory and securing doctrinal uniformity.
Interpretation B: Catholic Reformation. Historians in this tradition, including Hubert Jedin in the twentieth century, emphasize the pre-Lutheran reforms in Spain and Italy, the continuity of late medieval piety, and the genuine spiritual creativity of figures such as Teresa of Ávila and Philip Neri. In this view, Protestantism accelerated and shaped a renewal that was already under way and that cannot be reduced to reaction.

Evidence: The debate rests on chronology (what existed before 1517), on the aims stated in reforming texts, on the biographies of reformers, and on the institutional record of the Council and the Roman Inquisition. Most current scholarship does not choose between the two positions but treats them as overlapping, and many historians also place both within a wider comparative frame of confessional development, in which Lutheran, Reformed, and Catholic churches all sought to define doctrine and discipline believers, sometimes with the cooperation of states.

Why it matters: The label shapes what students notice. A story told as Counter-Reformation emphasizes conflict, repression, and politics, whereas one told as Catholic Reformation emphasizes education, spirituality, and charity. A fuller history needs both, and it must avoid assuming that either label explains all Catholic activity.

Student task: Select three developments from this chapter, for example the Theatines, the Roman Inquisition, and the Ursulines, and decide which label fits each best. Then explain what evidence would make you change your view, and whether a single label is adequate for the whole movement.

Multiple Perspectives

Living with Trent: Perspectives on Tridentine Reform

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified in sources such as visitation records and Inquisition files. They are not quotations from any single named person, and the groups did not hold equal power.

Reforming bishops: For a bishop such as Borromeo, the decrees offered authority to inspect, correct, and train, but they required money, staff, and political backing. A bishop who insisted on residence and visitations found himself in conflict with chapters, exempt orders, and secular officials, and success often depended on personal energy and the favor of rulers.
Parish priests: Many parish clergy faced new demands for examination, catechetical teaching, residence, and celibacy, with limited resources and income. Some welcomed training and status, others resisted inspection, and those living with partners or families, which was common in some regions, faced discipline and social stigma.
Laypeople: Parishioners found themselves examined on their knowledge of prayers, urged to confess annually, subjected to new rules of marriage and burial, and offered new devotions and confraternities. Many accepted and valued these changes, some adapted them to local custom, and some resisted festivals' regulation or the loss of long used practices.
Dissenters and minorities: Protestants, converts of doubtful sincerity, Jews, Moriscos, and those suspected of magic or heresy experienced the program as surveillance and constraint: books were banned, speech was monitored, residence in ghettos was required, and conformity was demanded, sometimes at the price of exile or death. Their own accounts are rarer and often survive only in trial records.
Continuity and Change Over Time

The Catholic Church before and after Trent

What Changed

  • Doctrines on scripture, justification, and the sacraments were defined more precisely, closing a range of previously permitted opinions
  • Diocesan seminaries, residence, visitation, catechesis, and parish registers became official expectations
  • The liturgy became more uniform through the Roman books of 1566–1570 and later
  • Rome gained a more bureaucratic administration, nuncios, and inquisitorial and censorship institutions
  • New orders, especially the Jesuits, built networks of colleges and missions

What Stayed the Same

  • The papacy, the episcopate, the sacramental system, clerical celibacy, and Latin worship remained
  • Lay patrons, royal control of appointments, and exemptions persisted in many places
  • Local saints, feast days, and customs survived alongside official norms
  • The church continued to depend on secular rulers for enforcement

Who experienced change most directly: Bishops, clergy, members of religious orders, and nuns faced the most direct changes in training, residence, and enclosure, while rural laypeople often experienced continuity in practice with gradual changes in instruction and confession. Why the pattern matters: It avoids both the claim that Trent created a wholly new church and the claim that nothing changed.

Cause and Effect

The Council of Trent

Long-term causes

Late medieval demands for reform of the head and members of the church, the failures of conciliar reform, humanist scholarship, Observant movements, and the growth of lay piety.

Short-term causes

The spread of Protestant churches, the failure of Regensburg, Charles V's pressure, Paul III's reforming appointments, and a temporary peace between Habsburg and Valois that made a council possible.

Event or transformation

Three periods of the Council between 1545 and 1563 produced doctrinal definitions and disciplinary decrees, followed by papal confirmation in 1564.

Immediate effects

Reconciliation with Protestants became unlikely; Catholic teaching was clarified; the papacy gained instruments for supervision; and bishops, rulers, and orders began the work of implementation.

Long-term consequences

A more disciplined and Rome oriented church, systematic clerical training, Catholic schooling and missions, and a durable confessional identity that shaped politics, family life, and culture into later centuries.

Historical complexity

Outcomes depended on rulers, finances, local elites, and the energy of particular bishops and orders, so that similar decrees produced different results in different places.

Note: The Council was not the only cause of Catholic renewal and was not a guarantee of success. Its effects must be traced region by region.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households and Parishes in a Catholic Community

For most people the parish was the main arena of religion. A family in a Catholic village after the 1560s might find that the priest now examined children on the catechism on Sunday afternoons, that marriages had to be preceded by banns and witnessed by the priest and recorded in a register, and that confession was expected at least once a year, usually before Easter. A woman whose daughter married secretly could no longer count on the church's recognition of the union, while a family hoping to prevent an unwanted match gained a new argument. Baptisms and burials were recorded. The parish church might be rearranged with a central altar, a tabernacle, a pulpit, and a confessional.

Households also expressed devotion in their own ways: with prints and crucifixes, a rosary, prayers said at meals, blessings of fields, and pilgrimages to shrines. They belonged to confraternities that paid for funerals and masses and lent money or provided dowries to poor girls. Some continued practices that clergy condemned, such as consulting healers or using charms. The history of Catholic renewal is partly a history of negotiation between the priest's instructions and the household's own sense of what protected family and community.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the renewal of Catholic Christianity between the late fifteenth and early seventeenth centuries, with the Council of Trent as its institutional center. Its governing argument is that Catholic reform was both continuous with older movements and reshaped by the Protestant challenge, that it reaffirmed doctrine while transforming discipline, education, and devotion, and that its effects depended on the people and institutions that carried it out.

Reform did not begin with Trent. Observant movements, the Devotio Moderna, lay confraternities, humanist scholarship, and the work of figures such as Cisneros in Spain and Giberti and the Oratory of Divine Love in Italy show a widespread concern with clerical discipline, education, and piety before 1517. New communities, including the Theatines, Capuchins, Barnabites, Ursulines, and the Jesuits, emerged in the 1520s and 1530s.

The papacy was slow to respond to Luther, feared a council, and was constrained by Habsburg-Valois conflict. Under Paul III, reforming cardinals were appointed, a report on abuses was drafted, the Regensburg colloquy of 1541 failed to reconcile the confessions, and the Roman Inquisition was founded. After these failures the Council of Trent was convened in 1545, meeting in three periods until 1563 and operating under papal and royal pressures.

Trent defined Catholic teaching on scripture and tradition, original sin, justification, the seven sacraments, the Eucharist, the Mass, penance, orders, and marriage. It did so in language that excluded Protestant positions while avoiding the resolution of internal Catholic disputes. It also issued disciplinary decrees on episcopal residence, visitation, preaching, the training of clergy through seminaries, clandestine marriage, and the enclosure of nuns, an agenda that differed from medieval practice in its institutional ambition.

Implementation was gradual and uneven. Pius IV confirmed the decrees in 1564, but rulers accepted them selectively, and enforcement depended on bishops, funds, and political support. Popes from Pius V to Sixtus V published liturgical books, reorganized the Curia, and expanded nunciatures. Bishops like Borromeo demonstrated what the decrees could achieve, whereas in other regions seminaries, parish discipline, and catechesis took generations to develop.

The Jesuits, approved in 1540, combined mobility, intellectual formation, and obedience to create a network of colleges and missions. They were one agent among many. Capuchins, Oratorians, older orders, diocesan clergy, and lay confraternities also shaped the movement. Women such as Teresa of Ávila and Angela Merici made major spiritual and educational contributions, while Tridentine enclosure constrained others, such as Mary Ward.

Renewal and coercion were intertwined. The Roman Inquisition and the Index enforced orthodoxy, and Catholic states and institutions placed pressure on Protestants, Jews, converts, and those suspected of heterodoxy. At the same time, charity, schools, hospitals, preaching, and sacred art flourished. Regional variation was large, from the inquisitorial and royally controlled church of Spain to the Gallican and divided situation in France, the princely renewal of Bavaria, the pluralist Habsburg lands and Transylvania, and the tolerant constitution of Poland-Lithuania.

Catholic missions carried the movement to the Americas, Africa, and Asia, where missionaries worked within the structures of Iberian empire and alongside local rulers, catechists, and converts who shaped the meaning of Christianity. The history of Catholic renewal was thus part of the history of European expansion, a connection developed in Chapters 68 through 73. The consequences of confessional division for European politics, including the French Wars of Religion and the Thirty Years' War, are examined in Chapters 66 and 67, and nothing in this chapter implies that those conflicts were inevitable.

A scholarly concept describing how churches and states in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries built distinct confessional identities and disciplined populations in parallel.
Tridentine
Relating to the Council of Trent or to the reforms and practices derived from it.
Council of Trent
The general council of the Latin church that met in three periods between 1545 and 1563 and defined doctrine and discipline.
Anathema
A formal ecclesiastical condemnation, used in the canons of Trent to exclude particular teachings.
Canon of scripture
The list of books accepted as scripture; Trent included the deuterocanonical books in the Old Testament canon.
Vulgate
The Latin Bible traditionally associated with Jerome, declared authentic for public use by Trent.
Tradition
Apostolic teachings and practices transmitted in the church outside scripture, held by Trent to be received with equal reverence.
Justification
The process by which a sinner is made right with God; for Trent, inner renewal through grace and cooperation, not faith alone.
Transubstantiation
The doctrine that the substance of bread and wine is changed into the body and blood of Christ in the Eucharist.
Sacrament
A rite instituted by Christ that confers grace; the Catholic church counted seven.
Penance
The sacrament of confession, contrition, absolution, and satisfaction for sins.
Tametsi
The 1563 decree requiring marriages to be celebrated before a priest and witnesses after banns.
Residence
The duty of bishops and parish clergy to live in their dioceses and parishes.
Pluralism
The holding of more than one church office with pastoral duties by one person.
Visitation
A formal inspection of a parish, house, or diocese by a bishop or delegate.
Seminary
A residential institution for training future priests, mandated by Trent for each diocese.
Catechism
A manual of Christian doctrine, often in question and answer form, such as the Roman Catechism of 1566.
Curia
The central administrative offices of the papacy, reorganized into congregations by Sixtus V in 1588.
Nuncio
A permanent papal diplomatic representative at a court, who also supervised church affairs.
Roman Inquisition
A papal tribunal for heresy established in 1542, distinct from the Spanish and Portuguese inquisitions.
Index of Prohibited Books
A list of works forbidden to Catholics, first issued by the papacy in 1559 and revised in 1564.
Spiritual Exercises
Ignatius of Loyola's structured course of meditations and discernment.
Ratio Studiorum
The Jesuit plan of studies of 1599.
Discalced Carmelites
The reformed Carmelites founded by Teresa of Ávila and John of the Cross, who followed a stricter observance.
Enclosure
The rule confining nuns to their convents, enforced after Trent.
Confraternity
A lay association for devotion and charity, such as Rosary and Christian Doctrine confraternities.
Padroado and Patronato
The rights granted by the popes to Portugal and Spain to organize and support the church in their overseas territories.
Baroque
A dramatic, emotive style of art and architecture associated with, but not limited to, Catholic patronage of the seventeenth century.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 65 matters because it shows that the religious division of sixteenth century Europe produced two kinds of change, not one. The Protestant churches reorganized worship, belief, and church government. The Catholic church, while keeping its institutional structure, also changed profoundly: it trained its clergy differently, taught its laity systematically, regulated its liturgy, and built new orders and a global missionary network. To study only the Protestant side or only the Catholic side distorts the history of both.

The chapter also reveals something about how institutions change. Trent defined an ideal, but whether it became a reality depended on bishops, rulers, finances, and local society, and generations passed before many of its provisions took effect. The gap between decree and practice, and the ways that rulers used reform for their own purposes, are patterns that return in the study of the early modern state in Part XII, where religion and administration become closely entwined.

For Volume III, the chapter shows that the confessional divisions of Europe were being consolidated at the same time that overseas empires were being built. Catholic missions moved with Iberian ships and soldiers, and Catholic ideas about orthodoxy and discipline traveled to the Americas and Asia, where they were adapted, resisted, and reinterpreted. The same period saw coercion and care, censorship and schools, enclosure and mysticism, all within a single historical movement.

The consequences remain to be traced. Chapter 66 turns to the Wars of Religion in France and the Dutch Revolt, where confessional identity, dynastic politics, and rebellion intersected, and Chapter 67 follows the escalation of these conflicts into the Thirty Years' War and the settlement of 1648. The stronger confessional identities that Catholic renewal helped create were one of the conditions of those conflicts, but they were not their sole cause, and no outcome was predetermined.
