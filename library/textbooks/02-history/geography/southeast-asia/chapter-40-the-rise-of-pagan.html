The Rise of Pagan

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Pagan was not simply the work of one conquering king, one migrating people, or one inspired religious reform. It was a kingdom assembled over generations in the dry heart of the Irrawaddy basin, from grain-producing districts, river routes, military followings, Pyu, Mon, and Burmese-speaking communities, and the ability of its rulers to turn all of these into durable political authority.

On the east bank of the middle Irrawaddy River, in the dry zone of present-day Myanmar, a plain of only a few dozen square kilometres holds the remains of well over two thousand brick temples, stupas, monasteries, and other monuments. Many are small and plain; a few are among the largest religious buildings ever raised in mainland Southeast Asia. The Bagan landscape was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2019, in recognition of its surviving evidence for a powerful regional kingdom at its height between the eleventh and thirteenth centuries. What the bricks do not preserve is nearly everything else: the timber palaces, the wooden houses of officials and cultivators, the granaries, the boats, and most of the records written on perishable materials. The great monuments are therefore both extraordinary evidence and a misleading one, since they survive precisely because they were religious and built of brick.

This chapter asks how such a place came to exist. More exactly, it asks how a regional settlement in the Irrawaddy basin acquired the agricultural resources, military capacity, political institutions, and ideological legitimacy necessary to become one of mainland Southeast Asia's major kingdoms between approximately the ninth and early thirteenth centuries. The question is not answered by listing kings. Anawrahta, Kyansittha, Alaungsithu, and Narapatisithu are central figures, and the chapter treats each carefully, but each acted within conditions that earlier generations of cultivators, local chiefs, soldiers, officials, and monks had helped create. Pagan's rise has to be explained from the ground up: from irrigable land and river transport, from competition among settlements, from the ability to feed and move people, and from the ways rulers presented themselves to the communities they governed.

The evidence for this history is unusually uneven, and the chapter's method follows from that fact. Contemporary evidence consists of archaeological remains and of inscriptions, some in Burmese, some in Mon, some in Pyu, some in Pali, cut in stone or written on other durable surfaces, chiefly to record religious donations. The Burmese chronicles (yazawin) that supply most of the familiar narrative of early Pagan, including the traditional foundation date of 849, the story of Anawrahta's conquest of Thaton in 1057, and the colourful accounts of royal rivalries, were compiled centuries after the events they describe. Two of the best known, the Maha Yazawin of the early eighteenth century and the official Hmannan Yazawin of the early nineteenth, belong to a world far removed from eleventh-century Pagan. The chapter therefore gives priority to contemporary evidence, uses the chronicles as later traditions worth analysing in their own right, and says plainly where the two cannot be reconciled.

The chapter's six divisions follow the logic of the assigned subject. Division I establishes the evidence, the dry-zone environment, the Pyu inheritance, and the emergence of Burmese-speaking communities. Division II examines the early settlement and the agricultural districts of Kyaukse and Minbu that sustained it. Division III, the largest, treats the reign of Anawrahta, conventionally dated 1044–1077, including his campaigns, his armies, and the disputed tradition of the conquest of Thaton. Division IV follows the dynasty through succession crisis and recovery under Sawlu, Kyansittha, Alaungsithu, and Narapatisithu. Division V analyses the institutions that made royal power sustainable: court, provincial administration, land and labor, river trade, and the capital itself. Division VI assesses the kingdom's population, its place in the wider Bay of Bengal world, and the real extent of its authority.

Two matters of terminology deserve notice at the outset. The name of the kingdom and city is rendered here as "Pagan," the form long established in English and used by the textbook outline; the transliteration "Bagan" has been official in Myanmar since 1989 and is used by UNESCO and in much recent scholarship. Both refer to the same place. Second, "Burmese" is used with care. It refers to a language and to those who spoke it, and the chapter distinguishes Burmese-speaking communities from the Pyu, Mon, and other populations with whom they lived. It does not treat early Pagan as the inevitable creation of a unified Burmese nation, an idea that belongs to modern nationalism and to later chronicle writing, not to eleventh-century evidence.

Pagan also deserves to be understood as a Southeast Asian creation rather than a passive recipient of outside influence. Its rulers and monks drew on Buddhist traditions from South Asia and Sri Lanka, on Sanskritic ideas of kingship, and on contacts with eastern India and China, but they selected, combined, and transformed these elements, as the textbook has argued since Chapter 1 and examines directly in Chapter 19. The chapter connects backward to Chapter 24 on the Pyu city-states and forward to Chapter 41, which treats the religious and social world of the Pagan kingdom, to Chapter 46 on the Yuan campaigns, and to Chapter 59 on the successor states after Pagan. Its task is the political, agricultural, military, and urban formation of the kingdom itself.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish contemporary inscriptions and archaeology from later Burmese chronicles, and explain how each type of evidence limits what can be said about early Pagan.
  2. Describe the geography of the middle Irrawaddy basin and dry zone, including Kyaukse, Minbu, and the Chindwin, and explain why control of irrigable land mattered politically.
  3. Explain what Pagan inherited from the earlier Pyu urban centers without treating it as the simple replacement of one people by another.
  4. Trace the formation of Pagan from a fortified settlement to a regional kingdom, distinguishing traditional chronology from securely dated evidence.
  5. Analyze the interaction of agricultural expansion, military organization, and royal patronage in the consolidation of Anawrahta's kingdom.
  6. Evaluate the competing scholarly interpretations of the traditional conquest of Thaton in 1057 without replacing one unquestioned narrative with another.
  7. Compare the reigns of Anawrahta, Sawlu, Kyansittha, Alaungsithu, and Narapatisithu in terms of legitimacy, succession, and institutional development.
  8. Interpret the Myazedi inscription as evidence for dynastic commemoration and for the linguistic complexity of Pagan's political world.
  9. Assess how Pagan governed provinces, mobilized labor and revenue, and used the Irrawaddy as a political and commercial artery.
  10. Distinguish territorial occupation, tributary relationships, political influence, and retrospective imperial geography when judging the extent of Pagan's authority.
  11. Connect Pagan's emergence to the wider Bay of Bengal and mainland Southeast Asian worlds.

Historical Context

The middle Irrawaddy basin had been home to complex societies for many centuries before Pagan. Chapter 24 examines the Pyu city-states, whose walled and moated centers at Beikthano, Halin, and Sri Ksetra (Thayekhittaya) show organized urban life, Buddhist and other Indic religious practice, literacy in Pyu and Pali, and agricultural production on a considerable scale in the first millennium CE. Chinese sources of the Tang period describe Pyu kingdoms and their contacts with the south-western Chinese kingdom of Nanzhao. Chinese sources report that Nanzhao forces raided a Pyu polity in 832 and carried away captives. Some historians have identified the attacked center with Halin, but the Chinese accounts do not name that city, and archaeological evidence does not establish that Halin was destroyed in the raid. The Pyu cities subsequently ceased to be the region’s dominant political centers, through a process that remains incompletely understood. This is not the same as saying that the Pyu vanished: Pyu-speaking communities, Pyu names, and Pyu-language inscriptions continued to appear into the twelfth century.

Meanwhile, other languages and communities were moving into and within the basin. Burmese, a Tibeto-Burman language, became increasingly prominent in the upper Irrawaddy and the Kyaukse region during the first millennium, while Mon, an Austroasiatic language associated with communities of the lower Irrawaddy, the Sittaung valley, and the Gulf of Martaban coast and with the wider Mon worlds discussed in Chapter 25, was a major language of Buddhist and royal expression. The surrounding uplands, whether the Shan hills to the east, the Chin hills to the west, or the northern borderlands, were home to communities whose relations with the plains were sometimes cooperative and sometimes violent. The Bay of Bengal coast offered Arakan and the Mon ports connections to eastern India and Sri Lanka, and the north offered routes toward Yunnan.

Historians therefore approach Pagan as a political outcome within a mixed and changing landscape rather than as the arrival of a ready-made kingdom. Older scholarship, much of it shaped by colonial-era chronicle reading and by early twentieth-century epigraphy, often told the story as a sequence of ethnic successions: Pyu, then Burmese conquerors who borrowed civilization from the Mon. More recent work has questioned both the neat ethnic sequence and the reliability of the chronicle tradition that supports it. This chapter builds on that revision. It asks how existing agricultural settlements, regional chiefs, military followings, religious institutions, and diverse populations combined, and how a succession of rulers learned to direct their resources toward a durable monarchy. Chapter 53 later examines the wider Theravada transformation of the mainland, and Chapter 41 examines the religious and social life that flourished within Pagan; this chapter concentrates on the political formation.

Section 40.1

Reconstructing the Origins of Pagan: Archaeology, Inscriptions, and Chronicles

Any account of Pagan's origins has to begin with a decision about which evidence takes priority. The decision is not neutral, because the available sources point in different directions. Archaeology offers settlement remains, fortifications, pottery, bricks, burial and monument sites, and the occasional radiocarbon date; it is physical, contemporary with what it records, and difficult to date precisely. Inscriptions offer written statements by donors, officials, and rulers, usually recording land or labor given to a religious foundation, and sometimes including royal genealogies, titles, and boundary descriptions. They are contemporary but selective: they say what donors wished to be remembered, not what a historian would like to know. The chronicles offer a continuous narrative, with kings, dates, battles, and motives, but they were written much later and for different purposes.

The chronicle tradition requires particular care. Burmese court historians of the early modern and modern periods compiled their narratives from earlier chronicles, palm-leaf records, inscriptions they could read, and oral traditions, and they shaped the result to serve the needs of their own courts. The Maha Yazawin was compiled in the early eighteenth century, and the Hmannan Yazawin (often called the Glass Palace Chronicle in English) was produced under royal commission in the early nineteenth. By then, the chroniclers were writing a unified history of a Burmese monarchy stretching back to Pagan and beyond, and they had reasons to supply regular royal successions, exact reign lengths, and neat narratives of foundation. Where the chronicles can be tested against inscriptions from Pagan itself, they sometimes agree and sometimes do not, and historians have learned to treat their precise dates for the ninth and tenth centuries with suspicion.

The modern study of the Pagan inscriptions began in the late nineteenth century with the work of colonial epigraphers, and it was expanded by published collections such as Epigraphia Birmanica and the multi-volume Inscriptions of Burma edited by Pe Maung Tin and G. H. Luce, followed by Luce's synthesis Old Burma—Early Pagan and the work of Burmese scholars such as Than Tun. These scholars built the standard reconstruction of Pagan's history by combining inscriptions with the chronicles. Later historians, notably Michael Aung-Thwin, challenged how far the chronicles could be trusted and how some inscriptions had been dated and interpreted, and archaeologists, among them Bob Hudson and colleagues working with the Myanmar Department of Archaeology, began producing new dating evidence from survey and excavation. The result is a field in which a reader needs to know the type of evidence behind each claim.

This chapter applies a simple hierarchy. Where contemporary inscriptions and archaeology speak, they take priority. Where the chronicles supply the only narrative, their account is presented as tradition, with its source and date made explicit. Where chronicle and inscription conflict, the conflict is stated. Dates in the chronicles are normally expressed in the Burmese Era, which begins in 638 CE; conversions to the Common Era are made here with the caution that a conventional date such as 1044 for Anawrahta's accession is a scholarly convention resting largely on this later reckoning, not a date fixed by a contemporary document.

Historical Vocabulary
Yazawin (Burmese chronicle)

Definition. Yazawin is the Burmese term for a chronicle of kings, a court-sponsored narrative history organized around royal reigns.

Historical context. The chronicles known today were composed long after Pagan, from roughly the fifteenth century onward in surviving forms, and the best-known compilations date from the early eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries. They drew on earlier material, some of it now lost.

Why it matters. The chronicles supply nearly all the familiar stories of early Pagan, including the foundation in 849, the conversion of Anawrahta, and the conquest of Thaton. Historians use them as evidence for how later courts understood the past as well as for the past itself.

Potential misunderstanding. A chronicle is not a contemporary record. Its precision of dates and its dramatic episodes can give a false impression of reliability.

Historical Vocabulary
Inscription (epigraphic record)

Definition. An inscription is a text cut into stone or another durable material, studied by the discipline of epigraphy.

Historical context. Thousands of inscriptions survive from the Pagan period, written in Burmese, Mon, Pyu, Pali, and Sanskrit-influenced forms. Most record donations to religious foundations, listing land, laborers, and the names of donors, witnesses, and officials.

Why it matters. Inscriptions are the closest thing to contemporary documentary evidence for Pagan, and they preserve information about land, social status, and royal titles that the chronicles omit.

Potential misunderstanding. Because most inscriptions are religious donations, they overrepresent the elite and the pious, and they say little about ordinary life or about political events that did not concern a donor.

Section 40.2

The Irrawaddy Basin, Central Dry Zone, and Agricultural Geography

Chapter 2 introduces the Irrawaddy as the great river of the western mainland. Pagan stands on its middle course, in what geographers call the central dry zone of Myanmar. This region lies in the rain shadow of the Arakan Yoma mountains to the west, and it receives far less monsoon rain than the delta to the south or the coastal ranges: in many places annual rainfall falls well below that of the wetter regions, and it is concentrated into a season that can fail. The landscape is one of dry plains, scrub, toddy palms, and seasonal streams, broken by river terraces, alluvial floodplain, and low ranges. It is not, at first sight, a natural location for a rice-based kingdom.

Its political advantages lay elsewhere. The Irrawaddy provided a north–south artery of movement, and the Chindwin, its major western tributary, which joins it a short distance upstream of Pagan, brought goods and people from the north-western uplands. Where streams descend from the hills they can be diverted: the Kyaukse plain to the north-east, watered by rivers such as the Zawgyi and Panlaung, and the districts around Minbu to the south-west, watered by the Mon and other streams, were places where weirs and canals could turn a dry landscape into reliable rice land. The extinct volcano of Mount Popa, south-east of Pagan, collects moisture and feeds streams that supported nearby settlement. A ruler who controlled these productive pockets and the river that linked them held an unusual combination of food, labor, and communications.

The environment therefore shaped the possibilities of Pagan without determining its history. Irrigated districts were scattered and relatively small, and they required continuing labor to maintain: weirs silted, canals needed clearing, and fields needed organization. Whoever could mobilize that labor, protect the works, and collect a share of the harvest gained power. Different political outcomes were possible. Competing chiefs might have controlled separate districts indefinitely; the Pyu centers had earlier organized the region in a different way. That a single center on the Pagan plain came to dominate several districts was the result of human organization, not of geography alone.

Drier lands outside the irrigated tracts supported other crops: millet, sesame, beans, and palms, as well as cattle, and later records of land and donations mention a range of products. The Pagan plain itself, with its sandy terraces and limited water, was less a farming center than a defensible and well-placed seat of power, close to rich districts and on the river. This distinction, between the places where the food was grown and the place where political authority was concentrated, helps explain much of what follows.

Section 40.3

The Pyu Inheritance and Earlier Political Networks

The Pyu cities discussed in Chapter 24 form the background to Pagan. Beikthano, Halin, and Sri Ksetra, each protected by walls or moats and each containing evidence of planned space, brick architecture, and ritual and burial sites, show that the Irrawaddy basin had supported substantial walled centers for centuries. Pyu communities wrote in a script adapted from Indic scripts, used Pali and Pyu in religious contexts, buried their dead in urns, produced distinctive silver coinage, and practiced Buddhist and other Indic religious traditions. The three cities were recognized by UNESCO as a World Heritage property in 2014, and they remain the main material evidence of the first millennium urbanism that preceded Pagan.

The Pyu inheritance was several things at once. It included settlement patterns: agricultural land that was already farmed and population already living in the Irrawaddy basin when Pagan's rulers began to expand. It included religious practices, which means that Buddhism was not a novelty introduced to a blank landscape. It included a tradition of literate administration and monument building, and probably a knowledge of water management, although the extent of Pyu irrigation is itself debated and should not be assumed to have been equivalent to that of later periods. And it included a model of the walled, ceremonial city as a seat of rulers.

It is equally important to say what the Pyu inheritance does not mean. There is no evidence that Pagan was founded by Pyu rulers, and no evidence that the Pyu were simply "replaced" by Burmese-speaking people in a single event. Chinese records of Nanzhao raids against Pyu communities in the 830s document disruption, although the identification of the attacked center with Halin is uncertain; these accounts do not prove the end of Pyu communities, and the continued use of Pyu in inscriptions at Pagan as late as the early twelfth century, including at the Myazedi, shows that Pyu-speaking people and Pyu cultural traditions remained present for generations. Linguistic change, which in this case involved the gradual spread of Burmese in areas where Pyu had been spoken, is a slower and more complicated process than conquest. Historians generally now describe a long period of coexistence, intermarriage, and shifting prestige in which Burmese eventually became the dominant language of the region.

Why then do the Pyu cities not continue as the political centers of the basin? Several factors are discussed in the scholarship, and none is conclusive. Military pressure from outside, the movement of populations, shifts in the control of water and agricultural land, changes in trade routes, and internal political competition all could have contributed. For this chapter, the important point is that the Pagan plain was part of a landscape already shaped by earlier settlement, and that its rulers could draw on populations, techniques, and ideas that predated them. Pagan was a new political center built within a very old inhabited world.

Section 40.4

Burmese-Speaking Communities and the Emergence of a New Political Center

The Burmese language belongs to the Tibeto-Burman branch of the Sino-Tibetan family, a classification discussed with the other mainland language families in Chapter 15. Its early speakers, according to most modern reconstructions, entered the Irrawaddy basin over an extended period from the north and north-east, and their communities are particularly associated with the irrigated districts such as Kyaukse. Older accounts sometimes attributed this movement to a single migration in the ninth century, linked to the Nanzhao attack on the Pyu. Current scholarship is more cautious. The evidence does not support a single mass invasion, and it is likely that Burmese speakers were present in the basin before the ninth century and increased in number and political weight through gradual migration, intermarriage, and the adoption of Burmese by other communities.

The earliest Burmese writing used a script derived from the script of the Mon, which in turn had Indic origins, and Burmese inscriptions begin to survive in the eleventh century, with claims for earlier examples disputed. Chinese sources and some inscriptions use a name resembling "Mranma" for the Burmese, a form that appears in the Pagan inscriptions. The adoption of writing for religious donations, a practice evidently learned from Mon and Pyu models, and its spread to Burmese-speaking donors, is evidence that these communities were participating in an existing literate Buddhist world rather than standing outside it. By the late eleventh century, Burmese was being used in public texts alongside Mon and Pali, and by the twelfth it was becoming the dominant language of the donations that survive in the greatest numbers.

A linguistic history must not be confused with an ethnic or national one. To say that Burmese became the language of the Pagan court and of most of its inscriptions is not to say that Pagan was the homogeneous creation of a "Burmese people." Individuals changed languages and identities, rulers patronized several languages, and the communities of the plain and of Kyaukse included Pyu, Mon, and Burmese speakers whose categories of belonging, which the sources describe unevenly, are not those of the modern nation-state. The idea of a continuous Burmese nation moving across the centuries from the Pagan kings to the present belongs to a later historiography that Chapter 1 has already invited students to read critically.

What the evidence does show is a political center emerging in a culturally mixed regional landscape. The Pagan plain, with its position on the river and its access to the irrigated districts, became the base for ruling families whose supporters included Burmese-speaking cultivators and warriors and whose religious and literary culture drew on Pyu and Mon traditions. The emergence of that center, and its transformation from one settlement among several to a kingdom, is the subject of the next division.

Division II

The Early Formation of Pagan

Settlement, local authority, and the economic foundations of consolidation

Section 40.5

Early Pagan, Founding Traditions, and the Archaeological Record

The chronicle tradition places the foundation of walled Pagan in 849 CE, under a ruler called Pyinbya, and it attaches to this event a story that the city grew from the union of a number of earlier villages, traditionally given as nineteen. This tradition has long been used as the starting point of Pagan's history, and the date 849 appears in many textbooks. It should be handled as a tradition. No contemporary inscription confirms it, the chronicle lists of kings between Pyinbya and Anawrahta carry precise reign lengths that historians do not regard as reliable, and the compilers of the chronicles had good reasons to create a clear origin and an unbroken line of kings.

Archaeology offers a different and more complicated picture. Survey and excavation on the Pagan plain, including work published from around 2000 by Hudson and colleagues, has suggested that the area was occupied well before the traditional foundation date, probably during the Pyu period, and that settlement continued and changed over several centuries. Radiocarbon dates and the study of ceramics and site layouts have been used to argue that the walled city was not a single act of foundation but the outcome of gradual growth, and the dating of the walls and gates is debated. A surviving gate, the Tharabha Gate, is traditionally associated with the early city, but the age of surviving fabric is a matter for architectural and archaeological analysis, not for tradition alone.

The difference matters. If Pagan was founded in a single reign in 849, then its rise to power in the eleventh century needs to be explained as the delayed outcome of a ninth-century foundation. If, as the archaeological evidence suggests, settlement was older and the walled city developed gradually, then the question changes: why did this particular settlement among many acquire the walls, the population, and the authority to dominate its neighbors? Answering that question requires looking at the surrounding districts, their agricultural base, and the military and political leadership that made one center preeminent.

Students should therefore carry two dates in mind. 849 is a date that appears in the tradition and is used by convention. The period between roughly the ninth and eleventh centuries is the more defensible archaeological range during which Pagan changed from a settlement within a Pyu-influenced landscape into a fortified royal center. Each statement is about different kinds of evidence, and neither should be forced into the form of the other.

Section 40.6

Early Rulers, Local Chiefs, and Political Consolidation

The chronicles list a succession of kings of Pagan between the foundation and Anawrahta, each with a name and a reign length. Few of these rulers appear in contemporary inscriptions, and some of the stories told about them, involving supernatural episodes, strange births, and dramatic usurpations, have the character of legend. Historians generally agree that a line of local rulers did exist, but they disagree about how many, how they were related, and how much authority they exercised. What can be said with confidence is that, by the early eleventh century, Pagan was a significant center with ruling families able to command followers and that other centers existed with comparable claims.

Political competition in this period is better understood through the structures of authority than through individual names. A ruler in the middle Irrawaddy depended on regional leaders, some of them heads of agricultural districts, some of them military commanders, some of them religious figures, whose loyalty could not be taken for granted. These leaders controlled people and land, and the ruler's power depended on the ability to persuade, reward, or compel them. Marriage alliances, the distribution of offices and land, gifts, and shared religious patronage were the instruments of this politics, and they appear in later inscriptions as well as in chronicle stories.

A more durable political center emerged where a ruling family was able to convert such relationships into regular claims on grain and labor. This is the process that the chapter examines throughout: the move from personal loyalty to institutions of revenue and service. It was slow, and it was reversible. The reigns of Sawlu and of the rulers after Alaungsithu show how quickly succession disputes could expose the fragility of the system. Before Anawrahta, the process was already under way; his achievement, discussed in Division III, was to extend and accelerate it, not to invent it.

The relative silence of the sources about this early period should itself be treated as a historical fact. The inscriptions that survive in abundance date from the later eleventh century onward, when donations to religious foundations became an established practice among those with land and status. Earlier rulers may have made donations recorded on perishable materials, or on stones now lost, but the written record that allows historians to see politics in detail begins just as Pagan's power was becoming large.

Section 40.7

Agricultural Expansion in the Kyaukse and Minbu Regions

The agricultural districts around Pagan are central to the history of the kingdom, and the relationship between agricultural expansion and political consolidation has been one of the most influential arguments in the scholarship. Kyaukse, north-east of Pagan, is a plain watered by streams descending from the Shan uplands, and later records show it as one of the most productive rice-growing areas of Upper Myanmar. Minbu, south-west of Pagan on the opposite side of the river, is also irrigated, and its early monuments and inscriptions show that it was a significant district. Both lay within reach of Pagan by river or road, which allowed a king to draw their produce and manpower to the capital.

Michael Aung-Thwin's study of irrigation in the heartland of Burma argued that the expansion of irrigated agriculture in these districts was the economic foundation of Pagan's rise. Control of grain-producing districts gave the court the means to feed soldiers, to support officials and religious establishments, and to exercise authority over regional leaders who depended on the same land. Other scholars accept the importance of agricultural resources while debating the dating, the scale, and the nature of the works. The chronicle tradition, which credits Anawrahta with building a number of weirs and canals at Kyaukse, may reflect a later memory of a ruler associated with agricultural expansion rather than documented engineering by a single king.

This is the point at which caution is especially necessary. Contemporary evidence for early irrigation works is uneven. Inscriptions record land and donations in the irrigated districts, which implies cultivation, but they do not identify who built a given weir or when. Weirs and canals were repaired, rebuilt, and extended over many centuries, so existing structures cannot be assumed to date from the eleventh century. The reasonable inference is that agricultural expansion in these districts proceeded over generations, led by local communities and regional leaders as well as kings, and that the court gained power by asserting claims over the resulting production and by sponsoring or protecting the works.

Agricultural expansion also had costs and risks. Irrigated rice demanded organized labor, vulnerable works, and a predictable social order. A drought, a flood that destroyed a weir, a conflict that disrupted maintenance, or an exodus of farmers could weaken a district rapidly. The strategic value of Kyaukse and Minbu therefore explains why they were repeatedly contested and why the control of such places, by conquest, alliance, or administration, was a central objective of Pagan's rulers.

Section 40.8

Early Urban Growth, Fortifications, and the Royal Settlement

The walled city of Pagan, known today as Old Bagan, occupies the river side of the plain. Its brick wall, much of it now eroded or lost to the river, enclosed an area that included the royal palace and associated institutions, and the surrounding plain held a number of related settlements, such as Myinkaba, Nyaung-U, and Minnanthu, each with its own monuments and population. The city should therefore be thought of as a core within a wider urban landscape, not as a single compact town. Wooden buildings were the norm for houses, palaces, and many monasteries, and these have left only traces in the archaeological record.

The royal palace is documented in later inscriptions and traditions, and excavations by Myanmar's Archaeology Department have identified brick foundations and platforms on the palace site, although interpretations of the remains vary. The brick walls, the gates, the river frontage, and the monuments inside and outside the walls together show a deliberate concentration of authority. Fortification served several purposes: defense against raids, control of access, and the visible assertion that this was a place of rule. The relationship between the city and the river was crucial, since boats bringing grain and people could reach the capital directly.

Urban concentration was linked to agricultural control. A capital of this kind needed a continuing supply of food, bricks, timber, and labor for construction, and it needed artisans, officials, soldiers, and religious communities who did not grow their own food. The growth of the royal settlement thus depended on the same irrigated districts discussed in §40.7 and on the ability of the court to command their produce. Conversely, the presence of a large concentrated population at the center made it easier to organize armies, to hold ceremonies, and to supervise the resources of the surrounding region.

The early development of the city is difficult to date in detail. Monuments in brick survive best, and many of the plain's major structures date from the eleventh century onward, but the settlements around the walls had a longer history. The fullest development of the capital, including its large-scale construction program and its demographic and economic organization, belongs to a period examined in §40.24 after the kingdom's power had been established.

Division III

Anawrahta and the Expansion of Royal Power

The chapter's principal political and military transformation

Section 40.9

Anawrahta's Accession and the Political Situation around 1044

Anawrahta (the Burmese form of the Sanskrit name Aniruddha) is conventionally dated as ruling from 1044 to 1077. The dates come from the chronicle tradition, adjusted by modern scholars, and they should be read as approximate conventions. Anawrahta stands at the center of the later Burmese memory of Pagan: he is the king who expanded the kingdom, who is associated with the Shwezigon and with the Buddhist reform stories treated in Chapter 41, and who figures as the hero of a series of dramatic episodes. Because later writers admired him, the stories told about him are more elaborate than the contemporary evidence supports.

The chronicles describe his accession as following a dynastic struggle in which he defeated a rival or predecessor, usually named in the chronicles as Sokkate, in combat. Such stories belong to a recurring type of chronicle narrative, in which a righteous prince defeats an unworthy ruler, and historians treat the particulars with caution. The broader historical reconstruction is more modest: Anawrahta came to power in a political landscape of competing regional leaders and ruling families, and he succeeded in establishing a stronger central authority than his predecessors. Whether he did so by overthrowing a rival, inheriting a position, or building on earlier consolidation is not certain.

Contemporary evidence for his reign is less abundant than for those of his successors. Inscriptions from the period of Kyansittha and later refer back to him, terracotta votive tablets bearing his name in Sanskrit and Pali have been found, and archaeology of the Pagan plain shows that major building activity was under way in and after his reign. A good deal of what is said about Anawrahta rests, accordingly, on retrospective sources. This does not make him a mythical figure, since the evidence for a powerful ruler in the mid-eleventh century is sufficient, but it does warn against attributing every development of the period to him.

The political situation around 1044 can be described in general terms. The Pagan court controlled the plain and parts of the surrounding districts, but other centers, regional leaders, and neighboring communities retained their own power. The upland margins were occupied by communities with their own leaders, the lower Irrawaddy and the Mon-associated coast formed another sphere, and the Chinese borderlands lay to the north-east. Anawrahta's reign is best understood as a period in which a ruling house with the right resources and a series of favorable decisions turned a leading position into a dominant one.

Section 40.10

Consolidating the Irrawaddy Heartland

The first task of any ruler who wished to dominate the middle Irrawaddy was to secure the heartland: the irrigated districts that fed the capital, the river routes that connected them, and the leaders who controlled them. The sources suggest that during and after Anawrahta's reign the Pagan court increasingly integrated Kyaukse, Minbu, and neighboring districts into its sphere, by appointing or confirming officials, by taking a share of production, by recruiting men for service, and by granting land and privileges to supporters. Later inscriptions, which record the sale and donation of land in these districts under royal and official oversight, show an administrative environment in which the court's authority was recognized.

Relations with local leaders were central. A district headman or regional chief might be bound to the king by oath, kinship, gifts, or service, and could expect recognition of his status and a share in the benefits of expansion. The court's power rested on managing these relationships. It could reward loyalty with offices and land, punish defection by force, and bind elites through marriage and ritual. The result was not a uniform bureaucratic state, but a network of arrangements that varied from district to district and that could be renegotiated at the death of a ruler.

The economic and military foundations of the expanding monarchy reinforced each other. Revenue in grain and labor allowed the court to maintain followers, build fortifications, and support religious foundations that enhanced its prestige. A larger following made it possible to enforce claims in the districts and to defend them against rivals. In turn, the success of the court attracted allies and discouraged opponents. Historians have pointed to this feedback between resources and power as the key to Pagan's rise, while also noting that the same feedback could operate in reverse if a king lost control of the districts.

Cause and Effect

How Agricultural Expansion and Political Consolidation Reinforced Each Other

Long-term causes

Earlier Pyu-period settlement and agriculture, the availability of irrigable land in Kyaukse and Minbu, gradual in-migration of Burmese-speaking communities, and an inherited tradition of walled centers and Buddhist institutions.

Short-term causes

The ability of the Pagan ruling house to command followers, to win the support of district leaders, and to extend protection and organization to productive districts in the generations before and during Anawrahta's reign.

Immediate trigger or context

Political competition among regional centers in the early and mid-eleventh century, and the consolidation under Anawrahta, conventionally dated from 1044.

Event or transformation

Integration of the irrigated heartland under a court able to draw on its grain and labor, producing a central authority larger than any local rival.

Immediate effects

Larger armies and followings, expanded building and religious patronage, more extensive campaigns, and the capacity to hold the heartland against challengers.

Long-term consequences

A durable kingdom centered on the middle Irrawaddy, institutions of land, revenue, and service that outlasted individual rulers, and a model of Burmese-centered monarchy later remembered as classical.

Historical complexity: Agricultural resources made consolidation possible but did not cause it automatically. Equivalent resources existed under rival leaders and under earlier Pyu rulers. The outcome depended on organization, military capacity, alliances, religious legitimacy, and the contingencies of succession, and the same processes could be reversed when a king's authority weakened.

Section 40.11

Campaigns, Conquests, and the Extension of Pagan's Influence

The chronicles credit Anawrahta with a series of conquests that carried Pagan's authority across much of present-day Myanmar and into neighboring regions: northward toward the Shan uplands and the Chinese frontier, westward toward Arakan, and southward to the Mon-associated lands of Lower Myanmar and the Tenasserim coast. These claims belong to the later memory of an ideal king, and some of them cannot be verified. Contemporary inscriptions rarely describe campaigns in detail, and where later inscriptions mention royal expeditions, they are usually brief and framed in the language of royal glory.

Historians therefore distinguish several kinds of relationship. Areas close to Pagan, including the irrigated districts and the river corridor, were the object of sustained control and probably of administration. Areas farther away, such as upland valleys and frontier districts, were more likely to be linked by campaigns, tribute, or the recognition of Pagan's overlordship by local leaders. Some regions, which the chronicles claim as conquests, may have been merely visited or raided, or have sent presents. The distinction between occupation, tribute, and influence, developed further in §40.27, is essential to reading the evidence.

Upper Myanmar offered the most reliable base for Pagan's expansion. Valleys such as the Mu to the north, the Chindwin region, and the upland margins were accessible and economically valuable, and the court's interest in them is plausible on geographic and economic grounds. Relations with the Shan hills and with communities toward the Chinese frontier combined military pressure with exchange. Campaigns into Lower Myanmar are the most contested, because they are tied to the Thaton tradition examined in §40.12, and because Pagan's lasting authority in the south was limited and variable.

A cautious summary is that Anawrahta's reign extended the political reach of Pagan significantly beyond the immediate heartland, and that the surrounding regions were brought into varied relationships with the court. The scale and permanence of that extension are matters of inference from archaeology, inscriptions, and later political history, not of documented territorial administration. The idea of a single, united "empire" stretching to the sea belongs to the retrospective picture of the chroniclers and later nationalist historians.

Section 40.12

The Traditional Conquest of Thaton and the Problem of Historical Evidence

The conquest of Thaton in 1057 is one of the best-known episodes in the traditional history of Myanmar. In the chronicle account, a Mon monk from Thaton named Shin Arahan came to Pagan and introduced Anawrahta to a purer form of Buddhism. Anawrahta then asked the king of Thaton, Manuha, for copies of Buddhist scriptures and relics. Manuha refused, and Anawrahta marched south, conquered Thaton, and brought back to Pagan the king, his court, monks, craftsmen, and the scriptures. In this telling, the conquest was the moment at which Burmese rulers acquired Mon learning, religion, and writing, and the later Pagan civilization was built on it.

The story is attractive, and it long served as a foundation for the view that the Burmese borrowed their civilization from the Mon. Its difficulties are nonetheless considerable. No contemporary inscription from eleventh-century Pagan records a conquest of Thaton. The full conquest narrative is preserved in chronicles compiled centuries later, especially in their eighteenth- and nineteenth-century forms. The fifteenth-century Kalyani inscriptions at Pegu, associated with King Dhammazedi, mention Anawrahta’s religious activities and discuss Thaton in separate passages, but do not explicitly narrate his conquest of that city. They have nevertheless been cited in later arguments about the story. In the meantime, the political circumstances in which the story was told had changed: Mon and Burmese kingdoms in later centuries had their own reasons to remember, and to shape, their relationship to Pagan.

Michael Aung-Thwin challenged the traditional account in the 1980s and after, arguing that the conquest narrative was a later construction, that the evidence for the political importance of Thaton in the eleventh century was thin, and that the idea of a Mon cultural origin of Pagan civilization had been exaggerated by chroniclers and by modern historians who accepted them uncritically. He pointed out that the Mon language was used at Pagan because it had prestige as a language of Buddhist and royal expression, and that its use does not require a conquest. Other scholars have accepted parts of his critique while keeping a more qualified view of Mon influence, of contacts between Pagan and Lower Myanmar, and of the possibility of a Pagan campaign in the south whose details were later embroidered. Archaeological work in the Thaton region has shown occupied early historic sites, though the dating of their principal remains and their relation to a political center in the 1050s remain debated.

Students should resist a simple conclusion in either direction. The evidence does not establish that Anawrahta conquered Thaton in 1057 and carried off its scriptures. It also does not establish that nothing happened, or that the Mon were unimportant to Pagan. Mon inscriptions at Pagan, Mon-influenced script, and Mon monks and scholars are all attested. What is in doubt is the specific event, the date, the scale, and above all the explanation of Pagan's culture as the consequence of a single conquest. Pali Buddhist texts and monastic lineages could have reached Pagan by several routes, including from Mon-speaking regions, Sri Lanka, and eastern India, and by several mechanisms, including exchange, travel, and patronage.

Historical Debate

Was Thaton Conquered in 1057?

The question: Did Anawrahta conquer Thaton in 1057, and did that conquest bring Mon religion, writing, and learning to Pagan, as the chronicle tradition and much twentieth-century scholarship maintained?

Traditional interpretation: The chronicles preserve a historical memory of a campaign against Thaton, which transferred Buddhist texts, monks, and craftsmen to Pagan and explains the strong Mon presence in Pagan's early culture. Older scholarship, including much work of the early and mid-twentieth century, generally accepted the outline of this account.
Critical interpretation: The conquest is first documented much later, serves the interests of later narrators, and is not confirmed by contemporary evidence. Mon cultural prestige at Pagan can be explained without conquest, and the idea of the Mon as civilizers of the Burmese reflects later political and colonial-era assumptions. Aung-Thwin's work is the best-known presentation of this view.
Intermediate approaches: Some scholars allow for a campaign or for political relations between Pagan and Lower Myanmar in the eleventh century, while rejecting the full chronicle narrative. Others emphasize that Mon-speaking communities, including those in the Pagan region itself, were participants in Pagan's culture long before and apart from any conquest.

Evidence: The sources include the absence of a contemporary record of conquest, the later chronicle accounts and the Kalyani inscription passages subsequently invoked in support of the tradition, Mon-language inscriptions at Pagan, the archaeology of Thaton and Lower Myanmar, and the evidence of Buddhist textual transmission from several regions.

Points of disagreement: Chronology and event (did a conquest occur, and when?), causation (did it cause the Mon influence on Pagan?), interpretation (what does the story mean?), and evidence (how much weight should late sources bear?).

Why it matters: How historians settle the question affects whether Pagan is understood as a civilization derived from the Mon, as an outgrowth of local Irrawaddy traditions, or as a mixed political culture. It also shows how political and ethnic narratives of later centuries can shape the interpretation of earlier evidence.

Student task: List the kinds of evidence that would be needed to establish a conquest in 1057. Which exist, and which are missing? Then explain how Mon influence might be accounted for without a conquest.

Section 40.13

Kingship, Religious Legitimacy, and Royal Patronage under Anawrahta

Kings in eleventh-century Pagan, like rulers elsewhere in mainland Southeast Asia, justified their authority through religious patronage. Building and endowing shrines, supporting monks, and commissioning sacred texts and images conferred merit, in the Buddhist understanding, and also created visible evidence of the ruler's wealth and standing. They tied communities of donors, officials, and religious specialists to the court. Under Anawrahta, the best-documented acts of patronage are the beginning of the Shwezigon pagoda near Nyaung-U, traditionally credited to him and finished in the next reign, and the votive tablets and other monuments associated with his name.

The relationship between religion and power was not a simple one. Rulers drew on several traditions at once: Buddhist ideas of the righteous king, Sanskritic royal titles and ritual, local cults of spirits and sacred places, and the practices of the Pyu and Mon predecessors. The chronicles stress Anawrahta's role in promoting a particular reformed Buddhism and in suppressing rival monastic groups, but the nature and extent of any such reform, and the character of the religious communities of his time, are examined in Chapter 41. For the political history, the point is that the king's support of religion helped create a loyal network of monks, donors, and local patrons and enhanced his claim to rule.

Patronage also had an economic side. Land and laborers dedicated to religious foundations could be removed from the immediate control of local rivals and placed under the protection of the court. Wise kings used donations to reward supporters and to reinforce their position in the districts, and the religious economy that developed, discussed in Chapter 41, became a major element of Pagan's resources. In this chapter it is enough to note that the political and religious uses of patronage were intertwined.

Royal self-presentation under Anawrahta is only partially documented, since the royal titles and genealogies that are best attested come from later reigns. The use of a Sanskrit-derived name, Aniruddha, the association with Buddhist merit, and the building projects of his reign suggest a deliberate effort to present the king in terms that would be recognized across the Buddhist world of South and Southeast Asia, while remaining legible to local communities.

Section 40.14

Armies, Military Mobilization, Fortifications, and Frontier Control

The chronicles give large numbers for the armies of Pagan, and these should be set aside: no reliable figures for eleventh-century forces survive. What can be reconstructed is the general character of military organization. Temple wall paintings and later inscriptions, together with comparative evidence from the region, suggest armies of infantry armed with swords, spears, and bows, supported by elephants and by cavalry where horses were available, and by boats for transport on the Irrawaddy and its tributaries. Fortified settlements, including the capital, served as bases and refuges.

Recruitment depended on the ruler's relations with his followers and district leaders. Men were raised through obligations of service owed to the king, through the retinues of regional lords and commanders, and through the groups that inscriptions describe as serving the court in various capacities. Later Burmese states relied on hereditary groups of royal servicemen, and historians debate how far this system existed in Pagan; Aung-Thwin, among others, has argued that similar arrangements were already in place. Where the evidence is thin, it is safest to say that service to the king, whether military or otherwise, was a primary form of obligation in the kingdom.

Supply and transport set limits on campaigns. Armies needed food, which in the dry zone came chiefly from the irrigated districts and was moved by river or road, and campaigning had to take account of the monsoon, which affected travel and the health of troops. This is one reason why control of productive land and transport routes was the foundation of military power: the king who could feed his soldiers and move them quickly had a decisive advantage, and the king who lost the districts lost the means to wage war.

Frontier control combined force and diplomacy. Hill districts, river corridors, and border regions were held by a mix of garrisons, allied leaders, and local arrangements, and campaigns were often directed at securing these rather than at conquering distant lands. The military power of Pagan thus rested not on a permanent standing army in the modern sense, but on the capacity to mobilize followers on a considerable scale, supported by agricultural production and by a network of fortified places and loyal commanders. Its limits were set by the same factors: supply, distance, seasons, and the loyalty of those who commanded the troops.

Division IV

Succession, Dynastic Consolidation, and the Mature Pagan Monarchy

Political continuity beyond the founding ruler

Section 40.15

Sawlu and the Succession Crisis after Anawrahta

Anawrahta was succeeded, according to the chronicles and the conventional chronology, by his son Sawlu, who is dated 1077 to 1084. The reign is short and poorly documented, and it is remembered chiefly as a period of trouble. The chronicle tradition presents Sawlu as an inadequate ruler who quarreled with his powerful general Kyansittha, who faced rebellion in the south, and who was eventually captured and killed in the course of a campaign in Lower Myanmar. Historians accept that the reign was unstable but treat the details of the stories, which turn on personal rivalries and betrayals, with caution.

The significance of the reign lies in what it reveals about the structure of the monarchy. A ruler's authority depended heavily on personal relationships with commanders and regional leaders, and when a strong king was succeeded by a weaker one, those relationships could unravel. The kingdom's institutions were not yet sufficiently developed to carry authority independently of the king. Distant districts, particularly in the south, which had been brought into Pagan's sphere under Anawrahta, could easily slip away, and commanders who had served the old king might become rivals to the new one.

The succession crisis also exposed the absence of a firm rule of succession. In many Southeast Asian courts of the period, rulers could be succeeded by sons, brothers, or other relatives, or by men who gained the support of the court and army, and disputes were common. Later Burmese kings and chroniclers would attempt to define more regular patterns, but the Pagan evidence shows that a strong claim usually combined royal descent, military backing, and the approval of leading figures. Kyansittha's emergence belongs in this context.

That the dynasty survived and the kingdom recovered shows that the foundations laid in Anawrahta's reign, the agricultural districts, the network of leaders, and the court itself, were stronger than the failures of an individual ruler. The recovery is the subject of the next section.

Section 40.16

Kyansittha and the Reconstruction of Royal Authority

Kyansittha, conventionally dated 1084 to 1112 or 1113, is the first ruler of Pagan about whom a substantial body of contemporary inscriptional evidence survives. His reign is documented by inscriptions in Mon, Burmese, and Pali, and by the monuments built or begun in his time, among them the Ananda temple, which is discussed in Chapter 41, and the completion of the Shwezigon. The inscriptions allow historians to compare the king's own presentation of his rule with the later chronicle stories about him, which describe an unusual career: a military servant of Anawrahta, a figure of uncertain parentage, a fugitive who eventually became king.

Legitimacy was the central problem of the reign, since Kyansittha did not come to the throne as the undisputed son of the previous king. The inscriptions suggest how he addressed it. They present him as a righteous ruler who restored order after a period of disruption, they use elaborate titles that include Sanskritic and Pali elements, and they associate him with divine and Buddhist kingship. Some texts present the king in terms drawn from the Indian tradition of the god-king, as a ruler of exceptional merit, and they stress his generosity to the religious community. They also emphasize the support of officials and regional leaders, and they stress the connections he built through marriage and alliance.

Political relations with regional leaders were crucial. Having come to power in the wake of Sawlu's death and the unrest of the southern provinces, Kyansittha had to reassert royal authority over districts and commanders who had acted independently. The inscriptions and later tradition suggest that he did so through a mixture of conciliation and force, and that he used alliances through marriage to bind important families to the throne. His preference for Mon in his own inscriptions, which has often been seen as evidence for the Mon cultural influence on Pagan, is also consistent with the use of Mon as a prestigious language of royal and religious expression and with the presence of Mon-speaking communities in the royal service.

The priority given to contemporary inscriptions makes the reign of Kyansittha a useful test of method. Where the chronicles tell stories of the king's romantic and dramatic personal life, the inscriptions concentrate on his religious benefactions, his titles, and his claims to just rule. The two kinds of source are not necessarily incompatible, but they answer different questions, and the historian should be careful not to let the more colorful narrative displace the more reliable record.

Creator: Court-associated authors and scribes at Pagan, writing in the Mon language, under or in the name of King Kyansittha · Date: late eleventh and early twelfth centuries CE, conventionally within his reign of 1084–1112/1113 · Type: Royal and dedicatory inscriptions on stone, including texts associated with the Shwezigon and other foundations; edited in the twentieth century in collections such as Epigraphia Birmanica and the Inscriptions of Burma

Context: Kyansittha's inscriptions were made at a time when the monarchy was recovering from instability and the king needed to present himself as a legitimate and meritorious ruler. They record donations of land and persons to religious foundations, describe the king's titles and deeds, and invoke the merit that would accrue to him and to his supporters.

Paraphrase / Synthesis (not a verbatim excerpt):

The inscriptions present the king with an elaborate royal style, describe him as a protector of the Buddhist religion and as a ruler who governs justly and generously, link his authority to divine and royal precedent in language drawn from Indian tradition, and record gifts to religious foundations together with the curses and blessings customary in such texts.

Analysis: The texts show how Kyansittha built legitimacy through ritual language, religious patronage, and association with sacred kingship. The use of Mon for a major royal text shows how important that language was in the court's public religious expression, and the combination of Mon, Pali, and Sanskritic vocabulary shows the layered political culture of the time.

Limitations: The inscriptions are royal and religious statements, not neutral reports. They do not describe the political crises of the reign, the opposition the king faced, or the experience of ordinary people. They are formulaic in places, and their claims to universal virtue should be read as claims, not as measured descriptions.

Perspective: The perspective of the king and his court. The voices of rivals, provincial leaders who resisted, cultivators, and the Pyu, Burmese, and other communities are not directly represented.

Section 40.17

Royal Succession, Multilingual Inscriptions, and the Myazedi Evidence

The Myazedi inscription, conventionally dated 1113, records a dedication made by a prince named Rajakumar at the Gubyaukgyi temple in Myinkaba, near Pagan. Its text appears in four languages, Pyu, Mon, Burmese, and Pali, in closely parallel versions. It became known to scholars in the late nineteenth century and has been prized ever since for several reasons: it is a dated royal-linked document, it gives information about the royal family and its religious benefactions, and, because the Pyu text could be compared with the others, it provided a key for the modern decipherment of the Pyu language.

For the political history of the period, the inscription is important as evidence for dynastic commemoration. It names Kyansittha as king, identifies Rajakumar as his son, and records the donation of a temple and the dedication of its merit to the king and to other persons. The chronicle tradition treats Rajakumar as the figure around whom the succession question turned, claiming that he gave up or was passed over in the succession in favor of Kyansittha's grandson Alaungsithu. This tradition cannot be confirmed from the inscription, which commemorates a donation and does not narrate a succession. Even so, the text shows that the royal family was a major patron, and that questions of descent and precedence were publicly marked.

The four languages are as significant as the content. Pyu, which was apparently no longer a major political language, appears alongside Mon and Burmese, the languages of royal and elite communication, and Pali, the language of Buddhist learning. The monument therefore preserves in one place the linguistic complexity of Pagan's political world: communities of Pyu, Mon, and Burmese speakers, a literate Buddhist elite, and a court that addressed them all. Whether each language was chosen to reach a specific audience, to honor a heritage, or to demonstrate the range of the donor's connections is a matter of interpretation.

UNESCO inscribed the Myazedi Quadrilingual Stone Inscription on its Memory of the World International Register in 2015, recognizing its documentary significance. The Myazedi has also been used in arguments about the relationship between language and identity. Its presence of Pyu in 1113 shows that Pyu as a written language persisted into the twelfth century, which complicates any simple narrative of Pyu disappearance. At the same time, the text gives no basis for assuming that the four languages corresponded to four separate peoples with distinct loyalties. The inscription is evidence of a multilingual court and elite culture, which is not the same as evidence of a multiethnic society in the modern sense.

Section 40.18

Alaungsithu and the Consolidation of Twelfth-Century Pagan

Alaungsithu, conventionally dated 1112 or 1113 to 1167, reigned for more than half a century, one of the longest reigns attributed to a Pagan king. Chronicle tradition identifies him as a grandson of Kyansittha. The length of his reign allowed a degree of stability that the previous generation had lacked, and it coincided with continuing construction at Pagan, including monuments such as the Shwegugyi and the Thatbyinnyu associated with his time. Inscriptions of the reign record donations and give evidence of a functioning court and a network of officials and donors.

Chronicle tradition credits Alaungsithu with extensive journeys, to the provinces of the kingdom and in some versions to places as distant as the Bay of Bengal coast and the Malay Peninsula. These claims are difficult to verify and some have the character of later embellishment. Even so, royal travel was probably important to the exercise of power. A king who visited districts, received the submission of local leaders, and distributed gifts and honors reinforced personal relationships that formal institutions alone could not sustain, and the movement of the court along the river and its tributaries was a way of binding the heartland together.

Communication networks were an essential part of consolidation. River transport, roads linking Pagan with the irrigated districts, and the movement of officials, messengers, and boats allowed orders and resources to travel, and the growth of written records of donations and land transactions suggests an increasing reliance on documentation. The court's authority was enhanced by its ability to resolve disputes, record transfers, and confirm rights, all of which are reflected in the inscriptions.

The end of the reign, as told in the chronicles, was violent: Alaungsithu is said to have been killed or deposed by his son Narathu, who then ruled briefly and in turn was assassinated. The details are the stuff of later narrative, but the sequence of short reigns that followed the death of Alaungsithu, conventionally dated to the years between 1167 and 1174, suggests that the success of a long reign did not eliminate the difficulties of succession. The monarchy had grown capable, but its dependence on the person and the family of the king remained.

Section 40.19

Narapatisithu and the Maturing Political Order

Narapatisithu, also called Sithu II and conventionally dated 1174 to 1211, came to the throne after the brief reigns that followed Alaungsithu. His reign, which lasted more than three decades, is the best documented of the Pagan monarchy in terms of inscriptions concerning land, donations, officials, and local administration. It saw continued building at Pagan, among the monuments associated with his time the Sulamani and the Dhammayazika, and substantial religious activity, including contacts with Sri Lanka that are discussed in Chapter 41.

The consolidation of governance during the reign can be seen in the records. Inscriptions show a court with a hierarchy of officials, with clearly identified procedures for land donation and sale, and with royal confirmation of rights and obligations. Regional authority was exercised through governors, local lords, and officials, and agricultural resources continued to be drawn from the irrigated districts and elsewhere. These patterns are examined in Division V; their importance here is that the reign shows a monarchy that had become capable of regular administration on a considerable scale.

Succession and institutional continuity were still matters of concern. The throne passed within the royal family, and the court's stability depended on the support of leading officials and commanders. Narapatisithu's reign saw continuity rather than crisis, and the monarchy continued under his successors, but there is no reason to suppose that Pagan had reached a permanent or uniform form. Its authority varied across its territory, its provinces retained their own leaders and arrangements, and it remained vulnerable to external pressure and internal change, issues that Chapters 46 and 59 take up.

The reign of Narapatisithu thus marks the maturity of the political order whose formation this chapter has traced, without marking an endpoint. The kingdom's later history, including its relations with the Yuan Mongols and its eventual fragmentation, belongs to the following chapters.

Section 40.20

Kingship, Royal Titles, Court Institutions, and Political Legitimacy

The king stood at the center of Pagan's political order. Inscriptions present him with elaborate titles that combine Pali, Sanskrit-derived, and local elements, and they describe him as protector of religion, source of justice, and giver of land and honors. These titles were not decorative: they expressed a theory of kingship in which the ruler's authority rested on merit accumulated in this and earlier lives, on descent, and on the support of the religious community. The king was also a military leader, a judge, and the owner or controller of land and people whose service he could claim.

Around the king was a court composed of members of the royal family, ministers and officials, military commanders, royal attendants, Brahmin ritual specialists, and religious figures. Inscriptions mention officials by title and role, and they show a hierarchy in which proximity to the king conferred status. Court ceremonies, including consecration, the reception of tribute and envoys, and religious festivals, publicly displayed the king's authority. Decisions of the court, recorded in the confirmation of donations and the settlement of disputes, translated that authority into practical government.

The relationship between royal ideology and practical government was close but not identical. The language of universal kingship could coexist with limited actual control, and a ruler who claimed to be a world-ruler might in practice depend on the cooperation of regional lords. Historians therefore read royal titles as statements of aspiration and legitimacy, and they test them against evidence of what the court could actually do: collect revenue, enforce judgments, raise troops, and protect donors' rights.

Historical Vocabulary
Dhammaraja (righteous king)

Definition. A Pali term meaning a king who rules in accordance with the Buddhist dhamma, often appearing as part of royal titles.

Historical context. The ideal of the righteous king, found in Buddhist literature and in political thought across South and Southeast Asia, was used by Pagan rulers, including Kyansittha, whose inscriptions incorporate related titles. It coexisted with other models of kingship drawn from Hindu tradition and local belief.

Why it matters. The ideal gave rulers a way to present patronage and justice as the basis of their authority, and it linked the court to a wider Buddhist world.

Potential misunderstanding. The title is an ideal and a claim, not proof that a king ruled justly or that his authority was Buddhist in a narrowly doctrinal sense.

Section 40.21

Provincial Administration, Local Rulers, and Regional Obligations

Pagan did not govern its territory through a uniform system of provinces. The sources suggest a range of arrangements. In the heartland, the court dealt with irrigated districts through officials, local headmen, and holders of grants of land or revenue. Beyond the heartland, it relied on regional lords or governors, some of them members of the royal family, some of them local leaders confirmed in their positions, and in outlying regions on allegiance, tribute, and periodic displays of force. The difference between direct administration, delegated authority, and looser allegiance is fundamental to understanding how the kingdom worked.

Regional elites negotiated their relationship with the court. In return for recognition and protection, they provided men, grain, and gifts, and attended or sent representatives to the capital. They also made their own donations to religious foundations, as inscriptions show, and these donations signaled both their piety and their place in the kingdom's hierarchy. A ruler who wanted to maintain control had to balance coercion and inducement, and local lords who felt neglected or threatened could withdraw cooperation.

Mobilizing provincial resources required mechanisms for recording and collecting obligations. Inscriptions that list the people and land assigned to a foundation, with boundaries and the names of witnesses, indicate the kind of documentation available to local officials. Royal servants were sent out to supervise and to enforce, and the king's own journeys, mentioned in §40.18, served similar ends. The system worked best near the capital and weakened with distance and with the king's attention.

Section 40.22

Land, Agricultural Production, Revenue, and Labor Mobilization

Agricultural expansion at Pagan was a continuing process rather than a single event. Inscriptions of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries record the sale, donation, and boundary-marking of land in considerable detail, which suggests that land had become a valued and regulated resource. Rice was the central crop in the irrigated districts, supplemented by crops suited to drier lands, and the produce was a major source of the wealth of the court, officials, and religious foundations.

Revenue and obligations took several forms. The court drew a share of agricultural production, probably in grain, from land under its control or claim, and it required labor and military service from those who owed it. Land rights were held in different ways: some land was held by cultivators, some by officials and local lords, some by the crown, and increasingly some by religious foundations. The relationships among these categories were complex and contested, and the inscriptions provide fragments of evidence rather than a complete system. The detailed economy of monastic and temple land endowments, which became very large, is treated in Chapter 41.

Labor mobilization underpinned irrigation maintenance, building projects, and military service. The weirs and canals of the irrigated districts required regular repair, and the construction of the capital's monuments, walls, and palaces drew on large numbers of workers, among them craftsmen as well as laborers. The way in which labor was organized, through obligations to the king, through grants of laborers to institutions, and through bondage and slavery, which the inscriptions mention in the context of donations, is a major theme of social history that later chapters revisit.

The relationship between revenue, land rights, and royal resources shaped the monarchy. A king who could confirm and enforce land rights could attract supporters and fund his projects; a king who lost control of the land lost both. At the same time, the growing proportion of land and labor assigned to religious foundations meant that revenue available to the crown could be reduced, a long-term tension that historians have debated. For the period of Pagan's rise, the key point is that the court learned to convert agricultural production into political power.

Section 40.23

River Transport, Markets, Trade, and Communications

The Irrawaddy was Pagan's main artery. Boats carried grain, people, building materials, and goods up and down the river, and the Chindwin and other tributaries extended the reach of the network. Control of river crossings and landing places gave the court a way to monitor movement and to levy dues. The river also connected Pagan with Lower Myanmar and the coast to the south and with the upper valley and the uplands to the north, making it both a political and a commercial route.

Exchange took place among agricultural regions, uplands, and the lower Irrawaddy. Dry-zone producers could exchange surplus grain, palm products, and other goods with upland communities for forest products, metals, and livestock, and with the south for salt, fish, and goods arriving by sea. Markets, which are mentioned in later sources, and fairs connected to religious festivals probably provided places for such exchange. Inscriptions mention goods and payments in kind, and silver and other metals served as media of exchange, but evidence for the scale of trade is limited.

External trade connected Pagan with a wider world. Chinese Song-period records note missions from Pagan, and goods from Yunnan, the Bay of Bengal, and South Asia reached the plain. Ports of the Gulf of Martaban and Arakan offered access to maritime networks, though Pagan itself lay far inland and its direct control over those ports varied. Historians must distinguish between documented trade, such as the diplomatic missions noted in Chinese records, and routes inferred from geography. It is reasonable to suppose that the court benefited from long-distance exchange, but the quantity and the importance of that trade compared with agriculture are not securely established.

Communications were an important element of political integration. The movement of officials, messengers, troops, and religious figures along the river and the roads kept the center in contact with the provinces, and the circulation of texts and ideas followed the same routes. A kingdom whose strength lay in managing relationships over distance depended on such links, and their disruption, by flood, drought, or conflict, could threaten the court's hold on its territory.

Section 40.24

Pagan as a Royal Capital: Urban Space, Construction, and Political Authority

By the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, Pagan had become a capital of remarkable scale. The walled city, the surrounding settlements, and the dispersed monuments across the plain formed an urban landscape in which religious buildings occupied much of the visible space. UNESCO recognizes the surviving landscape as evidence of a powerful regional kingdom at its height from the eleventh to the thirteenth century. The question for the historian is why a capital of this scale developed, and the answer lies in the political system that sustained it.

Brick was the principal material of the surviving monuments, and the demands of construction were extraordinary. Bricks had to be made, fired, and transported; timber was needed for scaffolding and roofing; plaster, stucco, wall paintings, and sculpture required specialist craftsmen. The organization of this work depended on the court's capacity to mobilize labor, to feed workers, and to direct materials, and it relied on the agricultural surplus and the service obligations discussed in §40.22. Donors from the royal family, the elite, and lower ranks built monuments of every size, which created a built landscape reflecting social hierarchy as well as piety.

The relationship between monumental construction and royal authority was close. Large foundations demonstrated the king's merit, wealth, and ability to command resources, and they bound religious communities and donors to the court. They also fixed claims to land and people in durable form. At the same time, the scale of building was not solely a royal project: officials, wealthy individuals, and local communities contributed, and the density of monuments reflects the participation of a broad elite. The temples' religious programs, the monastic communities, and the economics of Buddhist donations are treated in Chapter 41.

Urban organization also involved craft production, administration, and the daily life of the people who lived and worked in and around the city. Archaeology and inscriptions point to specialized workers, officials, soldiers, religious residents, servants, and cultivators on the plain and nearby. A capital of this size required food, water, and order, and it was dependent on the districts that supplied them. The vulnerability of such a concentration, to drought, flood, fire, and conflict, is part of its history.

Everyday Life and Social History

Life on the Pagan Plain

The surviving monuments say little about the people who built and used them. Most inhabitants lived in wooden and bamboo houses that have not survived, and they included cultivators who worked the irrigated fields, laborers and craftsmen who made bricks and built temples, fishermen and boatmen on the river, soldiers and servants of the court, and religious residents of monasteries. Inscriptions mention people given to religious foundations as servants and laborers, evidence that bondage and dependency were part of the social order.

Women appear in the inscriptions as donors, landholders, and members of elite families, which indicates that they could own and dispose of property, though their roles varied with status. The dry climate, seasonal rainfall, and dependence on irrigation shaped the rhythm of work, and the long dry season, the monsoon rains, and the harvest structured daily and religious life. Historians reconstruct these conditions from indirect evidence: land and donation records, archaeology, later descriptions, and comparison with the region, and they must be careful not to assume that elite monuments and royal texts represent the experience of the whole population.

Pagan's Regional Significance and Historical Assessment

Political integration, external relations, and synthesis

Section 40.25

Pyu, Mon, Burmese, and Other Communities within the Pagan Kingdom

The Pagan kingdom contained many communities, and its political order cannot be described as culturally homogeneous. Inscriptions in Pyu, Mon, Burmese, and Pali, together with personal names, titles, and religious practices, show Pyu, Mon, and Burmese speakers living and serving side by side. Beyond them lay the Shan and other hill peoples, Arakanese and other western neighbors, and communities from South Asia, including Brahmin specialists at court and merchants and religious travelers from India. Each group occupied a different place in the political order, and the evidence rarely allows a full account of their differing experiences.

Languages and administrative traditions interacted. Burmese gradually became the principal language of donation records and, by implication, of local administration, while Mon continued as a language of royal and religious expression, and Pali and Sanskritic vocabulary supplied the vocabulary of religion and kingship. Administrative practices, including the writing of land records, the use of titles, and the organization of service, drew on Pyu and Mon precedents as well as on local customs, and it is often difficult to assign a given institution to a single origin. The emergence of a Burmese-centered political order is therefore best understood as the result of a long process of interaction in which Burmese-speaking rulers and communities became politically dominant while others were incorporated.

Regional identities mattered, but they should not be confused with modern ethnic categories. People belonged to localities, families, patron networks, and religious communities, and they could shift their languages and loyalties over time. The kingdom's rulers drew on all of these resources, patronizing different languages and traditions as circumstances required. The ethnic labels familiar from later writing, which present Pyu, Mon, and Burmese as discrete peoples with separate histories, simplify a more fluid situation.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Pyu Political Traditions to Pagan

What Changed

  • The principal centers of power shifted from the Pyu walled cities to the Pagan plain, and political authority became more concentrated in a single royal capital
  • Burmese gradually replaced Pyu as the dominant spoken and written language of the region
  • Royal control over irrigated districts and over the labor and revenue of the heartland became more systematic
  • Religious patronage was organized on a much larger scale, and the built landscape grew far beyond that of the Pyu cities

What Stayed the Same

  • Dependence on the agricultural districts of the Irrawaddy basin and on river communication
  • The use of Buddhism and Indic religious and royal vocabulary in the expression of authority
  • Brick architecture, literacy in Indic-derived scripts, and the walled royal center
  • The presence of Pyu people and Pyu language, which persisted in Pagan into the twelfth century

When and how: Change was gradual and uneven, taking place over several centuries rather than in a single conquest. Elites and rulers experienced the shift most directly, while many cultivators probably experienced greater continuity in the rhythm of agricultural life, even as their obligations and overlords changed.

Why the pattern matters: It shows Pagan as an outgrowth of Irrawaddy traditions, not as a replacement civilization.

Section 40.26

Pagan and the Wider Political World

Pagan was connected to several neighboring worlds. To the east and north lay Shan and other upland communities and, beyond them, the kingdom of Dali in Yunnan, successor to Nanzhao, and the Song empire. To the west lay Arakan and the Bay of Bengal, with eastern India beyond. To the south lay the Mon-associated coast and the maritime routes to the Malay Peninsula and Sri Lanka. To the east, across the mountains, lay the Mon and Tai-associated lands of the Chao Phraya region and the Khmer realm of Angkor, the subject of Chapters 33 to 39. The relationships varied in kind, and the sources document them unevenly.

Diplomatic relations are best documented with China, where the Song records note missions from Pagan. These exchanges were framed in Chinese court terms as tribute, but from Pagan's perspective they probably served commercial and prestige purposes. Relations with eastern India, notably with the Buddhist sites of Bodh Gaya and with Bengal, are indicated by Pagan inscriptions, by evidence of repairs and donations at Bodh Gaya, and by the reception of Indian monks and ideas. Contacts with Sri Lanka are attested in both Burmese and Sinhalese sources: the Sinhalese chronicle Cūḷavaṃsa describes dealings involving rulers associated with Rāmañña, usually understood as a region of Lower Myanmar, although whether every such reference denotes the monarch at Pagan is disputed, and later Sri Lankan Buddhist traditions influenced Pagan, as Chapter 41 explains.

These connections should be distinguished by kind. Diplomatic exchange, commercial contact, religious pilgrimage and textual transmission, and political or military pressure had different mechanisms and different consequences, and it is a mistake to treat them as a single process of influence. Religious contacts, for example, did not imply political subordination, and the presence of Indian ideas at Pagan was the outcome of selection by Pagan's own rulers, monks, and donors. The kingdom was neither isolated nor a mere recipient of outside culture.

For state formation, the significance of these relations was twofold. External recognition and exchange enhanced the prestige of the court and provided it with goods, ideas, and models of kingship, and the need to manage frontiers and neighbors shaped the development of military and diplomatic practices. The wider political world also contained threats: the later pressure of the Mongol-Yuan empire on Pagan, examined in Chapter 46, would show how external events could unsettle a kingdom whose strength rested on internal arrangements.

Section 40.27

The Extent and Limits of Pagan's Political Authority

It is tempting to draw a map of Pagan's territory, usually as a shaded area covering most of present-day Myanmar and sometimes extending beyond it. Such maps appear in many textbooks and in popular writing, but the evidence for them is varied and often indirect. They depend partly on chronicle claims of conquest, partly on royal inscriptions that name distant regions, partly on the distribution of monuments and inscriptions in the Pagan style, and partly on later political geography projected backward. Each kind of evidence supports a different claim.

Territorial occupation implies administration, garrisons, and regular collection of revenue, and the evidence for it is strongest in the heartland of Kyaukse, Minbu, and the middle Irrawaddy. Tributary relationships imply recognition of the king's overlordship and periodic gifts or service, but not direct rule, and they probably characterized many outlying districts. Political influence, shown by religious and cultural connection, the presence of Pagan-style monuments, or alliances, is weaker still and does not demonstrate control. Retrospective imperial geography, the later picture of a vast realm, is a product of later chroniclers and modern historians and should be treated as an interpretation, not as evidence.

The extent of authority also varied over time. The court's reach could expand under strong rulers, contract under weak ones, and fluctuate with the loyalty of regional lords. Lower Myanmar, the Shan hills, Arakan, and the Tenasserim coast were each linked to Pagan in different ways at different periods, and none can be assumed to have been continuously and uniformly controlled. The best summary is that Pagan was a composite polity, with a core of administered territory and a surrounding zone of variable authority.

Section 40.28

The Rise of Pagan in Historical Perspective

The rise of Pagan can be understood as the interaction of several factors. Geography supplied irrigable districts and a river route but did not determine their use. Agricultural production, expanded over generations, provided food and labor. Political competition among local leaders created the conditions in which a single center could dominate. Military organization turned resources into force, though within the limits of supply and distance. Kingship and religious patronage supplied legitimacy and bound communities to the court. Urban development concentrated people, craftsmen, and resources in a capital that became both a symbol and an instrument of royal power. None of these factors was sufficient alone, and each depended on the others.

Nor was Pagan's rise inevitable. The Pyu cities had shown another model; rival centers might have prevailed; a succession dispute or military defeat could have changed the outcome, and the instability after Anawrahta and after Alaungsithu shows how close the monarchy came to losing what it had gained. The achievements of individual kings were real, and Anawrahta, Kyansittha, Alaungsithu, and Narapatisithu each contributed in different ways. But they worked within, and depended upon, the work of cultivators, local leaders, officials, soldiers, monks, and craftsmen, and on institutions that grew over time.

The chapter has also shown how historians' conclusions depend on the evidence they use. The chronicle tradition gives a dramatic story of founding, conquest, and reform, but the contemporary evidence suggests a more gradual and collective process, and it complicates inherited accounts of Mon civilization and Burmese conquest. This does not make the chronicles worthless, since they record how later generations understood their past, but it requires that they be used with care. The Thaton debate is the clearest example, and it reminds students that a famous narrative may not be a securely established fact.

Chapter 41 turns from the political formation of the kingdom to the religious and social world that developed within it: the Buddhist traditions, the monasteries, the architecture of devotion, the economy of donation, and the hierarchies of Pagan society. The political order established in this chapter provided the framework for those developments. The later fortunes of the kingdom, including external pressure and fragmentation, belong to Chapters 46 and 59.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has asked how a regional settlement in the Irrawaddy basin acquired the agricultural resources, military capacity, political institutions, and ideological legitimacy to become one of mainland Southeast Asia's major kingdoms. Its answer is that Pagan arose through the interaction of geography, agriculture, political competition, military organization, kingship, and urban development over several centuries, within a landscape already shaped by the Pyu, Mon, Burmese-speaking, and other communities.

The chapter began by establishing a hierarchy of evidence. Archaeology and contemporary inscriptions take priority over the Burmese chronicles, which were compiled centuries later and which supply the familiar dates and stories, including the foundation in 849 and the conquest of Thaton in 1057. Chronicles are valuable as evidence of later memory but cannot by themselves establish the facts of the ninth to eleventh centuries.

The middle Irrawaddy dry zone is a land of limited rainfall, but its streams and rivers allowed irrigated rice agriculture in districts such as Kyaukse and Minbu, and the Irrawaddy and Chindwin provided communication. These resources made possible a concentration of food and labor, though they did not determine that a single center would dominate. Human organization, not geography alone, explains the outcome.

The Pyu cities of Beikthano, Halin, and Sri Ksetra left a legacy of settlement, agriculture, literacy, religious practice, and walled centers on which Pagan drew. Pyu communities and language persisted into the twelfth century, and the idea of a simple replacement of Pyu by Burmese is not supported. Burmese-speaking communities grew in number and political weight gradually, and linguistic history should not be confused with ethnic or national continuity.

Early Pagan developed from settlement on the plain whose origins archaeology places earlier than the traditional foundation of 849. Local rulers and regional leaders competed for control of land and followers, and a more durable center emerged where a ruling family could convert personal relationships into regular claims on grain and labor. Agricultural expansion in Kyaukse and Minbu was a continuing process led by communities, leaders, and kings, and not the work of a single ruler.

Anawrahta, conventionally dated 1044 to 1077, presided over the principal expansion of royal power: integration of the heartland, campaigns beyond it, religious patronage, and military mobilization. Much of the detail comes from later sources. The traditional conquest of Thaton in 1057 is historically disputed. It is not documented by contemporary evidence, and critics such as Michael Aung-Thwin have argued that it is a later construction, while other scholars hold intermediate positions. The debate shows the limits of the chronicle tradition and the dangers of replacing one unquestioned narrative with another.

The reigns of Sawlu, Kyansittha, Alaungsithu, and Narapatisithu showed both the strength and the fragility of the monarchy. Succession crises revealed its dependence on personal loyalty, while the inscriptions of Kyansittha and the later Myazedi inscription illuminated the construction of legitimacy and the linguistic complexity of the court, in which Pyu, Mon, Burmese, and Pali all appeared. Narapatisithu's reign is the best documented of the mature monarchy.

Government rested on a king whose titles expressed ideals of righteous kingship, a court of officials and commanders, and a variety of provincial arrangements ranging from direct administration to looser allegiance. Land, revenue, and labor obligations turned agricultural production into political power, the Irrawaddy provided a commercial and political artery, and the capital, with its monuments, concentrated resources and displayed authority. Everyday life on the plain, which the monuments conceal, included cultivators, laborers, craftsmen, boatmen, soldiers, and dependents.

Pagan's relations with China, eastern India, Sri Lanka, and neighboring mainland communities enhanced its prestige and shaped its institutions without making it a passive recipient of outside influence. The extent of its authority must be assessed carefully: occupation, tribute, influence, and retrospective imperial geography are different things, and Pagan is best understood as a composite polity with a core of administered territory and a variable periphery.

The rise of Pagan was neither inevitable nor the achievement of one king. It resulted from the work of many people and the accumulation of institutions, and it created the political framework within which the religious and social developments of Chapter 41 took place, and against which the later events of Chapters 46 and 59 must be understood.

Applying a later concept, such as a unified Burmese nation, to an earlier period where it did not apply.

Chapter Reflection

The rise of Pagan matters because it shows how a major mainland kingdom could be built from agricultural resources, river communication, and political organization in a dry and seemingly unpromising environment. It illustrates a pattern found elsewhere in the textbook: the importance of controlling productive land and labor, and of converting that control into legitimacy through religious patronage and royal ceremony. It also shows the role of existing regional traditions, in this case Pyu, Mon, and Burmese-speaking, in shaping a new political center, rather than the arrival of a ready-made civilization.

The chapter's method matters as much as its narrative. The treatment of the Thaton tradition and the early chronology demonstrates how historians test inherited stories against contemporary evidence and how later political and ethnic concerns can shape the telling of the past. Students who understand why a chronicle's precise date or dramatic episode needs scrutiny are better prepared to read every later chapter, where chronicles, official histories, and national narratives recur as sources.

The kingdom that emerged shaped what followed. Its monuments, inscriptions, and institutions left a model of Burmese-centered monarchy that later courts remembered and sometimes invoked, its agricultural heartland remained central to the politics of Upper Myanmar, and its religious and social world, examined in Chapter 41, became a reference point for Buddhist kingship on the mainland. Its fragmentation, discussed in Chapter 59, and the pressures it faced from the Mongol-Yuan empire, in Chapter 46, belong to the same story.

Pagan also contributes to the textbook's larger argument about Southeast Asian agency. Its rulers, monks, and communities drew on ideas from South Asia, Sri Lanka, and China, but they selected, combined, and transformed them within their own political and environmental conditions. Understanding the rise of Pagan means recognizing that a region often described as a crossroads was also a place where powerful and original political orders were made.
