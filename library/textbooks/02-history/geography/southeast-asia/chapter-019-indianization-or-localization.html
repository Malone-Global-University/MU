"Indianization" or Localization?

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

In the mid-fifth century CE, a ruler in western Java named Pūrṇavarman commissioned a series of inscriptions carved into stone. One, associated with the Tugu locality, was written in Sanskrit and commemorated the completion of river works, along with a donation of cattle to Brahmans. Another, from Ciaruteun, compared the ruler's own footprints, carved into the stone itself, with the footprints of the god Viṣṇu. The language was transregional, the religious imagery in one inscription was recognizably South Asian in origin, and the script belonged to a family of writing systems that had developed on the Indian subcontinent. And yet the inscriptions described a specific Southeast Asian ruler, a specific Southeast Asian waterway, and a specific local claim to authority that no South Asian text had ever made on his behalf. Was Pūrṇavarman's Java becoming Indian, or was a Southeast Asian ruler using foreign materials to make a thoroughly local statement?

Chapter 18 traced how commercial and technological networks connecting Southeast Asia to South Asia, China, and the wider Indian Ocean intensified across the last centuries BCE and the early centuries CE, carried by merchants, mobile craftspeople, and Southeast Asian intermediaries whose local knowledge made the entire system function. That chapter also examined early commercial writing on seals from Khao Sam Kaeo and Khao Sek, but it closed by drawing a deliberate boundary: it examined beads, metals, ceramics, ports, and narrowly practical writing, not the sustained religious, literary, and courtly uses of writing, nor religious traditions or royal courts more broadly. This chapter now crosses that boundary directly. It asks what happened when the same networks that carried carnelian and bronze also began carrying religious specialists, sacred texts, prestige languages, political vocabulary, artistic conventions, and courtly ritual.

For much of the twentieth century, historians answered this question with a term that has since become one of the most contested in the entire field: "Indianization." Early users of the term often pictured Southeast Asia as a cultural blank slate, awaiting the arrival of a fully formed Indian civilization that colonists, missionaries, or traders then implanted across the region. Some especially expansive versions of this idea, associated with the "Greater India" concept popular in certain writing of the 1920s and 1930s, imagined literal Indian colonies stretching across the Bay of Bengal; this concept drew on both European colonial-era Orientalist scholarship and, for quite different political purposes, Indian nationalist scholarship associated with the Greater India Society, which used the idea of an expansive ancient Indian civilizational reach to construct a source of pride and historical stature during the era of colonial rule. This chapter explains why that picture, though it correctly identified real and historically significant South Asian influence, badly misdescribed the historical process actually documented by inscriptions, archaeology, and art.

The corrective offered by later generations of scholars, and adopted as this chapter's central framework, does not simply reverse the older error by denying that South Asian influence mattered. Instead, it insists on a more precise historical question: not "how much did India influence Southeast Asia," but "how did Southeast Asian rulers, religious specialists, craftspeople, and communities select, translate, and transform the specific cultural materials that reached them, and for what local purposes?" This reframing, associated above all with the historian O. W. Wolters and his concept of localization, treats the origin of a cultural form and the meaning it acquired in Southeast Asia as two separate historical questions, neither of which can substitute for the other.

This chapter's governing method, applied consistently to Sanskrit, scripts, royal titles, Hindu and Buddhist religious traditions, and artistic forms alike, borrows directly from the interpretive discipline Chapter 18 developed for beads and metal objects. A cultural form resembling a South Asian model does not, by itself, establish that South Asian specialists created it, that Southeast Asian elites understood it identically to their South Asian counterparts, or that its adoption erased what existed before it. Students must instead ask a sequence of careful questions: Was this object, text, or practice imported directly? Was it produced locally using imported materials or technique? Was it produced locally using imported technique but reshaped according to local convention? And what specific local political, religious, or social purpose did it serve once it entered Southeast Asian hands?

A further theme threading through this chapter concerns the limits of the evidence itself. Sanskrit inscriptions, stone temples, and elite sculpture disproportionately document the activities of rulers, donors, and religious specialists, precisely the social groups most likely to leave durable, legible records. This chapter repeatedly asks students to distinguish court culture, visible and well documented, from the far less visible religious and social life of farmers, fishers, and artisans, whose beliefs and practices only rarely surface in the kinds of evidence historians of this period possess.

Finally, this chapter draws its own boundary, just as Chapter 18 did before it. It does not attempt to narrate the political history of Funan, Chenla, Champa, Dvāravatī, Kutai, or Tarumanagara in detail; those specific polities receive full treatment beginning in Chapter 20 and continuing through Part V. This chapter's task is to equip students with the interpretive tools, the vocabulary of Indianization, localization, and convergence, and the habit of distinguishing origin from local meaning, that will make the cultural evidence encountered in those later chapters intelligible rather than misleading.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Define and historically situate the term "Indianization," including its early twentieth-century origins and its association with colonization and "Greater India" theories.
  2. Explain why archaeological evidence for indigenous social complexity before extensive Sanskritic court culture weakened older diffusionist models.
  3. Analyze O. W. Wolters's concept of localization and apply the distinction between a cultural form's origin and its local meaning.
  4. Evaluate the "convergence" model as an alternative to a simple binary between Indianization and localization.
  5. Identify the range of possible agents, merchants, Brahmans, Buddhist monastics, artisans, rulers' emissaries, and traveling Southeast Asians, who may have carried cultural traditions across the Bay of Bengal.
  6. Trace the emergence of Sanskrit as a transregional prestige language within the broader Sanskrit cosmopolis, distinguishing this from earlier, narrower commercial uses of writing examined in Chapter 18.
  7. Explain how Indic-derived scripts were adapted as writing technologies for multiple, often unrelated, spoken languages.
  8. Assess why Southeast Asian rulers' adoption of Sanskrit names, titles, and political vocabulary enhanced legitimacy without implying political subordination to South Asian powers.
  9. Distinguish general early evidence for divine association in kingship from the specific, much later Angkorian devarāja concept.
  10. Describe the selective, uneven adoption of Hindu and Buddhist religious traditions and the continued vitality of local religious practices alongside them.
  11. Evaluate artistic and architectural evidence using the same interpretive categories, imported object, local production with imported technique, and local transformation, developed for material culture in Chapter 18.
  12. Distinguish elite court culture from broader social transformation, and explain why Sanskrit inscriptions cannot be assumed to document the beliefs or practices of entire populations.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. When Sanskrit, Hindu and Buddhist traditions, Indic scripts, political vocabulary, artistic forms, and court rituals appeared in Southeast Asia, were Southeast Asian societies becoming "Indianized," or were they selectively constructing new local cultures from materials circulating through a wider Asian world?
  2. Why did the historiography of this subject move from early colonization and "Greater India" theories toward localization and, more recently, convergence models?
  3. What kinds of evidence allow historians to distinguish an imported religious or artistic form from a locally transformed adaptation of that form?
  4. Who plausibly carried cultural traditions across the Bay of Bengal, and why can historians rarely identify a single exclusive mechanism of transmission?
  5. Why does adopting Sanskrit, Hindu or Buddhist religious institutions, or Indic-derived artistic conventions not imply that a Southeast Asian ruler or community became politically or culturally subordinate to South Asia?
  6. Why must Sanskrit court inscriptions be read as evidence of elite culture rather than as evidence for an entire population's beliefs and practices?
  7. Why is there no single date or single region in which Southeast Asia "became Indianized"?
  8. Why does this chapter caution against treating the Angkorian devarāja concept as representative of early Southeast Asian kingship in general?
  9. How does the distinction between a cultural form's origin and its local meaning help historians avoid both overstating and understating South Asian influence?

Historical Context

This chapter depends directly on the foundation established across Chapters 11 through 18. Those chapters demonstrated substantial indigenous Southeast Asian social complexity, craft specialization, regional maritime exchange, and, by the last centuries BCE, trans-Asian commercial connection, all documented before the clearest archaeological and epigraphic evidence for extensive Sanskritic court culture appears in the historical record. This chronological sequence carries real interpretive weight. If Southeast Asian societies had already developed dense regional networks, specialized craft production, and, at sites such as Khao Sam Kaeo, cosmopolitan ports incorporating South Asian materials and even early commercial writing in Brāhmī script, then any later adoption of Sanskrit, Hindu and Buddhist religious institutions, or Indic-derived political vocabulary must be understood as building upon an already sophisticated and historically dynamic society, not as the founding moment of Southeast Asian civilization itself.

At the same time, this chapter must reckon honestly with a genuine and significant historical development: beginning in the early centuries CE and continuing with increasing visibility over the following centuries, Southeast Asian rulers, religious institutions, and artistic traditions began drawing extensively on Sanskrit language, Hindu and Buddhist religious concepts, Indic-derived writing systems, and South Asian artistic and architectural conventions. This is not a historiographical illusion to be explained away; it is a real transformation requiring careful historical explanation. The question this chapter poses is not whether this transformation happened, but how best to characterize the process by which it happened and the degree of agency exercised by Southeast Asian participants within it.

Twentieth-century scholarship approached this question through several overlapping interpretive frameworks that did not simply succeed one another cleanly. Diffusionist and colonization-oriented models emerged from two distinct, and politically quite different, sources: European colonial-era Orientalist scholarship, which frequently interpreted Southeast Asian evidence through a framework centered on South Asia as an active civilizing source, and, separately, Indian nationalist scholarship of the 1920s and 1930s, particularly associated with the Greater India Society, which used the same broad idea of extensive ancient Indian cultural reach for a very different purpose: constructing an expansive and prestigious ancient Indian civilizational history as a source of confidence and historical legitimacy during an era of colonial subjugation. Both currents, despite their different political motivations, tended to describe the process using language emphasizing India as source and Southeast Asia as recipient. Later scholarship, while still using the term "Indianization," generally abandoned literal colonization narratives in favor of describing the spread of Indian religious, political, and cultural forms through trade, religious mission, and elite emulation. It was with the mid-twentieth-century work of scholars including J. C. van Leur and, later and more influentially, O. W. Wolters, that Southeast Asian initiative and agency moved to the center of the analysis, culminating in the localization framework this chapter treats as its primary interpretive tool, alongside more recent convergence models associated with scholars such as Hermann Kulke. These approaches should be understood as overlapping tendencies rather than a strict linear succession, since scholars within each period disagreed among themselves, and later interpretations also benefited from a considerably expanded archaeological, epigraphic, and art-historical evidentiary record unavailable to earlier scholars.

Section 19.1

From Trade to Cultural Transformation

Chapter 18 established that Southeast Asia's expanding commercial connections to South Asia, China, and the wider Indian Ocean rested on merchants, mobile craftspeople, and local intermediaries whose knowledge and labor made an increasingly complex system of exchange function. That chapter also established, through the evidence of Brāhmī-script seals at Khao Sam Kaeo and Khao Sek, that South Asian writing had already entered Southeast Asian commercial life in a narrow, practical form well before the period this chapter examines.

This chapter asks what happened when the same routes, ports, and human connections that carried beads, metal, and commercial inscriptions began carrying something considerably more culturally consequential: sacred texts and the specialists who could read and interpret them, religious images and the craftspeople who knew how to carve them, political vocabulary that claimed cosmic and dynastic significance, and ritual practices that could reorganize how a court presented its own authority. It is essential to state clearly, and to repeat throughout this chapter, that commercial contact created the conditions and opportunities for this cultural transmission but did not mechanically or automatically cause it. Intensified trade explains why South Asian religious specialists, texts, and artistic conventions became available to Southeast Asian communities; it does not by itself explain why particular rulers, religious communities, or artisans chose to adopt, adapt, or ignore what became available, nor does it explain the specific local forms that resulted.

Section 19.2

What Did Historians Mean by "Indianization"?

Before evaluating the term "Indianization," students must understand what earlier generations of scholars actually meant by it, since the word has carried substantially different meanings at different points in its own history, and since it emerged from more than one intellectual and political source. European colonial-era Orientalist scholarship, observing Sanskrit inscriptions, Hindu and Buddhist monuments, Indic-derived scripts, and Sanskritic royal names across Southeast Asia, frequently interpreted this evidence through a framework centered on South Asia as the active, civilizing source and Southeast Asia as the receiving, and by implication less historically significant, periphery. At the same time, and for quite different political reasons, Indian nationalist scholars of the 1920s and 1930s, particularly those associated with the Greater India Society, embraced and promoted a similarly expansive picture of ancient Indian cultural reach across Southeast Asia, using it to construct a source of civilizational pride and historical legitimacy during a period of colonial rule in South Asia itself. Despite their different motivations, both currents of scholarship used the explicitly expansive phrase "Greater India" to describe the resulting cultural zone, and both tended to describe literal or near-literal colonization, organized settlement, or cultural conquest by South Asian dynasties and communities.

Later twentieth-century scholarship, while continuing to use the term "Indianization," generally abandoned the literal colonization narrative in favor of describing the spread of Indian religious, political, linguistic, and artistic forms through more plausible mechanisms, including trade, religious mission, and voluntary elite emulation by Southeast Asian rulers seeking prestige and legitimacy. This more sophisticated "Indianized states" formulation avoided the crudest colonial and nationalist assumptions of the earliest scholarship, but it could still leave the impression that Southeast Asian history before this cultural transformation had been undocumented, undifferentiated, or historically insignificant, and that Southeast Asian societies were essentially passive vessels waiting to be filled with imported civilization.

Section 19.3

Why the Older Model Became Unsatisfactory

The evidence examined throughout Chapters 11 through 18 of this textbook directly undermines the assumptions embedded in the older Indianization framework. Archaeological research has documented substantial indigenous social complexity, agricultural intensification, metallurgical sophistication, craft specialization, and extensive regional maritime exchange across Southeast Asia well before the clearest evidence for extensive Sanskritic court culture appears in the historical record. The Bronze and Iron Age societies examined in Chapter 16, the regional maritime networks examined in Chapter 17, and the increasingly cosmopolitan trans-Asian commercial world examined in Chapter 18, including the specialized craft production and early commercial writing documented at Khao Sam Kaeo, together establish a Southeast Asia that was demonstrably complex, dynamic, and historically active long before any substantial adoption of Sanskrit, Hindu or Buddhist religious institutions, or Indic-derived political vocabulary.

This chronological sequence carries a direct interpretive consequence. A civilization already capable of organizing specialized craft production, long-distance exchange networks, and increasingly differentiated political communities was in no meaningful sense a blank slate awaiting external civilization. Whatever role South Asian cultural materials later played in Southeast Asian political and religious life, they entered a region with its own deep history, its own existing institutions, and its own capacity for selective judgment about what to adopt, adapt, or decline.

Section 19.4

Localization: Foreign Materials, Local Statements

The concept of localization, developed most influentially by the historian O. W. Wolters, provides this chapter's central interpretive tool and deserves careful, sustained attention. Localization describes a process in which foreign cultural materials, whether a religious concept, a piece of political vocabulary, an artistic convention, or a specific narrative or text, are not simply copied or transplanted intact into a new setting. Instead, local actors select particular elements from a wider field of available foreign material, and then reconstruct, reinterpret, and refit those elements into existing local beliefs, institutions, and political arrangements, producing a result that could not be predicted merely from knowledge of the foreign original.

A single governing formulation captures the concept's significance for this entire chapter: the origin of a cultural form and the meaning it acquired in Southeast Asia are two different historical questions. Establishing that a script derived from a South Asian writing system, that a religious image depicts a deity worshipped in South Asia, or that an inscription uses Sanskrit vocabulary tells historians something true and important about origin. It tells them comparatively little, by itself, about what that script, image, or inscription meant to the Southeast Asian ruler, religious community, or artisan who used it, what specific local purpose it served, or how it related to existing local beliefs and political arrangements. Answering those further questions requires examining the specific Southeast Asian context in which the borrowed material was actually used, not merely tracing its foreign genealogy.

Section 19.5

Beyond a Binary: Indianization, Localization, or Convergence?

Students should resist replacing one oversimplified model with another. The historical question this chapter examines is not adequately framed as a choice between "India mattered a great deal" and "India did not matter much at all." South Asian religious specialists, sacred texts, prestige language, and artistic and architectural traditions genuinely moved across the Bay of Bengal in substantial and historically consequential ways, and recognizing Southeast Asian agency in selecting and transforming these materials does not require minimizing their real presence and influence.

A useful further development in this scholarly conversation is the "convergence" model, associated particularly with the historian Hermann Kulke, which describes South and Southeast Asian political and cultural development during this period as increasingly interacting processes occurring on both sides of the Bay of Bengal simultaneously, rather than a single, already-finished South Asian civilization being exported wholesale to a receiving Southeast Asia. This model helps students understand that South Asian political and religious institutions were themselves still developing and diversifying during the very centuries examined in this chapter, meaning that Southeast Asian rulers and religious communities were not adopting a single, fixed "Indian model" but were instead selecting from an actively evolving, regionally varied South Asian cultural field, and, in turn, contributing to broader patterns of exchange and mutual influence across the Bay of Bengal.

This chapter therefore treats "Indianization," localization, and convergence not as three competing final answers from which students must choose one, but as three stages in an evolving scholarly conversation, each of which corrected genuine limitations in what preceded it while sometimes introducing new limitations of its own. The term "Indianization" retains some usefulness as informal shorthand for the broad field of South Asian cultural influence on Southeast Asia, but it becomes actively misleading when treated as describing a one-way causal process or the transmission of a single, coherent, finished cultural package.

Section 19.6

Who Carried Cultural Traditions?

Chapter 18 established that craft knowledge and technical skill could move across the Bay of Bengal through several possible mechanisms, including mobile specialists, extended residence, and various forms of technological transfer, without requiring mass migration or foreign political control. This chapter applies the same interpretive caution to the movement of cultural and religious material, while resisting the temptation to identify one single, exclusive mechanism.

Plausible carriers of Sanskrit, religious traditions, political concepts, and artistic conventions include merchants and their associated households, who may have brought religious practice and specialists along with commercial goods; Brahmans, whose ritual and Sanskrit textual expertise made them valuable to rulers seeking legitimacy and access to a prestigious transregional idiom; Buddhist monks and, where evidence permits, nuns, whose monastic networks and pilgrimage routes connected South and Southeast Asian religious communities; artisans and craftspeople, whose movement Chapter 18 already documented in a commercial context and who plausibly carried religious and artistic conventions alongside technical skills; scribes and other literate specialists, capable of producing and interpreting Sanskrit and Prakrit texts; emissaries sent by South Asian or Southeast Asian rulers for diplomatic or religious purposes; and Southeast Asians themselves who traveled to South Asia for trade, pilgrimage, or religious and political education and then returned home with new knowledge, texts, and connections.

Historians, including the influential 1970s analyses of I. W. Mabbett, have emphasized that the surviving evidence does not support identifying any single one of these mechanisms as the exclusive or even the predominant channel of transmission. Different cultural materials likely moved through different combinations of these channels, at different times, in different regions, and no comprehensive accounting of the process can responsibly reduce it to one simple explanation.

Section 19.7

Sanskrit: A Prestige Language across Political Boundaries

Sanskrit's emergence as a major medium of political and religious expression across Southeast Asia deserves sustained, careful attention, both because of its historical significance and because it is easily misunderstood. Chapter 18 already introduced students to an earlier and narrower phenomenon: the short, practical Prakrit inscriptions written in Brāhmī script found on seals at Khao Sam Kaeo and Khao Sek, dating to the fourth through second centuries BCE and reflecting the commercial and mercantile use of South Asian writing at an early cosmopolitan port. This chapter examines a later, considerably larger, and functionally different development: the growing use of Sanskrit specifically, rather than Prakrit, for royal, religious, commemorative, and ideological purposes, beginning with some of the earliest securely dated Southeast Asian examples in the fourth and fifth centuries CE.

Historians studying this phenomenon across South and Southeast Asia together have found it useful to speak of a "Sanskrit cosmopolis," a term describing the vast transregional zone, extending across South Asia and much of Southeast Asia from approximately 300 to 1300 CE, within which Sanskrit functioned primarily as a prestigious, learned medium rather than as an everyday vernacular or general lingua franca, shared among trained literate and religious elites across a wide transregional sphere, through which rulers, religious institutions, and literate elites could participate in a shared political and intellectual world. This framework offers students a considerably more accurate picture than a simple narrative of "Indians teaching Southeast Asians Sanskrit." By way of a loose analogy, Sanskrit's role resembled, in certain respects, the role Latin played in much of medieval and early modern Europe: a learned, transregional language of religion, law, and elite culture, used alongside, rather than replacing, numerous local spoken languages, and adopted voluntarily by elites across a wide geographic area precisely because of the prestige and connective power it offered, not because any single political authority imposed it uniformly. Students should treat this comparison as an illustrative analogy rather than an exact functional equivalence, since Sanskrit and Latin operated within different religious, political, and social systems; specialists in this field generally stress that there is little evidence Sanskrit ever functioned as an everyday spoken language or a general commercial lingua franca in Southeast Asia, in contrast to its sustained use for elite political, religious, and literary purposes.

Understanding Sanskrit's role within this cosmopolis helps explain why a Southeast Asian ruler might choose to commission a Sanskrit inscription: doing so allowed that ruler to participate in a prestigious, widely recognized political and religious idiom shared with courts and religious institutions across a vast geographic range, from South Asia through mainland and island Southeast Asia, while the specific content of any given inscription remained entirely free to address purely local persons, places, and concerns.

Section 19.8

Scripts: Borrowing a Technology, Creating New Written Cultures

Students should understand Indic-derived scripts as adaptable writing technologies rather than as permanently "Indian" possessions once adopted elsewhere. A script, understood simply as a system of graphic symbols representing sounds or syllables, can in principle be adapted to represent the sounds of almost any spoken language, regardless of that script's place of origin. Scripts derived from South Asian traditions, including various forms related to Brāhmī, were adapted by Southeast Asian scribes to represent the sounds of Southeast Asian languages, which often differed considerably from Sanskrit, Prakrit, and other South Asian languages in their sound systems, requiring genuine technical innovation rather than simple copying.

The chronological pattern of this adaptation matters for understanding the broader localization argument. In the earliest centuries examined by this chapter, surviving inscriptions using Indic-derived scripts predominantly record Sanskrit or, in some Buddhist contexts, Pali, the two major transregional literary languages associated with the Sanskrit cosmopolis and with Buddhist textual and monastic traditions respectively. Only in later centuries, examined more fully in subsequent chapters, does inscriptional evidence for Southeast Asian vernacular languages, written using these same adapted scripts, become substantially more visible and numerous. This chronological sequence should not be mistaken for evidence that vernacular languages were somehow absent or unimportant during the earlier period; it more likely reflects the specific commemorative, religious, and legitimizing purposes for which formal, durable inscriptions were produced, purposes for which the transregional prestige of Sanskrit and Pali offered particular advantages that a purely local language did not.

This section supports an important analytical distinction that recurs throughout this chapter: script ancestry does not equal linguistic identity, and linguistic identity does not equal political subordination. A Southeast Asian community that adopted a script ultimately derived from South Asian writing traditions, and that used that script to write Sanskrit for certain formal purposes, remained free to speak, and eventually to write, its own distinct language, and its adoption of Sanskrit for particular commemorative or religious purposes implied nothing whatsoever about political allegiance to any South Asian authority, since no unified South Asian political authority existed to which such allegiance could have been owed.

Section 19.9

Royal Names, Titles, and Political Vocabulary

Southeast Asian rulers' adoption of Sanskrit names, titles, genealogical formulas, and concepts of universal or cosmologically sanctioned rulership represents one of the clearest and best-documented forms of selective cultural adoption examined in this chapter. Rulers commissioning Sanskrit inscriptions frequently took Sanskrit royal names or epithets, employed genealogical conventions associated with South Asian political tradition, and invoked concepts of kingship carrying cosmological or religious significance drawn from Hindu or, in other contexts, Buddhist textual traditions.

The corpus of inscriptions associated with the ruler Pūrṇavarman of western Java, dating to approximately the mid-fifth century CE, provides an especially clear and well-documented example, precisely because it includes more than one inscription serving different specific purposes. The Tugu inscription, in Sanskrit, commemorates the completion of river works, describing a waterway project completed within a specified span of days during a particular year of the ruler's reign, and records an accompanying donation of cattle to Brahmans, a conventional South Asian form of pious royal giving. A separate inscription, from Ciaruteun, employs a different convention entirely: it compares Pūrṇavarman's own footprints, carved directly into the stone, with the footprints of the god Viṣṇu, an explicit act of Vaiṣṇava religious association. At the same time, the specific content of these inscriptions, taken together, addresses entirely local concerns: a concrete waterway project within Pūrṇavarman's own polity in western Java, and a specific ruler's specific claim to religious and political authority, neither of which has any counterpart or referent in South Asia itself. Pūrṇavarman thereby used a transregional prestige language and, in one case, a recognizable South Asian religious idiom, to make claims that were, in their substance, entirely about his own specific Southeast Asian polity. That a single ruler could deploy the shared Sanskrit epigraphic repertoire for two such different purposes, hydraulic commemoration and religious self-association, itself illustrates the selective, purpose-driven character of localization this chapter has emphasized throughout.

This pattern illustrates why adopting Sanskrit political vocabulary enhanced, rather than compromised, a ruler's local legitimacy. By participating in a widely recognized and prestigious transregional political idiom, a Southeast Asian ruler could present his authority as connected to a broader, cosmologically significant order, while using that same idiom to make claims that served entirely local political purposes: legitimizing succession, commemorating specific achievements, or asserting authority over specific local populations and territories. Students should resist concluding from this evidence that a single, fixed "Indian model of kingship" existed, which Southeast Asian rulers simply imported and applied; the evidence instead shows considerable variation in how different rulers, in different times and places, selected and combined available political vocabulary to serve their own specific circumstances.

Section 19.10

A Caution about the Devarāja

This chapter pauses here for an explicit methodological warning, because a common error in older, less careful accounts of this subject leaps directly from evidence for Sanskrit royal titles and divine association to the specific concept known as the Angkorian devarāja, a term associated with a Khmer royal cult known chiefly through later Angkorian inscriptional evidence, particularly the eleventh-century Sdok Kak Thom inscription, and examined in full historical and historiographical context in Volume III. Specialists continue to debate the devarāja's precise ritual meaning, its relationship to concepts of royal divinity, and even the reliability of later interpretations built upon the Sdok Kak Thom text; this chapter does not attempt to resolve that specialized debate, which belongs properly to the chapters of Part VII examining Angkorian kingship directly. What this chapter does insist upon is a narrower methodological point: collapsing several centuries of considerable political, religious, and regional variation into a single formula, "Indian influence produced Southeast Asian god-kings," badly misrepresents the historical evidence regardless of how the specific devarāja debate is eventually resolved.

The evidence examined in this chapter supports a more general and considerably more modest conclusion: some Southeast Asian rulers, in various times and places, associated themselves with divine figures or cosmological concepts drawn from Hindu or Buddhist traditions as one among several strategies for enhancing political legitimacy. This general pattern of divine association in kingship should be understood as a broad and varied phenomenon, appearing in different specific forms in different Southeast Asian polities across many centuries, not as evidence for a single, coherent "Indian model of divine kingship" uniformly transplanted across the region. The specific and still-debated Angkorian devarāja concept, when it later receives full examination, should be understood as one particular, historically specific, and thoroughly localized development within a specific Khmer context centuries after the period this chapter primarily examines, not as the natural or inevitable outcome of the more general and much earlier processes described here.

Section 19.11

Hindu Traditions: Śiva, Viṣṇu, Ritual, and Selective Adoption

Students should avoid treating "Hinduism" as a single, standardized religious system that either did or did not spread to Southeast Asia as a complete package. South Asian religious tradition during this period encompassed considerable internal diversity, including Śaiva traditions centered on the worship of Śiva, Vaiṣṇava traditions centered on the worship of Viṣṇu, Brahmanical ritual specialists and their textual traditions, and numerous devotional, philosophical, and regional variations, all of which could and did circulate separately, unevenly, and in different combinations across different parts of Southeast Asia.

Archaeological evidence, including small Hindu religious images recovered from sites in southern Vietnam and elsewhere, documents the circulation of Indic religious imagery through the same commercial and cultural networks examined throughout this chapter and in Chapter 18. The presence of such images at a particular site indicates that Indic religious imagery had reached that location and had been considered worth acquiring, displaying, or using; it does not, by itself, establish the specific religious meaning that imagery held for the Southeast Asian individuals or communities who possessed it, which must be reconstructed, where possible, from additional contextual evidence rather than assumed directly from the image's South Asian iconographic origin.

The evidence further indicates that Southeast Asian rulers and communities could patronize a particular Hindu tradition, such as the Vaiṣṇava association evident in Pūrṇavarman's Ciaruteun inscription, without thereby eliminating other religious practices, whether other Hindu traditions, Buddhist institutions, or indigenous beliefs, from the same society or even the same court. Religious patronage in this period should be understood as an additive and selective process rather than an exclusive conversion replacing everything that preceded it.

Section 19.12

Buddhism and Multiple Religious Networks

Buddhism requires the same careful, undogmatic treatment this chapter has applied to Hindu traditions. Buddhist monks, and, where evidence permits, nuns, together with ritual texts, images, reliquaries, formulas, and the broader institutional networks of monasteries and pilgrimage routes, moved across the Bay of Bengal and through Southeast Asia during the period this chapter examines, carried by some combination of the mechanisms discussed in Section 19.6. Both Pali and Sanskrit served as vehicles for Buddhist textual and ritual traditions, reflecting the existence of multiple, not always closely coordinated, Buddhist textual and institutional lineages within South Asia itself, each of which could establish its own distinct connections with particular Southeast Asian communities.

Archaeological and inscriptional evidence from various parts of mainland Southeast Asia documents the extensive circulation and, crucially, the local adaptation of Buddhist ritual forms, including formulaic devotional inscriptions and ritual objects whose specific local use, placement, and social function often diverged considerably from their use in South Asian contexts. Students should resist the assumption that early Southeast Asian Buddhism straightforwardly corresponded to any single later national Buddhist tradition, whether the Theravada Buddhism that came to dominate much of the mainland in later centuries, examined in Chapter 53, or the Mahayana traditions important in other contexts; the earlier period examined in this chapter involved a more fluid, regionally varied, and still-developing religious landscape, within which multiple Buddhist textual and institutional traditions coexisted and interacted before later processes of consolidation and differentiation, addressed in subsequent chapters, produced the more distinct regional Buddhist traditions familiar from later centuries.

Section 19.13

Local Religions Did Not Disappear

This section addresses a point of central importance to the entire chapter's argument. The adoption of Hindu and Buddhist religious traditions, examined in the two preceding sections, did not require, and generally did not produce, the disappearance of existing local religious beliefs and practices concerning ancestors, territorial and household spirits, sacred landscapes, fertility, and the ritual potency associated with rulers and sacred places. Instead, the evidence, read carefully and without projecting later, more institutionally distinct religious categories backward onto this earlier period, more often supports a pattern of layering, in which imported religious traditions were absorbed into, combined with, or used to reinterpret and enhance existing local religious frameworks, rather than replacing them outright.

Comparative scholarship on processes of religious and cultural borrowing more broadly has emphasized that such borrowing frequently amplifies and reinforces existing local beliefs rather than erasing them, a pattern with direct application to the Southeast Asian evidence examined in this chapter. A local ruler's sacred authority, previously understood through indigenous concepts of ritual potency or connection to ancestral or territorial spirits, could be reinforced and elaborated, rather than discarded, through the additional vocabulary of Hindu or Buddhist cosmological legitimacy. A sacred mountain, river, or other landscape feature already significant within local belief could be reinterpreted, without necessarily losing its earlier significance, through association with Hindu or Buddhist sacred geography. Students should therefore approach the religious history examined in this chapter expecting to find layering, addition, and reinterpretation as the dominant pattern, rather than searching for evidence of wholesale religious replacement, which the evidence, on the whole, does not support.

Section 19.14

Artistic Forms: Copy, Adaptation, or New Creation?

This chapter now applies to artistic and architectural evidence the same interpretive discipline Chapter 18 developed for beads, ceramics, and metal objects. A sculpture, temple design, or ornamental motif resembling a South Asian model does not, by itself, establish that a South Asian artisan produced it, that it was imported as a finished object, or that its Southeast Asian audience understood it identically to a South Asian audience. Students should instead ask a now-familiar sequence of questions: Was this object imported as a finished piece? Was it produced locally by a foreign-trained or foreign-born artisan working within a Southeast Asian context? Was it produced by a Southeast Asian artisan trained in imported conventions? And, most importantly, was it deliberately modified, combined with local motifs, or otherwise transformed into a recognizably distinct regional style?

Scholarship on Southeast Asian art and architecture of this period increasingly emphasizes precisely this pattern of local transformation. Southeast Asian sculptors and architects who worked with Indic-derived iconographic and architectural conventions frequently modified proportions, combined South Asian religious iconography with locally significant motifs or materials, and adapted architectural forms to local building materials, climate, and ritual requirements, producing results that a careful observer can recognize as related to, but genuinely distinct from, their South Asian points of reference. This pattern anticipates, in the realm of art and architecture, the much more elaborate and well-documented regional artistic traditions examined in later chapters covering the classical kingdoms of Volume III, including the temple architecture of Angkor, Java, and Pagan.

Section 19.15

Court Ritual and the Politics of Prestige

Beyond specific religious traditions and artistic conventions, Southeast Asian courts selectively adopted a wider repertoire of practices associated with South Asian courtly and religious culture, including Sanskrit praise poetry composed to celebrate a ruler's achievements and lineage, the employment of Brahmanical ritual specialists to conduct particular ceremonies, genealogical conventions linking a ruling family to prestigious or cosmologically significant ancestry, calendrical and astronomical knowledge associated with South Asian learned traditions, monumental ceremonial architecture and public ritual, and religious donations recorded in Sanskrit inscriptions as acts of royal piety and legitimacy.

These adoptions should not be understood as merely decorative or superficial borrowings undertaken for their own sake. Each provided rulers with genuinely useful new resources for expressing hierarchy, articulating cosmological and religious legitimacy, and asserting participation in a prestigious transregional cultural and political sphere shared with other courts across South and Southeast Asia. A ruler who could commission Sanskrit praise poetry, patronize learned Brahmanical or Buddhist specialists, and construct monumental religious architecture thereby demonstrated access to resources, knowledge, and connections that reinforced his authority among his own subjects and rivals alike, quite apart from whatever specific religious or cosmological content those resources carried.

Section 19.16

What Ordinary People Did, or Did Not, Adopt

This section provides an essential corrective to a chapter that has, of historical necessity, focused heavily on rulers, religious specialists, and elite courtly culture. The surviving evidence for this period, Sanskrit inscriptions, royal temple architecture, and elite religious sculpture, disproportionately documents precisely these elite groups, since they alone possessed the resources, motivation, and specialized personnel required to produce durable, formal, commemorative records. This evidentiary bias creates a serious risk of historical distortion if left unaddressed: a modern reader encountering an impressive Sanskrit royal inscription or an elaborate Hindu temple might reasonably, but incorrectly, conclude that the entire population of the surrounding region had somehow become fluent in Sanskrit, adopted Brahmanical ritual practice, or abandoned indigenous customs.

The evidence does not support such a conclusion, and careful historians explicitly guard against it. Sanskrit literacy was probably concentrated among a relatively small body of specially trained religious, scribal, and courtly specialists, but the surviving evidence does not permit historians to quantify that group securely, and any specific numerical estimate would be unsupported speculation rather than a defensible historical claim. Most of the population engaged in agriculture, fishing, craft production, and local trade, examined more fully in this textbook's ongoing treatment of Southeast Asian social and economic history, continued to live according to local customs, languages, and religious practices that a royal Sanskrit inscription would not directly reflect or describe. This chapter's central historical task, therefore, requires distinguishing court culture, well documented but narrow, from broader social transformation, poorly documented but almost certainly far more limited and far slower than a reader focused only on elite sources might assume.

Section 19.17

Uneven Geography and Chronology

No single date marks the moment when Southeast Asia "became Indianized," and no single description applies uniformly across the entire region. The adoption of Sanskrit, Hindu and Buddhist religious institutions, and Indic-derived artistic and political conventions occurred at markedly different times, with markedly different intensities, and in markedly different specific forms across the Mekong region, central and southern Vietnam, the Thai-Malay Peninsula, the territory of present-day Myanmar, Java, Borneo, Sumatra, and other parts of the region, each of which is examined in its own right in the chapters of Part V and beyond.

Some Southeast Asian communities, during this period and later, made heavy and sustained use of Sanskrit for royal and religious purposes; others relied more heavily on Pali, particularly within Buddhist institutional contexts; and still others maintained local languages and indigenous political and religious institutions largely unaffected by these transregional developments for considerably longer periods. Some communities adopted Hindu religious iconography prominently while maintaining political institutions that owed relatively little to South Asian models; others combined religious and political adoption in different proportions and combinations. This pervasive unevenness across both geography and chronology itself provides strong evidence against any model describing a single, uniform "wave" of cultural diffusion sweeping across the region at one time; it instead supports the more historically accurate picture of numerous separate, locally specific processes of selective adoption, unfolding according to each community's own particular circumstances, interests, and existing institutions.

Section 19.18

How Historians Recognize Localization

Having established the theoretical framework of localization in Section 19.4, this section offers students a practical method for recognizing localization in specific pieces of evidence, a method they should apply throughout their study of the chapters that follow. Historians look for imported vocabulary used in demonstrably novel local meanings, distinct from its use in South Asian sources; foreign artistic forms modified through local iconographic conventions, materials, or proportions; Sanskrit inscriptions whose specific content describes distinctly local political arrangements, place names, or achievements with no South Asian counterpart; Indic religious practices deliberately combined with, or mapped onto, indigenous sacred geography, such as locally significant mountains, rivers, or ancestral sites; local-language inscriptions employing Indic-derived scripts, once such inscriptions become more common in later centuries; and foreign ritual forms reorganized around specifically local dynastic, communal, or territorial concerns rather than reproducing their South Asian ritual context unchanged.

This method should feel familiar, since it directly extends the interpretive discipline Chapter 18 developed for material culture: imported materials or techniques combined with local production, local demand, and local stylistic preference indicate genuine local agency and transformation, not passive reception. Students who apply this method carefully to the evidence encountered in Chapter 20 and beyond, examining the earliest documented Southeast Asian states, will be equipped to recognize localization at work rather than mistaking every Sanskrit inscription or Hindu image for straightforward evidence of Indian cultural dominance.

Section 19.19

From Localization to the Earliest Documented States

This chapter's final task is to point forward toward Chapter 20 and the chapters of Part V beyond it, which examine the earliest well-documented Southeast Asian states in detail. As Southeast Asian rulers began commissioning Sanskrit inscriptions, patronizing Hindu and Buddhist religious institutions, participating in the wider court culture of the Sanskrit cosmopolis, and controlling increasingly important nodes within the trans-Asian commercial networks examined in Chapter 18, historians gain access to entirely new forms of evidence, textual and epigraphic evidence, largely unavailable for the earlier periods examined in Chapters 11 through 18, which relied almost exclusively on archaeological evidence.

This new evidence, however, brings its own serious interpretive challenges, which this chapter's framework directly anticipates. An inscription naming a ruler, or a Chinese dynastic account naming a polity such as "Funan," cannot simply be mapped onto a specific archaeological culture, a precisely bounded territory, or a modern national state. The relationship between textual claims, whether local inscriptions or foreign accounts, and the archaeological record they purport to describe is itself a serious historical and methodological problem, one that Chapter 20 examines in detail through the specific case of Funan, Chinese textual accounts, and the archaeological evidence from Óc Eo in the Mekong Delta. Students entering that chapter should carry forward this chapter's central lessons: distinguish origin from local meaning, distinguish elite textual evidence from broader social reality, and never assume that a name, a title, or a religious symbol straightforwardly and transparently describes the full historical reality of the society that produced it.

Historical Vocabulary
Indianization, Localization, Sanskritization, and Cosmopolis

Indianization is an older historiographical term describing the spread of South Asian religious, political, linguistic, and artistic forms into Southeast Asia. Its earliest twentieth-century usage was often associated with literal colonization narratives and the term "Greater India"; later usage described a more voluntary process of trade-based and elite-emulation-based cultural spread, though it could still understate Southeast Asian agency.

Localization, developed by the historian O. W. Wolters, describes the process by which Southeast Asian communities selected and reconstructed foreign cultural materials to serve local purposes, distinguishing a cultural form's foreign origin from the local meaning it acquired.

Sanskritization, a term more commonly used in South Asian historical and sociological studies, refers to the adoption of Sanskritic religious and cultural practices by particular communities; in the Southeast Asian context examined in this chapter, it should be used cautiously and only to describe genuine adoption of Sanskrit-associated practices, not as a synonym for "Indianization" generally.

Cosmopolis, particularly the "Sanskrit cosmopolis," describes a transregional cultural and political zone unified not by a single state or ethnicity but by shared participation in a prestigious learned language and its associated literary, religious, and political culture, extending across South and much of Southeast Asia from approximately 300 to 1300 CE.

Why these terms matter: Using each term precisely, rather than treating them as interchangeable synonyms, allows students to track the actual historiographical development this chapter describes and to avoid importing the assumptions embedded in earlier, less careful terminology into their own analysis.

Evidence Window

Sanskrit Inscriptions versus Archaeology

What each source shows: Sanskrit inscriptions provide precisely dated, often named, textual evidence for specific rulers, religious dedications, and courtly claims, but they represent only the perspective and priorities of the small elite capable of commissioning them. Archaeological evidence, including settlement patterns, craft production, and everyday material culture, provides a broader if less precisely dated window onto social and economic life, but it rarely names specific individuals or explains the meaning particular objects held for the people who used them.

Why combining them matters: Used together, inscriptional and archaeological evidence can check and qualify one another. An inscription's grand claims about a ruler's cosmological authority can be tested against archaeological evidence for the actual scale of that ruler's settlement, resources, and building projects; conversely, archaeological evidence for continued indigenous craft production and settlement patterns can qualify an inscription's implicit suggestion that a court had become thoroughly and exclusively Sanskritic.

What neither source can fully resolve: Neither inscriptional nor archaeological evidence alone can fully reconstruct the religious beliefs, social experience, or political loyalties of the vast majority of the population who left neither inscriptions nor easily interpretable material remains behind.

Questions for students:

  1. Why might a Sanskrit inscription overstate the extent of a ruler's actual authority or the depth of Sanskritic culture within his polity?
  2. How might archaeological evidence for continued indigenous craft production qualify the impression created by an elaborate Sanskrit inscription?
  3. Why is it important to read inscriptional and archaeological evidence together rather than relying on either type alone?
Inscription Study

Pūrṇavarman's Inscriptions: Tugu and Ciaruteun, Western Java

Creator: Commissioned under the authority of the ruler Pūrṇavarman · Date: approximately the mid-fifth century CE · Type: Two related but distinct Sanskrit royal inscriptions, part of a wider inscriptional corpus associated with this ruler

Context: The Tugu inscription commemorates river works, describing a waterway project completed within a stated number of days during a specific year of Pūrṇavarman's reign, and records a donation of cattle to Brahmans, a conventional form of royal religious giving. The Ciaruteun inscription is a separate text that compares carved impressions of Pūrṇavarman's own footprints with the footprints of the Hindu god Viṣṇu, an explicit act of Vaiṣṇava religious association. Both inscriptions use Sanskrit language and an Indic-derived script and follow certain conventions of royal commemoration recognizable within the broader Sanskrit cosmopolis, but they serve different specific purposes.

Paraphrase / Synthesis (not a verbatim excerpt):

Scholarly descriptions of the Tugu inscription's content indicate that it commemorates local river works, completed within a stated timeframe during Pūrṇavarman's reign, alongside a donation of cattle to Brahmans. Scholarly descriptions of the Ciaruteun inscription indicate that it uses carved footprint impressions and accompanying Sanskrit text to compare the ruler with Viṣṇu. Because these are paraphrases and syntheses of scholarly description rather than verbatim translations, students should consult specialized translations for each inscription's precise wording, and specific technical and textual details of both inscriptions remain subjects of ongoing scholarly discussion.

Analysis: This inscriptional corpus is valuable to students of this chapter because, taken together, its two texts combine, across a single ruler's reign, every major theme this chapter has examined: a transregional prestige language, a South Asian-derived religious idiom used in one text but not the other, and Indic-derived script, all mobilized to commemorate distinct local achievements and claims with no direct South Asian counterpart or referent. The fact that one ruler produced both a purely commemorative, Brahmanically pious inscription and a separate, explicitly Vaiṣṇava religious inscription illustrates the selective and purpose-specific character of localization.

Limitations: These inscriptions document the ruler's own self-presentation and religious patronage; they do not directly document how his subjects understood or experienced either the river-works project or the religious claims made in the Ciaruteun inscription, nor do they establish how widely Sanskrit literacy or Vaiṣṇava religious practice extended beyond the royal court itself.

Questions for students:

  1. Why might a single ruler produce two inscriptions serving such different specific purposes using the same general transregional repertoire?
  2. What does the Tugu inscription's content suggest about the relationship between Sanskrit epigraphy and local governance in early Java?
  3. What can these inscriptions not establish about religious practice or political loyalty beyond the royal court itself?
  4. How do these inscriptions together illustrate Wolters's distinction between a cultural form's origin and its local meaning?
Art-Historical Method Study

Prototype, Adaptation, and Localization

The method: Historians of Southeast Asian art compare a South Asian religious sculpture or architectural form, such as an image of Viṣṇu or Śiva following recognized South Asian iconographic conventions, with a specific Southeast Asian example drawing on the same general iconographic tradition, in order to identify precise points of similarity and precise points of local modification. This is a method of analysis, applicable to the specific dated images and monuments examined in the classical-period chapters beginning with Part V, rather than a single case study in its own right; students should expect to apply it directly to named, dated objects and buildings, such as the Hindu and Buddhist sculpture and temple architecture of Funan, Champa, Dvāravatī, and early Java, once those societies are examined in later chapters.

What typically stays the same: Core iconographic identifiers, such as a deity's characteristic attributes, posture, or associated symbols, often persist across the comparison, allowing the deity or religious concept to remain recognizable to anyone familiar with the wider Hindu or Buddhist iconographic tradition.

What typically changes: Southeast Asian examples frequently show modified body proportions, different materials suited to local availability and craft traditions, the combination of the primary iconographic form with additional locally significant motifs or decorative elements, and adaptations reflecting local ritual practice, climate, or architectural convention.

What the method teaches: Recognizing a shared iconographic tradition alongside specific, deliberate local modification allows students to avoid two opposite errors: mistaking a locally transformed Southeast Asian object for a simple, unmodified copy of a South Asian original, and mistaking genuine, documented iconographic connection for evidence of separate, unrelated invention.

Questions for students:

  1. Why is the persistence of core iconographic identifiers alongside local modification the expected pattern under a localization model, rather than either pure copying or pure invention?
  2. What kinds of local modification would count as the strongest evidence for deliberate artistic transformation, rather than simple technical limitation?
  3. Why might a Southeast Asian ruler or religious community have wanted a recognizable South Asian iconographic form even while modifying its specific details?
  4. When you encounter a specific dated sculpture or temple in a later chapter, what evidence would you need to gather before applying this method responsibly to that object?
Historical Debate

From "Greater India" to Localization: How Should Historians Explain South Asian Cultural Influence?

The four tendencies below are presented in approximate chronological order of their emergence, but they overlapped considerably rather than replacing one another cleanly; scholars within each period disagreed among themselves, and later tendencies also benefited from a substantially expanded archaeological, epigraphic, and art-historical evidentiary record.

The question: How should historians characterize the process by which Sanskrit, Hindu and Buddhist religious traditions, and South Asian-derived political and artistic conventions became established features of early Southeast Asian court culture?

Tendency one — colonization and "Greater India": Both European colonial-era Orientalist scholarship and, for different political reasons, Indian nationalist scholarship of the 1920s and 1930s associated with the Greater India Society described literal Indian settlement, conquest, or cultural colonization, using the term "Greater India" to describe the resulting cultural zone and treating Southeast Asia as an essentially passive recipient of a fully formed foreign civilization, despite the very different purposes each current of scholarship had for advancing this picture.
Tendency two — the "Indianized states" model: Mid-twentieth-century scholarship generally abandoned literal colonization narratives, describing instead the voluntary spread of Indian religious, political, and artistic forms through trade and elite emulation, while still sometimes leaving the impression that Southeast Asian societies were primarily receiving, rather than actively shaping, this cultural material.
Tendency three — localization: Building on earlier challenges from historians such as J. C. van Leur, O. W. Wolters's localization framework placed Southeast Asian selection, reconstruction, and transformation of foreign cultural materials at the center of the analysis, distinguishing a cultural form's origin from its local meaning.
Tendency four — convergence: More recent scholarship, associated with historians including Hermann Kulke, has proposed understanding South and Southeast Asian cultural and political development during this period as increasingly interacting processes unfolding on both sides of the Bay of Bengal, rather than a finished civilization exported from one settled center to a receiving periphery.

Evidence: These tendencies drew on overlapping bodies of inscriptional, archaeological, and art-historical evidence, but interpreted that evidence through different assumptions about historical agency, the coherence of "Indian civilization" as a single exportable package, and the direction of historical causation; later tendencies also benefited from a considerably expanded evidentiary record unavailable to earlier scholars.

Why it matters: This textbook does not conclude that "Indianization is simply wrong" and should never be used; rather, it treats the term as potentially useful informal shorthand for a broad field of South Asian cultural influence, while insisting that it becomes actively misleading whenever it is treated as describing a one-way causal process, a coherent and unified cultural package, or a Southeast Asia lacking historical agency of its own.

Student task: Using the evidence examined throughout this chapter, particularly the Pūrṇavarman inscriptional corpus and the discussion of religious layering in Section 19.13, evaluate which of the four tendencies described above best accounts for the specific evidence discussed, and explain what would be lost by relying exclusively on any single one of these four historiographical tendencies.

Multiple Perspectives

Encountering a New Religious and Political Idiom

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified from inscriptional, archaeological, and comparative evidence. They are not quotations from any specific named historical individual.

From the perspective of a Southeast Asian ruler: Commissioning a Sanskrit inscription and patronizing a recognized religious tradition offers a new and prestigious vocabulary for expressing authority and cosmological legitimacy, useful precisely because it connects a local claim to a much wider and widely respected political and religious world, without requiring the abandonment of existing local sources of legitimacy.
From the perspective of a Brahman or Buddhist religious specialist active in Southeast Asia: Whether resident, visiting, or locally trained, a religious specialist's authority and usefulness to a Southeast Asian court depend on the ability to perform recognized rituals, compose Sanskrit or Pali texts, and interpret cosmological concepts in ways that serve the specific needs of the patron and community, rather than on reproducing South Asian religious practice unchanged.
From the perspective of a Southeast Asian craftsperson working in an imported iconographic tradition: Learning to carve a recognizable Hindu or Buddhist image requires real technical training, but the finished work still reflects locally available materials, locally trained hands, and, often, deliberate incorporation of locally significant motifs alongside the core iconographic elements that make the image recognizable within a broader religious tradition.
From the perspective of a member of a local farming or fishing community: Ancestor veneration, spirit beliefs, and locally significant sacred sites continue to organize daily religious life largely independent of a distant court's Sanskrit inscriptions, though elements of newly prominent religious traditions may gradually become woven into existing local practice over time, without displacing it entirely.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Commercial Connectivity to Cultural Selection

What Changed

  • The scope of South Asian materials moving through Southeast Asian networks expanded from commodities and craft techniques, examined in Chapter 18, to religious traditions, prestige language, political vocabulary, and artistic conventions
  • Southeast Asian rulers began commissioning Sanskrit inscriptions and patronizing Hindu and Buddhist religious institutions in ways that generated new, durable, and precisely dated historical evidence
  • Indic-derived scripts, previously used for narrow commercial purposes at sites such as Khao Sam Kaeo, became vehicles for religious, political, and eventually vernacular literary expression

What Stayed the Same

  • Southeast Asian communities continued to exercise selection and control over what was adopted, how it was adapted, and for what local purposes it was used, exactly as Chapter 18 documented for commercial and technological exchange
  • Local religious beliefs and practices concerning ancestors, spirits, and sacred landscapes persisted alongside newly prominent Hindu and Buddhist traditions rather than disappearing
  • The great majority of the population continued to live according to local languages, customs, and religious practices largely undocumented by elite Sanskrit inscriptions

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents the error of describing this period as a moment when Southeast Asia's own history was superseded by imported civilization, when the evidence instead shows imported cultural materials being absorbed into, and selectively reshaping, patterns of local agency already well established by the commercial world examined in Chapter 18.

Cause and Effect

Why Selective Adoption Occurred

Long-term causes

The trans-Asian commercial networks examined in Chapter 18, including the mobility of merchants, craftspeople, and, plausibly, religious specialists, created a sustained channel through which South Asian religious, linguistic, and artistic materials became available to Southeast Asian communities over an extended period.

Short-term causes

Individual rulers' specific political needs, including the desire to legitimize succession, commemorate particular achievements such as public-works projects, or distinguish themselves from rivals, created immediate incentives to adopt prestigious transregional vocabulary and religious patronage at particular moments.

Effect

Southeast Asian rulers and religious communities selectively adopted Sanskrit, Hindu and Buddhist religious traditions, Indic-derived scripts, and associated artistic and ceremonial conventions, producing genuinely new, locally distinctive combinations rather than uniform replicas of South Asian originals.

This chapter's response

Consistent application of the localization framework throughout, careful distinction between elite court culture and broader social practice, and explicit separation of general early evidence for divine kingship from the specific, much later Angkorian devarāja concept examined in Volume III.

Historical complexity: Selective cultural adoption cannot be reduced to a single cause, such as commercial contact alone or elite political ambition alone; it resulted from the interaction of sustained commercial and human connectivity with specific, varied, and locally determined political and religious motivations across many different Southeast Asian communities.

Everyday Life and Social History

Village Religion beneath the Court

Beneath the Sanskrit inscriptions, Hindu and Buddhist temples, and courtly ceremonies that dominate the surviving evidence for this period lay the daily religious and social life of farming, fishing, and artisan communities, whose experience this chapter can only partially reconstruct. Village life during this period very likely continued to center on agricultural and fishing cycles, household and community rituals directed toward ancestors and local spirits, and locally specific sacred sites, including particular trees, stones, springs, or landscape features believed to hold protective or generative power, largely independent of whatever religious or political developments were unfolding at a distant royal court.

Where evidence permits reconstruction, historians have found reason to believe that elements of newly prominent Hindu or Buddhist religious traditions gradually entered village religious life over time, not through wholesale conversion but through a slow, uneven process of incorporation, in which a new deity, ritual formula, or religious specialist might be added to an existing repertoire of local practice, valued for the additional ritual or cosmological resources it offered rather than adopted as a replacement for what already existed. This pattern, difficult to document in specific detail for the earliest centuries examined in this chapter, becomes increasingly visible in the better-documented societies examined in later chapters, and it should inform how students read even the most impressive courtly religious evidence: as a partial, elite-centered window onto a far broader and more religiously layered society.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the commercial and technological networks traced in Chapter 18 became channels for a considerably more culturally consequential form of exchange: the movement of Sanskrit, Hindu and Buddhist religious traditions, Indic-derived scripts, political vocabulary, artistic conventions, and courtly ritual between South Asia and Southeast Asia during the early centuries CE. The chapter's central argument holds that South Asian cultural influence was real and historically significant, but that Southeast Asian societies did not simply receive a ready-made foreign civilization; rulers, religious specialists, craftspeople, and communities selected, combined, translated, and transformed imported cultural materials according to their own local interests and existing traditions.

The chapter traced the historiography of this subject through four major, historically overlapping tendencies rather than a strict linear succession: colonization and "Greater India" narratives, advanced by both European colonial-era Orientalist scholarship and, for different political purposes, Indian nationalist scholarship of the 1920s and 1930s, which imagined literal Indian settlement, conquest, or cultural colonization; the mid-twentieth-century "Indianized states" model, which abandoned colonization narratives but still emphasized Southeast Asia as a receiving periphery; O. W. Wolters's localization framework, which placed Southeast Asian selection and transformation at the center of the analysis by distinguishing a cultural form's origin from its local meaning; and more recent convergence models, associated with historians including Hermann Kulke, which describe South and Southeast Asian cultural and political development as increasingly interacting processes rather than a one-way transmission from a finished civilization to a passive recipient. These tendencies overlapped considerably, and later scholarship benefited from a substantially expanded archaeological, epigraphic, and art-historical evidentiary record unavailable to earlier scholars.

The chapter examined Sanskrit's emergence as a transregional prestige language within the broader Sanskrit cosmopolis, distinguishing this later, more elaborate religious and courtly usage from the narrower, earlier commercial use of Brāhmī-script Prakrit already documented at Khao Sam Kaeo and Khao Sek in Chapter 18. It examined Indic-derived scripts as adaptable writing technologies, capable of representing Southeast Asian vernacular languages as well as Sanskrit and Pali, and it established the important distinction that script ancestry does not equal linguistic identity, and linguistic identity does not equal political subordination.

Using the mid-fifth-century inscriptional corpus of Pūrṇavarman of western Java, including the Tugu inscription commemorating river works and a Brahmanical donation and the separate Ciaruteun inscription comparing the ruler's footprints with those of Viṣṇu, as a central case study, the chapter demonstrated how a single Southeast Asian ruler combined transregional Sanskrit language and, in one text, South Asian religious imagery with entirely local political content, commemorating specific local achievements and claims that had no South Asian counterpart. The chapter explicitly cautioned against collapsing this general pattern of divine association in kingship into the specific, much later Angkorian devarāja concept, which belongs to a distinct historical and regional context examined in Volume III.

Turning to religion, the chapter treated both Hindu traditions, including Śaiva and Vaiṣṇava devotion, and Buddhist traditions, carried through monastic and textual networks using both Pali and Sanskrit, as internally diverse and selectively adopted rather than uniform, exclusive religious packages. It emphasized that local religious beliefs concerning ancestors, spirits, and sacred landscapes persisted alongside these traditions, most often through processes of layering and reinterpretation rather than replacement.

The chapter applied the same interpretive method developed in Chapter 18 for material culture to artistic and architectural evidence, asking whether a given object was imported, locally made using imported technique, or deliberately transformed into a new regional style, and it examined court ritual and the politics of prestige as a genuine source of new political resources for Southeast Asian rulers rather than merely decorative borrowing.

Throughout, the chapter insisted on distinguishing elite court culture, well documented through Sanskrit inscriptions and monumental religious architecture, from the far less visible religious and social life of the broader population, cautioning students against assuming that impressive elite evidence documents the beliefs or practices of an entire society. It further established that this cultural transformation occurred unevenly across both geography and chronology, with no single date or single description applicable across the whole of Southeast Asia, a pattern that itself argues against any model describing a uniform wave of cultural diffusion.

The chapter closed by equipping students with a practical method for recognizing localization in the evidence they will encounter in subsequent chapters, and by pointing forward to Chapter 20's examination of the earliest documented Southeast Asian states, cautioning that an inscription naming a ruler or a foreign account naming a polity cannot simply be mapped onto a specific archaeological culture or modern territorial state, a problem Chapter 20 examines directly through the case of Funan and Óc Eo in the Mekong Delta.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Indianization
An older historiographical term describing the spread of South Asian religious, political, linguistic, and artistic forms into Southeast Asia, ranging in usage from literal colonization narratives to more voluntary models of elite emulation.
Localization
The concept, developed by O. W. Wolters, describing how Southeast Asian communities selected and reconstructed foreign cultural materials to serve local purposes, distinguishing a cultural form's origin from its local meaning.
Convergence
A model, associated with Hermann Kulke, describing South and Southeast Asian cultural and political development as increasingly interacting processes occurring on both sides of the Bay of Bengal, rather than a one-way transmission.
Sanskrit cosmopolis
The transregional cultural and political zone, extending across South and much of Southeast Asia from approximately 300 to 1300 CE, unified by shared participation in Sanskrit as a prestigious learned language.
Greater India
A term, advanced in the 1920s and 1930s by both European colonial-era Orientalist scholarship and Indian nationalist scholarship for different political purposes, imagining Southeast Asia as a zone of extensive Indian cultural or colonial reach; now rejected by mainstream scholarship as a mischaracterization of the historical process.
Prakrit
A group of Middle Indo-Aryan languages, distinct from classical Sanskrit, documented in the early commercial seal inscriptions at Khao Sam Kaeo and Khao Sek examined in Chapter 18.
Brāhmī script
An ancient South Asian writing system and the ancestor of numerous later scripts, including those later adapted to write Southeast Asian languages.
Epigraphy
The study of inscriptions, a central category of evidence for this chapter and for the classical Southeast Asian polities examined in Volumes II and III.
Vaiṣṇava
Relating to the worship of the Hindu god Viṣṇu, a tradition associated with the ruler Pūrṇavarman's inscriptions examined in this chapter.
Śaiva
Relating to the worship of the Hindu god Śiva, one of several distinct Hindu traditions that circulated separately and unevenly across Southeast Asia.
Devarāja
A term associated with a Khmer royal cult known chiefly through later Angkorian inscriptional evidence, especially the eleventh-century Sdok Kak Thom inscription, examined fully in Volume III; its precise ritual meaning and relationship to ideas of royal divinity remain debated among specialists, and this chapter cautions against treating it as representative of early Southeast Asian kingship in general.
Cultural selection
The process by which a community chooses which available foreign cultural materials to adopt, adapt, or decline, based on local interests and existing institutions.
Court culture
The religious, artistic, linguistic, and ceremonial practices associated with a royal court, disproportionately documented in surviving elite evidence such as Sanskrit inscriptions.
Prestige language
A language valued and used for its social and political prestige rather than as a population's native spoken language, exemplified by Sanskrit's role within the Sanskrit cosmopolis.
Iconography
The conventional visual symbols and forms used to represent a deity, concept, or narrative in art, examined in this chapter through the comparison of South Asian prototypes and Southeast Asian adaptations.
Sanskritization
A term, more commonly applied within South Asian historical and sociological studies, describing the adoption of Sanskritic religious and cultural practices; used cautiously in the Southeast Asian context to avoid conflation with "Indianization" generally.
Diffusionism
An older historiographical assumption holding that cultural traits spread outward from a small number of advanced centers to passively receiving peripheries, underlying much early Indianization scholarship.
Elite emulation
The process by which rulers and elites voluntarily adopt prestigious foreign cultural forms to enhance their own status and legitimacy.
Religious layering
The historical pattern in which imported religious traditions are combined with, or added onto, existing local religious beliefs and practices rather than replacing them outright.
Vernacular inscription
An inscription written in a local Southeast Asian language, using an Indic-derived script, becoming substantially more common in centuries following the period primarily examined in this chapter.
Political theology
A system of religious ideas used to explain and legitimize political authority, exemplified in a general early form in this chapter and in a specific, later Angkorian form in Volume III.
Sacred landscape
Specific local geographic features, such as mountains, rivers, or stones, understood within indigenous belief systems as possessing protective, ancestral, or generative significance, often layered together with imported religious traditions.
Pūrṇavarman
The mid-fifth-century ruler of a polity in western Java whose inscriptional corpus, including the Tugu inscription commemorating river works and the separate Ciaruteun inscription comparing his footprints with Viṣṇu's, anchors this chapter's central case study.
O. W. Wolters
The historian whose localization framework provides this chapter's central interpretive tool.
Hermann Kulke
The historian associated with the convergence model of South and Southeast Asian cultural and political interaction.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it equips students with the single most important interpretive tool they will need for understanding the classical Southeast Asian polities examined throughout the remainder of this textbook: the ability to distinguish a cultural form's foreign origin from the local meaning it acquired. Without this distinction, a reader encountering Sanskrit inscriptions, Hindu temples, or Buddhist monuments at Funan, Angkor, Pagan, Srivijaya, or Majapahit might easily mistake genuine local political and religious innovation for simple foreign imitation, missing the historical agency and creativity that this chapter has worked to make visible.

The larger process this chapter reveals, selective cultural borrowing reshaped according to local interests rather than passive reception of a foreign package, previews a pattern this textbook will encounter repeatedly in later centuries, including the uneven spread of Islam across the archipelago examined in Chapters 62 through 66, and the selective adaptation of European commercial, religious, and administrative institutions during the early modern and colonial periods examined in later volumes. Understanding this chapter's framework carefully therefore equips students with an interpretive habit of mind applicable across the entire remaining scope of this textbook, not merely to the specific centuries examined here.

This chapter also connects directly backward to Chapter 18's argument about Southeast Asian agency in commercial and technological exchange, extending that same argument into the more culturally freighted domains of religion, language, and political ideology, and it connects forward to Chapter 20's examination of the earliest documented Southeast Asian states, whose Sanskrit inscriptions and Chinese textual accounts must be read with exactly the interpretive caution this chapter has modeled.

Finally, this chapter's deliberate restraint, its refusal to narrate the specific political history of Funan, Champa, or other early states, and its explicit caution against reading the later Angkorian devarāja concept backward into this earlier period, models an important historical discipline: recognizing the proper boundaries of one's own chapter and trusting the larger structure of the textbook to address related questions in their appropriate place. Chapter 20 now takes up directly the specific textual and archaeological problem of Funan and the Mekong Delta, building on both the commercial foundation established in Chapter 18 and the interpretive framework established here.
