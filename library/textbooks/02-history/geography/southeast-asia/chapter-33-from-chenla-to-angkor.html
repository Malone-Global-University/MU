From Chenla to Angkor

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Angkorian kingship was not born in a single day, in a single place, or in an empty landscape. It emerged from a long reorganization of Khmer-speaking regions in which older lineages, temple foundations, river corridors, and religious networks were reworked, and its founders were remembered, two centuries later, in a story that explains the past as much as it records it.

Two very different kinds of evidence stand at the entrance to the history of Angkor. The first is a stone stele of 1052 CE, set up at a temple site now in eastern Thailand, known to scholars as the inscription of Sdok Kak Thom and catalogued as K.235. Written in Sanskrit and Old Khmer, it traces the descent of a family of royal priests across more than two centuries and tells how an early ninth-century king, Jayavarman II, came to rule, how a learned Brahmin performed a rite for him, and how the land of the Khmers was made independent of a place called Javā. The second kind of evidence is not a text at all. It lies beneath the forest canopy of the Phnom Kulen plateau, north-east of the later city of Angkor, where airborne laser scanning has revealed a planned landscape of temple foundations, roads, embankments, and ponds, dating to the period when the Angkorian tradition was taking shape.

One of these sources preserves a claim about the past, composed by a family with its own interests in remembering it. The other preserves a physical record of what people actually built. Where the two meet, they can strengthen one another. Where they diverge, they warn the historian that a foundation narrative is not the same as a foundation event. This chapter is organized around that tension. It asks how the politically diverse Khmer-speaking regions of the seventh and eighth centuries developed into the royal order associated with Jayavarman II, and how much of the traditional story of that development can actually be established.

The chapter belongs at the start of Volume III because Angkor is so often presented as a beginning, the sudden arrival of an empire. Older accounts frequently described a succession of kingdoms, Funan giving way to Chenla and Chenla to Angkor, each replacing its predecessor in an orderly sequence. Chapters 20 and 21 have already shown why this scheme requires reassessment. The societies of the lower Mekong, the Tonle Sap basin, and the middle Mekong were never a single kingdom that changed its name. They were networks of rulers, families, temples, and communities, with centers that rose and fell and with relationships that shifted over generations. This chapter takes that complicated inheritance as its starting point and concentrates on the late seventh through early ninth centuries.

The chapter does not retell the general history of Chenla, which belongs to Chapter 21, and it does not survey the institutions of the mature Angkorian state, which are examined in Chapters 34 through 39. Its task is narrower and, in some ways, harder: to explain a transition. That requires reading several kinds of evidence against one another, including contemporary Sanskrit and Old Khmer inscriptions, Chinese dynastic histories, the architecture and settlement archaeology of the pre-Angkorian and early Angkorian periods, and much later royal and priestly accounts. It also requires restraint. Jayavarman II is the central figure of the chapter, yet his biography, his campaigns, and even his precise itinerary are far less secure than popular summaries suggest.

Restraint, however, does not produce an inconclusive chapter. The more carefully the evidence is examined, the stronger the underlying historical argument becomes. Between roughly the late seventh and early ninth centuries, Khmer political life was reorganized around new relationships among rulers, regional elites, religious specialists, and the productive landscapes of the Mekong and the Tonle Sap. The founding tradition recorded in 1052 is one of the ways later Khmer courts understood and used that reorganization. Archaeology, meanwhile, shows an early royal landscape at Phnom Kulen that no one tradition fully explains.

The chapter also resists a tempting error: treating Southeast Asian societies as passive recipients of Indian religious ideas, or of dominating neighbors. Sanskrit titles, Śaiva ritual, and the language of universal kingship were adopted and recombined by Khmer rulers and priests for their own purposes, in the way Chapter 19 has already discussed. Jayavarman II and his contemporaries were not receiving a finished model of kingship. They were building one, in a landscape already crowded with temples, lineages, and claims.

Finally, the chapter prepares the ground for what follows. Chapter 34 turns to the institutions through which Angkorian kings governed, Chapter 35 to the city's hydraulic landscape, and Chapter 36 to its temples and religious life. To understand those later developments, students first need to understand what existed before them, and why the early ninth century came to be remembered as the moment when something new began.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the beginning of an Angkorian royal tradition must be distinguished from the creation of the monumental city and empire familiar from later centuries.
  2. Describe the political, religious, and geographic inheritance that the late seventh- and eighth-century Khmer-speaking regions received from the pre-Angkorian period.
  3. Assess why the Chinese terms "Land Chenla" and "Water Chenla" cannot be read as two territorially bounded successor states.
  4. Distinguish among contemporary inscriptions, Chinese records, archaeological evidence, and much later dynastic tradition in reconstructing early Angkor.
  5. Trace the evidence for succession, regional authority, and competing centers of power from the reign of Jayavarman I through the late eighth century.
  6. Analyze the ways in which agriculture, river networks, skilled labor, kinship, and temple endowment made political consolidation possible.
  7. Evaluate what is and is not known about Jayavarman II, including his possible identification with rulers named in inscriptions of 770 and 781.
  8. Interpret the Sdok Kak Thom inscription as a retrospective source shaped by the interests of a royal-priestly family.
  9. Compare competing interpretations of Javā and of the relationship between the Khmer regions and maritime Southeast Asia.
  10. Evaluate the 802 tradition, the concept of cakravartin, and the disputed meaning of devarāja without treating the later account as a single documented founding event.
  11. Connect the archaeology of Phnom Kulen and Roluos to the written tradition of Mahendraparvata and Hariharalaya.
  12. Explain what changed and what continued in Khmer political organization around the early ninth century.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

Historical Context

The lands in which Angkor eventually arose are defined by water. The Mekong, flowing south through the region, meets the Tonle Sap system in a landscape where the seasonal reversal of the Tonle Sap river floods a great lake each year, enriching fisheries and the surrounding plains. North of the lake lie the lower slopes of the Kulen hills, whose streams drain toward the plains. East and southeast are the lower Mekong and its delta, the region examined in Chapter 20 in connection with Funan and Oc Eo. This varied environment supported rice agriculture, fishing, and river travel, and linked the interior to the sea. It was not a single political space. The settlements of the lower Mekong, the Stung Sen valley, the Tonle Sap shores, and the middle Mekong near Vat Phou each developed their own ties and rivalries.

By the seventh century, Khmer-language inscriptions and Sanskrit inscriptions record rulers whose titles and temple foundations show familiarity with Indian religious and political vocabulary. Chinese sources of the same period describe a polity they call Zhenla, a name conventionally rendered Chenla in English. As Chapter 21 explains, the relationship between the Chinese "Zhenla" and the rulers named in the surviving inscriptions is not straightforward, and modern scholarship has become cautious about treating Chenla as one unified kingdom. Powerful rulers such as Isanavarman I, associated with the great site of Sambor Prei Kuk, and Jayavarman I, whose inscriptions are widely distributed, demonstrate that royal authority could extend across considerable distances. They do not demonstrate that a stable, centralized territorial state outlived them.

The century or so after Jayavarman I is therefore a period of reorganization rather than simple decline or simple succession. Chinese accounts describe a division. Inscriptions record queens, local lords, and temple founders. Southeast Asia more broadly was also changing: maritime trade networks, treated in Chapters 28 through 32, were being reshaped by Srivijaya and by the Central Javanese courts, and Cham polities on the Vietnamese coast, treated in Chapter 22, were active neighbors. It is against this regional background that the Khmer-speaking political world was transformed, and that Jayavarman II came to be remembered as the founder of a new order. Historians must resist two opposite errors here. The first is to treat the eighth century as an empty interval between Chenla and Angkor. The second is to treat the early ninth century as a decisive, single moment of creation. This chapter adopts neither.

Division I — The Historical Problem: From Chenla to Angkor

Section 33.1

The Problem of Angkor's Origins

"Angkor" is used in several different senses, and much confusion about its origins comes from sliding between them. It can refer to a place, the area north of the Tonle Sap in present-day Siem Reap province where the great temple complexes stand. It can refer to a period, conventionally dated from the early ninth century to the fifteenth. It can refer to a political order, a tradition of kingship exercised from a series of royal centers. And it can refer to the monumental city of Angkor Thom and the temple of Angkor Wat, which were built centuries after the events this chapter describes. A foundation story that explains one of these meanings does not automatically explain the others.

The central distinction of this chapter is therefore between the beginning of an Angkorian political tradition and the creation of the city. When later Khmer inscriptions looked back to Jayavarman II, they were describing the origin of a line of kings and a form of royal legitimacy, not the construction of Angkor in its famous form. The first royal centers associated with the tradition lay elsewhere, on the Kulen plateau and at Roluos. The great concentration of building around Yasodharapura began only near the end of the ninth century, and the temples most often associated with the name Angkor date to the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. These later developments are the subject of Chapters 35 through 38.

A second point follows. A founding date does not establish the birth of a unified empire. The traditional date of 802 refers, in later inscriptions, to the accession and consecration of Jayavarman II, which the Sdok Kak Thom account associates with a royal rite. Whether that rite coincided with the political unification of a large territory, with a limited local consolidation, or with something that later tradition simply projected back onto an earlier king is exactly what the chapter must examine. Historians generally agree that the late eighth and early ninth centuries saw a significant reorganization of Khmer political life. They disagree, sometimes sharply, about its scale, its causes, and its relationship to the traditional narrative.

Finally, "origins" must not be understood as emergence from nothing. Brick temples, Sanskrit and Old Khmer inscriptions, royal lineages, and organized landscapes existed in the seventh century and earlier. The question is not how a civilization appeared, but how an existing world of competing centers was reconfigured. Treating Angkor as the culmination of an inevitable process would be equally misleading, since the outcomes of the eighth and ninth centuries depended on contingent political choices, marriages, alliances, and military outcomes that we can only partly reconstruct.

Section 33.2

The Political World Inherited from Chenla

Chapter 21 examined the political formations that followed Funan, and only the elements most relevant here need to be recalled. In the seventh century, rulers appear in Khmer and Sanskrit inscriptions across a wide arc, from the middle Mekong around Vat Phou, through the Stung Sen valley and the Tonle Sap region, to the lower Mekong and the area near the Bassac and the coast. Some of these rulers claimed descent from earlier royal houses, some bore titles that implied sovereignty over other lords, and several founded or endowed temples dedicated to Śiva, Viṣṇu, and the combined form of Harihara. The Old Khmer language, used in inscriptions from at least the early seventh century (a dated example of 611 CE from Angkor Borei is widely cited as among the earliest), reveals that local Khmer speech was a language of record and administration, not merely of the countryside.

What persisted into the eighth century was a way of organizing authority more than a territorial state. Rulers drew on kinship networks and on alliances with local lords, called in the inscriptions by titles such as kamraten. They endowed temples with land, cattle, and dependent personnel and recorded these gifts on stone. They relied on Brahmins and other religious specialists, some descended from families that had served earlier rulers. And they depended on productive agricultural land and on river transport to supply their followers and ritual establishments. These practices did not disappear when a particular royal line failed. They provided the framework within which later rulers, including those of the ninth century, operated.

The seventh-century achievements of Isanavarman I and Jayavarman I show both the reach and the limits of that framework. Isanavarman's center at Isanapura, where inscriptions and extensive brick temple groups survive, was evidently a major seat of royal and religious activity. Jayavarman I's inscriptions are found at many locations, which suggests recognition of his authority over a considerable area. Yet neither ruler's reign proves that a single, lasting bureaucratic state existed. After Jayavarman I, as the next sections will show, the surviving evidence points to fragmentation and contested succession rather than a smooth institutional continuity.

The chapter therefore begins its detailed narrative with the late seventh century, not with Funan or with the early Chenla rulers. The aim is to ask what the post-Jayavarman I world made possible, and how ninth-century rulers drew on its resources.

Section 33.3

Land Chenla, Water Chenla, and the Problem of Political Unity

The best-known description of eighth-century political change comes not from Khmer sources but from Chinese dynastic histories, especially the New Tang History (Xin Tang shu), compiled in the eleventh century from earlier Tang records. These texts report that the country of Zhenla, which had earlier sent missions to the Chinese court, divided in the early eighth century into two parts. One, called "Land Zhenla" or sometimes by a name rendered Wendan or Polou, lay in hilly country. The other, "Water Zhenla," lay toward the sea and was described as a land of lakes and marshes. Both sent embassies to the Tang court at various points in the eighth century, and the histories give them separate rulers and separate reputations.

This passage has often been read as a straightforward map of two successor states, a northern upland kingdom and a southern lowland one, from whose later reunion Angkor emerged. That reading is attractive because it seems to explain what Jayavarman II later accomplished. But it rests on several assumptions that the evidence does not support. The Chinese descriptions are general and brief, and they reflect the information that envoys and merchants brought to the Tang court, filtered through Chinese categories. They do not provide boundaries, capitals that can be securely matched with known sites, or a precise chronology. Nor do the Khmer and Sanskrit inscriptions of the eighth century name two kingdoms called Land and Water Chenla.

A more cautious interpretation treats the Chinese division as evidence that outside observers perceived a break in the political unity they had earlier associated with Zhenla, and that eighth-century rulers in different regions operated with substantial autonomy. The terms may refer to broad ecological and political orientations, one toward the middle Mekong uplands and the other toward the lower Mekong, the delta, and the maritime world, rather than to formal states. This accords better with what the inscriptions show: multiple local rulers, temple founders, and lords whose relationships to one another shifted over time. The surviving record supports political plurality more clearly than it supports either a united Chenla or two cleanly defined successor kingdoms.

Historical Vocabulary
Zhenla (Chenla) and Kambuja

Zhenla, rendered "Chenla" in older English usage, is a Chinese name for a polity or group of polities that sent embassies to the Chinese court from the early seventh century. It was used by Chinese writers, not by the rulers or inhabitants of the region themselves, and the Khmer and Sanskrit inscriptions do not use it. Modern historians have used it as a convenient label for the pre-Angkorian period, but its precise referent in Chinese texts, and its relationship to the political entities named in the inscriptions, remain debated.

Kambuja is a Sanskrit-derived name found in Khmer inscriptions, associated in later tradition with a legendary union of a sage, Kambu, and a celestial woman, Merā. From it derive the later European forms "Cambodge" and "Cambodia." In inscriptions it denotes the land or the people over whom Khmer rulers claimed authority, and its use by Angkorian rulers is part of how they represented their own kingdom.

Why it matters: The two terms come from different languages, different observers, and different purposes. Treating them as the names of one continuous kingdom, which gradually became Angkor, is the error this chapter seeks to avoid.

Section 33.4

How Historians Reconstruct Early Angkor

The evidence for the period examined in this chapter is of four broad kinds, each with distinctive strengths and weaknesses. The first is inscriptions in Sanskrit and Old Khmer carved on stone stelae and temple doorposts. Those dated to the seventh and eighth centuries are contemporary evidence. They record temple foundations, donations of land and personnel, royal titles, genealogies, and dedications to deities. They were commissioned by rulers, officials, and wealthy donors, and they therefore tell us mostly about elite religious patronage. They seldom describe battles, administration, or the lives of ordinary people, and they survive unevenly, since many have been lost or damaged.

The second kind is Chinese historical writing. Tang dynastic histories and compilations record embassies from Zhenla and describe its geography, customs, and political divisions. These texts are valuable because they offer a view from outside and, in some cases, a chronology independent of the Khmer inscriptions. They are limited by the interests of their authors, the second-hand nature of much of their information, and the difficulty of matching Chinese names and descriptions to places known from other evidence.

The third is archaeology. Excavations, surveys, architectural study, and, in recent years, airborne laser scanning have revealed temples, settlements, canals, and embankments that can be dated by style, stratigraphy, and scientific methods. Archaeology cannot name a king or record a speech, but it can show what was actually built, where, and often when, and so it can test claims made in texts. The fourth is later dynastic and priestly tradition, above all the inscription of Sdok Kak Thom, which preserves a version of the Jayavarman II story composed in the mid-eleventh century. This is a retrospective source: it was written by people who had reasons to remember the early ninth century in a particular way.

Historians combine these sources with care for several recurring problems. Retrospective legitimation is one: a later dynasty or priestly family may shape its account of the past to support present claims. Missing records are another: the absence of an inscription does not prove that an event did not occur, nor does its presence prove that the event unfolded as described. Disputed identifications of places and persons are common, since a name such as Indrapura or Jayavarman may refer to different places or rulers. Finally, chronology is uncertain, because inscriptions are dated by regnal and calendar systems that must be converted and sometimes reconstructed. Scholarly critiques of the older Funan-Chenla-Angkor succession, notably those that emphasize the different languages and purposes of Chinese and Khmer sources, have made these problems central to modern research. The rest of the chapter applies these methods to each claim in turn.

Division II — Regional Powers and Transformations in the Eighth Century

Section 33.5

After Jayavarman I: Succession, Jayadevī, and Regional Authority

Jayavarman I, whose reign is usually placed between about 657 and 681, is the last ruler of the seventh century whose inscriptions are widely scattered across the Khmer-speaking regions. His death opened a period for which the evidence is thin and difficult to interpret. The inscriptions that survive do not provide a continuous list of kings, and historians have long recognized that the older practice of constructing a tidy royal succession from them was partly guesswork. What can be said is that royal authority after him was exercised through women as well as men, and that rival centers claimed or exercised power.

The most prominent figure is Jayadevī, a royal woman whose name appears in inscriptions associated with the early eighth century. The bilingual Sanskrit and Old Khmer inscription K.904, from the Western Baray and dated 713 CE, refers to her, and the surviving evidence is usually read as indicating that she held royal authority, whether as the daughter of Jayavarman I, as a regent, or as a ruler in her own right. The exact nature of her power is disputed, and her prominence in the record should not be turned into a detailed biography. Her case is nonetheless significant. It shows that royal legitimacy in this period could pass through women and that succession was not governed by a single fixed rule. Later Angkorian genealogies themselves stressed descent through both male and female lines, a pattern that should be understood as a feature of Khmer political and kinship practice rather than an anomaly.

Historians should also resist the assumption that a single royal lineage continued to rule the whole region. The Chinese reports of division, the multiple inscriptional centers, and the later claims of rival families all point in a different direction. A more accurate picture is of several lineages and local powers, linked by marriage and religious patronage and competing for prestige, resources, and recognition. Some may have claimed descent from the great rulers of the seventh century, because such claims were valuable. Whether those claims were genuine, partly invented, or politically convenient reconstructions is often impossible to determine. The eighth century is best understood as a period of regional authority, in which the capacity of any one ruler to command others was limited and reversible.

Section 33.6

Sambhupura, Isanapura, and the Regional Centers of Power

The political geography of the eighth century can be understood through several centers, each tied to a river corridor and each with its own religious and political tradition. Isanapura, at Sambor Prei Kuk in the Stung Sen valley, had been a major royal seat in the seventh century, and the extensive brick temples there demonstrate a substantial investment in religious architecture and settlement. Chapter 21 treated its architecture; the point here is its later fate. The site shows continued activity after the seventh century, and its inscriptions and buildings indicate that the area retained religious significance. It was part of the landscape that eighth- and ninth-century rulers inherited, not a ruin they replaced.

Sambhupura is generally identified with Sambor, on the Mekong in present-day Kratie province, although the identification of ancient names with modern places is always provisional. In the traditions associated with Jayavarman II, Sambhupura appears as one of the places in which he is said to have resided. To the north lay the upper Mekong and the region of Vat Phou, where temple foundations tied to the mountain of Lingaparvata had long been significant, and which the Chinese sources may have meant by "Land" Zhenla. The Tonle Sap region and the plains to its north, where the later Angkorian centers would arise, formed another cluster, linked to the lake's fisheries and to the Kulen hills. The lower Mekong and the Bassac, historically connected to Funan and to maritime trade, completed the pattern.

These centers should be understood as nodes in a network of relationships rather than as capitals of fixed states. Rulers moved among them, married across them, and sought recognition at one another's temples. Rivers provided the routes, and the Mekong and the Tonle Sap system tied together places that might seem distant on a map. Changing political relationships, the shift of prestige from one center to another, and the capacity of a ruler to bring several centers into his orbit mattered more than the architecture of any one site.

Section 33.7

Royal Families, Local Elites, and Sacred Foundations

Political influence in the eighth century was built through families. A ruling house needed marriage alliances with lords of neighboring areas, the loyalty of local leaders who controlled land and people, and the support of religious specialists who could consecrate its claims. Inscriptions repeatedly show elite individuals founding temples, installing images, and endowing them with fields, livestock, and dependent workers, and recording the genealogies that justified their standing. A foundation was at once an act of devotion, an economic arrangement, and a public statement of status, since it was written in a durable medium and placed where it would be seen.

Competing families used these tools differently. A royal claimant might stress descent from a famous earlier king, as an eighth-century or ninth-century aspirant would be tempted to do. A regional lord might emphasize long local roots, his control of an important shrine, or service to a more powerful ruler. Religious endowments could create obligations: those who received gifts, whether Brahmins or temple servants, were bound to the donor's family, and the temple became a point around which local loyalties gathered. Religious specialists, including priests, ritual experts, and teachers, were also influential in themselves. Their knowledge of Sanskrit and Śaiva ritual gave them access to the language of kingship and a capacity to legitimize rulers, an issue that Chapter 34 examines in the context of court institutions.

It would be misleading to treat these processes as purely top-down. Temples depended on the labor and produce of surrounding villages, and their establishment required the consent, or at least the acquiescence, of local communities. Villagers contributed rice, cloth, services, and sometimes their own persons as temple dependents. They also had the ability to withdraw support, to relocate, or to ally with a rival. The following feature illustrates how a single development, the founding of a major temple, would have been viewed differently by those involved.

Section 33.8

Agriculture, River Networks, and the Foundations of Political Power

Political consolidation required resources, and in the Khmer regions the primary resource was rice. The Tonle Sap plains, the Mekong floodplain, and the river valleys supported rice cultivation using a combination of rainfall, floodwater, and local embankments and channels. Fisheries, especially around the Tonle Sap, supplied protein and trade goods. Palm sugar, forest products, salt, iron, and ceramics were produced and exchanged regionally, although the evidence for their organization in this period is uneven. Rulers did not create this productive base. They sought to draw on it, by controlling land, taxing or demanding produce and labor, and attaching dependent workers to their temples.

River networks were equally important. Boats carried rice, goods, officials, and armies. A ruler who could secure the main waterways, whether the Mekong, the Tonle Sap river, or the streams descending from the Kulen hills, could move people and supplies and could influence trade. The Kulen plateau is especially significant because its streams feed the plains to the south, and because it provided sandstone for later temple construction. Skilled labor, including builders, stone workers, brickmakers, metalworkers, and ritual specialists, was another requirement. Large temples and later hydraulic works demanded organizational capacity that had to be developed and maintained over generations.

Environmental conditions made consolidation possible, but did not make it inevitable. The same landscapes supported many small and medium centers, as the eighth-century evidence shows. Climate, flood patterns, and soil fertility varied from place to place, and political outcomes depended on how rulers, families, and communities chose to organize themselves. The following feature considers how these conditions appeared at the level of households.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Rice, and Rivers in the Pre-Angkorian and Early Angkorian Countryside

Most people in the Khmer regions were farmers and fishers living in villages near rivers, ponds, and fields. Their daily rhythm followed the monsoon: preparing and planting fields as the rains began, tending crops, and harvesting later in the year, while the flooding of the Tonle Sap and the Mekong shaped fishing and transport. Households cultivated rice, kept cattle and other animals, gathered forest products, and made tools, pottery, and cloth. The inscriptions, which were composed by and for elites, tell us this indirectly, through the lists of fields, livestock, and dependents given to temples.

Those lists reveal a social hierarchy. Old Khmer inscriptions use the term khñuṃ for dependent personnel attached to temples and to families, a category that historians interpret variously as servants, bonded or hereditary dependents, and temple workers, and whose precise legal status is debated. Women and men appear in these lists, sometimes by name, along with the tasks they performed, such as cultivation, service, or ritual duties. Temples were therefore also economic institutions, holding land and employing people, and the obligations of those who worked for them shaped community life.

Evidence is limited, and it is important not to project the better-documented conditions of the twelfth century back onto the eighth. What can be said is that villagers were the foundation of the economy, that their labor and produce supported elite ritual and political ambitions, and that a ruler who wished to build or fight depended on the cooperation of the rural population. Archaeological work on settlements, field systems, and waterworks, including surveys using lidar, continues to expand what can be known.

Division III — Jayavarman II and the Reorganization of Khmer Power

Section 33.9

Political Competition in the Late Eighth Century

By the late eighth century, the Khmer regions were an arena of competing powers. Inscriptions of this period name rulers, lords, and temple founders in different areas, and the surviving record, though sparse, does not suggest a single dominant authority. The Chinese reports of division belong to the same picture. Rulers sought recognition from neighbors, and the patronage of temples remained a principal arena of rivalry. The period is best understood as one of fluid relationships in which any ambitious ruler had to negotiate with several centers of power.

Later tradition reconstructed this era as one that required reunification. The founding narrative preserved at Sdok Kak Thom implies a land that needed to be made whole and independent, and later royal inscriptions stress the achievement of a ruler who brought the country under a single authority. This framing is understandable: it supports the claim of the dynasty that traced its origins to Jayavarman II. But it is a retrospective interpretation. It describes the eighth century in the terms that a later court found most useful, and it may exaggerate the degree of disorder before the founder or the degree of unity that he achieved.

Historians therefore need to distinguish between demonstrated conflict and speculative conquest narratives. There is evidence of rivalry and change. There is little contemporary evidence of a particular sequence of wars by which a single king subdued the others. The regional context offers some additional hints. Cham inscriptions from the coast of Vietnam record attacks on temples at places such as Kauthara and Panduranga in the 770s and 780s, attributed to seaborne raiders whose origin the texts associate with "Java." Whether these events are connected to Khmer politics, and whether the raiders came from Java, the Malay world, or somewhere else, is debated, and Section 33.15 returns to the question. What the raids show for certain is that maritime violence and long-distance mobility were part of the political environment of the late eighth century. Any account of Khmer consolidation must situate it in that wider context, rather than in a purely internal story of rivalry among Khmer lords.

Section 33.10

Who Was Jayavarman II?

Remarkably little about Jayavarman II is known from sources contemporary with his reign. No surviving inscription issued in his name can be securely dated to his lifetime. Most of what is said about him comes from inscriptions of later kings, which refer to him with reverence, and above all from the Sdok Kak Thom inscription of 1052. Those sources provide a general outline: he came from somewhere called Javā, ruled in several places, was consecrated as king at Mahendraparvata, and eventually resided at Hariharalaya, where he died and received the posthumous name Parameśvara. The reliability of each element is a separate question.

Some scholars have tried to connect him to rulers attested earlier. Inscriptions dated 770 and 781 name a king called Jayavarman, and one proposal is that this king was Jayavarman II, early in his career, which would place his political activity decades before the traditional date of 802 and would imply a long reign or an early period of regional rule before the consecration. Others consider that the Jayavarman of these inscriptions may be a different ruler, or may represent a local king whose relationship to the later founder cannot be established. Because Jayavarman was a common royal name, the identification depends on inference, and the evidence does not settle it.

The chronology of his reign is also disputed. The conventional start is 802 (Śaka 724), a date found in later retrospective inscriptions such as K.382 and K.598 rather than in K.235 itself, while his death is placed variously at about 835 or about 850, depending on how later reigns are reckoned. Later genealogical traditions linked subsequent kings to him, though descent claims in this period must be treated as legitimating statements rather than documented family trees.

What is known independently of the eleventh-century accounts may be summarized briefly. Later contemporary inscriptions of the late ninth century, notably those of Indravarman I, treat Jayavarman II as an ancestral royal figure and place his centers in specific regions. The archaeology of Phnom Kulen and Roluos shows royal and religious centers of the early ninth century. And the contemporaneous political environment, with its multiple centers and maritime links, is documented. The narrative that joins these elements into a biography is the work of later tradition, and this chapter treats it accordingly.

Section 33.11

Indrapura and the Emergence of a New Royal Power

According to the Sdok Kak Thom inscription, Jayavarman II came to reign at a place called Indrapura. The text presents this as the first of the royal centers associated with him, but it does not tell us how he obtained power there, what army or alliance he commanded, or what kind of political unit Indrapura was. The location of Indrapura remains uncertain. Several sites have been proposed by scholars, some in the Mekong region east of the Tonle Sap and some elsewhere, and none has been generally accepted. The problem is complicated by the fact that Indrapura is also the name of an important Cham center in present-day central Vietnam, so the name alone cannot guide identification.

Even without a secure location, the tradition suggests an important point. A new ruler did not arrive in an empty political space. He established a foothold in a landscape already populated by lords, temples, and communities, and his success depended on whether he could win their cooperation or neutralize their opposition. To "reign at" Indrapura meant, in practical terms, that he gained recognition from the people and institutions of a particular area and built the basis of a following there. His later movements, examined in the next section, can be understood as the expansion of that foothold.

The story of arrival also fits the tradition that Jayavarman II came from elsewhere. If he was an outsider, whether from a distant region or from a rival Khmer line, he may have used marriage, religious patronage, and the favor of local specialists to enter existing networks. If the tradition of an external origin was instead shaped by later considerations, it may reflect a wish to present the founder as coming from beyond the existing political order. Either reading shows that the founding of a new power involved negotiation with the established world, not simple replacement of it.

Section 33.12

Sambhupura, Amarendrapura, and Hariharalaya

The founding tradition describes the king moving among several centers. After Indrapura, the inscription says he reigned at places including Sambhupura and Amarendrapura, went to Mahendraparvata, and finally settled at Hariharalaya. Each name must be assessed separately. Hariharalaya is securely located in the Roluos area southeast of Siem Reap, where later Angkorian temples such as Preah Ko and Bakong stand, so that identification rests on both texts and archaeology. Sambhupura is usually associated with Sambor on the Mekong, though its identification with the Sambhupura of the tradition is not certain. Amarendrapura is very uncertain; scholars have placed it in the region west of the Tonle Sap or elsewhere, without agreement.

The itinerary is therefore a mixture of firm and uncertain points, and it is preserved in a source written two centuries later. It should not be treated as a precise campaign diary. Even so, the pattern of movement is historically meaningful. Early Southeast Asian rulers did not always reside in a single permanent capital. Royal presence, ritual, and the redistribution of resources could be achieved by moving among centers, establishing foundations, and receiving the allegiance of local lords. Mobility could be a strategy of political integration, as the king visited and strengthened ties with different regions. It could also be a response to threats or opportunities.

It is also important not to equate the successive names with a straightforward conquest sequence. A ruler might transfer his residence because a region was loyal, because it was rich, because it was sacred, or because it was safe. The movement from the Mekong or the plains toward the Kulen plateau and then to Roluos is consistent with a ruler seeking a secure and ritually significant base and later a productive and accessible one. The archaeology, discussed in Sections 33.18 and 33.19, provides the best independent evidence for the last two.

Section 33.13

Regional Consolidation: Warfare, Alliances, and Religious Authority

How could a single ruler strengthen his authority over a landscape of competing powers? Several mechanisms are plausible, though they differ in how well they are documented. Royal patronage is the best attested: later inscriptions show kings founding and endowing temples, and the archaeology shows major construction. Elite alliances, probably cemented through marriage and shared religious commitments, are strongly suggested by the importance of kinship in the inscriptions. Relationships with religious specialists are directly attested in the Sdok Kak Thom tradition, though that source is retrospective. Military force is the least documented. Warfare undoubtedly occurred, since conflict between rival rulers is a normal feature of early states, but we lack contemporary accounts of campaigns or armies for the period of Jayavarman II.

Control of strategic locations is a further possibility. A ruler who held a defensible, ritually prestigious site such as the Kulen plateau, or who commanded important river junctions, would enjoy advantages in security, communication, and revenue. Yet this too is inference: the archaeology shows what was built at certain places but not how or why a ruler acquired them. Historians therefore describe these mechanisms as possible, and they weigh them according to the evidence available for each.

Cause and Effect

Long-term causes

A shared tradition of Sanskrit and Old Khmer inscription, temple endowment, and royal titles; an agricultural base capable of supporting dependents; river networks linking regions; the weakening of earlier royal lines after Jayavarman I.

Short-term causes

Competition among regional centers; the possibility of external pressure or maritime raiding in the late eighth century; opportunities for an ambitious ruler to build alliances with local lords and religious specialists.

Immediate context

A ruler, remembered as Jayavarman II, establishes a foothold, moves among several centers, and is later said to have been consecrated at Mahendraparvata. The sequence and timing are known mainly from retrospective tradition.

Effects

Creation of royal centers on the Kulen plateau and at Hariharalaya; a tradition of ritual legitimacy that later rulers claimed; a line of successors who built on these foundations.

Documented and inferred: Temple patronage and the archaeology of the royal centers are documented. Elite alliances, relationships with priests, and military action are partly documented or inferred. No single mechanism explains the outcome, and the relative weight of each cannot be determined from the surviving evidence.

Historical complexity: It would be wrong to explain consolidation by the personal genius of one ruler. It also depended on the choices of lords, priests, and communities, and on contingencies, such as the failure of rival claimants, that we can rarely see.

Division IV — The Founding Tradition and Its Historical Problems

Section 33.14

The Sdok Kak Thom Inscription and the Memory of Jayavarman II

The inscription catalogued as K.235 was found at the site of Prasat Sdok Kok Thom, in what is now Sa Kaeo province in eastern Thailand, near the border with Cambodia, an area that in the eleventh century lay within the Khmer sphere. It is carved on a stone stele with text in both Sanskrit and Old Khmer, and it is conventionally dated to 1052 CE, on the basis of the latest date cited in its text (Śaka 974). Its immediate purpose was to record the foundation of a Śaiva sanctuary and the endowments given to it, and to set out the genealogy and privileges of the priestly family that had charge of the foundation. It was commissioned by a priest named Sadāśiva, a descendant of the line it describes, who is identified as a teacher in the service of King Udayādityavarman II.

Central to the text is a family history stretching back to the time of Jayavarman II. It traces the priestly line from Śivakaivalya, the priest associated with the founder, through generations of descendants who served successive kings, and it records the honors, lands, and titles the family held. The account of Jayavarman II is embedded in this family history. It states that he came from Javā to reign at Indrapura, that he moved among several centers, that a Brahmin named Hiraṇyadāma was summoned to perform a ritual that would make Kambujadeśa independent of Javā and establish the king as a universal ruler, and that the priest Śivakaivalya was entrusted with the ritual associated with the devarāja. The inscription does not itself supply the date 802. The conventional Śaka 724 (802 CE) for Jayavarman II's accession comes from other retrospective inscriptions, including K.382 and K.598, and scholars combine these strands of later evidence with the K.235 narrative of the ritual.

A careful reading emphasizes that this is not an eyewitness account. Roughly two and a half centuries separate the events from the inscription. The text was composed by a family with a strong interest in the prestige of its own ancestors. A story that placed its founder at the center of the king's legitimacy served that interest. This does not make the account false. Family and temple traditions often preserved genuine memories, and details such as the names of the centers receive partial confirmation from other evidence. But it means that every statement requires testing, and that its framing, a story of a ritual that created kingship and independence, may reflect eleventh-century concerns as much as early ninth-century realities.

Section 33.15

The Problem of Java: Foreign Domination, Return, or Political Memory?

The most debated phrase in the Sdok Kak Thom account concerns Javā. The inscription states that Jayavarman II came from Javā and that the ritual of Hiraṇyadāma was intended to make Kambujadeśa independent of Javā. Read literally, this seems to describe a period during which the Khmer lands depended on an external power, followed by a liberation. In the early twentieth century, the French scholar George Coedès developed an influential reading along these lines. He identified Javā with the island of Java, then under the Śailendra rulers, and proposed that the Khmer regions had been subject to Javanese overlordship in the later eighth century, with Jayavarman II as a prince who returned, perhaps after a period of residence in Java, and secured independence.

That interpretation drew on several pieces of evidence: the tradition itself; Cham inscriptions recording attacks from the sea in the 770s and 780s, which some scholars linked with Javanese forces; the Ligor stele from the Malay Peninsula, which carries two distinct inscriptions: Face A, dated 775, praises a ruler of Śrīvijaya and records Buddhist foundations, while Face B, unfinished and undated, refers to a ruler of the Śailendra family, and the relationship between the two faces and the chronology of Face B are disputed; a medieval Arabic account of a Khmer king punished by a ruler of a maritime kingdom, a story whose historical reliability is uncertain; and some stylistic parallels between Central Javanese and early Khmer art. The resulting picture was of a Khmer realm brought into the orbit of a maritime power and then freed from it.

Subsequent scholarship has questioned nearly every link. The Sdok Kak Thom text says nothing about conquest or tribute. The Ligor stele shows Śrīvijaya and, on its undated Face B, Śailendra connections on the peninsula, but it cannot securely date Śailendra activity to 775 and says nothing about Cambodia. The Arabic anecdote is a later tale. Cham raids need not have involved Khmer territory at all. Alternative interpretations of Javā have therefore been proposed. Some scholars have suggested that Javā refers to a place in the Malay world, including Sumatra or the peninsula, or even to the Cham lands, since Cham texts also used the name, or to a region elsewhere in the Mekong world. Others argue that the phrase is a ritual or rhetorical device: a way for the eleventh-century priestly tradition to affirm that the kingdom stood independent of any outside power, with no necessary historical foundation in an earlier dependency. Still others accept a real external connection but see it as a matter of cultural or dynastic contact, not political subordination.

Historical Debate

The Khmer regions experienced a period of dependency in the late eighth century, ended by Jayavarman II. This view draws on the tradition, Cham raid inscriptions, the Ligor stele (whose Śailendra face is undated), and art-historical parallels.
Interpretation B — A different place: Javā denotes another location, possibly in the Malay world, the Cham lands, or elsewhere, and so the tradition describes a different relationship, or a different place of origin for the king. This view stresses that the text offers no direct evidence for the island of Java.
Interpretation C — Political memory or ritual device: The statement of independence expresses the ideology of the later Angkorian court, or the self-presentation of the priestly lineage, and need not reflect a historical period of subordination.

Evidence: All interpretations rely on the Sdok Kak Thom text, on the Cham inscriptions and the Ligor stele, on Arabic and Chinese sources, and on art-historical comparison, but they weigh these differently.

Points of disagreement: The debate concerns terminology (what the place-name referred to), interpretation (whether the passage is historical or rhetorical), and the strength of the supporting evidence. These interpretations are not strictly exclusive; a historical connection with maritime Southeast Asia could coexist with rhetorical use of the name.

Why it matters: If Interpretation A is correct, the origin of Angkorian kingship lies in resistance to an external empire and in the wider maritime politics examined in Chapters 28 through 32. If B or C is more persuasive, the origin is primarily an internal Khmer process shaped by later memory. The chapter does not assume that the Śailendras unquestionably occupied Cambodia.

Section 33.16

The 802 Tradition: Mahendraparvata and Universal Kingship

The conventional founding date of Angkorian kingship, 802 CE, rests on a Śaka year (724) given in later retrospective inscriptions, including K.382 and K.598, for Jayavarman II's accession, converted to the Common Era by adding 78. Historians combine this date with the narrative of the Mahendraparvata ritual preserved in the Sdok Kak Thom inscription (K.235), which does not itself state the year. Modern textbooks, tourism literature, and national narratives often present it as the year in which Angkor, or the Khmer Empire, was founded. As the previous sections have indicated, this is a significant simplification. The date is found only in later sources, and the specific ceremony is not independently documented in 802. It refers to a ritual event on a mountain, not to the construction of a city or the political unification of a territory. And it may represent a symbolic or retrospective anchoring of a longer process.

The tradition links the event with Mahendraparvata, the "mountain of the great Indra," generally identified with the Phnom Kulen plateau, and with a ritual designed to make the king a cakravartin, a universal monarch. The choice of a mountain as the site of royal consecration drew on a widespread South and Southeast Asian association of mountains with divine power and cosmic order, and Chapter 34 examines how such ideas informed Angkorian political thought. The ritual would have involved a Brahmin ritual specialist, a Śaiva liturgy, and public ceremonies witnessed by the king's supporters, and its meaning lay partly in the audience it addressed: the lords and communities whose allegiance the new king needed.

Historical Vocabulary
Cakravartin

Definition: A Sanskrit term, literally "turner of the wheel," for an ideal universal monarch whose rule extends over the world, found in Buddhist, Hindu, and Jain traditions.

Historical context: Early Southeast Asian rulers adopted the language of the cakravartin in inscriptions and ritual to express supreme sovereignty. In the Sdok Kak Thom account, the rite performed at Mahendraparvata is said to have made Jayavarman II such a ruler.

Why it matters: The term was an ideal and a claim, not a measure of actual territory. A king described as cakravartin did not necessarily control a large empire.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not read the title as proof that Jayavarman II ruled a vast, unified state in 802.

Distinguishing the later account of a ritual foundation from a securely documented single-day event is essential. It is possible that a consecration took place at Mahendraparvata in or near 802. It is also possible that the date and the ritual were shaped by later tradition, or that the consecration marked one stage in a longer process of consolidation. What can be said with confidence is that early ninth-century rulers invested in a major royal landscape on the Kulen plateau, and that later Khmer kings regarded a ritual of this kind as the origin of their legitimacy.

Section 33.17

Devarāja, Śaiva Ritual, and the Construction of Royal Legitimacy

The Sdok Kak Thom inscription says that the Brahmin Hiraṇyadāma taught rites drawn from Śaiva scriptures and that Śivakaivalya, the priest, was made responsible for the cult of the devarāja. These details tie the origin of the royal order to a particular religious tradition, Śaivism, the worship of Śiva, which was widely supported in the Khmer regions. They also introduce the family of priests that would hold privileged positions at court for generations, and they illustrate the later court's claim to ritual continuity from the time of the founder. Whether the family's role in the ninth century was as prominent as the inscription suggests cannot be confirmed independently.

Historical Vocabulary
Devarāja

Definition: A Sanskrit compound that can be read as "god-king," "king of the gods," or "the god who is king." Khmer inscriptions also use the Old Khmer expression kamraten jagat ta rāja, "lord of the universe who is king," in related contexts. The meaning of the term, and what it referred to, is disputed.

Historical context: A widely influential twentieth-century interpretation understood the devarāja as a cult in which the living king was identified with a god, represented by a sacred image or lingam. Later scholars, including Hermann Kulke, argued that the term referred instead to a deity, probably a form of Śiva, who was the protective royal god of the kingdom, and others have proposed further variations. The inscriptions are brief and ambiguous.

Why it matters: The term is central to the Angkorian legitimation of kingship, but its meaning shapes how historians understand Khmer religion and politics.

Potential misunderstanding: The devarāja should not be assumed to mean worship of the living king as a god. The evidence does not establish that.

Legitimacy in this period was built from several resources: ritual consecration by authoritative specialists; association with sacred places such as a mountain; the possession of a sacred image or emblem; claims of descent; and the endowment of temples. The devarāja tradition belonged to this repertoire, and the later court's emphasis on the continuity of the ritual from Jayavarman II's time was itself a legitimating claim. A ruler who could show that the cult had been transmitted without interruption could claim to stand in the line of the founder. This helps explain why priestly families such as that of Sdok Kak Thom were eager to record their role, and why the account of the origin was elaborated.

It is also worth noting that Khmer religion was not exclusively Śaiva. Viṣṇu, Harihara, Buddhist figures, and local spirits all received worship, and the court's Śaiva orientation did not exclude other traditions. Chapter 36 explores the religious life of Angkor more fully. For present purposes, the point is that Śaiva ritual provided a language of royal authority, and that its ritual specialists acquired political influence through their ability to supply it.

Division V — Mahendraparvata and the Emergence of Angkorian Kingship

Section 33.18

Mahendraparvata and the Archaeology of Phnom Kulen

Phnom Kulen is a sandstone plateau rising north-east of Siem Reap, long recognized as a sacred place and as a source of stone and water. Its forested slopes conceal numerous ruined temples and other remains, and for decades it was only partially surveyed. French and Cambodian archaeologists documented brick and stone sanctuaries there, such as Prasat O Paong and Prasat Damrei Krap, and noted that their architecture was early. Because later Khmer inscriptions associated the mountain with Jayavarman II's consecration, scholars suspected that the plateau had been an early royal center, but evidence of its extent and organization was limited.

Airborne laser scanning, or lidar, transformed the picture. The technique fires laser pulses from an aircraft and records the returns, allowing researchers to digitally remove the vegetation and see the ground surface. Surveys conducted in the early 2010s by the Cambodian Archaeological Lidar Initiative, led by Damian Evans and colleagues, revealed previously unknown features beneath the forest. A 2019 study published in the journal Antiquity described an extensive planned urban landscape of roughly 40 to 50 square kilometers on the plateau, identified with Mahendraparvata. It includes temple foundations, a network of axes and roads, embankments and ponds, and habitation features that suggest a substantial settlement. The planning indicates deliberate design and organized labor, though a modern estimate of area is not a measure of population or of political reach.

What does this evidence independently support? It shows that a major royal and religious center existed on the plateau, that it was laid out according to a plan, and that it incorporated water management. The architectural style and associated dating place it in the early Angkorian period, consistent with the traditions that associate Jayavarman II with the mountain. It supports the claim that rulers of this era invested heavily in a new royal landscape. It does not, by itself, confirm the details of the ritual in 802, the identity of the king who built it, or the political extent of his authority. Scientific dating of individual features continues to refine the chronology, and interpretations may change. The case illustrates how archaeology and texts can corroborate and also limit each other: the physical remains make the tradition of a mountain center credible, while the tradition supplies a name and a narrative the remains cannot provide.

Section 33.19

Hariharalaya, Royal Continuity, and the First Angkorian Successors

According to the tradition, Jayavarman II eventually left the mountain and settled at Hariharalaya, on the plains at Roluos, where he died. The name combines Hari (Viṣṇu) and Hara (Śiva) and refers to the composite deity Harihara, honored in pre-Angkorian religion. The Roluos area lies in the productive plain south of the Kulen hills, with access to water from streams descending from the plateau and to the Tonle Sap. The surviving monuments of the group, including Preah Ko, Bakong, and Lolei, are largely the work of Indravarman I and Yasovarman I in the late ninth century, but earlier brick structures and settlement remains suggest activity in the preceding generations. The first great Angkorian-period temples were thus built on a site that had already been developed.

Uncertainty surrounds Jayavarman II's death and succession. The date of his death is placed between about 835 and 850, and the inscriptions do not give a continuous, secure account of his immediate successors. Jayavarman III is generally listed as the next king, but the evidence for his reign is limited. Indravarman I, who came to the throne in 877, is the first ruler of the new tradition for whom there is abundant contemporary evidence. His inscriptions record the building of Preah Ko, the temple Bakong, and a large reservoir known as the Indratataka, and they show a king capable of mobilizing labor and materials on a considerable scale. He emphasized his connection to the lineage of Jayavarman II, which suggests that descent from the founder was already valuable.

Yasovarman I, who succeeded him in 889, expanded the program of royal construction, built additional reservoirs, and founded a new capital, Yasodharapura, in the area where Angkor would later develop. These reigns are the subject of later chapters, and this chapter notes them only to show continuity. The royal centers of the tradition, Mahendraparvata, Hariharalaya, and Yasodharapura, are different places, built at different times, and should not be confused with one another or with the later Angkor Thom. The movement from the mountain to Roluos and then northward reflects shifting political and environmental priorities, and the combined evidence indicates that the early ninth century laid the foundation, not the completed form, of the Angkorian order.

Section 33.20

From Regional Polities to Angkor: Continuity, Change, and Historical Interpretation

Bringing the evidence together, what changed in the early ninth century, and why might it matter? The most important transformations concern royal legitimacy, political relationships, and the organization of centers. Rulers in the Angkorian tradition presented their authority as grounded in a ritual of universal kingship, tied to a sacred mountain and a hereditary priestly lineage. They built royal centers on a scale and with a degree of planning that exceeded most earlier foundations, as the Kulen archaeology shows. And they appear to have drawn a wider range of regions into their orbit, though the extent and permanence of that incorporation grew gradually. Subsequent chapters will show how these tendencies developed into institutions and a distinctive state.

At the same time, much continued. The use of Sanskrit and Old Khmer inscriptions, the endowment of temples with land and dependents, the reliance on Brahmin ritual specialists, the importance of kinship and marriage, the centrality of rice agriculture and river transport, and the religious tradition of Śaivism and Harihara all had deep roots in the pre-Angkorian world. The early Angkorian kings did not invent these practices. They reorganized them, and they claimed continuity with a past that they also reshaped.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Khmer Political Organization around the Early Ninth Century

What Changed

  • A tradition of consecration as universal king, linked to a mountain and a priestly lineage, became the foundation of royal legitimacy
  • Royal centers were planned on a larger scale, as at Phnom Kulen and later Roluos
  • A single dynastic tradition increasingly claimed descent from one founder, replacing a plurality of competing lines
  • Rulers gradually brought more regions into a shared network of obligation and recognition

What Stayed the Same

  • The agricultural and riverine base, and the labor of villagers who sustained it
  • Inscriptions in Sanskrit and Old Khmer, temple endowments, and dependent personnel
  • The importance of kinship, marriage, and religious specialists in political life
  • The mobility of rulers among centers and the fluidity of authority at the margins

Pace and experience of change: Change was gradual and uneven, not abrupt. It was experienced most directly by elites, priests, and the communities attached to new royal centers, while many rural households experienced continuity in their daily lives. Why it matters: Recognizing both change and continuity avoids the error of treating Angkor as either a sudden creation or a mere extension of earlier Chenla.

The historiographical lesson is equally important. The foundation narrative of Sdok Kak Thom, the 802 date, and the image of a single liberator-king have shaped modern national memory as well as scholarship, and they remain attractive because they are simple. The evidence points to a more complex process, in which later tradition and surviving remains illuminate different aspects of the same transformation. The inquiry now shifts to the functioning of the state this process created. Chapter 34 examines the institutions of Angkorian kingship, including court offices, provincial relationships, elite networks, and the scholarly debates over the mandala and devarāja models.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the politically diverse Khmer-speaking regions of the seventh and eighth centuries developed into the royal order associated with Jayavarman II, and how much of the traditional foundation story historians can establish. Its central argument is that Angkorian kingship emerged through a gradual and contested reorganization of an already complex political world, and that the later tradition of a single founding act and a single liberator-king illuminates how later courts remembered the process rather than how it unfolded.

The chapter began by distinguishing the beginning of an Angkorian political tradition from the construction of the monumental city. The first belongs to the late eighth and early ninth centuries and to royal centers on the Kulen plateau and at Roluos. The second belongs largely to the following centuries. A foundation date, the chapter argued, does not establish the birth of a unified empire.

The inherited world included rulers, temples, inscriptions, and landscapes from the seventh century, notably Isanapura and the reign of Jayavarman I. Chinese descriptions of Land and Water Zhenla were examined as evidence for eighth-century political change, but not as maps of two bounded successor states. The terms Zhenla and Kambuja were shown to derive from different observers and purposes, and the older sequence of Funan, Chenla, and Angkor was set aside as an oversimplification.

The reconstruction of the period requires several kinds of evidence: contemporary inscriptions, Chinese histories, archaeology, and retrospective dynastic and priestly accounts. Each has strengths and limits, and the problems of retrospective legitimation, missing records, disputed identifications, and uncertain chronology run through the whole period.

In the eighth century, succession after Jayavarman I was uncertain, women such as Jayadevī held royal authority, and regional centers including Isanapura, Sambhupura, the Vat Phou region, and the Tonle Sap plains were linked by river corridors. Families, local elites, and religious specialists used kinship, temple endowment, and ritual to build influence. Their efforts depended on the rice agriculture, fisheries, skilled labor, and dependent personnel that villagers and producers supplied.

Jayavarman II, the central figure of the chapter, is known mainly from later sources. Inscriptions of 770 and 781 naming a king Jayavarman may or may not refer to him, and his itinerary through Indrapura, Sambhupura, Amarendrapura, and Hariharalaya combines secure and uncertain identifications. The mechanisms of consolidation, including patronage, alliances, religious authority, and possibly force, were assessed according to how well each is documented.

The Sdok Kak Thom inscription, composed in 1052, was treated as a retrospective source shaped by a priestly family. Its references to Javā, the consecration of 802 at Mahendraparvata, the idea of the cakravartin, and the devarāja cult were each examined. The meaning of Javā remains debated, and the chapter did not assume that the Śailendras occupied Cambodia. The 802 date was shown to rest on later retrospective inscriptions, distinct from the K.235 ritual narrative, and devarāja was shown to be a disputed term that should not be read automatically as worship of the living king.

Archaeology adds an independent line of evidence. Lidar surveys of Phnom Kulen have revealed a planned landscape of roughly 40 to 50 square kilometers identified with Mahendraparvata, demonstrating substantial early royal investment. Hariharalaya at Roluos provided the next royal base, and Indravarman I and Yasovarman I, the first well-documented Angkorian rulers, built on these foundations. Mahendraparvata, Hariharalaya, and the later monumental Angkor are distinct places and projects.

The early ninth century was therefore a consequential turning point, marked by a new basis of legitimacy, larger planned royal centers, and a growing dynastic tradition, but it was also deeply continuous with earlier agriculture, religion, kinship, and inscriptional practice. The next chapter turns to the institutions through which Angkorian kings governed.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a beginning is made. Angkor is often presented as a sudden achievement, but the evidence reveals a long reorganization in which families, priests, local lords, and farming communities reworked an inherited world. Learning to see that process, and to separate it from the memory of a single founding act, is a lesson that applies to the origins of many states.

The chapter also reveals how historians work with fragmentary and retrospective evidence. A priestly inscription of 1052 and a laser-scanned landscape on Phnom Kulen answer different questions. Neither is sufficient alone, and each corrects the temptations of the other. The unresolved debates over Javā and devarāja are not failures of scholarship but reminders that careful interpretation sometimes means leaving questions open.

What the chapter set in motion continued for centuries. The ritual claims, the royal centers, and the dynastic memory of Jayavarman II became resources for later kings, who built reservoirs, temples, and an administrative order on their foundations. At the same time, deep continuities in agriculture, kinship, religion, and local community life persisted beyond any change of ruler, which is why the history of Angkor cannot be told as the history of kings alone.

Within the larger textbook, the chapter returns to its recurring theme: Southeast Asian societies were historical actors who selected, adapted, and transformed ideas and institutions, not passive recipients of outside influence. Chapter 34 now turns from the emergence of Angkorian kingship to its political order, asking how rulers governed the world that this transformation created.
