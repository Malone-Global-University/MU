After Pagan: Myinsaing, Pinya, Sagaing, and Ava

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Pagan's political authority fragmented in the late thirteenth century, but the rice fields, canals, villages, monasteries, and service obligations of Upper Burma did not disappear. They became the contested prize of successive courts.

Travelers who move through the dry-zone plains of central Myanmar today cross a landscape that already carried a dense human imprint in the fourteenth century: brick monasteries and pagodas, irrigation channels descending from the Shan plateau, river ports on the Irrawaddy and Myitnge, and villages whose names appear in stone inscriptions. Nearly all of this landscape had been shaped, or at least reorganized, during the centuries when Pagan was the dominant royal center of the Irrawaddy basin, a history examined in Chapters 40 and 41. What changed after about 1280 was not the existence of this agricultural and religious world but the question of who could command it.

That question produced a remarkable sequence of royal centers within a few dozen kilometers of one another. The brothers Athinkhaya, Yazathingyan, and Thihathu rose from regional military command to dominate Myinsaing, in the Kyaukse irrigation zone. They displaced Kyawswa, who had acceded at Pagan on 30 May 1289 and received formal Yuan recognition only on 20 March 1297; a contemporary inscription already calls him deposed on 17 December 1297, although Chinese reports place his removal in 1298. Within the following two decades, courts appeared at Pinya (conventionally 1313) and Sagaing (c. 1315–1316). After a crisis of northern invasion in the 1359–1364 period, a new capital was founded at Ava (Inwa) in 1365, at the junction of the Irrawaddy and Myitnge. Each of these centers claimed some portion of Pagan's inheritance. None restored the reach of Pagan at its height.

The chapter's central historical question is therefore a question about recurrence and limits. Why did Upper Burma, with its productive farmland and inherited institutions, repeatedly generate new kingdoms but only unevenly generate durable political authority? The answer developed here is not a single cause. It combines the fiscal and landholding arrangements inherited from Pagan, the demands of warfare on a mostly agrarian manpower base, the political weight of Tai-speaking (Shan) frontier rulers, the competition of rival dynastic lines, and the autonomy of southern centers such as Hanthawaddy, Prome, and Toungoo.

The evidence for this period is uneven and must be handled with care. Burmese royal chronicles, compiled centuries later, supply a continuous narrative of reigns and battles, but they were written by court historians who imposed dynastic order on earlier confusion. Stone inscriptions, which are contemporary, record donations, dates, and occasional glimpses of war and hardship, yet they were not written to narrate political history. Chinese records from the Yuan and Ming courts provide external dates and diplomatic information, but they describe the region from the perspective of imperial administrators. Historians such as Than Tun built reconstructions by testing chronicle claims against inscriptions; Jon Fernquest and others have used Chinese and Tai sources to reframe the Ava period within the frontier politics of Yunnan.

This chapter also resists two simplifying frames. The first treats the era as a "dark age" that follows Pagan's splendor and precedes later empires. The second treats the three brothers and their successors as foreign "Shan" conquerors replacing "Burmese" kings. The sources do not support either frame without heavy qualification. Courts of this period patronized Buddhist institutions, endowed monasteries, commissioned inscriptions, and maintained administrative practices continuous with Pagan, while their rulers' ancestry and alliances combined Burmese, Tai, and regional elements in ways that modern ethnic categories describe poorly.

The chapter's chronological focus is approximately 1280–1500. Twenty sections, organized in five divisions, move from sources and geography through the Mongol wars and the brothers of Myinsaing; to Pinya, Sagaing, and the Shan frontier; to the agricultural, social, and religious foundations of the period; to the crisis of 1364 and the founding of Ava; and finally to Ava's long wars, succession politics, and condition around 1500. Hanthawaddy appears here chiefly as Ava's rival; its fuller history belongs to Chapter 60. The fall of Ava in 1527 and the Toungoo conquests lie beyond this chapter and are treated in Chapter 84.

Learning Objectives

  3. Assess the relative contributions of Mongol invasion, fiscal and landholding arrangements, and administrative change to the weakening of Pagan.
  4. Explain how Athinkhaya, Yazathingyan, and Thihathu established Myinsaing and why conventional "Shan" labels require qualification.
  5. Trace the foundation of Pinya and Sagaing, distinguishing conventional dates and different senses of "foundation."
  6. Analyze how irrigation, royal land, monastic estates, and labor obligations shaped political competition and military mobilization.
  7. Reconstruct the everyday experience of cultivators, artisans, soldiers, captives, and women during prolonged warfare, using inscriptional evidence.
  8. Evaluate the crisis of 1359–1364 and the distinction between the events of 1364 and the founding of the Ava capital in 1365.
  9. Explain why Swa Saw Ke's reign was a process of institutional rebuilding and why Ava could not permanently reunify the Irrawaddy and Sittang worlds.
  10. Interpret whether the post-Pagan centuries are best understood as decline, reconstruction, or long-term transformation.

Historical Context

By the mid-thirteenth century, Pagan had developed into the principal royal center of the central Irrawaddy basin. Its kings drew on irrigated rice land in Kyaukse and elsewhere, patronized Buddhist monasteries on a very large scale, and issued donations recorded in stone. Scholars disagree over how centralized this system was, and over how far royal authority reached beyond the dry-zone core. Michael Aung-Thwin has argued that Pagan's political economy depended on a relationship among crown, cultivated land, and religious institutions; that relationship is central to understanding both Pagan's strength and the vulnerabilities that followed. Earlier developments in Burmese Buddhism are examined in Chapter 41; Pagan's rise is the subject of Chapter 40.

Pagan's last decades coincided with the expansion of the Yuan dynasty into Yunnan. Mongol-Yuan forces defeated Pagan's army at the frontier in 1277, campaigned further in the 1280s, and reached the Pagan region by 1287, a sequence that Chapter 46 compares with other Yuan campaigns in Southeast Asia. Kyawswa acceded at Pagan in 1289, but he did not receive formal Yuan recognition until March 1297. Military pressure and rival claims followed rather than a stable Chinese administration. The Chinese frontier thus became both a source of threat and a source of recognition that competing rulers attempted to use.

A third element of the context is the Tai-speaking world to the north and east. Tai-speaking communities, examined in Chapter 54, had been settling and forming polities across the northern mainland, including areas of the Shan plateau, the upper Irrawaddy and Chindwin valleys, and Yunnan. Burmese-language sources call many of these groups "Shan." That term is a Burmese rendering that covers diverse communities, not a single political bloc. Tai rulers could be allies, in-laws, mercenaries, tributaries, or enemies of Upper Burmese courts, sometimes all in the same generation.

Finally, Upper Burma stood beside two other zones whose histories are covered in neighboring chapters: the Mon-speaking lower Burma coast and delta, whose kingdom of Hanthawaddy is the subject of Chapter 60, and the Tai kingdoms of the east and southeast, including Lan Na and Ayutthaya, treated in Chapters 56 and 57. The regional pattern of fourteenth- and fifteenth-century mainland politics, with Theravada Buddhism increasingly shared (Chapter 53) and political authority repeatedly reconfigured, forms the background for Chapter 59. Victor Lieberman has interpreted this larger pattern as a recurring cycle of integration, fragmentation, and reintegration, a framework this chapter tests rather than assumes.

Section 59.1

Reconstructing Post-Pagan Burma: Chronicles, Inscriptions, and Foreign Records

The central argument of this chapter is that the fragmentation of Pagan did not erase the economic and religious foundations of Upper Burma. The agricultural institutions and settlement networks of the Irrawaddy basin persisted, but control over them became contested. Making that argument responsibly requires understanding how historians know what they claim to know, because the three principal kinds of evidence for this period tell different stories and carry different kinds of authority.

The narrative backbone of most older histories is the Burmese royal chronicle tradition. U Kala's Maha Yazawin, compiled around 1724, assembled earlier chronicles and other materials into a long dynastic history. The Hmannan Maha Yazawindawgyi, compiled by a royal commission between 1829 and 1832, revised and extended the older work, adding material from inscriptions and other sources and resolving conflicts among earlier accounts. These are valuable historical witnesses, but they are retrospective: they were composed roughly four centuries (U Kala) to more than five centuries (the Hmannan) after the events of this chapter, at courts that had their own ideas of proper kingship, legitimate succession, and the relationship of Burmese kings to Shan and Mon neighbors. Where they provide reign lengths, genealogies, and ethnic labels, those details should be treated as traditions to be tested, not as records.

Stone inscriptions are the principal contemporary documentary evidence. Donors, often members of royal and local elites, commissioned inscriptions to record gifts of land, laborers, and goods to monasteries and pagodas, and to establish the merit gained and the boundaries protected. Inscriptions use the Burmese Era, a dating system whose year numbers are 638 less than the corresponding Common Era years in most cases; an inscription dated 737, for example, corresponds to 1375 CE. The great modern collections include Duroiselle and Blagden's Epigraphia Birmanica and Luce and Pe Maung Tin's Inscriptions of Burma, and the historian Than Tun used inscriptions systematically in his 1959 study of Burmese history between 1300 and 1400. Inscriptions are limited in a different way: they are scattered, concentrated among elite donors, and they mention kings and wars only incidentally.

External records provide a third check. Yuan-period Chinese sources, including dynastic histories, record the Mongol campaigns and the Yuan court's dealings with Pagan and Myinsaing. For the later fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, the Ming court's records, accessible in Geoff Wade's searchable English-language translation of the Ming Shi-lu (Veritable Records of the Ming), document tribute missions, frontier conflicts, and requests for recognition involving Ava and Tai rulers. These records are valuable for dating and for revealing the northern frontier, but they describe Southeast Asian polities through the categories and interests of imperial officials.

Archaeology and surviving monuments complete the picture. Brick religious buildings, city walls, and monastic sites around Pinya, Sagaing, Ava, and Myinsaing provide evidence of settlement and patronage, although dating of many structures depends on associated inscriptions. A final caution concerns English-language access. The well-known English translation by Pe Maung Tin and G. H. Luce, The Glass Palace Chronicle of the Kings of Burma (1923), translates the early portions of the chronicle associated with Pagan; it does not provide English translations of the later Myinsaing–Ava narrative. This chapter therefore does not use it as a source for fifteenth-century Ava, and where chronicle content is summarized below, it is identified as chronicle tradition mediated through modern scholarship, not as verbatim translation.

Section 59.2

Upper Burma after Pagan: The Irrawaddy Basin, Kyaukse, and the Geography of Power

Upper Burma, the central dry zone of the Irrawaddy basin, is a region of relatively low rainfall that has nevertheless supported dense settlement for much of its recorded history. The reason is irrigation. Streams descending from the Shan plateau, including the Zawgyi, Samon, and Panlaung, feed the Myitnge River, which joins the Irrawaddy near the sites of later Ava and Sagaing. Weirs, canals, and tanks distributed this water across the Kyaukse plain, making it one of the most reliably productive wet-rice districts in the region. Other irrigated and cultivated districts lay near Minbu on the west bank of the Irrawaddy, around Pagan itself, and in the Mu valley to the northwest, though the specific hydraulic works of each period remain debated.

This geography helps explain why rival courts could coexist. Because productive land, and the people who worked it, lay within a compact area, a determined commander who controlled a handful of districts could sustain a court, an army, and a program of religious patronage. The cities of Myinsaing, Pinya, Sagaing, and Ava lie within a short distance of one another, and the rivers that joined them also divided them: the Irrawaddy and Myitnge served as highways, as defensive barriers, and as sources of the surplus that financed rivalry.

Routes mattered as much as farmland. The Irrawaddy corridor led north to Mogaung and the upper Chindwin and Irrawaddy valleys, connecting Upper Burma to northern Tai polities and to routes toward Yunnan. Overland tracks climbed the Shan plateau toward the east, where valleys held Tai principalities. To the south, the river and the Sittang corridor led toward Prome, Toungoo, and Hanthawaddy. These routes carried troops, merchants, monks, and refugees, and they made the dry zone both a regional hub and a frontier of several worlds.

Geography, however, constrains rather than dictates. The same landscape had supported Pagan's kingdom and later supported several competing courts; no environmental feature determined which pattern would prevail. What the geography provided was a durable material base: grain, labor, and river transport. What varied was the political capacity to claim them, through gifts, service obligations, military command, and alliances with local elites and monastic communities. The central tension of the chapter, between the persistence of this base and the instability of those who ruled over it, begins with this fact.

A methodological note belongs here. Inscriptions mention villages, irrigation channels, and field boundaries in the dry zone, but they rarely say who built or repaired a canal or when. Historians therefore cannot assume that each new court invested in irrigation; the evidence more often shows courts inheriting working systems and claiming rights over their produce. Whether maintenance declined, held steady, or improved in the fourteenth century remains an open question, which is one reason this chapter speaks of persistence of the agricultural base rather than of expansion.

Section 59.3

The Mongol Campaigns and the Breakdown of Pagan's Imperial Authority

Pagan's last decades were dominated by its confrontation with the Yuan dynasty. After the Mongols established control in Yunnan, relations between the Yuan court and Pagan deteriorated over tribute demands, envoys, and frontier jurisdiction. Chinese and Burmese traditions agree that a Yuan force defeated Pagan's army in 1277 in the frontier zone usually identified with Ngasaunggyan (Vochan), and that further campaigns in the early 1280s pushed down the Irrawaddy valley. The Burmese chronicle tradition associates this period with Narathihapate, remembered in later tradition by a name meaning "the king who fled from the Chinese," and reports that he withdrew south and was killed in 1287, in one version by his own son at Prome.

The year 1287 is conventionally treated as a turning point, and it was. Yuan forces reached the Pagan region; Narathihapate's authority collapsed; and the prospect of continuous rule from Pagan ended. Yet historians now caution against reading 1287 as the instantaneous disappearance of Pagan society. Monasteries continued to receive donations; Pagan continued to be inhabited and to appear in inscriptions; kings recognized by the Yuan continued to be installed there for decades. The collapse was political, and it unfolded unevenly.

What the invasion most obviously damaged was the capacity of the crown to command revenue and manpower. A court under military pressure must raise troops and provisions; if local officials, monastic estates, or provincial commanders cannot or will not supply them, royal authority weakens even without battlefield disaster. In 1287 and after, succession was contested, regional commanders held independent military power, and local authorities gained room to bargain. The three brothers who dominated Myinsaing emerged from precisely this setting.

Whether invasion alone explains Pagan's fragmentation is a debated question. A narrow Mongol-centered account has the advantage of an unmistakable event, but it leaves unexplained why Pagan failed to recover as other states recovered from invasions. Scholars who emphasize internal conditions point to the long accumulation of land and labor in religious hands, to changing administrative practices, and to the dependence of royal revenue on grants that could not easily be reclaimed. A balanced reading treats the Mongol campaigns as a major factor, and as an event that exposed and accelerated problems already present, rather than as the sole cause.

Later tradition framed Narathihapate's fall in moral terms. The chronicles associate his reign with extravagant building, notably the Mingalazedi pagoda, and with arrogance toward the Yuan envoys, and they explain his defeat partly as the consequence of these faults. Historians read such passages as part of the chronicles' way of explaining dynastic failure through royal character, not as reliable evidence of causes. The more useful questions concern the material conditions of the reign: how much revenue and manpower the crown could actually raise, how many local commanders obeyed it, and how monastic and local authorities responded when royal demands increased.

Historical Debate

Did the Mongols Destroy Pagan?

The question: To what extent did the Yuan invasions, as opposed to internal political, fiscal, and religious developments, cause the collapse of Pagan's royal authority?

Interpretation A — the invasion was decisive: Older narratives, shaped by chronicle emphasis on Narathihapate's flight, gave the Mongols the leading role, portraying Pagan as brought low by a foreign conquest. The strength of this view is its alignment with the chronicle's own narrative and the clear chronology of defeat.
Interpretation B — internal fiscal and religious change was decisive: Michael Aung-Thwin argued that the expansion of tax-exempt religious landholding progressively reduced the crown's economic base, leaving Pagan fragile regardless of the invasion, and that the Mongol attack did not amount to a thorough conquest or destruction of the city.
Interpretation C — structural cycle with multiple factors: Victor Lieberman placed Pagan's fall within a broader pattern of political cycles on the mainland, in which early "charter" states reached the limits of their administrative systems and fragmented in roughly the same decades across the region; the Mongol campaigns were one of several pressures.

Evidence: Chronicle accounts of the flight and death of Narathihapate; Chinese records of the campaigns; inscriptions recording land donations and exemptions; archaeological evidence from Pagan and Kyaukse.

Points of disagreement: The debate concerns causation and scale: how much of Pagan's weakness preceded the invasion, how far the Yuan forces actually occupied or damaged the capital, and how reliable the chronicle account is.

Why it matters: If the invasion was decisive, the post-1287 period is a story of recovery from foreign catastrophe. If fiscal and religious structures were decisive, the problem of political authority was internal and likely to persist, as it did in the successor courts.

Student task: Identify one piece of evidence that would most clearly distinguish an invasion-driven collapse from a fiscal collapse, and explain what each interpretation predicts you would find in it.

Section 59.4

The Three Brothers and the Rise of Myinsaing

Athinkhaya, Yazathingyan, and Thihathu are the central figures of the transition. The chronicle tradition describes them as sons of a Shan chieftain and as commanders in Pagan's service during the Mongol wars. In the aftermath of 1287, they held authority over the Myinsaing district in the Kyaukse zone, a position that gave them command of one of the most productive agricultural regions of Upper Burma and access to the manpower of its villages. Their power was regional and military before it was royal.

The conventional label "Shan" requires qualification. It reports the brothers' reputed paternal ancestry, but it does not describe the world in which they governed. They operated within Burmese Buddhist court culture, used titles and institutions inherited from Pagan, made donations to monasteries, and relied on local Burmese-speaking elites and Tai-speaking allies alike. To describe the rise of Myinsaing as a simple replacement of "Burmese" rulers by "Shan" rulers is to apply later ethnic categories to a society in which political identity was shaped by lineage, patronage, military service, and alliance. Dynastic marriage and regional networks were as significant as ancestry.

The brothers' authority was also unusual in form. The sources describe a shared or overlapping exercise of power rather than a single king. They are associated with Myinsaing, but the chronicles also remember a division of roles among them, and later writers saw the brothers as a ruling group. This arrangement fit the circumstances: in a period when the legitimacy of the Pagan line was damaged and military power was dispersed, a fraternal command could hold together a coalition of local elites and armed followers more readily than a single claimant.

According to the chronicle tradition, the brothers removed Kyawswa from effective royal authority in the crisis of 1297–1298. The inscriptional sequence is more precise than a single undifferentiated date: Kyawswa was anointed king at Pagan on 30 May 1289, received formal Yuan imperial recognition on 20 March 1297, and was called a dethroned king in an inscription dated 17 December 1297. Chinese records place his removal in June–July 1298. These are distinct events and competing source chronologies, not proof that Yuan recognition had begun in 1289. The brothers consolidated Kyaukse's agricultural and military networks; Pagan itself survived as a diminished royal center rather than disappearing.

Because the chronicles are retrospective, historians compare their narrative with dated inscriptions and Yuan records. They also distinguish Kyawswa's sons: the Pagan successor Maṅ Lulai (commonly associated with Saw Hnit), who held a royal audience on 8 May 1299 with support from the Myinsaing brothers, was not Kumārakassapa, a rival claimant who fled to Yunnan. The Yuan emperor recognized Kumārakassapa on 22 June 1300; he entered Pagan during the 1301 campaign and departed with the withdrawing army. Than Tun's reconstruction (History of Burma: A.D. 1300–1400, 1959, pp. 119–122) makes this distinction clear. Authority was assembled through local armies, inscriptions, and competing diplomatic claims, not transferred in a single unambiguous ceremony.

Division II · Sections 59.5–59.8

Pinya, Sagaing, and Competing Centers of Royal Authority

Yuan intervention, Thihathu, dynastic division, and the Shan frontier

Section 59.5

The Yuan Intervention, Myinsaing's Resistance, and the End of Mongol Military Pressure

The removal of a ruler formally recognized by the Yuan in March 1297 helped precipitate another confrontation. After Kumārakassapa fled to Yunnan, the Yuan emperor proclaimed him rightful king on 22 June 1300. A Yuan expedition entered Upper Burma in 1301 and reached the Myinsaing region, while Kumārakassapa briefly occupied Pagan. The brothers retained their fortified position in the Kyaukse zone. The contemporary reconstruction published by Than Tun dates the Yuan retreat to 6 April 1301 and the abolition of the Yuan's Cheng-mien province to 4 April 1303. These are distinct military and administrative developments. The chronicle tradition supplies colorful stories about negotiation and military leaders, but those details should not be confused with independently dated events.

Distinguish three things here. First, Yuan military intervention was real and substantial, but the expedition retreated in April 1301 without dislodging the Myinsaing brothers. Second, the later 1303 abolition of Cheng-mien marked an administrative change, not the beginning of the retreat. Third, the withdrawal did not restore the old Pagan empire. Maṅ Lulai, supported by the brothers, remained associated with a reduced Pagan kingship; Kumārakassapa, backed by the Yuan, went back with its army. Royal titles, local power, and foreign investment in a claimant were separate matters.

Why did the absence of a large occupying army not restore the old order? Several answers can be offered. The administrative and fiscal system that had underpinned Pagan was damaged, and regional commanders had gained the means to resist. The brothers controlled the key agricultural zone and its manpower. Religious institutions held land and loyalty that could be redirected toward a new patron. And the Yuan, preoccupied with other theaters and with the difficulty of maintaining distant garrisons, had little capacity for permanent administration. The result was a political vacuum filled by locally rooted power.

The episode also reveals something about the diplomacy of the period. Chinese recognition was a resource that rulers could seek and use, but it was not a form of control. The brothers resisted the Yuan militarily, and later rulers of Upper Burma would again engage the Chinese court, particularly the Ming, when it suited them. Diplomatic recognition and actual administration were different things, a distinction that returns when Ava's relations with Yunnan are examined in Section 59.16.

The Pagan royal line did not disappear after Kyawswa's removal. Maṅ Lulai (often linked with Saw Hnit) held his first recorded audience at Pagan on 8 May 1299, supported by the Myinsaing brothers. His brother Kumārakassapa instead sought Yuan backing and was recognized by the Yuan on 22 June 1300; after accompanying the 1301 invasion to Pagan, he departed with the retreating force. The survival of two distinct claims demonstrates why imperial recognition did not settle who governed the irrigated heartland. In the long run the Myinsaing rulers, rather than either claimant at Pagan, controlled the decisive military and agricultural resources.

Section 59.6

Thihathu and the Foundation of Pinya

Of the three brothers, Thihathu emerged as the figure who most clearly assumed royal status. After the deaths of his brothers (Athinkhaya is traditionally said to have died about 1310), he consolidated authority and, around 1313 by the conventional dating, established a new capital at Pinya, a short distance from Myinsaing on the Irrawaddy side of the plain. He took royal titles and presented his court as a legitimate successor to Pagan.

The move from Myinsaing to Pinya is instructive. Myinsaing had been a military base in the Kyaukse zone. Pinya was a capital for a king: a site for palace, monasteries, and the rituals that constitute kingship, closer to river transport and to the Irrawaddy corridor. The choice shows a ruler converting regional military power into a recognized royal order. It also shows how irrigation resources, Buddhist institutions, and older political centers shaped the geography of royal power, since the new capital depended on the Kyaukse surplus while seeking religious prestige through patronage.

Legitimacy was claimed in several ways. Thihathu's court adopted royal titles and ceremonial forms continuous with Pagan's tradition. Chronicle traditions associate the court with dynastic and marital ties to the Pagan royal house, a claim that, whatever its details, expressed the importance of descent from an established line. The court also patronized monasteries and religious building, which provided the merit and public visibility that kings were expected to display. But claiming legitimacy was different from controlling territory. Pinya's kings asserted authority over a broad inheritance, yet in practice their effective power depended on the districts and elites that actually supplied them.

That gap between claim and control is a recurring feature of the period. Pinya could make claims on behalf of all of Upper Burma and could receive recognition from rulers and communities that found it advantageous. It could not prevent the appearance of a rival at Sagaing, nor consistently compel the loyalty of peripheral districts, nor insulate itself from northern pressure. The foundation of Pinya thus marks the re-establishment of kingship, not the restoration of Pagan.

Pinya's court was also a patron of religious building and of monasteries, and inscriptions from the Pinya period are among the evidence historians use to trace its donors and officials. The chronicle tradition lists a sequence of Pinya kings following Thihathu, beginning with Uzana I, but the order and lengths of these reigns are conventional and should be treated with the caution described in Section 59.1. What the sequence does indicate is that royal succession at Pinya was repeatedly contested within the family and that the court's ability to project authority depended on the personal relationships of each ruler with local elites and with the court at Sagaing.

Why it matters: The pattern shows that dynastic change and institutional change are different things, and that a new court can inherit an old system without being able to reproduce its reach.

Section 59.7

Saw Yun, Sagaing, and the Division of Upper Burmese Authority

Within a few years of Pinya's founding, a second royal center appeared across the Irrawaddy at Sagaing. Tradition associates it with Saw Yun, a close member of the Myinsaing dynastic household (the chronicles generally present him as a son of Thihathu, though genealogical details vary between sources), and places the event around 1315–1316.

The chronology deserves care. Pinya's foundation is conventionally dated to 1313, while Sagaing's emergence is placed around 1315–1316. The range reflects what the sources are describing. One tradition may refer to an initial political separation, another to the founding of the capital, and another to formal independence or the assumption of royal title. Chronicle accounts do not always agree. This chapter therefore treats the separate chronological claims as separate, avoiding a single decisive year, and notes that the separation of authority was a process.

Sagaing's emergence illustrates the problem of divided authority. The two courts shared an agricultural region and a dynastic ancestry. Each claimed royal status. Each sought control of villages, irrigation districts, monastic patrons, and military manpower. Their relationship oscillated between rivalry and cooperation, and chronicle tradition records marriages and alliances along with conflict. Because the territory at stake was small and the stakes were high, quarrels over a canal district or a group of villages could escalate into questions of royal legitimacy.

Divided authority had economic and political costs. Competition encouraged each court to bid for the loyalty of local elites with grants and exemptions, a practice that could reduce the revenue available to the crown. It encouraged each ruler to look outward for allies, including northern Tai rulers whose military assistance could tip a local contest and whose own interests were not identical to those of either court. And it meant that no single court could mobilize the full resources of the region against external pressure. The weakness would be exposed in the northern crisis described in Section 59.13.

Religious geography adds a further dimension. The hills around Sagaing became, in later centuries, a renowned center of Buddhist monasteries and learning. How much of that reputation dates to the period of the Sagaing court is a question for epigraphic and archaeological study rather than for assumption. It is reasonable to suppose that rulers who competed for religious prestige sought to endow monastic foundations in and around their capitals, but the pace and scale of such endowment before 1364 remain matters of inference from a limited number of inscriptions.

Historical Vocabulary
Royal Titles, Appanages, Mueang, and Political Allegiance

Royal titles in this period include Burmese terms for king and lord (min) and Sanskritic and Pali-derived throne names that connected a ruler to Buddhist and Indic models of kingship. Titles were part of a claim to legitimacy and were not proof of territorial control: two courts could use royal titles simultaneously.

Appanage is a European term for a territory assigned to a royal kinsman to govern, typically in exchange for loyalty and service. Historians use it for arrangements in which princes or relatives held districts, but the term imposes a European category, and local practice varied. Sawbwa is the Burmese rendering of the title of a Shan (Tai) ruler; the equivalent Tai term underlies the word for a principality. Mueang is a Tai term for a principality: a town or valley center with surrounding settlements and a ruling house, often tied to others by marriage, tribute, and military ties.

Political allegiance in this world was layered and negotiated. A local lord might send gifts to one court, marry into another, and serve a third in war. Why it matters: Reading these relations as fixed borders between modern-style states would misrepresent them. Potential misunderstanding: "Kingdom" in this chapter does not imply a bounded territorial state; it refers to a court with a changing network of claims and obligations.

Section 59.8

Shan Principalities, Northern Migration, and the Frontier Political World

North and east of the dry zone lay a world of Tai-speaking communities and principalities whose history is examined more fully in Chapter 54. Centers associated with Mohnyin and Mogaung in the north, Mong Mao on the Yunnan side, and valleys across the Shan plateau formed political networks that interacted continuously with Upper Burma. Burmese sources group these polities under the label "Shan," but the label obscures their variety: different rulers, different histories, shifting rivalries, and no common command.

Relations between Upper Burmese courts and Tai polities took several forms that should be distinguished. Migration brought Tai-speaking families into the dry zone and its margins over generations, as farmers, soldiers, and officials, and the presence of such families helps explain why ancestry labels for the brothers of Myinsaing are complicated. Military intervention involved Tai armies entering Upper Burma, whether as allies of a court or as independent invaders. Intermarriage tied ruling families together, so that a Tai ruler might be the kinsman of a Burmese king. Tribute and exchange involved gifts, recognition, and obligations that did not necessarily imply subordination. And political autonomy meant that many Tai principalities governed themselves while engaging with one or more larger courts.

Yunnan added a further dimension. First under the Yuan and then under the Ming, the Chinese court sought to organize the frontier by recognizing local rulers and by establishing offices and garrisons, with uneven success. Tai rulers on the Yunnan side, such as the leaders associated with Mong Mao, could be both partners and rivals of Chinese authority. A change in Chinese policy could therefore alter the military balance for Upper Burma by reshaping the opportunities and pressures facing northern Tai rulers.

The key analytical point is to avoid treating the Tai frontier as a unified ethnic or military bloc. An invasion from the north in one decade might be undertaken by a specific ruler pursuing his own aims; in another, Tai troops might serve an Upper Burmese king. Reading these events as the repeated assault of "the Shans" on "the Burmese" would flatten a complex political world and would misrepresent the many Tai-speaking people who lived within the dry zone as subjects, officials, and allies.

The Tai side of this relationship is itself known mostly through sources written later or from outside: Burmese chronicles, Chinese frontier records, and Tai chronicle traditions that were compiled in subsequent centuries. Each reflects a distinct perspective and agenda. The result is that the political history of Mohnyin, Mogaung, and Mong Mao in the fourteenth century can be reconstructed only in outline, and historians working on the frontier have emphasized the need to combine Burmese, Chinese, and Tai evidence, a method followed most fully in Fernquest's study of the Ava-Ming frontier.

Division III · Sections 59.9–59.12

Agriculture, Labor, Landholding, and Buddhist Society

Agricultural production, property and revenue, everyday life, and religious institutions

Section 59.9

Irrigated Rice Agriculture, the Kyaukse Plain, and Political Competition

A political history dominated by battles and reigns cannot explain why kingdom after kingdom arose within the same agricultural region. The explanation begins with irrigated rice. On the Kyaukse plain and in other dry-zone districts, channels fed by streams from the Shan plateau allowed reliable wet-rice cultivation in a landscape where rainfall alone was marginal. Many of the works that distributed this water, weirs, canals, and embankments, were already in place or in operation before the period of this chapter, though their construction history is discussed in Pagan-era scholarship and is not settled in detail. Maintaining them required organized labor, coordination among villages, and dependable authority over water use.

Agriculture supported political competition in specific ways. A district with dependable harvests produced grain for taxation, for provisioning troops, and for the support of monastic communities. It also produced people: cultivators who could be called to service and households that could be settled or resettled. A court that controlled such a district gained both revenue and military manpower, and a court that lost it lost both. This is why Myinsaing, Pinya, Sagaing, and Ava were all located near the productive core, and why the districts of Kyaukse recur in conflicts across the period.

The same dependence made agrarian societies vulnerable. Irrigation systems can be disrupted by flood, drought, or neglect; a failure of rains or a breach of an embankment could reduce yields sharply. War compounded these risks. Armies requisitioned grain and labor; campaigns drew men away from planting and harvest; fortifications and raids damaged channels and villages; and captives might be removed from their home districts altogether. A prolonged war could therefore reduce the very revenues that kings needed to sustain it. A ruler's incentive was to protect cultivated districts while also drawing on them, a tension that appears in the inscriptional evidence examined in Section 59.12.

Agricultural resilience also helps explain the persistence of the region. Because the productive base could recover once fighting ended and because monasteries and local lords retained records and claims over land, the plain could support a new court after an old one fell. Continued cultivation did not guarantee political stability. It made reconstruction possible and kept the region an attractive prize for ambitious commanders.

Section 59.10

Royal Land, Monastic Estates, Revenue, and Labor Obligations

Control of land and labor was the practical basis of royal power. Inscriptions of the Pagan and post-Pagan periods record donations of land, often paddy fields, and of dependents or workers, to monasteries and pagodas. Such donations could carry exemptions from some royal claims, which made them attractive to donors seeking religious merit and to institutions seeking security. The donor might be a king, a queen, a court official, or a local landholder; the recipient, a monastic community or a religious foundation with its own staff and tenants.

The relationship between crown and religious institutions was double-edged. Royal patronage of Buddhism was a core element of legitimate kingship, and kings who endowed monasteries gained prestige and the loyalty of the monastic communities. But land and labor donated to the Sangha were often removed from direct royal claim. As Michael Aung-Thwin has argued, the accumulation of such property could erode the crown's revenue base over generations. Scholars debate how far this occurred, how permanent exemptions were, and whether monastic estates were as fully insulated from royal demands as some interpretations suggest. For the period of this chapter, the evidence shows that monasteries and local elites held substantial resources that kings had to negotiate with and could not simply command.

A distinction is essential for using inscriptions properly: an actual grant is different from an idealized royal claim. A king's inscription may declare generosity and cosmic authority; an inscription recording a local donor's purchase and dedication of land documents a transaction in which specific plots, boundaries, and witnesses are named. The first tells historians about royal ideology; the second tells them about land tenure and local power. The chapter uses both, but does not treat the first as evidence of what a court controlled.

The relevant evidence also requires caution about institutions. Later institutional arrangements for monastic landholding, recorded in later centuries, should not be projected backward onto the fourteenth century without evidence. Contemporary donations show what was happening in the period; later compilations may reflect different systems. In this chapter, the consistent finding is that kings, local elites, and monastic communities all shared claims on the same agricultural resources, and that political authority therefore depended on bargains, grants, and personal ties, as much as on command.

Section 59.11

Farmers, Craftspeople, Soldiers, Captives, and the Social Consequences of Warfare

Most people in fourteenth-century Upper Burma were cultivators. They lived in villages, farmed irrigated and rain-fed fields, raised livestock, and paid dues and rendered service to patrons: a king, a local lord, or a monastery. Evidence about them is thin and indirect. Inscriptions record the transfer of land and dependents but seldom describe the daily life of those who worked the land; chronicles focus on rulers. Historians reconstruct rural life from the pattern of donations, the physical remains of villages and monastic complexes, and later comparative evidence, with appropriate caution.

Alongside farmers were craftspeople, boatmen, traders, and religious specialists. Potters, builders, and artisans supplied the demand created by temple construction and urban centers. Boatmen and merchants moved grain, salt, and other goods along the Irrawaddy and Myitnge and across the routes to the Shan plateau. Soldiers were drawn from cultivating households through service obligations, from lords' retinues, and from allies; in the period of rival courts, men could be called repeatedly to arms. Monks and nuns occupied a distinct and respected position, and monastic communities employed lay dependents.

Warfare reached these people in specific ways. Service obligations took men from fields at critical seasons. Requisitions of grain strained household stores. Fighting around towns could destroy crops and storage. Captives, taken in raids and campaigns, could be moved to new districts, assigned to a ruler or commander as dependents, or given to religious foundations; the movement of captives was a feature of mainland warfare and a means by which victors acquired labor. Hunger followed disruption, and famine risk rose when cultivation was interrupted. Inscriptional evidence of such hardship appears in Section 59.12.

Women's roles are visible chiefly in religious and economic contexts. Women appear as donors in inscriptions, owning or controlling property that they could dedicate to monasteries, and they appear within households and local economies as managers of farm labor, textile production, and trade. Evidence for women outside elite and donor contexts is limited, and the historian should avoid generalizing from elite women to all women. What the record does show is that women could hold property and act as religious patrons, and that their giving was part of the social response to the hardships of the period.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living through Political Fragmentation

For a household on the Kyaukse plain, the founding of a new capital or the rise of a rival court was not an abstract event. It meant a new set of claims on grain and labor, new officials, and the possibility of conscription. Yet daily rhythms continued: planting and harvest tied to the monsoon and the irrigation cycle, the maintenance of channels, the festivals and merit-making of the Buddhist calendar, and the obligations of kinship and neighborhood.

Historians must read carefully for what these lives involved. Donations by non-royal people, including women, suggest a wide base of religious participation. References to captives and dependents show that unfree status and forced movement were part of social life. Differences between districts, depending on proximity to rival courts and routes, produced uneven experiences: a village near a contested frontier faced different risks from one in a secure interior. The record is too sparse for numerical claims about population or mortality, and this chapter makes none.

Section 59.12

Theravada Buddhism, Monastic Patronage, and Religious Continuity

Buddhism was the common framework of religious life in Upper Burma throughout the period, and the Sangha its most durable institution. The Theravada tradition, with its Pali scriptural base and monastic discipline, had become established in the dry zone before this chapter begins; its regional development is traced in Chapter 53 and its Pagan-period institutions in Chapter 41 and in Than Tun's earlier studies of Buddhism in Burma between 1000 and 1300. After Pagan's political decline, monasteries continued to function, receiving land and labor, training monks, and preserving texts.

Royal and lay support took many forms: gifts of land and laborers, construction and repair of temples and monasteries, offerings of robes and food, and the commissioning of inscriptions to record merit. Rulers competed for religious prestige, since patronage of respected monks and monasteries conferred legitimacy. This competition could benefit religious institutions: rival courts at Pinya, Sagaing, and later Ava each had reasons to endow monasteries and to attract learned monks. Monastic communities also included varied traditions, with village-based monks and forest-dwelling communities that emphasized meditation and ascetic discipline; the balance among them shifted over time and is difficult to reconstruct in detail.

Religious continuity should not be mistaken for political neutrality. Monasteries held land and influence, had relationships with specific patrons, and could be drawn into disputes over succession. Yet their institutional life did not depend on any one court. A monastery endowed under one king could continue under another, as new rulers sought the merit and legitimacy of supporting established institutions. In this way Buddhist institutions provided a continuous thread across the political ruptures of the period, helping explain why successive courts adopted similar religious forms.

Buddhism also gave a vocabulary for understanding hardship. Merit-making, donation, and the transfer of merit to relatives and to all beings offered believers a response to war, famine, and loss. A 1375 inscription by the widow of Toniū Man, reproduced and translated by Than Tun (1959, pp. 129–130), recounts three military expeditions by Ava's king and the donor's food relief. It links religious gifts to the documented social consequences of warfare and shows a woman acting as a named donor.

Monastic life also had an educational dimension. Monasteries trained boys and young men in reading, in Pali, and in Buddhist texts, and they served as repositories of manuscripts and as centers for ordination and discipline. Because literacy in this period was concentrated in religious and court circles, these institutions were also the principal producers of the written culture, inscriptions included, on which historians now depend. This is a reminder that the evidence for political history is itself a product of religious institutions whose priorities were not primarily political.

Division IV · Sections 59.13–59.16

The Crisis of 1364 and the Emergence of Ava

Northern invasions, Thado Minbya, the new capital, and restoration of royal government

Section 59.13

The Northern Invasions and the Collapse of Pinya and Sagaing

By the middle of the fourteenth century, the two-court order of Pinya and Sagaing had lasted for about four decades, with periods of rivalry and cooperation. It ended in a crisis whose chronology is best given as 1359–1364. Jon Fernquest's study situates this crisis within the wider political upheaval of the Tai frontier zone in 1359–1368, when the political map of northern and eastern polities was being rearranged and when pressures from Yunnan, Tai principalities, and Upper Burma interacted.

Burmese chronicle tradition associates the invasions with northern Tai rulers from the Maw region and with leaders linked to Mohnyin, and records attacks on the Sagaing and Pinya region that culminated in the destruction or occupation of political centers around 1364. The details differ among sources, and historians should reconstruct events from multiple kinds of evidence: chronicles, inscriptions, and Chinese frontier records. Why did the northern frontier matter? Because the Tai principalities had long interacted with Upper Burma through trade, marriage, and war; in this crisis, the divided state of the dry zone made it a tempting and vulnerable target.

The northern frontier was not a single unified enemy. The invading forces came from specific polities with their own aims, and other Tai rulers had different relations with the Upper Burmese courts, some as allies. The invasions also did not represent the simple conquest of one ethnic group by another. Tai-speaking families already lived in the dry zone, courts had intermarried with northern houses, and some political actors shifted sides as circumstances changed. To understand 1364, historians must reconstruct a web of local rivalries, not a clash of two fixed peoples.

The consequences were severe. Political centers were damaged or occupied; the authority of the Pinya and Sagaing dynasties collapsed; agricultural districts suffered disruption as armies moved, requisitioned, and fought; and local elites and monasteries faced uncertainty about patronage and protection. At the same time, the crisis created an opportunity for a leader able to rally survivors and claim the inheritance of the old courts. That leader's emergence is the subject of the next section.

The sources for the crisis illustrate the problem of reconstructing military events from partial testimony. Chronicle traditions name particular invading leaders and campaigns, but they differ on sequence and detail, and the Chinese records that illuminate Tai-frontier politics in the same decades were written to explain the Chinese court's own frontier concerns. Historians therefore speak of a crisis spanning several years and involving several actors, rather than of a single invasion with a single commander. The outcome, in any case, was clear: by 1364 neither of the older courts could command the dry zone.

Section 59.14

Thado Minbya and the Foundation of Ava

Thado Minbya emerged from the Sagaing-Pinya political world as the figure who seized authority in the aftermath of the crisis. Chronicle tradition presents him as a claimant who traced royal descent and who gained the allegiance of survivors of the old courts; genealogical details vary between sources and should be treated as traditions, not secure facts. The sources agree on the outcome: he assumed kingship over the Upper Burmese core and chose to build a new capital not at the damaged centers of Pinya or Sagaing but at a site at the junction of the Irrawaddy and Myitnge, known to later tradition as Ava (Inwa).

The chronology requires two distinctions. The consolidation of royal power, the process by which Thado Minbya took authority after the destruction of Pinya and Sagaing, belongs to 1364. The foundation of the new capital is conventionally dated to 1365. These dates should not be merged into one undifferentiated moment: the first concerns political reunification of the surviving elements of the old courts under one claimant; the second concerns the physical and ritual creation of a capital city. Political authority preceded, and then was given monumental expression by, the new site.

The location had practical advantages. A capital at the Irrawaddy–Myitnge confluence gave command of river traffic on both waterways, access to the agricultural districts of Kyaukse and the surrounding plain, and a defensible position with natural water barriers. It was close to the old centers, allowing the new court to absorb their networks of officials, monasteries, and landholders, but distinct from them, marking a fresh start. The new capital's claims to legitimacy invoked Pagan: Ava's kings presented themselves as heirs to the old royal tradition, with Buddhist patronage and Pali-Sanskritic royal ideology, though their actual descent and base of power lay in the intervening courts.

Foundation was not consolidation. Thado Minbya's reign was short, conventionally ending in 1367, and the new capital's survival depended on what followed. The new kingdom had to secure the agricultural heartland against rivals, manage local elites and monasteries, deal with the northern frontier, and decide what to do about the south. The reign that did most to meet these needs, that of Swa Saw Ke, is examined in Section 59.15.

Early Ava's problems were immediate. A new king had to reassure monastic communities that their endowments would be respected, persuade local lords and surviving court officials to accept his claims, and secure the frontier against further northern incursions. The chronicle tradition credits Thado Minbya with campaigns in the north and with efforts to establish his authority, but his reign was short, and the work of making Ava a stable kingdom fell to those who came after him. This is one reason historians emphasize that the founding of the capital marked the start of a political project and not its completion.

Section 59.15

Swa Saw Ke and the Rebuilding of the Upper Burmese Kingdom

Swa Saw Ke, conventionally reigning from 1367 to 1400, is the ruler most associated with turning Ava from a new foundation into a functioning kingdom. The chronicle tradition presents him as a member of the Ava-Sagaing elite connected to Thado Minbya by descent or marriage, and credits him with energetic military and administrative leadership. His reign illustrates that Ava's stability was achieved over decades, through repeated measures, and was not an automatic result of the 1365 foundation.

Historians infer several strands of institutional rebuilding from inscriptions, chronicle traditions, and the pattern of later events, while acknowledging that the sources are fragmentary. The first was securing the agricultural heartland. Ava depended on the productive districts of Kyaukse and the surrounding plain, and the consolidation of royal claims there, together with the repair or maintenance of irrigation, was essential. Lacking comprehensive tax records, historians cannot measure the revenue the crown derived, but the repeated concern with these districts in the sources shows their priority.

The second strand was mobilizing manpower. The long campaigns of the reign, including the beginning of the war with Hanthawaddy in the 1380s, depended on the ability to call up soldiers, boatmen, and provisioners from cultivating communities and local lords. The third was managing local elites. Rather than governing each district directly, the court relied on appointed governors, many of them kinsmen or trusted followers, and on negotiated relationships with landholders and monastic communities. This was an effective method in the short term, but it created figures with independent military and economic resources who could become rivals or, in later generations, defect.

The fourth strand was religious patronage. Endowments to monasteries and support for learned monks reinforced legitimacy and secured the cooperation of influential institutions. The inscription of 1375 examined in Section 59.12 belongs to this world of donations and dependence. The fifth was frontier and external diplomacy. Swa Saw Ke had to balance relations with Tai polities to the north and east, with the Ming court's expanding presence in Yunnan, and with southern neighbors, using a mixture of marriage, gifts, recognition, and force. Chinese sources from the Ming period record contact with Ava from the later fourteenth century, and these can be consulted in the Ming Shi-lu translations for specific dated entries.

The reign's limits are as instructive as its achievements. Ava under Swa Saw Ke recovered the core of Upper Burma and projected force toward the south, but it did not create an administration that could reliably transmit authority beyond the dry zone, nor did it resolve the problem of succession. The death of the king in 1400 was followed by a short, disrupted reign of his son and a contested accession, a pattern that would recur. Rebuilding institutions did not by itself prevent the political competition that those institutions were designed to contain.

Section 59.16

Ava, the Shan Frontier, Ming China, and the Southern Kingdoms

Ava's position was defined by four sets of relationships: with the Tai frontier to the north and east, with Ming Yunnan, with the Mon-centered kingdom of Hanthawaddy in the south, and with the intermediate towns and corridors along the Irrawaddy and Sittang. Managing these relationships simultaneously was among Ava's chief political tasks.

A significant episode in the 1440s involved Ming campaigns against the Mong Mao polity and Ming pressure on Ava concerning Tai leaders seeking refuge or aid. Fernquest's published analysis identifies a Ming Shi-lu entry dated 11 March 1396 about Ava's complaints over frontier warfare, while Geoff Wade's 2004 study cites Ming instructions in 1447 calling on Ava and Mu-bang to provide military assistance against Mong Mao. These are identified dated episodes, not evidence that the Ming exercised direct administration throughout Upper Burma. The descriptions in this textbook are mediated through the named scholarship rather than newly verified translations of the Chinese originals.

It is essential to distinguish diplomatic recognition from effective administration. Chinese titles and offices offered to Ava's rulers and to Tai chiefs expressed Ming ideas of hierarchy, but they did not mean that Ming officials governed in Upper Burma, and Ava's rulers used diplomacy for their own purposes, to gain prestige, trade, and security against rivals. Developments in Yunnan nevertheless affected the northern frontier: the strengthening or weakening of Ming power altered the choices of Tai rulers, who might move toward Ava, the Ming, or each other.

Southward, Ava looked toward the Irrawaddy and Sittang corridors. Prome on the Irrawaddy and Toungoo on the Sittang were strategically placed towns that could serve as forward bases or as semi-independent centers; the fortunes of Ava's wars with Hanthawaddy depended in part on controlling them. Hanthawaddy, a kingdom with its own political traditions, its own commercial base on the coast, and its own claims to legitimacy, was not a rebellious province of Ava but an independent rival, a point developed in Section 59.17 and in Chapter 60. Ava's southern relations thus resemble its northern ones: they involved competing centers and negotiated authority, not a simple hierarchy.

The records of tribute and recognition also need to be read in both directions. Ming entries recording missions from Ava use the Chinese imperial vocabulary of submission and office; Burmese narratives describe royal exchange and diplomacy differently, if at all. Ava's rulers could seek trade, prestige, and strategic security without accepting that Ming claims corresponded to full political control. The 11 March 1396 Ming Shi-lu reference, identified by Fernquest, is a specific anchor for teaching this distinction; the original Chinese text has not been independently collated for this chapter.

Division V · Sections 59.17–59.20

Ava, Hanthawaddy, and the Fifteenth-Century Political Order

Extended warfare, succession, frontier politics, and the legacy of post-Pagan transformation

Section 59.17

Ava and Hanthawaddy: The Forty Years' War and the Limits of Reunification

The conflict between Ava and Hanthawaddy, conventionally dated from about 1385 to 1424 and known as the Forty Years' War, dominated the political life of both kingdoms for a generation and more. The conventional dates frame a long sequence, not a single continuous war: the sources describe separate campaigns, intervals, truces, and renewals, and historians use the label as a convenient shorthand. Hanthawaddy's own history is the subject of Chapter 60; here the southern kingdom appears as Ava's rival.

The principal figures were, on Ava's side, Swa Saw Ke, his successor Minkhaung I, and Minkhaung's son Minye Kyawswa, a celebrated commander in the chronicle tradition; and on Hanthawaddy's side, Razadarit, whose long reign is remembered for survival and counterattack. The war began with Ava's attempt, under Swa Saw Ke, to extend its power into the south. Its motives are given in the chronicles in dynastic terms, but plausible additional motives include access to the coast and its trade, control of corridors, and the prestige of reuniting the Irrawaddy and delta regions.

The geography of the war shaped its course. The Irrawaddy provided a route for Ava's boats and armies; fortified towns such as Prome and Toungoo, and delta and riverine strongholds, controlled the approaches; and the seasonal rhythms of the monsoon and the rice cycle governed when campaigns could be mounted. Recruitment from cultivating communities, the provisioning of armies, and alliances with intermediary lords were essential. Neither kingdom could easily hold what it conquered: garrison towns could be besieged and retaken, local lords changed sides, and logistics over long river lines were difficult. The death of Minye Kyawswa in 1415 during the campaign, a major blow for Ava, is recorded as a landmark in the chronicle tradition.

The war ended without a decisive victory. By about 1424 the exhausted kingdoms settled into a more guarded relationship, with neither able to establish durable dominance over the other. The result is central to the chapter's argument: even with productive land and inherited institutions, Ava could not reunify the Irrawaddy and Mon-centered southern worlds. Rival political systems with their own resources and legitimacy resisted conquest, and the costs of the effort drained Ava's resources. Section 59.18 turns to the internal politics that followed.

Section 59.18

Succession, Mohnyin Thado, and Ava's Mid-Fifteenth-Century Politics

The end of the Forty Years' War did not bring stability. Ava's politics in the 1420s and after were dominated by succession problems. Minkhaung I died in 1421, according to the chronicle tradition, and the following years saw a rapid succession of rulers, intrigues within the court, and rivalries among princes and provincial governors. The practice of assigning important districts to kinsmen, useful in the days of Swa Saw Ke, produced figures with their own followings and ambitions, and the absence of a clear rule of succession meant that deaths often turned into struggles.

In 1426, Mohnyin Thado took the throne at Ava. Chronicle tradition associates him with the northern Tai political world of Mohnyin, and his accession illustrates the weight of northern Tai politics in Ava's succession, even as he ruled within the Burmese Buddhist royal tradition. The reign, conventionally ending in 1439, is noteworthy for the interplay between a court at Ava and northern Tai connections, and for the continuing question of how much authority the center held over dependent territories. The label "Shan" again requires caution: Mohnyin Thado's ties did not make Ava a Shan state, and the court continued to patronize Buddhist institutions and to use Burmese-language administration.

Later fifteenth-century rulers, notably Narapati, who reigned from the early 1440s (conventionally to the late 1460s), worked to restore royal authority and manage the problems of the frontier, including the Ming–Mong Mao conflict discussed in Section 59.16. Their reigns show cycles of recovery and renewed difficulty. Rulers could mobilize troops and win victories, issue donations, and receive recognition from neighbors, but succession struggles, conflicts between court factions, and the shifting allegiances of northern and southern lords repeatedly undermined consolidation.

The extent to which Ava recovered effective control over its dependent territories should not be overstated. Ava's kings claimed authority over a wide range of districts and lords, and in good years many of them acknowledged it. But recognition was negotiated and conditional, and control weakened with distance from the dry-zone core. Because chronicle traditions tend to present reign after reign of royal claims, historians must look to inscriptions and external records to judge what the court actually commanded. The pattern of the mid-century is one of persistence of institutions and recurrent political fragility.

The mid-century reigns are also a reminder of how closely Ava's internal politics were tied to its frontiers. The Ming campaigns against Mong Mao in the 1430s and 1440s altered the northern balance, and Ava's rulers had to decide how far to shelter refugees, how to respond to Chinese demands, and how to protect their northern districts. At the same time, the southern frontier remained alive, as Hanthawaddy and the intermediate towns kept Ava's armies and officials engaged. A court that had to manage northern, southern, and internal rivalries at once had little margin for the slow work of administrative consolidation.

Section 59.19

Ava around 1500: Regional Autonomy, Cultural Continuity, and Political Limits

Toward the close of the fifteenth century, Ava remained a prestigious royal capital with a productive heartland, but its political reach had become uneven. Southern centers such as Prome and Toungoo increasingly acted with a degree of autonomy, governed by officials or princes who held substantial local power and could choose how closely to align with Ava. The northern frontier remained a source of challenge, with Tai rulers able to exert pressure and to intervene in Ava's affairs. Royal succession problems continued to produce instability.

This evaluation requires balance. It would be a mistake to read the situation around 1500 as inevitably leading to the fall of Ava in 1527; that event and the sixteenth-century Toungoo conquests belong to Chapter 84 and are not examined here. Contemporaries did not know the outcome, and Ava retained real strengths: agricultural wealth, an established ritual and administrative tradition, and prestige derived from its association with Pagan's legacy. The limits were also real. Kings could claim authority beyond the areas they directly controlled, but claims and control were different, and the autonomy of southern centers is a good example.

Cultural and religious life remained vigorous. Buddhist monastic communities continued to teach, preserve texts, and receive royal and lay patronage. The Ava period is associated with a flourishing of Burmese-language monastic literature and poetry, with scholar-monks writing in Burmese as well as in Pali, a development that suggests the confidence of a literate religious culture even in a politically divided age. Historians note that the names and dates of individual authors and works require care, since later tradition can be uncertain, but the general pattern of continuing scholarship is well established.

The condition of Ava around 1500 thus reflects the chapter's central paradox. A kingdom with a productive agricultural heartland and strong inherited traditions had repeatedly rebuilt royal authority, yet each reconstruction had been limited by succession disputes, warfare, and the autonomy of regional centers. The institutions that had survived Pagan's fragmentation, irrigated agriculture, monastic patronage, and royal ritual, were still in place; the capacity to bind distant regions to a single court remained unsettled. The synthesis in Section 59.20 evaluates what this pattern means.

The evidence for the autonomy of Prome and Toungoo is largely chronicle-based and concerns princely appointments, local lords, and episodes of rebellion or realignment in the later fifteenth century. These accounts show that governors of such towns could command local followings, control river and road routes, and choose their moment to defy or support Ava. Toungoo's rise as a regional power belongs to the very end of the period and the sixteenth century, and its full story is the subject of Chapter 84. For the purposes of this chapter, the important point is that Ava's dependent territories were bound to it by negotiation and by the prestige of its court, rather than by an administrative apparatus capable of enforcing obedience.

Section 59.20

After Pagan: Fragmentation, Political Reconstruction, and the Transformation of Upper Burma

The period from about 1280 to 1500 produced four successive centers of royal authority in Upper Burma: Myinsaing, Pinya, Sagaing, and Ava. Their emergence followed a recognizable logic. The agricultural region of the dry zone, especially Kyaukse, continued to produce the grain and manpower on which courts depended. Buddhist institutions continued to supply legitimacy and continuity. Rival commanders and dynastic lines competed for control of these resources, and each court, in turn, drew on inherited political language to claim authority.

What prevented a return to a uniformly governed empire? Several factors worked together. The fiscal and landholding arrangements inherited from Pagan left crowns dependent on negotiated relationships with monasteries and local elites. Warfare, both within Upper Burma and against Tai and southern neighbors, drained resources and disrupted cultivation. Tai frontier politics introduced powerful regional actors with interests of their own, and Ming Yunnan changed the strategic environment. Succession practices produced recurrent crises. And the southern kingdoms, especially Hanthawaddy, had their own resources and legitimacy.

The question of how to characterize the period has produced differing judgments. Calling it simply a decline overlooks the vitality of religious and literary life, the rebuilding of royal institutions, and the persistence of agriculture. Calling it simply reconstruction underplays the real costs of warfare and the failure to restore Pagan's reach. The most defensible reading is a long-term transformation: a reconfiguration of political authority around a more decentralized, negotiated order, resting on the same agrarian and religious foundations.

The governing question of the chapter can now be answered directly. Upper Burma moved from Pagan's fragmentation to Ava through a sequence of regional commanders and rival courts, each building on the same agricultural region and Buddhist institutions, and each partly constrained by them. Successive rulers struggled to restore durable authority not because the region lacked resources but because the arrangements through which those resources were held, shared with monasteries, local elites, and dependent lords, made command difficult, because warfare consumed what it was meant to protect, and because dynastic succession and frontier politics repeatedly reopened questions of legitimacy.

Two cautions apply to this conclusion. First, the story should not be told as a Burmese-versus-Shan narrative. Tai-speaking rulers, officials, soldiers, and families were participants in the political life of the dry zone throughout the period, and the modern ethnic categories that organize later national histories do not describe the loyalties and alliances of these centuries well. Second, the story should not be told as a prelude to what followed. Later developments, including the sixteenth-century Toungoo conquests (Chapter 84) and the full history of Hanthawaddy and Mon society (Chapter 60), grew out of this world, but they were not its destiny, and people living in 1450 or 1500 faced open choices and uncertain outcomes.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the fragmentation of Pagan did not eliminate the agricultural, political, and Buddhist institutions developed in Upper Burma. Competing rulers drew on those inherited resources to establish Myinsaing, Pinya, Sagaing, and eventually Ava. Their success depended on control over cultivated land, manpower, religious patronage, and alliances with regional elites, while Shan/Tai frontier politics, repeated warfare, and dynastic succession prevented the restoration of a uniformly governed empire.

The evidence for the period is uneven. Royal chronicles, notably U Kala's Maha Yazawin and the Hmannan Maha Yazawindawgyi, provide a continuous but retrospective narrative; inscriptions provide contemporary but fragmentary evidence; and Yuan and Ming records offer external dating and frontier context. Reign dates, genealogies, and ethnic labels are therefore often conventional and should be tested, not assumed.

Pagan's weakening in the late thirteenth century followed Yuan military pressure, with 1277 and 1287 as turning points. The invasion was a major factor but not automatically the sole cause: debates among historians, including arguments about fiscal erosion from religious landholding and comparative cycles of mainland states, show that internal conditions also mattered. The year 1287 marked a political rupture, not the instant disappearance of Pagan society.

The brothers Athinkhaya, Yazathingyan, and Thihathu rose from regional military command to control Myinsaing and, according to chronicle tradition, deposed Kyawswa in 1297. Their conventional description as "Shan" reports reputed ancestry but not the complexity of their political world, which combined Tai and Burmese connections within a Burmese Buddhist court culture. The Yuan intervention of about 1300–1303 failed to dislodge them, and its end did not restore Pagan.

Thihathu's foundation of Pinya (conventionally 1313) re-established kingship, and Saw Yun's Sagaing (c. 1315–1316) divided royal authority within a single agricultural region. Both courts claimed legitimacy from Pagan, but their effective control depended on the districts and elites that supplied them. Tai principalities such as Mohnyin, Mogaung, and Mong Mao interacted with these courts through migration, intervention, intermarriage, tribute, and autonomy, and cannot be treated as a unified bloc.

Agriculture, landholding, and religious institutions explain why kingdoms repeatedly formed in the same place. Irrigated rice in Kyaukse and nearby districts supported revenue and military mobilization, while war could disrupt cultivation. Kings, local elites, and monasteries shared claims on land and labor. Everyday life, including the experiences of farmers, artisans, soldiers, captives, and women, was shaped by service obligations and war, as the 1375 inscription analyzed by Than Tun illustrates. Theravada Buddhist institutions provided continuity across political ruptures.

The crisis of 1359–1364 ended the Pinya–Sagaing order. Thado Minbya consolidated power in 1364 and Ava was founded in 1365, two events that should be kept distinct. Swa Saw Ke's reign (conventionally 1367–1400) was a process of institutional rebuilding involving the agricultural heartland, manpower, local elites, religious patronage, and frontier diplomacy. Ava's relations with the Tai frontier, Ming Yunnan, and the southern kingdoms combined diplomacy and force, and recognition was not administration.

The Forty Years' War with Hanthawaddy (conventionally c. 1385–1424) showed the limits of reunification. Neither kingdom could dominate the other, and the costs drained both. Mid-century succession struggles, the accession of Mohnyin Thado in 1426, and the efforts of rulers such as Narapati reveal persistent fragility. Around 1500 Ava retained prestige and a vigorous religious and literary culture, but southern centers such as Prome and Toungoo were increasingly autonomous, setting the stage for developments treated in Chapters 60 and 84.

The best overall interpretation is long-term transformation. The period was neither simple decline nor simple reconstruction, but a reconfiguration of authority in which the same agrarian and religious foundations supported repeated state-building under conditions that favored negotiated, decentralized power. Understanding this pattern is the key to the chapter's governing question: why successive rulers struggled to restore durable authority despite agricultural resources and inherited institutions.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 59 matters because it shows how political authority can be rebuilt repeatedly from the same foundations without becoming stable. The agricultural and religious institutions of Upper Burma survived Pagan's fall, and they made possible Myinsaing, Pinya, Sagaing, and Ava. The same institutions, with their negotiated claims on land and labor, constrained what any court could command.

The chapter also connects Upper Burma to wider processes. The Yuan and Ming courts, Tai principalities, and the Mon-centered south all shaped Ava's choices, and the pattern of fragmentation and reconstruction resembles developments elsewhere on the mainland, as Chapters 54 through 58 and 60 and 61 show. Treating Upper Burma as an actor in these networks, not a passive recipient of outside pressures, is consistent with the textbook's approach.

What survived beyond 1500 includes the dry-zone core, Buddhist monastic learning, and the problem of binding distant regions to a center. Later chapters, including Chapters 84 and 85 on the Toungoo empires, show how new rulers addressed that problem differently. Understanding the earlier period helps students avoid reading later outcomes back into it.

Finally, the chapter models a way of doing history: separating contemporary evidence from retrospective tradition, labeling conventional dates, resisting modern ethnic categories, and following everyday life beneath court politics.
