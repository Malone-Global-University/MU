Cave Art, Burials, Tools, and Symbolic Worlds

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

A hand pressed against limestone rock, pigment blown around its edges, leaves an outline that has lasted for tens of thousands of years. The hand that made it is gone, along with the name of the person who owned it, the words that person spoke, and whatever story, ritual, or memory the image was meant to carry. What remains is the outline itself: durable, silent, and open to more than one interpretation.

Deep within limestone caves on the island of Sulawesi, in chambers reachable only by crawling through narrow passages or climbing steep cliff faces, archaeologists have documented hundreds of images made by human hands during the Late Pleistocene: stencilled outlines of hands, some missing fingers or parts of fingers; paintings of wild pigs and dwarf buffalo rendered with careful attention to posture and form; and, more rarely, scenes in which animal and part-human figures appear together in what some researchers have described as narrative compositions. Similar discoveries, though often less extensively dated or published, come from caves and rockshelters on Borneo and from scattered sites elsewhere in island and mainland Southeast Asia. Alongside these painted and stencilled images, archaeologists working in caves and open-air sites across the region have recovered deliberately buried human remains, shell and stone ornaments perforated for stringing, ochre fragments worn down through use, and stone, bone, and shell tools shaped with evident skill and, in some cases, considerable planning.

This evidence raises a set of questions that this chapter treats as its central concern. What can a hand stencil, a burial, a bead, or a stone tool actually demonstrate about the people who made it? What separates a description of physical evidence from an interpretation of what that evidence meant to the people who created it? When historians and archaeologists speak of "symbolic behavior," what precisely are they claiming, and what are they not in a position to claim?

These are not merely abstract methodological puzzles. They matter because popular writing about early rock art and burial practices frequently moves very quickly from observation to interpretation, describing a painted scene as though it were a photograph of a remembered event, or describing a burial as proof of a specific religious belief, without acknowledging how much interpretive work stands between the physical evidence and the confident claim. This chapter asks students to slow down at exactly that point: to notice the gap between what a hand stencil, an ochre-stained tool, or a carefully positioned skeleton can establish and what a historian or archaeologist infers from it, and to treat that gap not as a failure of the evidence but as an honest and permanent feature of archaeological reasoning about people who left no writing behind.

The chapter also has a comparative and historiographical purpose. For much of the twentieth century, discussions of early human symbolic behavior, cave painting, ornamentation, deliberate burial, were dominated by evidence from Europe, particularly the painted caves of France and Spain such as Lascaux and Altamira. This evidence supported an influential narrative describing a sudden "creative explosion" or "human revolution," in which fully modern symbolic capacities were thought to have appeared abruptly, often associated narrowly with the ancestors of later European populations. Discoveries made in Southeast Asia over the past several decades, particularly on Sulawesi, have complicated this picture substantially, showing that image-making, ornamentation, and other behaviors once treated as distinctively European appeared, in some cases, at comparably early dates on the far side of the Wallace Line, thousands of kilometers from the caves that had previously anchored the story. This chapter situates Southeast Asian evidence within that larger, still-developing global conversation, while resisting the temptation to replace one triumphalist story, in which Europe was first, with another, in which a single Southeast Asian site becomes the new "origin point" for human creativity. No single cave, however spectacular its imagery, can bear that interpretive weight.

This chapter also marks a deliberate shift in the textbook's argument. Chapter 6 through Chapter 8 traced the presence of various hominin and early human populations in the region; Chapter 9 examined what population genetics can and cannot establish about ancestry and interaction among these groups. This chapter turns from questions of who was present to questions of what those communities did: how they used caves, what they made, how they treated their dead, how they adorned their bodies, and how they organized technological knowledge and, potentially, social relationships across the landscape. Where Chapter 9 emphasized ancestry and population history, Chapter 10 emphasizes behavior, evidenced primarily through archaeology rather than genetics.

Finally, this chapter deliberately stops short of a full treatment of one of the region's best-known archaeological traditions, the Hoabinhian, which is examined in its own right in Chapter 11. Some of the tools and technologies discussed here overlap chronologically and geographically with Hoabinhian assemblages, but this chapter treats them as evidence for social and symbolic life in the Late Pleistocene generally, leaving the specific question of what "Hoabinhian" means as a category, its geographic range, and its place in debates over regional technological tradition, for the chapter that follows.

By the end of this chapter, students should be equipped not simply with a catalog of impressive discoveries, but with a disciplined way of thinking about what physical evidence for behavior can and cannot tell historians about the inner, social, and symbolic lives of people who lived tens of thousands of years before any written record existed anywhere in the world.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish between archaeological evidence for a behavior and an interpretation of that behavior's meaning.
  2. Explain why caves and rockshelters preserve unusually rich Late Pleistocene evidence while accounting for the resulting preservation bias.
  3. Describe the major rock art discoveries of Sulawesi and Borneo, including dating methods and the chronological uncertainties involved.
  4. Analyze depicted animal and human or part-human figures without overstating what can be established about narrative meaning or intent.
  5. Assess the significance of ochre, beads, shells, and other ornamental objects for understanding personal display and possible social communication.
  6. Evaluate what deliberate burial practices can and cannot demonstrate about social relationships and concepts of death.
  7. Explain how stone, bone, and shell tool technologies serve as evidence for planning, skill transmission, and local adaptation.
  8. Connect archaeological assemblages to coastal, island, and forest subsistence strategies without resorting to environmental determinism.
  9. Interpret evidence for mobility, contact, and exchange between Late Pleistocene communities with appropriate caution regarding identity.
  10. Critique the historical concept of "behavioral modernity" and explain why many specialists now favor an uneven, cumulative model of symbolic development.
  11. Compare regional variation in archaeological evidence across mainland and island Southeast Asia.
  12. Apply careful, evidence-based reasoning when confronted with claims about the meaning of prehistoric art, ornament, or burial.

Historical Context

By the period this chapter addresses, roughly the final tens of thousands of years of the Late Pleistocene before the onset of the Holocene around 11,700 years ago, the human landscape of Southeast Asia already had a long and complicated history. Chapters 6 and 7 examined the deep antiquity of hominin occupation in the region, including Homo erectus populations on Java and the distinctive, geographically restricted populations represented by Homo floresiensis on Flores and Homo luzonensis on Luzon. Chapter 8 traced the arrival and spread of anatomically modern Homo sapiens across mainland and island Southeast Asia, a process that unfolded over a long span of time, along routes that remain the subject of ongoing research and revision, and evidenced at sites such as Tam Pà Ling in northern Laos by at least around 68,000 years ago, with other sites elsewhere in the region contributing additional, sometimes contested, evidence. Chapter 9 then examined what ancient and modern DNA can and cannot reveal about the relationships among these populations, including interactions with Denisovan-related groups, while cautioning against equating genetic ancestry with later ethnic or cultural identity.

This chapter picks up the story at the point where the evidentiary record shifts in character. Where genetics can trace biological ancestry and population movement, it can say very little about what people believed, valued, communicated, or did with their hands and their communities on a daily basis. For that kind of evidence, archaeologists turn to the durable material traces of behavior: the pigments applied to cave walls, the objects placed with the dead, the beads strung and worn, and the tools shaped, used, and sometimes deliberately curated. These forms of evidence are unevenly distributed across the region, concentrated disproportionately in caves and rockshelters where preservation conditions are unusually favorable, and they are unevenly studied, with some areas, particularly parts of Sulawesi and Borneo, having received intensive archaeological attention in recent decades while other areas remain comparatively under-researched.

It is important to state clearly what this chapter does not attempt. It does not attempt to identify the specific religious beliefs, languages, ethnic identities, or social structures of Late Pleistocene Southeast Asian communities, because the evidence does not support such specific claims. It does not treat the region's Late Pleistocene inhabitants as an early, incomplete version of later, named Southeast Asian peoples, since doing so would project later identities backward onto populations who lived tens of thousands of years before any of the language families, states, or ethnic categories discussed in later chapters existed in a form we could recognize. Rather, this chapter treats the material evidence as a body of testimony that must be read carefully, on its own terms, informed by controlled excavation, secure dating, and comparative analysis, while remaining honest about the limits of what physical remains can tell us about the inner and social lives of people who left no written record.

Section 10.1

Reading Behavior from Material Remains

Archaeology dealing with periods before writing must reconstruct behavior indirectly, through objects, marks, and spatial patterns left behind by activities that were never intended as records for future observers. A hand stencil was not created to inform archaeologists forty thousand years later that people occupied a particular cave; it was made for reasons specific to the people who made it, reasons that are not stated anywhere in the surviving evidence. This basic condition shapes everything in this chapter.

It is useful to distinguish three different levels of claim that archaeologists make about material evidence. The first is a claim about the physical evidence itself: a particular pigment was applied to a particular rock surface in a particular way, at some point before an overlying mineral crust formed. This kind of claim, when supported by careful excavation, laboratory analysis, and appropriate dating methods, can be established with reasonable confidence. The second is a claim about the behavior that produced the evidence: someone deliberately selected this location, prepared or obtained pigment, and applied it in a controlled manner, rather than the mark resulting from an accident or a natural process. This kind of claim usually requires careful argument, since natural staining, insect activity, and other non-human processes can sometimes mimic the appearance of deliberate marks. The third, and most demanding, is a claim about the meaning or purpose behind the behavior: what the image was intended to represent, what social or ritual function it served, or what beliefs motivated its creation. This kind of claim is the most difficult to support, because meaning is rarely legible directly from the physical trace alone.

This chapter repeatedly returns to this three-part distinction. A hand stencil demonstrates, with a high degree of confidence, that a hand was placed against a wall and that pigment was deliberately applied around it. It demonstrates, with somewhat less certainty, that the act carried some significance to the people who performed and observed it, since the same result could in principle be produced accidentally, though the frequency, placement, and careful execution of many stencils argue against pure accident. It does not, on its own, demonstrate what that significance was: whether the stencil marked a rite of passage, recorded a presence, served as a form of communication among specific individuals, or held meanings entirely disconnected from any of these modern guesses. Historians and archaeologists who move too quickly from the first level of claim to the third risk mistaking their own speculation for established fact.

This does not mean that interpretation should be avoided. Interpretation is a necessary and valuable part of archaeological reasoning, and this chapter will engage with serious scholarly interpretations of rock art, burial, and ornamentation throughout. The discipline required is not to avoid interpretation but to label it honestly, distinguishing between what the evidence directly shows and what a researcher, however well-informed, proposes as a plausible reading of that evidence.

Section 10.2

Caves as Archaeological Archives

A striking proportion of the evidence examined in this chapter comes from caves and rockshelters, particularly those formed within limestone karst landscapes such as the Maros-Pangkep region of southern Sulawesi and the Sangkulirang-Mangkalihat karst of eastern Borneo. This concentration of evidence reflects preservation conditions rather than an accurate picture of where Late Pleistocene people spent most of their time.

Caves offer several conditions favorable to the survival of archaeological material. Rock surfaces sheltered from direct rainfall and sunlight can preserve pigment for tens of thousands of years, particularly where mineral crusts later form over painted images, sealing them against further weathering while also, usefully for archaeologists, providing material that can itself be dated. Cave floor sediments, protected from surface erosion, can preserve stratified sequences of deposits, tools, food remains, and burials in relatively undisturbed order, allowing archaeologists to establish relative chronology through careful excavation. Limestone environments also frequently produce the calcium carbonate deposits used in uranium-series dating, discussed further in Section 10.3, giving archaeologists a valuable independent method for estimating the age of associated art and deposits.

These same conditions, however, create a significant preservation bias. Open-air sites, riverbank camps, coastal settlements, and other locations where people plausibly spent a great deal of time have generally left far less durable evidence, since organic materials decay more readily in exposed conditions and since many such locations have long since been altered by erosion, flooding, sea-level change, or later human activity. It would therefore be a serious error to conclude that Late Pleistocene Southeast Asian communities lived primarily or even substantially inside caves. Ethnographic and archaeological evidence from many mobile hunting, gathering, and fishing societies indicates that caves and rockshelters have more commonly served as temporary shelters, workshops, storage locations, or places for specific activities, including but not limited to image-making and burial, rather than as year-round or exclusive dwellings. The richness of cave archaeology reflects what survived to be found, not necessarily what was most central to Late Pleistocene daily life.

Recognizing this bias matters for the rest of the chapter. When this textbook discusses hand stencils, burials, or tool assemblages recovered from cave contexts, students should understand that these represent a disproportionately visible fraction of a much broader, and largely invisible, range of Late Pleistocene activity that occurred across open landscapes, coastlines, and forests that have left far fewer traces for archaeologists to recover.

Section 10.3

The Earliest Rock Art of Island Southeast Asia

Among the most significant contributions Southeast Asian archaeology has made to the global study of early human behavior is the documentation of rock art in the limestone caves of Sulawesi and, to a somewhat lesser extent, Borneo. This section examines what has been found, how it has been dated, and what those dates can and cannot establish.

The Maros-Pangkep karst of southern Sulawesi contains hundreds of documented rock art panels distributed across dozens of cave and rockshelter sites. The imagery includes hand stencils, produced by pressing a hand against the rock and applying pigment, typically by spitting or blowing it from the mouth, around the hand to leave a negative silhouette; figurative depictions of local animal species, including the Sulawesi warty pig and the dwarf buffalo known as the anoa; and, more rarely, composite scenes in which multiple figures, sometimes including therianthropic, or part-human, part-animal, forms, appear together. Similar hand stencils and figurative imagery have been documented in the Sangkulirang-Mangkalihat karst of eastern Borneo, where ongoing research continues to refine the local chronology. More recently, a hand stencil has also been documented at Liang Metanduno on Muna Island, off the southeastern coast of Sulawesi, a location outside the intensively studied Maros-Pangkep karst that has considerably extended both the geographic range and the demonstrated antiquity of the region's dated rock art.

Establishing the age of this art has depended heavily on uranium-series dating, a method that measures the radioactive decay of uranium isotopes within thin mineral crusts, sometimes called cave "popcorn" or coralloid speleothems, that form naturally over painted surfaces after the pigment has been applied. Because the mineral crust forms after the painting, dating the crust provides a minimum age for the art beneath it: the painting must be at least as old as the overlying crust, though it could, in principle, be considerably older. Where a mineral crust also underlies a painted layer, a maximum age can sometimes be established as well, bracketing the art's true age between two dated layers. A refined version of this technique, laser-ablation uranium-series imaging, allows researchers to sample and date extremely thin layers of mineral crust with greater spatial precision than earlier methods, and its application to previously dated Sulawesi panels has in some cases pushed minimum age estimates significantly earlier than initial published figures. Applying uranium-series methods to samples from Sulawesi and, most recently, Muna Island has produced a developing sequence of minimum age estimates: a hand stencil at Liang Metanduno on Muna Island at least approximately 67,800 years old, currently the oldest such constraint documented in the region; a newly described composite scene of human-like figures and a pig at Leang Karampuang, dated using laser-ablation imaging to at least approximately 51,200 years old; the composite scene at Leang Bulu' Sipong 4, revised through the same improved method from an original estimate of approximately 43,900 years to at least approximately 48,000 years old; a painted depiction of a pig at Leang Tedongnge at least 45,500 years old; and hand stencils at Leang Timpuseng at least approximately 39,900 years old. These are, it is important to stress, minimum ages derived from particular dating methods applied to particular samples; they establish a lower bound on antiquity rather than a precise date of creation, and the substantial revision of the Bulu' Sipong 4 estimate through improved methodology is itself a reminder that even well-published minimum ages remain provisional and subject to change as dating techniques continue to improve.

These dates matter historically because they are comparable to, and in several cases considerably older than, the best-documented early rock art from Upper Paleolithic Europe, long treated in much scholarship as the benchmark for early human image-making. This comparison does not mean that Sulawesi or Muna Island should simply replace southwestern Europe as a new single point of origin for representational art; the global archaeological record for early image-making remains incomplete, unevenly preserved, and unevenly investigated across many regions of the world, and it is likely that further discoveries, in Southeast Asia and elsewhere, will continue to reshape this picture, exactly as the 2026 Muna Island discovery reshaped a chronology that had seemed comparatively settled only a few years earlier. What the Sulawesi and Muna Island evidence does establish clearly is that the capacity and inclination to create durable pigment images on rock surfaces was not confined to any single region, population, or even a single well-studied karst landscape during the Late Pleistocene, and that image-making traditions were already well established in island Southeast Asia by a very early date, with the Muna Island discovery in particular showing that this capacity extended well beyond the intensively investigated caves of Maros-Pangkep.

Section 10.4

Animals, Human Figures, and Narrative Images

Beyond hand stencils, the most discussed category of Late Pleistocene rock art from Sulawesi involves depictions of animals and, more rarely, human or part-human figures, sometimes arranged in apparent scenes involving multiple figures. This section examines what can responsibly be said about such imagery.

Painted animal figures at Sulawesi sites depict local fauna, most prominently the Sulawesi warty pig and the anoa, rendered with attention to characteristic body shape, posture, and, in some cases, distinguishing anatomical features. The choice of subject matter, animals that were locally present and presumably significant as sources of food or as culturally meaningful creatures, is itself informative: it suggests that the imagery drew on the immediate environment and experience of the people who made it, rather than depicting imported or imagined creatures unconnected to daily life. Beyond this general observation, however, claims about why particular animals were chosen, what emotional or symbolic associations they carried, or what specific event, if any, a given image commemorated, move beyond what the image alone can establish.

Two composite scenes from the Maros-Pangkep karst have attracted particular scholarly and popular attention: the panel at Leang Bulu' Sipong 4 and, following its dating and publication, the panel at Leang Karampuang, which now carries the earlier minimum age of the two, discussed in Section 10.3. The Bulu' Sipong 4 panel includes painted figures that combine human and animal characteristics, described by researchers as therianthropes, positioned near depictions of animals interpreted as a wild pig and dwarf buffalo. The Karampuang panel is different in kind: it presents human-like, or anthropomorphic, figures, without the animal features that define a therianthrope, apparently interacting with a pig. Researchers involved in publishing the Karampuang find have described it as the oldest currently known surviving example of a composed, narrative-style scene, and some popular accounts have gone further still, describing such panels as proof of the world's oldest storytelling. This textbook treats such claims with considerable caution. That a panel presents multiple figures in apparent spatial relationship to one another is a defensible observation from the image itself. That a panel depicts a specific narrative sequence, an episode with a beginning, middle, and end intended to be "read" in order, is a more ambitious interpretive claim, one that assumes a great deal about the artist's intent and about how the panel's original viewers would have understood it, assumptions that cannot be verified from the paint alone. Similarly, describing a panel as evidence for "storytelling" implies a specific communicative practice, verbal narrative accompanying or explaining the image, that the image cannot directly confirm; these panels demonstrate that their makers were capable of combining multiple figures into a single deliberate composition, which is itself a historically significant finding, without requiring the additional and less certain claim that a specific story was being told or that a single artist's compositional intent can be recovered directly from the surviving paint.

Therianthropic imagery, figures that merge human and animal features, deserves particular caution. Such imagery has sometimes been interpreted, drawing on ethnographic analogy with more recent societies, as evidence for beliefs involving transformation between human and animal states, shamanic practice, or particular cosmological ideas. These interpretations are not unreasonable as hypotheses, since therianthropic imagery does recur in various forms across many later documented cultures with such beliefs, but they remain hypotheses rather than established facts when applied to Late Pleistocene evidence that predates any surviving textual or oral tradition by tens of thousands of years. Students should be able to describe therianthropic figures accurately as combining human and animal visual elements while distinguishing that description from any specific claim about the belief system that produced them.

Section 10.5

Pigment, Ornament, and Personal Display

Rock walls were not the only surfaces on which Late Pleistocene Southeast Asian communities appear to have applied color or attached significance to appearance. Excavations at cave sites across the region have recovered pieces of ochre, a naturally occurring iron-oxide pigment ranging in color from yellow through red to purple-brown, some showing wear patterns, ground facets, or striations consistent with deliberate use for coloring surfaces, objects, or possibly the human body itself. Ochre's widespread presence across many Late Pleistocene sites worldwide has made it one of the most frequently cited categories of evidence for early symbolic or aesthetic behavior, though, consistent with the reasoning developed in Section 10.1, the mere presence of ochre demonstrates its collection and modification more securely than it demonstrates any specific use or meaning.

Alongside ochre, archaeologists have recovered small perforated objects, shell beads, and modified animal teeth or bone fragments interpreted as personal ornaments, from cave sites in various parts of the region. Perforation, whether achieved by drilling, grinding, or another technique, transforms a shell or tooth from a found object into one that could be strung and worn, suspended, or attached to clothing or other materials that have not survived archaeologically. The deliberate selection of particular shell types, sometimes transported some distance from their source, and the consistent placement of perforations in comparable positions across multiple specimens, argue for intentional manufacture according to a shared technique rather than incidental damage.

What can such ornaments reasonably be interpreted to show? At a basic level, they demonstrate that some individuals invested time and skill in creating objects whose primary apparent function was decorative or display-related rather than narrowly practical, suggesting that personal appearance carried some form of social significance. Comparative reasoning drawn from later, better-documented societies suggests several plausible functions for such ornamentation, marking group membership, signaling individual identity or achievement, serving in exchange relationships, or accompanying ritual practice, but the archaeological evidence from any single Late Pleistocene site rarely permits confident selection among these possibilities. Where similar ornament types appear across multiple sites separated by some distance, discussed further in Section 10.9, this may suggest shared conventions or contact between communities, though again without specifying exactly what the shared convention communicated.

Students should resist two opposite errors when considering this evidence. The first error treats ornamentation as a trivial or purely decorative afterthought unworthy of serious historical attention. The investment of time, the selection of particular materials, and the consistency of technique visible in these objects indicate that personal adornment mattered to the people who practiced it, whatever its precise social function. The second error treats ornamentation as straightforward proof of specific social structures, such as formal rank systems or elaborate status hierarchies, that the evidence alone cannot establish. A perforated shell demonstrates manufacture and, very probably, display; it does not by itself demonstrate the existence of chiefs, castes, or other specific social institutions that would not be documented with any confidence until much later periods examined in subsequent chapters.

Section 10.6

Burials and the Treatment of the Dead

Deliberate burial represents one of the most direct forms of evidence archaeologists have for the social lives of Late Pleistocene communities, because intentional interment, whether carried out by a single individual or by several people acting together, requires recognizing a death, making decisions about the body, and often investing time and effort in a process that may serve social or ritual purposes alongside more practical ones, such as managing decomposition or protecting a body from scavengers. At the same time, burial evidence is also one of the categories most vulnerable to overinterpretation, and this section addresses both its value and its limits.

Archaeologists distinguish deliberate burial from natural or accidental deposition by examining several lines of evidence: whether a body was placed in a deliberately dug pit rather than simply left exposed or fallen into a natural crevice; whether the body shows a consistent, non-random position, such as being flexed or extended in a particular orientation; whether sediment covering the body appears to have been deliberately placed rather than accumulated naturally over time; and whether objects, pigment, or other materials appear in direct and non-accidental association with the remains. Where several of these indicators converge, archaeologists can reasonably conclude that a burial was intentional rather than the result of natural processes such as a body falling into a sinkhole or being covered by cave collapse.

Cave and rockshelter sites across mainland and island Southeast Asia have produced examples of such deliberate burials dating to the Late Pleistocene, some showing particular body positioning, such as flexed or crouched positions, and some accompanied by ochre staining of the remains or the surrounding sediment, or by small associated objects. At Liang Tebo, a cave site in the karst of East Kalimantan, Borneo, excavators documented an intentional primary burial dated to roughly 31,000 to 30,000 years ago, remarkable not only as clear evidence of deliberate interment but because skeletal analysis of the individual indicated that a lower leg had been surgically amputated well before death, with the bone showing evidence of healing. This find demonstrates that at least some Late Pleistocene communities in the region possessed detailed anatomical knowledge and were capable of sustained post-operative care, since the individual evidently survived for a considerable period after the procedure; it stands as one of the clearest examples of the kind of careful, situated interpretation this chapter has emphasized, since the burial and the healed amputation together support strong, specific claims about medical knowledge and social support, without requiring speculation about the community's broader religious beliefs. The presence of ochre in direct association with burials, in particular, has attracted considerable scholarly attention, since ochre application to a body appears to require an active decision unconnected to any obvious practical function related to decomposition or preservation.

What can such burials responsibly be interpreted to demonstrate? They provide strong evidence that the individuals involved recognized death as an event calling for a specific communal response, that they were willing to invest time and effort in that response, and that particular treatments, positioning, or the application of pigment, were considered appropriate or meaningful in ways that distinguished intentional burial from simple disposal. This already represents historically significant evidence for social organization and shared practice within a community.

What such burials do not, on their own, establish is any specific set of religious or cosmological beliefs. It is tempting, particularly in popular writing, to describe a burial accompanied by ochre or grave goods as evidence for "belief in an afterlife," but this specific inference requires additional assumptions that the burial itself cannot confirm. A community might treat its dead with great care and ritual attention for reasons involving grief, social obligation, concern about the physical presence of a corpse, or beliefs about ongoing relationships with the deceased that do not necessarily correspond to any of the specific afterlife concepts documented in later, textually attested religious traditions. This textbook consistently describes such burials as evidence for socially meaningful treatment of the dead while explicitly declining to specify the particular beliefs involved, unless later, comparably aged evidence, such as associated symbolic material with clearer interpretive context, supports a more specific claim.

Section 10.7

Tools as Social and Technological Evidence

Stone, bone, and shell tools represent the most abundant category of material evidence recovered from Late Pleistocene sites across Southeast Asia, and they offer a different, complementary kind of insight into behavior than rock art, ornament, or burial: evidence for planning, skill, and the transmission of technical knowledge across generations and, potentially, across communities.

Assemblages recovered from cave and open-air sites across the region show considerable diversity in raw material selection, reflecting the varied local availability of stone types, and in the techniques used to shape tools, ranging from simple flaking to more controlled reduction sequences that required planning several steps ahead to achieve a desired final form. Bone and shell were also worked into tools in various parts of the region, sometimes shaped into points, scrapers, or other implements suited to particular tasks, such as processing plant material, working hides, or extracting shellfish from their shells. The presence of tools showing standardized forms, repeated across multiple examples at a site or across nearby sites, suggests that particular techniques were being taught, learned, and reproduced according to a shared template, evidence for the transmission of specialized knowledge within and potentially between communities.

The concept of "curated" versus "expedient" technology is useful here. Expedient tools are made quickly from locally available material for immediate use and then discarded, showing minimal investment of time or forethought. Curated tools, by contrast, are made with greater investment of time and skill, often maintained, resharpened, or carried between locations rather than discarded after a single use, suggesting that their makers anticipated future need and planned accordingly. Late Pleistocene Southeast Asian assemblages generally include a mixture of both approaches, varying by site, raw material availability, and, presumably, the specific tasks at hand, indicating a flexible and situationally responsive technological strategy rather than a single fixed toolkit applied uniformly across all circumstances.

It is important to state clearly what this section does not attempt. It does not attempt to provide the detailed technological, geographic, and historiographical treatment of the flaked-stone tradition that archaeologists have conventionally grouped under the label "Hoabinhian," a term, its geographic range, its chronology, and the significant debates over its usefulness as a category, which are the dedicated subject of Chapter 11. Some of the tools described in this section overlap in time and space with material later classified as Hoabinhian, but this section treats tool evidence broadly, as one line of testimony about planning, skill, and technological flexibility among Late Pleistocene communities generally, leaving the specific archaeological category and its debates for the following chapter.

Section 10.8

Coastal, Island, and Forest Adaptations

The tools, food remains, and site locations examined by archaeologists provide evidence for how Late Pleistocene communities made a living from the diverse environments of Southeast Asia, environments that included dense tropical forest, river valleys, coastal margins, and, for populations on islands such as Sulawesi and Flores, environments accessible only by deliberate water crossings.

Shell middens, accumulated deposits of discarded mollusk shells, document sustained exploitation of coastal and riverine shellfish resources at various sites, while fish remains and, in some cases, evidence for the capture of pelagic, open-water fish species at island sites suggest that at least some communities possessed watercraft and fishing technology capable of exploiting marine resources beyond the immediate shoreline. Faunal remains from cave deposits document the hunting of a range of terrestrial animals, varying by region and the locally available fauna, including, on Sulawesi, species such as wild pigs and the anoa depicted in the rock art discussed in Section 10.3 and 10.4. Plant remains, though generally less well preserved than bone, shell, or stone, provide evidence in some contexts for the exploitation of tubers, fruits, and other forest resources, suggesting a broad and flexible subsistence strategy rather than dependence on any single food source.

This evidence should be read as demonstrating behavioral flexibility rather than as evidence for environmental determinism, the idea that a particular environment mechanically produced a particular way of life. Communities living in similar environments did not necessarily adopt identical subsistence strategies, and evidence for regional variation, discussed further in Section 10.11, cautions against assuming that ecology alone dictated behavior. Rather, the evidence suggests that Late Pleistocene Southeast Asian communities possessed the knowledge and technology to exploit a wide range of available resources, adapting their strategies to locally specific combinations of forest, river, and coastal or marine environments, and that this flexibility likely contributed to the successful, long-term occupation of environments as different from one another as inland limestone karst, river valleys, and island coastlines separated by open water.

Section 10.9

Social Networks, Mobility, and Exchange

Some categories of archaeological evidence discussed earlier in this chapter, particularly ornament types and raw material sources, provide indirect evidence for contact, mobility, and possibly exchange between different Late Pleistocene communities, though this evidence must be interpreted with particular caution.

Where shell types used for ornaments at a given site derive from sources some distance away, or where similar techniques of perforation or shaping recur across sites separated by substantial geographic distance, archaeologists reasonably infer some form of connection between the communities involved, whether through direct movement of people, through exchange of finished objects or raw materials passed along a chain of intermediary groups, or through the independent transmission of shared techniques via more casual or periodic contact. Similarly, the raw materials used for stone tools sometimes derive from sources located some distance from the site where the finished tool was recovered, suggesting either that people traveled to obtain particular stone types directly or that materials moved between groups through some form of exchange or trade relationship.

This evidence supports a picture of Late Pleistocene Southeast Asia in which communities were not simply isolated, self-contained units but participated, at some level and with some regularity, in wider social networks extending beyond their immediate local area. This is a historically significant point, since it complicates any assumption that small-scale, mobile societies of this period were necessarily isolated or lacked meaningful contact with other groups.

At the same time, this textbook resists a common overinterpretation of such evidence: the assumption that shared artifact styles or raw material sources straightforwardly indicate a shared ethnic identity, language, or bounded cultural group in the sense those terms carry in later historical periods. Archaeological "cultures," defined by recurring combinations of artifact types, do not correspond in any simple or automatic way to specific peoples, languages, or self-conscious identities; multiple distinct communities could share similar tool-making traditions through contact, imitation, or independent invention without considering themselves part of a single group, and a single community could in principle draw on multiple different technological traditions. Evidence for contact and exchange should therefore be read as evidence for social connection and the movement of materials, techniques, or people, not as evidence for a specific bounded ethnic or linguistic community projected backward from later periods.

Section 10.10

What Counts as "Symbolic Behavior"?

The evidence examined throughout this chapter, rock art, ornamentation, deliberate burial, and technologically sophisticated tool production, has historically been discussed under the broad heading of "symbolic behavior" or, in an influential and now heavily revised body of older scholarship, "behavioral modernity." This section examines that concept directly, since how historians and archaeologists define and apply it substantially shapes how the evidence in this chapter is understood.

Through much of the twentieth century, discussions of early human symbolic capacity were organized around a model proposing that fully modern cognitive and symbolic abilities, the capacity for complex language, representational art, elaborate ritual, and abstract thought, emerged relatively suddenly, often associated with the Upper Paleolithic period in Europe beginning around 40,000 to 50,000 years ago. This model, sometimes described as a "human revolution" or "creative explosion," proposed that earlier evidence for symbolic behavior elsewhere in the world was sparse, ambiguous, or simply absent, implying that modern behavioral capacities appeared as a relatively abrupt package, closely associated with the specific population that produced the celebrated painted caves of France and Spain.

This model has faced substantial revision over recent decades, for several converging reasons. Archaeological evidence for ochre use, deliberate burial, and ornamentation has been documented at African sites dating tens of thousands of years earlier than the proposed European "revolution," suggesting a much more gradual, geographically dispersed development of these behaviors rather than a single sudden origin. Population genetics, examined from a different angle in Chapter 9, can establish ancestry, population divergence, and patterns of admixture among ancient and modern groups, but it cannot directly demonstrate when complex language or particular cognitive capacities emerged; that limitation itself is a caution against assuming any single line of evidence, genetic or archaeological, can settle the question of when "modern" behavior began. And, directly relevant to this chapter, the dated rock art of Sulawesi, discussed in Section 10.3, demonstrates that sophisticated figurative and narrative image-making was occurring in island Southeast Asia by dates comparable to, and in some interpretations predating, the classic European examples, at a location thousands of kilometers from the sites that had anchored the older model.

In light of this evidence, many specialists now favor models emphasizing cumulative, uneven, and context-dependent development of symbolic behaviors rather than a single sudden threshold crossed at one time and place. On this view, behaviors such as pigment use, ornamentation, and representational image-making may have developed gradually, appeared and disappeared unevenly in the archaeological record due to preservation and sampling factors discussed in Section 10.2, and been expressed differently according to local materials, environments, and social circumstances, rather than spreading outward from a single point of origin as a unified "revolution." This does not mean that nothing changed over time, evidence for such behaviors does become more visible and widespread later in the Late Pleistocene, but it cautions against treating any single site, region, or population as the sole origin of human symbolic capacity.

This chapter adopts this more cautious, globally informed position throughout, treating "symbolic behavior" not as a single threshold that either has or has not been crossed, but as a broad category encompassing varied practices, image-making, ornamentation, burial ritual, and possibly others not well preserved archaeologically, that different communities developed, expressed, and transmitted in their own ways across a very long span of time.

Historical Vocabulary
Behavioral Modernity

Definition: A term used in archaeology to describe a proposed set of cognitive and cultural traits, including complex language, symbolic thought, representational art, ornamentation, and ritual, thought by earlier scholarship to distinguish fully "modern" human behavior from that of earlier hominins or earlier populations of Homo sapiens.

Historical context: The concept developed largely from twentieth-century European archaeology, where a marked increase in visible symbolic material coincided with the Upper Paleolithic period, leading some researchers to propose a relatively sudden behavioral "revolution" associated with anatomically modern humans in that specific region and period.

Why it matters: The concept shaped how archaeologists worldwide searched for, and interpreted, evidence of early symbolic behavior, sometimes leading researchers to treat evidence from outside Europe as secondary confirmation of a European pattern rather than as independently significant.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should be cautious of treating "behavioral modernity" as a fixed threshold that either had or had not been reached by a given population. Current evidence, including the Southeast Asian material examined in this chapter, favors a more gradual, geographically uneven, and cumulative picture of how symbolic behaviors developed and became archaeologically visible, rather than a single global switch flipped at one time and place.

Historical Vocabulary
Taphonomy

Definition: The study of the processes, both natural and cultural, that act upon organic and material remains between the time of their original deposition and the time of their recovery by archaeologists, affecting what survives, in what condition, and in what arrangement.

Historical context: Taphonomic processes include decay, erosion, water movement, animal disturbance (such as burrowing or scavenging), sediment compaction, and human activity occurring after the original deposit, any of which can alter, destroy, or create misleading patterns in the archaeological record.

Why it matters: Careful taphonomic analysis allows archaeologists to distinguish, for example, a genuinely deliberate burial from a body that came to rest in a similar position through natural processes, or a deliberately placed object from one that migrated into a deposit through later disturbance. Without such analysis, claims about deliberate human behavior risk resting on evidence that could equally be explained by non-human processes.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should understand that the mere presence of an object or a body within a cave deposit does not, by itself, prove deliberate human action; the surrounding context, sediment, and spatial patterning must be carefully assessed before such a conclusion is drawn.

Section 10.11

Regional Diversity across Mainland and Island Zones

A recurring caution throughout this chapter has been the risk of treating a single, especially well-documented site or region as representative of Late Pleistocene Southeast Asia as a whole. This section addresses that risk directly by comparing the state of evidence across different parts of the region.

Sulawesi's Maros-Pangkep karst has received unusually intensive archaeological investigation over the past several decades, producing the extensively dated rock art sequence discussed in Sections 10.3 and 10.4. This intensity of research reflects both the genuine richness of the archaeological record in this specific karst landscape and the sustained scholarly attention the region has attracted precisely because of its early, spectacular discoveries, a self-reinforcing pattern common in archaeology, where well-known sites attract further research funding and attention, while less-studied regions remain comparatively underexplored regardless of what evidence they might eventually yield. The 2026 dating of a hand stencil at Liang Metanduno on Muna Island, well outside Maros-Pangkep, offers a useful corrective to this pattern: it demonstrates that the oldest currently known rock art constraint in the region does not come from the most intensively studied karst landscape, and it suggests that comparably early evidence may await discovery in other, still under-investigated parts of island Southeast Asia. Borneo's Sangkulirang-Mangkalihat karst has produced comparable, though generally less extensively dated, evidence, and other parts of Borneo, together with other islands across Wallacea, including Flores, discussed in Chapter 7 in connection with Homo floresiensis, and Timor, contain karst landscapes that could plausibly preserve similar evidence but have not yet received equivalent research investment.

Mainland Southeast Asia presents a different pattern. Cave sites in mainland regions, including areas of present-day Laos, Vietnam, Myanmar, and Thailand, have produced Late Pleistocene deposits containing tools, faunal remains, and, in some cases, burials, but documented rock art comparable in scale and dated antiquity to the Sulawesi sequence remains less extensively documented on the mainland, a pattern that may reflect genuine differences in past practice, differences in preservation conditions such as the relative scarcity of the specific limestone conditions needed for uranium-series dating, differences in the intensity of research attention, or some combination of all three factors. Students should be cautious about concluding from this pattern that image-making was necessarily rarer on the mainland; the absence of extensively dated mainland rock art may reflect what has been found and studied so far rather than what once existed.

The Philippine archipelago, though the subject of significant research regarding early hominin and human occupation, discussed in Chapters 7 and 8 in connection with Homo luzonensis and early modern human dispersal, has similarly not yet produced a rock art sequence comparable in scale to Sulawesi's, again a pattern that may reflect research history as much as past behavior. Evidence for burial, ornamentation, and tool technology, by contrast, has been documented more broadly across mainland and island sites alike, suggesting that these categories of behavior, while unevenly documented, were probably practiced more widely across the region than the currently uneven distribution of rock art evidence might suggest on its own.

The overall lesson of this comparison is methodological as much as substantive: archaeological visibility is not the same as historical importance or prevalence. Sulawesi's rock art is genuinely remarkable and genuinely significant to global discussions of early human behavior, but its prominence in this chapter, and in the broader scholarly literature, reflects a combination of preservation conditions, dating opportunities, and research history that should not be mistaken for proof that Sulawesi was uniquely or exceptionally important compared to other parts of Late Pleistocene Southeast Asia whose comparable evidence, if it exists, has simply not yet been found, dated, or published.

Section 10.12

Reconstructing a Late Pleistocene Social World

Bringing together the evidence examined throughout this chapter, what general picture can be responsibly drawn of Late Pleistocene Southeast Asian communities, without overstepping what the evidence actually supports?

These were communities capable of sophisticated, planned technological production, evidenced by curated tool technologies requiring foresight and by watercraft and fishing capabilities implied by the successful occupation of islands separated from the mainland and from each other by substantial stretches of open water. They were communities that applied pigment to rock surfaces in deliberate, sometimes elaborate compositions, including figurative depictions of locally significant animals and, in at least one well-documented case, a composition combining human, animal, and part-human figures whose precise meaning remains a matter of scholarly interpretation rather than settled fact. They were communities that adorned themselves or their belongings with worked shell, stone, and pigment, in ways that likely carried social significance even where the specific significance cannot be recovered. They were communities that responded to death through deliberate, sometimes elaborate treatment of the body, indicating socially organized and meaningful practices surrounding mortality, again without confirming any specific belief system behind those practices. And they were communities connected, at least intermittently, to other communities at some distance, through the movement of raw materials, techniques, or people, participating in social networks broader than any single, isolated settlement.

At the same time, this reconstructed picture remains fragmentary and heavily shaped by what has survived and what has been studied. Archaeology preserves almost nothing of the languages these communities spoke, the specific social structures, kinship systems, or leadership arrangements they organized themselves around, the detailed content of their beliefs about the world, or the individual biographies, relationships, and everyday concerns of the people who made the objects and left the traces this chapter has examined. What survives is a partial, materially biased record, disproportionately drawn from caves and rockshelters, disproportionately concentrated in a small number of intensively studied regions, and inevitably silent on enormous dimensions of human experience that left no durable physical trace.

Holding both of these observations together, genuine and historically significant evidence for sophisticated behavior, alongside genuine and important limits on what that evidence can establish, is the central intellectual habit this chapter has sought to build. It is a habit that will serve students well not only in the following chapter's treatment of the Hoabinhian tradition, but throughout the textbook's subsequent engagement with archaeological evidence for periods before, and in some cases even after, the emergence of written sources.

The Sulawesi and Muna Island Rock Art Chronology

Type of evidence: Rock art panels (hand stencils and figurative and composite painted scenes) together with associated dating samples (uranium-series and laser-ablation uranium-series imaging of overlying mineral crusts) · Location: Limestone caves within the Maros-Pangkep karst, southern Sulawesi, and Liang Metanduno on Muna Island, southeastern Sulawesi · Date range: Minimum age estimates ranging from approximately 39,900 to at least 67,800 years, depending on the specific panel, site, and dating method used, examined in Section 10.3

Context: These caves were documented and dated through a combination of long-standing local and regional awareness of the sites, systematic archaeological survey, and the application of uranium-series dating, and more recently laser-ablation uranium-series imaging, to thin calcium-carbonate crusts that formed over painted surfaces after the pigment had been applied. Because the crusts postdate the paintings beneath them, the resulting dates establish minimum ages for the art rather than precise creation dates, and improved methods have in some cases substantially revised earlier published estimates.

Description (not a verbatim written source, since none exists for this material):

At Liang Metanduno on Muna Island, a hand stencil has produced a minimum age estimate of at least approximately 67,800 years, currently the oldest such constraint documented for rock art in the region. At Leang Karampuang, in the Maros-Pangkep karst, a newly described composite scene combining human-like, anthropomorphic figures and a pig has been dated using laser-ablation uranium-series imaging to a minimum age of at least approximately 51,200 years. At Leang Bulu' Sipong 4, a wide panel presenting multiple painted animal figures together with several smaller therianthropic figures, originally dated to a minimum of approximately 43,900 years, has been redated using the same improved method to at least approximately 48,000 years. At Leang Tedongnge, a painted depiction of a Sulawesi warty pig has produced a minimum age estimate of at least 45,500 years, and at Leang Timpuseng, several hand stencils have produced a minimum age estimate of approximately 39,900 years.

Analysis: This developing sequence demonstrates that figurative and, in several cases, compositionally complex image-making was well established across a wide area of island Southeast Asia, extending beyond the single karst landscape that had originally anchored the region's rock art chronology, by dates comparable to, and in several instances considerably preceding, the celebrated Upper Paleolithic art of Western Europe. It also demonstrates the value and the limits of uranium-series-based dating: the method offers a scientifically grounded minimum age but cannot establish the maximum age or the precise date of creation without additional bracketing evidence, and refinements to the method, such as laser-ablation imaging, can substantially shift even previously published, widely cited estimates.

Limitations: Each figure above is a minimum age; the true age of the underlying pigment at any given site could be somewhat or substantially older. The interpretation of the Leang Bulu' Sipong 4 and Leang Karampuang panels as "hunting scenes" or narrative compositions, though proposed by the excavating researchers, extends beyond what the painted imagery alone can confirm, and alternative interpretations, or the possibility that no single coherent narrative was intended, remain open.

Perspective: This evidence reflects the archaeological and scientific perspective of the excavation and dating teams; it does not, and cannot, directly access the perspective, intentions, or beliefs of the original artists, whose own understanding of the images they created is not recoverable from the physical evidence alone.

Questions for students:

  1. What does uranium-series dating actually measure, and why does it produce a minimum age rather than an exact date for the underlying rock art?
  2. What can the Leang Karampuang and Leang Bulu' Sipong 4 panels demonstrate with confidence, and what remains a matter of interpretation rather than established fact?
  3. Why might researchers and popular media differ in how cautiously they describe a discovery such as this one?
  4. Why does the substantial revision of the Bulu' Sipong 4 date, and the discovery of even older art at Muna Island, matter for how confidently any single date should be treated as final?

Historical Debate

Was There a "Creative Explosion," or Something Else?

The question: Did fully modern human symbolic behavior, including representational art, ornamentation, and elaborate burial, emerge relatively suddenly at a particular time and place, or did such behaviors develop more gradually, unevenly, and in multiple regions, becoming visible to archaeologists only as preservation and discovery allowed?

Interpretation A — a relatively rapid emergence associated with an identifiable behavioral threshold: Some researchers continue to argue that there is a meaningful increase in the quantity, complexity, and diversity of symbolic material beginning at a certain point in the Late Pleistocene, and that this pattern, even if not confined to a single region, reflects a genuine change in cognitive or social capacity rather than simply improved preservation or increased archaeological attention.
Interpretation B — a gradual, cumulative, and geographically dispersed development: Other researchers, drawing on early African evidence for ochre use and ornamentation, and now the dated Southeast Asian rock art examined in this chapter, argue that symbolic behaviors developed gradually over a much longer period and in multiple regions, and that their apparent concentration in certain times and places reflects preservation, discovery, and research history as much as any genuine behavioral threshold.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same expanding body of archaeological evidence from Africa, Europe, and Southeast Asia, but differ in how much weight to give to the timing and distribution of currently documented finds versus the likelihood that undiscovered or undated evidence exists elsewhere.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and emphasis rather than the existence of the underlying archaeological finds themselves, and it is significantly shaped by the incomplete and unevenly distributed nature of the global archaeological record, discussed in Section 10.11.

Why it matters: How historians and archaeologists resolve this question shapes whether particular regions, including island Southeast Asia, are treated as marginal confirmations of a pattern first identified elsewhere or as independently significant sources of evidence about how and when human symbolic behavior actually developed.

Student task: Using the evidence presented in Sections 10.3, 10.4, and 10.10, construct an argument for each interpretation, then explain which position the currently available Southeast Asian evidence seems to support more strongly, while identifying what kind of future discovery could change your assessment.

Multiple Perspectives

Reading the Same Evidence: Three Archaeological Specialties

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives representing different specialties within archaeology, not quotations from any single named researcher.

From the perspective of a rock art and dating specialist: The priority is establishing secure chronology: identifying suitable mineral crusts, applying uranium-series methods carefully, and being explicit that resulting dates are minimum ages. Claims about a painting's meaning matter less, at this stage of analysis, than claims about when it was made and how confidently that date can be defended.
From the perspective of a burial and mortuary archaeologist: The priority is careful excavation and taphonomic analysis: distinguishing deliberate burial from accidental deposition, documenting body position and any associated materials precisely, and resisting the temptation to translate a burial directly into a specific religious belief without additional, independent supporting evidence.
From the perspective of a population historian working primarily with genetic evidence (examined in Chapter 9): The priority is ancestry and population relationships over long timescales; this specialist would caution that genetic evidence cannot confirm which specific population created a given painting or burial, and that behavioral and biological evidence must be combined carefully rather than assumed to track one another automatically.

Considering these three specialties together illustrates why claims about Late Pleistocene symbolic life in Southeast Asia typically depend on combining multiple independent lines of evidence, dating science, careful excavation, and population history, rather than relying on any single method or discovery in isolation.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From a European-Centered Model to a Global Picture

What Changed

  • The geographic scope of evidence considered central to discussions of early human symbolic behavior expanded from a near-exclusive focus on Ice Age Europe to include Africa and, increasingly, island Southeast Asia
  • Dating methods advanced from relative and less precise techniques to uranium-series analysis capable of producing scientifically defensible minimum ages for rock art
  • The dominant interpretive model shifted from a sudden "creative explosion" tied to a specific region toward a more gradual, cumulative, and geographically dispersed model of development

What Stayed the Same

  • The fundamental methodological challenge, distinguishing evidence for behavior from confident claims about meaning, has remained constant regardless of which region's evidence is under discussion
  • Caves and rockshelters have continued to provide a disproportionate share of well-preserved evidence throughout the history of this field
  • Scholarly caution regarding overinterpretation of symbolic or ritual meaning has remained a persistent theme even as the geographic scope of evidence has broadened

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the expansion of evidence and the persistence of core methodological challenges helps students understand that new discoveries change the geographic and chronological picture without eliminating the underlying interpretive difficulties this chapter has emphasized throughout.

Cause and Effect

Why Southeast Asian Evidence Changed the Global Debate

Long-term causes

Limestone karst landscapes across Sulawesi and Borneo had preserved rock art and associated mineral crusts for tens of thousands of years, providing a substantial body of latent evidence long before it was systematically investigated by archaeologists using appropriate scientific methods.

Short-term causes

The application of uranium-series dating, and later laser-ablation uranium-series imaging, to Sulawesi and Muna Island rock art beginning in the 2010s and continuing through the mid-2020s, combined with sustained excavation at sites such as Leang Bulu' Sipong 4, Leang Karampuang, and Liang Metanduno, produced securely dated evidence that could be directly compared, on scientific terms, with the best-known European examples.

Effect

The resulting minimum age estimates showed that sophisticated image-making, including figurative and composite compositions, was present in island Southeast Asia at dates rivaling or predating the classic European sequence, undermining the older assumption that early symbolic art developed first, or most significantly, in a single region.

This chapter's response

Consistent emphasis throughout on distinguishing dated evidence from interpretive claims, on avoiding the substitution of one single-origin narrative for another, and on treating Southeast Asian evidence as a genuinely significant, but not uniquely originating, contribution to a still-developing global picture.

Historical complexity: The shift in scholarly understanding reflects both genuine new evidence and changes in where and how intensively archaeologists have chosen to look, a reminder that the history of archaeological knowledge is itself shaped by research priorities and available resources, not solely by the underlying ancient reality.

Everyday Life and Social History

Ordinary Practice behind Extraordinary Evidence

It is easy for a chapter organized around dramatic discoveries, dated rock art, therianthropic imagery, deliberate burial, to lose sight of the ordinary, repeated, everyday character of the practices that produced this evidence. A hand stencil required someone to gather or prepare pigment, travel to a particular cave location, and spend time executing the image, activities embedded within a daily and seasonal round of movement, gathering, hunting, fishing, tool-making, and social interaction that has left far less durable evidence than the stencil itself. A burial required a community to pause its ordinary activities, dig a pit, position a body, and in some cases apply pigment or include objects, a set of actions requiring cooperation, shared understanding of appropriate practice, and, presumably, some form of communication among the participants.

Ornamentation, too, points toward everyday social life: perforated shells and worked ochre were presumably worn or displayed not only, or even primarily, during exceptional ritual occasions, but potentially as part of ordinary daily appearance and interaction within and between communities. Considering this evidence in terms of everyday practice, rather than only as evidence for extraordinary events, helps students avoid the common tendency to imagine Late Pleistocene life as consisting mainly of dramatic ritual moments, when the vast majority of any community's time was almost certainly occupied by the practical, repetitive, and largely archaeologically invisible work of finding food, making and maintaining tools, caring for children, and navigating relationships within and between communities, activities this chapter's more dramatic evidence can only partially illuminate.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has shifted the textbook's attention from the population history examined in Chapters 6 through 9 to the question of what Late Pleistocene Southeast Asian communities actually did: how they used caves, what images they made, how they treated their dead, how they adorned themselves, and how they organized technological knowledge. Throughout, the chapter has insisted on a disciplined distinction between evidence for behavior and interpretation of its meaning, a distinction introduced in Section 10.1 and applied consistently to every category of evidence discussed afterward.

The chapter examined why caves and rockshelters, particularly within limestone karst landscapes, preserve an unusually rich record of Late Pleistocene material culture, while cautioning that this preservation bias should not be mistaken for evidence that people lived primarily inside caves. It then turned to the rock art of Sulawesi, the Maros-Pangkep karst in particular, and of Muna Island and Borneo, explaining how uranium-series dating and, more recently, laser-ablation uranium-series imaging of mineral crusts overlying painted surfaces has produced a developing sequence of minimum age estimates: a hand stencil at Liang Metanduno on Muna Island at least approximately 67,800 years old, a composite scene at Leang Karampuang at least approximately 51,200 years old, the Leang Bulu' Sipong 4 panel revised to at least approximately 48,000 years old, a painted pig at Leang Tedongnge at least 45,500 years old, and hand stencils at Leang Timpuseng at least approximately 39,900 years old, among the oldest securely dated rock art known anywhere in the world.

The chapter gave particular attention to the composite panels at Leang Bulu' Sipong 4, combining painted animals with therianthropic, part-human, part-animal figures, and Leang Karampuang, combining a painted pig with human-like, anthropomorphic figures, while cautioning against overconfident claims that either panel represents a specific narrative "hunting scene" or constitutes proof of early storytelling, distinctions that extend beyond what the painted imagery alone can establish. It then examined ochre and perforated ornaments as evidence for personal display and possible social communication, and deliberate burial, anchored by the intentional interment and healed limb amputation documented at Liang Tebo in Borneo, as evidence for socially organized responses to death and, in that specific case, detailed anatomical knowledge and sustained care, in both cases emphasizing what the evidence demonstrates and what it does not demonstrate about specific religious or cosmological beliefs that cannot be confirmed from physical remains alone.

Tool technologies were examined as evidence for planning, skill transmission, and technological flexibility, distinguished explicitly from the dedicated treatment of Hoabinhian technology reserved for Chapter 11. Subsistence evidence, including shell middens, faunal remains, and evidence for the exploitation of coastal, riverine, and terrestrial resources, demonstrated adaptable, flexible strategies rather than environmentally determined behavior, while evidence for raw material movement and shared ornament styles pointed toward social networks extending beyond individual communities, interpreted cautiously without assuming these networks corresponded to fixed ethnic or linguistic groups.

The chapter addressed directly the historiographical concept of "behavioral modernity" and the older "human revolution" model, explaining why accumulating evidence from Africa and, crucially, from Sulawesi has led many specialists to favor a more gradual, geographically dispersed, and cumulative model of symbolic development rather than a single sudden threshold associated with any one region. It then compared the uneven distribution of evidence across mainland and island Southeast Asia, cautioning that the prominence of Sulawesi in current scholarship reflects research history and preservation conditions as much as any unique historical importance, and closed by synthesizing the chapter's evidence into a cautious but historically substantive picture of Late Pleistocene social and symbolic life.

Throughout, the chapter has modeled a specific form of historical and archaeological reasoning: taking material evidence seriously as a source of genuine historical knowledge while refusing to extend that evidence beyond what it can actually support. This reasoning prepares students directly for Chapter 11, which narrows the discussion to a single, extensively debated archaeological tradition, the Hoabinhian, and for the broader methodological demands of archaeological evidence that recur throughout the rest of this textbook.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Rock art
Images, including paintings, stencils, and engravings, created on natural rock surfaces such as cave walls; used broadly in this chapter to refer to the pigment-based imagery documented at Sulawesi and Borneo cave sites.
Hand stencil
An image created by placing a hand against a rock surface and applying pigment around it, leaving a negative outline of the hand; among the most common and long-lived forms of Late Pleistocene image-making documented in the region.
Figurative art
Imagery that depicts recognizable subjects, such as animals or human figures, as distinguished from purely abstract or geometric marks.
Therianthrope
A figure, painted or otherwise depicted, that combines human and animal characteristics; documented at Leang Bulu' Sipong 4 and interpreted cautiously in this chapter given the difficulty of establishing the intended meaning of such imagery. Distinguished from the human-like, non-animal figures documented at Leang Karampuang.
Uranium-series dating
A scientific dating method measuring radioactive decay of uranium isotopes in mineral deposits, applied to crusts overlying rock art to establish minimum ages for the underlying pigment.
Minimum age
An age estimate establishing that an object or image must be at least a certain age, without necessarily establishing its true or maximum age; the standard result of dating a mineral crust that formed after a painting was made.
Ochre
A naturally occurring iron-oxide pigment, ranging from yellow to deep red in color, recovered in worked and unworked forms from many Late Pleistocene sites and used for painting, staining, or other purposes not always specifiable from the material alone.
Perforated ornament
A shell, tooth, or other small object modified with a hole to allow suspension or attachment, interpreted as evidence for personal adornment or display.
Deliberate burial
The intentional interment of a human body, distinguished archaeologically from accidental or natural deposition through evidence such as dug pits, consistent body positioning, and deliberately placed sediment or objects.
Grave goods
Objects deliberately placed with a burial, interpreted as evidence for socially meaningful mortuary practice, though not necessarily indicative of any specific belief about an afterlife.
Taphonomy
The study of natural and cultural processes affecting material remains between deposition and recovery, essential for distinguishing deliberate human behavior from post-depositional disturbance.
Preservation bias
The tendency of certain conditions, such as cave environments, to preserve archaeological evidence disproportionately well compared to other contexts, potentially distorting the overall picture of past behavior.
Lithic technology
Stone tool technology, encompassing the raw materials, techniques, and resulting forms used to produce stone implements.
Curated technology
Tools made with significant investment of time and skill, maintained or carried for future use, contrasted with expedient tools made quickly for immediate, short-term use.
Behavioral modernity
A contested archaeological concept describing a proposed set of traits associated with fully modern human cognition and culture; substantially revised by evidence discussed in Section 10.10 favoring a more gradual and geographically dispersed model of development.
Symbolic behavior
A broad category of practices, including image-making, ornamentation, and ritualized treatment of the dead, understood in this chapter as varied and unevenly documented rather than as a single uniform trait.
Karst
A landscape formed by the dissolution of soluble rock, typically limestone, often producing caves, sinkholes, and other features; the Maros-Pangkep and Sangkulirang-Mangkalihat regions discussed in this chapter are prominent examples.
Rockshelter
A shallow cave-like recess in a rock face, offering shelter without extending deeply underground; frequently used and studied alongside true caves in Late Pleistocene archaeology.
Wallacea
The biogeographic zone of islands, including Sulawesi, Flores, and Timor, lying between the Sunda and Sahul continental shelves, distinguished by its distinct fauna and, for early human history, its requirement of deliberate water crossings for human occupation.
Assemblage
The full set of artifacts and other material evidence recovered from a particular archaeological context, such as a single excavated layer or site.
Exchange network
A pattern of movement of goods, materials, or techniques between communities, inferred archaeologically from evidence such as non-local raw materials or shared artifact styles.
Regional variation
Differences in archaeological evidence and inferred behavior across different parts of Southeast Asia, emphasized throughout this chapter as a caution against generalizing from any single well-documented site or region.
Interpretation versus evidence
The chapter's central methodological distinction between what physical evidence directly demonstrates and what a researcher proposes as a plausible but unproven reading of that evidence's meaning.
Shell midden
An accumulated deposit of discarded mollusk shells, providing archaeological evidence for sustained exploitation of shellfish resources.
Therianthropic imagery
Visual imagery combining human and animal features, discussed in this chapter primarily in connection with the Leang Bulu' Sipong 4 panel and distinguished from the human-like, non-therianthropic figures at Leang Karampuang.

Identification & Significance

Liang Metanduno (Muna Island, southeastern Sulawesi)
Cave site outside the Maros-Pangkep karst containing a hand stencil dated by laser-ablation uranium-series imaging to a minimum age of at least approximately 67,800 years, currently the oldest such constraint documented for rock art in the region, examined in Sections 10.3 and 10.11.
Leang Karampuang (Sulawesi)
Cave site in the Maros-Pangkep karst containing a newly described composite scene of human-like (anthropomorphic, not therianthropic) figures and a pig, dated using laser-ablation uranium-series imaging to a minimum age of at least approximately 51,200 years, currently regarded as the oldest known surviving composed representational scene, examined in Section 10.4 and the Evidence Window.
Leang Timpuseng (Sulawesi)
Cave site in the Maros-Pangkep karst containing hand stencils dated by uranium-series analysis to a minimum age of approximately 39,900 years, examined in Section 10.3 and the Evidence Window.
Leang Tedongnge (Sulawesi)
Cave site containing a painted depiction of a Sulawesi warty pig dated to a minimum age of at least 45,500 years, among the oldest currently dated figurative animal paintings known, examined in Section 10.3.

Cave site containing a composite panel of painted animals and therianthropic figures, originally dated to a minimum age of approximately 43,900 years and later revised, using laser-ablation uranium-series imaging, to a minimum age of at least approximately 48,000 years, discussed at length in Sections 10.3, 10.4, and the Evidence Window regarding the limits of narrative interpretation and the provisional nature of published dates.

Sangkulirang-Mangkalihat karst (Borneo)

The biogeographic boundary and zone relevant to understanding the seafaring capability implied by human occupation of islands such as Sulawesi, examined in the Geography Study feature and connected to Chapter 8's discussion of early dispersal.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to sit with an uncomfortable but productive tension: the material evidence for Late Pleistocene Southeast Asian life is genuinely rich, genuinely significant, and genuinely capable of reshaping global understanding of early human behavior, and at the same time that evidence resists easy, confident translation into the kinds of stories, beliefs, and social structures that popular accounts often attach to it. Learning to hold both of these truths at once, rather than collapsing into either uncritical enthusiasm or dismissive skepticism, is one of the most transferable skills this textbook can offer.

This chapter also matters because it demonstrates, concretely, how new evidence can overturn a long-standing and confidently held scholarly narrative. The "human revolution" model dominated discussions of early human symbolic behavior for decades, built substantially on evidence from a single region. The dated rock art of Sulawesi did not simply add a footnote to that model; it helped drive a fundamental reconsideration of where, when, and how such behaviors developed, illustrating that even well-established scholarly frameworks remain open to revision as new evidence, new dating methods, and new research priorities emerge.

The chapter's caution against treating any single site as representative, whether Sulawesi's karst caves or the therianthropic panel at Leang Bulu' Sipong 4, also carries forward a theme introduced in Chapter 1 and repeated throughout this textbook's early chapters: the danger of collapsing a large, diverse region into a single, convenient story. Just as Chapter 1 warned against treating Southeast Asia as a uniform crossroads between India and China, this chapter has warned against treating one karst landscape as a stand-in for the symbolic life of an entire region and an immense span of time.

Looking ahead, the technological evidence introduced in Section 10.7, deliberately left underdeveloped in anticipation of Chapter 11, sets up a direct transition into a focused study of the Hoabinhian tradition, where many of the same methodological questions, what tools can and cannot tell us about the people who made them, will recur in a more specific archaeological and historiographical context. The habits of mind built in this chapter, distinguishing evidence from interpretation, recognizing preservation bias, resisting single-origin narratives, and taking material evidence seriously without overstating its implications, will remain essential tools for the chapters that follow.
