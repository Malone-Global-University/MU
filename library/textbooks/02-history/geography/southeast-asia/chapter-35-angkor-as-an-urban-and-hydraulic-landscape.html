Angkor as an Urban and Hydraulic Landscape

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Angkor was not a collection of temples supported by an irrigation system. It was an extensive, historically evolving urban-agricultural landscape of reservoirs, canals, roads, neighborhoods, ponds, shrines, and fields, built and rebuilt over centuries, whose achievements and vulnerabilities both arose from the interaction of people, infrastructure, water, land, and changing environmental conditions.

Most visitors to Angkor today encounter a landscape of stone: the five towers of Angkor Wat rising above a wide moat, the carved faces of the Bayon, the tree-entangled walls of Ta Prohm. For well over a century, scholars encountered it in much the same way. Epigraphers read the Sanskrit and Old Khmer inscriptions that temples carried, art historians classified styles, and conservators stabilized collapsing sandstone and laterite. Since stone was what survived, stone was what the history of Angkor was about. The houses of ordinary people, built of wood, bamboo, and thatch, had long since decayed, and the fields and ponds that surrounded the temples seemed to belong to a vanished background rather than to the history itself.

That picture has been overturned within a single generation of research. Beginning with aerial photography in the 1930s and accelerating after radar mapping around the turn of the twenty-first century and airborne laser scanning in 2012, archaeologists have shown that the temples stood within a vast, inhabited, and heavily modified landscape. A comprehensive archaeological map published in 2007 identified a connected settlement and infrastructure complex exceeding 1,000 square kilometers, far larger than the walled and moated enclosures at its center. The lidar survey published in 2013 revealed planned street grids, thousands of small ponds, and low residential mounds beneath forest canopy that no ground survey had been able to see. A demographic study published in 2021 modeled a population of roughly 700,000 to 900,000 across the wider Greater Angkor Region at its thirteenth-century height, an estimate that is a scholarly model rather than a census.

The water systems at the heart of this landscape are among the most studied features of any premodern Southeast Asian society. The great rectangular reservoirs called barays, together with moats, canals, embankments, and spillways, occupied an environment in which rainfall is concentrated into a single wet season and the surrounding soils are often sandy and low in fertility. How these works functioned, who built and maintained them, and what they were for have been debated since the 1960s. Were they primarily irrigation tanks for a rice economy, as an influential early thesis argued? Were they ritual and symbolic pools, flood-control devices, urban amenities, or all of these at once? This chapter argues that the evidence supports multiple functions and a network that was repeatedly modified rather than designed once, and that no single answer has been established for every feature.

The chapter has a specific place in this textbook's division of labor. Chapters 33 and 34 traced the political formation of Angkor and the institutions of Khmer kingship, and Chapter 36 examines temples and religion. Chapter 37 and Chapter 38 treat the reigns of Suryavarman II and Jayavarman VII, and Chapter 39 reconstructs everyday life in the Angkorian world. Chapter 61 returns to the broader transformation of Angkor in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. The present chapter concentrates on the physical city: its geography, the methods by which archaeologists reconstruct it, its settlement structure, its water engineering, its agricultural base, its roads, its environmental history, and the changing scholarly interpretations of what kind of place it was.

The evidence is unusually varied. Inscriptions record foundations of reservoirs and temples and the endowment of rice fields, but say little about household ponds or ordinary dwellings. A single eyewitness account survives from a Chinese visitor, Zhou Daguan, who stayed in 1296–1297. Archaeological excavation, sediment cores, radiocarbon dating, remote sensing, tree-ring climate records, and computer modeling supply the rest. Each of these evidence types answers some questions and not others, and a recurring task for the student is to separate what has been observed (a feature in the landscape), what has been dated (when it was made or used), and what has been inferred (what it was for and how many people it served).

Finally, the chapter asks students to avoid two opposite temptations. One is to treat Angkor as a mystery city that vanished in a single catastrophe. The other is to treat its engineering as a triumph that makes its eventual transformation a puzzle about failure. Angkor's infrastructure was impressive, adaptable, and also complicated, and it was maintained for centuries within a monsoon environment of real variability. Parts of the Angkor area remained occupied and sacred long after the period examined here. The chapter introduces the environmental and infrastructural side of change and leaves the comprehensive reassessment of Angkor's political, religious, and economic transformation to Chapter 61.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the Kulen hills, the monsoon rainfall regime, the gentle southward gradient, and the Tonle Sap shaped the opportunities and risks facing Angkorian settlement and agriculture.
  2. Describe the principal methods archaeologists use to reconstruct Angkor, including field survey, excavation, aerial photography, radar, lidar, sediment coring, radiocarbon dating, and geographic information systems.
  3. Distinguish what lidar can reveal (surface topography beneath vegetation) from what it cannot establish alone (date, function, occupation history, population).
  4. Trace the development of Angkorian urban centers from the Phnom Kulen and Roluos areas to Yaśodharapura and the later formal grids around Angkor Wat and Angkor Thom.
  5. Distinguish the ceremonial core, the connected settlement landscape of roughly 1,000 square kilometers, and the roughly 3,000-square-kilometer Greater Angkor Region used in demographic research.
  6. Analyze the functions of barays, canals, moats, embankments, spillways, and household ponds as parts of a changing water network rather than as isolated monuments.
  7. Evaluate the evidence for and against the claim that Angkorian rice agriculture depended on centrally managed irrigation or on multiple annual harvests.
  8. Assess how roads, causeways, and canals integrated Greater Angkor, and how labor for construction and maintenance was organized.
  9. Compare the older hydraulic-city thesis with later multipurpose interpretations, using evidence from reservoirs, canals, settlement archaeology, and environmental records.
  10. Interpret the evidence for sedimentation, erosion, drought, flood risk, and modeled network vulnerability while separating observed change from simulation.
  11. Evaluate why Angkor can be described as a low-density urban complex and the limits of describing it as exceptionally large among premodern cities.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. How did a monument-centered history of Angkor produce an incomplete picture of the city, and what kinds of evidence corrected it?

  6. What distinguishes the built-up core, the connected urban complex, and the Greater Angkor Region, and why do their areas differ?

Historical Context

The Angkor area lies on a low plain north of the Tonle Sap, the great lake of the lower Mekong system, and south of the sandstone plateau of Phnom Kulen. Chapter 2 introduced the river systems of the mainland, and Chapter 4 explained the monsoon on which the rhythm of life here depends. Settlement in the lower Mekong region and the Tonle Sap basin long predates Angkor. Earlier agricultural communities, the Funan and Chenla-era polities examined in Chapters 20 and 21, and the pre-Angkorian centers discussed in Chapter 33 all occupied this wider world. Angkor did not appear on empty land. It grew in a region already settled, already farmed, and already connected by rivers and overland routes to coastal and inland neighbors.

The political story of the early ninth to thirteenth centuries, covered in Chapters 33 and 34, supplies the framework within which the physical city developed. According to a later inscriptional tradition, Jayavarman II was consecrated on Phnom Kulen around 802. His successors established centers at Hariharalaya in the Roluos area and then at Yaśodharapura, the city later known as Angkor. Rulers sponsored temples, reservoirs, and monumental enclosures, and recorded such foundations in Sanskrit and Old Khmer inscriptions. These records are indispensable for chronology, but they are royal and temple documents. They illuminate elite patronage far better than they illuminate the villages, ponds, fields, and households that made up most of the inhabited landscape.

Environmentally, the region has a monsoon climate in which most rain falls between roughly May and October, followed by a pronounced dry season. For farmers the issue is not only the total rainfall but its timing, since rice requires standing water during growth and the rains can arrive late, end early, or fall in intense bursts. The region's soils, notably sandy ones, are not uniformly fertile. Storing water, moving it, draining it, and keeping floods away from settlement were therefore recurrent practical problems, and Angkorian engineering responded to them in successive ways over centuries.

The scholarly context matters as much as the historical one. For most of the twentieth century, research at Angkor was shaped by the French conservation and epigraphic tradition associated with the École française d'Extrême-Orient and by the idea, advanced especially by Bernard-Philippe Groslier, of Angkor as a hydraulic city sustained by rice irrigation. The Greater Angkor Project, which began in the 1990s and brought together Australian, French, and Cambodian researchers, and the later lidar surveys, reopened these questions with new tools. Students should understand that the physical shape of Angkor, as it is now known, is partly a discovery of the last three decades, and that many interpretive questions remain open.

Section 35.1

Angkor Beyond the Monuments

The modern image of Angkor as a ruined city rediscovered in the jungle owes much to nineteenth-century European accounts, above all the publications of the French naturalist Henri Mouhot after his visit in 1860. The image is misleading in two ways. Angkor Wat was never abandoned: it remained a Buddhist site of worship, and Khmer communities, monks, and pilgrims knew and used much of the area throughout the centuries in which Europeans later claimed to have discovered it. And the idea of a lost city presented Angkor as a set of monuments overtaken by forest rather than as an inhabited landscape whose residents had left traces of a different kind.

For the first century or so of systematic scholarship, the monuments were the primary object of research for understandable reasons. They were large, visible, inscribed, and in need of conservation. The École française d'Extrême-Orient established a conservation program at Angkor, and the study of inscriptions and architecture produced sophisticated chronologies of foundations and styles. This work remains foundational, and the chapters of this textbook that treat reigns and temples depend upon it. But it created a built-in bias. A city known principally through its stone temples was naturally imagined as a ceremonial center, with a royal court, priests, and attendants, rather than as a landscape with hundreds of thousands of residents, farmers, and craft workers.

Aerial photography began to change this in the 1930s, when early flights over the Angkor plain showed the outlines of embankments, rectilinear field patterns, and ponds that were invisible at ground level. In the following decades, scholars including Bernard-Philippe Groslier worked to connect these features to the great reservoirs and to a model of agricultural production. The significance of these early observations is that they identified the right question: how did the temples relate to the land around them? Their limitation was that the evidence was partial, difficult to date, and often interpreted through a single hypothesis about irrigation, an issue examined in Section 35.22.

The central argument of this chapter is that the temples, though magnificent, were only the most durable part of a much larger working landscape. Residents lived in neighborhoods of perishable houses clustered around ponds and local shrines. Farmers worked fields that extended between and beyond the monumental precincts. Channels, embankments, and reservoirs moved and stored water through the monsoon year. Roads and causeways linked districts to one another and to river and lake transport. Treating this whole as a single historical subject, which we will call Greater Angkor, changes the questions historians ask: not only who ruled and what they built, but how people lived, produced food, managed water, and adapted as conditions changed.

Treating Angkor as a landscape also changes the kind of history that can be written. A history of kings and monuments depends on inscriptions and architecture. A history of a landscape draws on archaeology, geoscience, remote sensing, and modeling, and it has to deal with long processes, such as gradual sedimentation or incremental expansion of settlement, that no inscription records. The remainder of this division introduces the geography and methods that make that kind of history possible.

Section 35.2

The Tonle Sap Basin, Monsoons, and Natural Geography

Angkor lies at the northern edge of the Tonle Sap basin, a broad low-lying plain in northwestern Cambodia. To the north rises Phnom Kulen, a sandstone plateau whose streams flow southward and southwestward toward the lake. To the south lies the Tonle Sap itself. The slope of the plain between plateau and lake is very gentle, which has major consequences for water. Streams descending from the Kulen foothills cross terrain with little fall and are prone to spreading, meandering, and shifting course. Any structure built to store or direct water had to work with slopes so slight that small differences in embankment height or channel alignment mattered.

The lake adds a distinctive feature. The Tonle Sap is connected to the Mekong by the Tonle Sap River, which changes direction seasonally. In the dry season the river drains the lake toward the Mekong. When the Mekong rises in the monsoon, its waters push back up the river into the lake, which expands several-fold in area and floods surrounding forest and low land. This seasonal flood pulse makes the lake one of the most productive freshwater fisheries in the world. For Angkor, it meant a dependable source of fish and a seasonally shifting shoreline south of the settled plain, as well as a large body of water into which the local drainage ultimately flowed.

Rainfall is the second structuring condition. The monsoon, introduced in Chapter 4, brings most annual rain to the region between roughly May and October. The dry months that follow are long enough that water retained from the wet season could be valuable for drinking, gardens, livestock, and some cultivation. Yet rainfall varies from year to year. A late onset can delay planting, an early end can strand a crop, and a particularly intense monsoon can produce floods. Opportunities and risks both arise from this concentration of rainfall, and neither makes any particular political or engineering outcome inevitable. The monsoon explains the problems a society faced, not the specific solutions it chose.

Soils complete the picture. Parts of the Angkor plain have sandy soils that drain quickly and hold nutrients poorly, so the productivity of rice fields depended on local water retention, silt deposition, and management. The area is not uniform, and the environment supported many kinds of land use: paddies in low areas, gardens and orchards on higher ground, forest, and wetland. The Kulen hills also supplied materials. The sandstone of many Angkorian temples came from quarries in the Kulen area, and archaeologists have argued that some canals were used to transport building stone, an example of how the water network served purposes beyond agriculture.

Section 35.3

How Archaeologists Reconstruct the City

Reconstructing a city whose houses were built of wood and thatch and whose largest features are earthworks requires methods that can detect subtle physical traces and then date and interpret them. Archaeologists working at Angkor have combined several approaches, each with distinct strengths and weaknesses, and students should treat them as complementary rather than interchangeable.

Field survey involves walking the landscape and recording features such as temple ruins, mounds, ponds, embankments, and surface scatters of pottery and brick. It produces direct observation and yields material that can be studied, but it is slow and dependent on visibility. Dense forest, cultivation, and modern settlement all obscure features. Excavation reveals stratigraphy, the sequence of deposits, and yields datable material. A trench across an embankment, a pond, or a canal can show how it was built, whether it was modified, and when it was used. Excavation is the most reliable way to establish the date and construction method of a feature, but it can sample only a tiny fraction of the landscape, and one excavated pond does not date its neighbors.

Remote sensing covers methods that observe the landscape from above. Aerial photography in the early twentieth century and satellite imagery later showed patterns of embankments and fields. Synthetic aperture radar, flown over Angkor around 2000, penetrated some vegetation and, together with ground verification, supported the comprehensive archaeological map published in 2007. Lidar, discussed in Section 35.4, extended this approach dramatically. These methods map the surface and are best at showing the extent and form of features. They do not themselves date them.

Dating and environmental reconstruction draw on other tools. Radiocarbon dating of charcoal, seeds, and other organic material provides calendar-age estimates with stated ranges, not exact years. Inscriptions with dates, where a feature can be linked to one, supply historical anchors, although matching a text to a physical structure requires care. Sediment cores taken from reservoirs, moats, rivers, and the lake record changes in deposition, vegetation, and erosion over time through pollen, charcoal, and chemical signatures. Tree-ring records from forests elsewhere in the region, notably Vietnam, supply a climatic series that can be compared with archaeological chronologies.

Geographic information systems bring these datasets together. In a GIS, archaeologists can overlay a topographic model, mapped ponds, temples, and canals, radiocarbon dates, and modern land use, and test spatial hypotheses, such as whether ponds cluster around certain roads. The method is powerful, but a map is a representation built from selected data. A GIS can display thousands of features as if they coexisted when they may belong to different centuries. A central methodological discipline for the rest of this chapter is to ask of any map or total whether its features are contemporaneous, dated, and verified.

Section 35.4

The Lidar Revolution: Revealing the Hidden City

In 2012, a helicopter-mounted laser scanner surveyed several hundred square kilometers of terrain in the Angkor region, including the Angkor core, parts of Phnom Kulen, and other sites. The results, published in 2013 by the Khmer Archaeology LiDAR Consortium led by Damian Evans and colleagues, were among the most consequential discoveries in the archaeology of mainland Southeast Asia. Later surveys, including the larger Cambodian Archaeological Lidar Initiative of 2015, extended the technique to more areas and other sites. The technology allowed archaeologists to see surface topography beneath tropical forest in ways that a century of ground survey could not.

Historical Vocabulary
Lidar

Definition. Lidar (light detection and ranging) is a remote-sensing method in which a scanner on an aircraft emits rapid laser pulses and records the time they take to return. Some pulses are reflected by leaves; others pass through gaps and reflect from the ground. Software separates the ground returns from the vegetation returns and builds a digital terrain model, a detailed map of ground-surface elevation with the vegetation removed.

Historical context. At Angkor, lidar revealed low earthworks, such as embankments, mounds, ponds, and the raised lines of roads, that were hidden beneath forest or modified by modern fields. It showed that many features recorded individually by earlier surveys belonged to coherent patterns, including grids.

Why it matters. Lidar changed the scale and confidence of archaeological mapping. It also introduced a risk: striking images can appear self-explanatory.

Potential misunderstanding. Lidar does not detect buildings of wood and thatch that no longer exist, does not "see" through the ground, and does not establish the age, function, or occupation history of a feature. Those questions require excavation, dating, and interpretation.

What the survey revealed fell into several categories. First, it clarified the layout of districts around the monumental centers. Around Angkor Thom and the area of Angkor Wat, lidar showed networks of streets and embankments forming rectilinear blocks, demonstrating a degree of planning in the urban fabric beyond the temple enclosures themselves. Second, it showed numerous small rectangular ponds and low mounds in dense distributions, evidence of residential clusters of a kind that earlier mapping had recorded only in part. Third, it revealed canals, roads, and field boundaries connecting these districts. Fourth, on Phnom Kulen, it showed a substantial settlement with planned elements at Mahendraparvata, offering new evidence relevant to the earliest Angkorian-period centers.

Equally important is what lidar cannot do. A visible embankment may have been built in one century and reused in another. A grid of streets seen in a terrain model may represent several phases of construction or a palimpsest, in which successive layers of activity are superimposed. A mound might be a residence, a shrine platform, or a spoil heap. Ground verification, through targeted excavation, test pits, and survey, remains necessary, and since the 2013 publication researchers have conducted ground checks and excavations to date and interpret lidar-detected features. A responsible reading of any lidar image therefore asks: what is observed, what is dated, and what is inferred?

Archaeological Evidence Study

Comparing Conventional Imagery with Lidar-Derived Topography

Students should compare, using published images (such as those in the 2013 lidar publication and in earlier mapping), a conventional aerial or satellite photograph of forested terrain near Angkor with a lidar-derived terrain model of the same area.

  • In the photograph, note what is visible: forest canopy, cleared fields, temple roofs, roads, and modern settlement.
  • In the terrain model, note what appears: linear embankments, rectangular depressions, low mounds, and patterns of regular blocks.
  • Identify features in the terrain model that cannot be seen in the photograph, and features visible in the photograph that the model does not show.
  • For each lidar feature, list what additional evidence (excavation, radiocarbon dates, pottery, inscriptions) would be needed to establish its date and function.

Analysis Questions:

  1. What kinds of past activity leave surface traces that lidar can detect, and what kinds do not?
  2. Why might a regular grid in a terrain model reflect several construction phases rather than one plan?
  3. How should a historian describe a lidar-detected feature whose date is unknown?

Section 35.5

From Hariharalaya to Yaśodharapura: The Early Urban Centers

The political sequence connecting Jayavarman II, Indravarman I, and Yaśovarman I belongs to Chapters 33 and 34. Here the concern is the geography of settlement. The earliest Angkorian-period centers did not form a single city that gradually expanded. They were successive and partly overlapping foci of monumental construction and settlement across a landscape, each with its own relationship to water and land.

On Phnom Kulen, lidar has revealed a substantial settlement with embankments and enclosures at Mahendraparvata, a name found in later inscriptions. The traditional date of c. 802 for Jayavarman II's consecration is derived from a later inscription and is treated as traditional. The plateau is a defensible, well-watered location near stone quarries and the headwaters of the streams that later supplied Angkor, and its use as an early ceremonial and settlement site fits an interpretation in which the Kulen served as both a political base and a resource zone. The nature and chronology of occupation there are active research questions.

In the Roluos area on the plain, the center called Hariharalaya developed in the late ninth century. Here the reservoir called Indratataka, associated with Indravarman I, was dug, and the temples of Preah Ko (dedicated 879), Bakong (881), and Lolei (893, on an island in the reservoir) were built. The combination is significant. A large reservoir was an element of the early urban and ceremonial layout from the start, and temples were placed in relation to it. Surrounding settlement, ponds, and fields are less well known than the monuments, but field and lidar work has shown that the monuments stood within a wider inhabited area.

Under Yaśovarman I, the focus shifted to the Angkor plain. The new center, Yaśodharapura, was organized around Phnom Bakheng, a temple hill, and the East Baray, a reservoir whose scale exceeded that of Indratataka. The shift, from Roluos to the area north of it, is better understood as a relocation of the principal royal and ceremonial focus than as the abandonment of the older center, where activity continued. Yaśodharapura was not a single static city. Its focus moved again over the following centuries, as later rulers built new temples and enclosures, so that what visitors now call Angkor is the cumulative result of several urban phases.

One implication is that Angkor's physical form was shaped by an accumulation of decisions across generations. Earlier works were not simply replaced. Roads, embankments, and reservoirs were reused, bypassed, and reconnected, a theme developed in Section 35.19. Treating the founding of Yaśodharapura as the beginning of a finished city would obscure the long, incremental process by which its landscape took shape.

Section 35.6

Temple Centers, Royal Precincts, and Planned Urban Grids

Angkor's monumental centers were not isolated islands. The great temples and enclosures were surrounded by districts of streets, ponds, and dwellings, and the relationship between the two changed over time. Archaeological mapping suggests a contrast between earlier phases, in which temples were set amid more open and irregular settlement, and later phases, in which formal grids of streets and embankments structured the districts around the central monuments, especially between the eleventh and thirteenth centuries.

Angkor Thom, the walled and moated enclosure that includes the Bayon, forms roughly a three-kilometer square and encloses an area of about nine square kilometers. Within its walls, lidar and ground survey show regular blocks and streets, together with ponds and other features. Angkor Wat, with an enclosure of roughly 1.5 by 1.3 kilometers and a wide surrounding moat, is also set within a gridded landscape that extends beyond the moat. The monumental precincts and the planned districts were not simply adjacent. They formed integrated spaces, in which orientation, drainage, and access were considered together.

Interpreting this planning requires care. Evidence for a regular grid shows that a degree of design was applied, but it does not establish a single master plan or a single builder. The grids probably developed in phases, with older earthworks incorporated, extended, or altered. Within them, not every block need have been densely occupied. Some areas may have been open, cultivated, or used for ritual. The grid shows organization of space. How that space was used, and by whom, must be established by excavation.

The inhabitants of the central districts are only partly visible. Inscriptions from temple foundations record priests, officials, and dependents attached to the temples, and the central precincts evidently housed members of the court and elite. Archaeological evidence suggests that residential occupation extended well beyond these elites, and that the central districts were not purely ceremonial. How densely they were occupied, and how that density compared with the surrounding settlement, is one of the questions discussed in Section 35.8. For the political and religious institutions that gave the central precincts their meaning, see Chapters 34 and 36.

Section 35.7

Neighborhoods, Occupation Mounds, Ponds, and Local Shrines

Most Angkorian people did not live in stone buildings. Stone and brick were reserved for temples and a few other structures, and houses were built from timber, bamboo, thatch, and other perishable materials, probably often raised on posts, as in many later Southeast Asian houses. The surviving traces of these dwellings are indirect. They include low mounds of earth on which houses stood, scatters of pottery, hearth remains, and the ponds and paths associated with them.

The characteristic residential unit that archaeologists have mapped at Angkor is the cluster: a low occupation mound, a rectangular pond, and sometimes a small brick shrine or foundation, surrounded by gardens and fields. The mounds provided dry ground during the wet season and are sometimes surrounded by small embankments. The ponds, discussed in Section 35.14, supplied water for household and community use. Small brick shrines and temples, many recorded in surveys, served local religious needs and are evidence of foundations by local patrons or communities, not only by kings.

These clusters were connected by paths, small roads, and in places channels, and set within a matrix of rice fields, orchards, and other land. The pattern produced a landscape in which houses and farmland were interspersed, in contrast to the compact, walled, densely built city familiar from other premodern regions. Archaeologists who describe Angkor as an agrarian-based, low-density settlement point to precisely this interpenetration of residence and production, a concept examined in Section 35.23.

Reading the mounds and ponds as evidence of households requires caution. A mound may have been occupied for a century or for a few years. A pond may have been dug, extended, silted up, and re-dug. Radiocarbon and pottery dates from excavated examples show that occupation extended across several centuries, but they cannot be assumed to apply to every feature. For this reason, counts of mounds or ponds are not census data. They are a starting point for estimating how many people might have lived in the landscape, as Section 35.8 explains.

Section 35.8

How Large Was Angkor? Population, Density, and Urban Boundaries

"How large was Angkor?" has no single answer, because the question can refer to at least three different things. The ceremonial core comprises the walled and moated precincts and the principal temples, an area measured in tens of square kilometers at most. The connected settlement landscape identified in the comprehensive archaeological map of 2007 is the area of linked temples, reservoirs, roads, canals, and settlement features, which that mapping estimated at roughly 900 to 1,000 square kilometers or somewhat more. The Greater Angkor Region used in recent demographic research is a larger study area of roughly 3,000 square kilometers. These are analytical areas chosen for different purposes. They are not interchangeable measurements of a city wall or built-up footprint, and a statement that Angkor covered a given number of square kilometers should always say which area is meant.

Estimating population is harder still. No census, tax roll, or comprehensive household register survives. Scholars have therefore used proxies, including the density of mounds and ponds, the extent of cultivable land and its estimated productivity, and the number and scale of temples and their recorded dependents. A 2021 study, which modeled population across the Greater Angkor Region through time, arrived at a peak of approximately 700,000 to 900,000 inhabitants in the thirteenth century. These are modeled estimates. They depend on assumptions about how many people lived in a household, how many of the mapped features were occupied at the same time, and how features were distributed across the study area. Changing any assumption changes the result, and the authors of such work present them as ranges.

The estimate is nonetheless useful because it demonstrates the scale of the system that required provisioning and water. It implies a population in the hundreds of thousands, dispersed over a landscape rather than packed within walls. It also illustrates a methodological point central to this chapter: the number depends on a dating framework. The 2021 model required estimates of when features were in use, and the ponds and mounds that lidar reveals are not individually dated. Statements about "the population of Angkor" are therefore more reliable when they are accompanied by the period, the area, the method, and the range.

Comparisons with other premodern cities should also be treated cautiously. Angkor is described by researchers as one of the most extensive known premodern urban complexes, a statement that depends on how "urban" and "extent" are defined and on how well other cities have been mapped. A low-density landscape that sprawls over hundreds of square kilometers is not comparable by area alone with a compact, densely populated city. Section 35.23 returns to this question. For now, the safest formulation is that Angkor was exceptionally extensive, supported a large population by any premodern standard, and organized that population in a form quite different from compact walled cities elsewhere.

Division III — Water Engineering and the Hydraulic Network
Section 35.9

Managing Water: Storage, Movement, and Seasonal Control

The central practical problem at Angkor was seasonal. During the wet season, rain and runoff from the Kulen hills could produce more water than was useful and sometimes more than settlements could safely tolerate. During the dry season, the same area could have too little. A society occupying this plain, and building monuments, houses, and fields on very gentle slopes, needed ways to collect and hold water, move it where it was needed, drain it where it was not, and keep it out of places it could do damage.

It is useful to separate four functions that are often blended in popular accounts. Storage means retaining water for later use, in reservoirs, ponds, and moats. Distribution means directing water to places of use, through canals, channels, and controlled releases. Drainage means removing excess water from settlement and cultivated land. Flood mitigation means protecting built areas from high flows, by diversion, embankment, and the temporary holding of surges. A single structure might serve several functions, or only one, and the same structure might be used differently in different centuries. Archaeologists must ask, for each feature, which function the evidence supports.

Historical Vocabulary
Baray and the hydraulic network

Definition. A baray is a large rectangular reservoir of the Angkorian period, formed by digging and by raising earthen embankments on one or more sides, often oriented to the cardinal directions. The Khmer inscriptions apply the Sanskrit-derived term taṭāka (pond or tank) in names such as Indratataka and Yaśodharatāṭāka. The hydraulic network is the modern scholarly term for the interconnected system of reservoirs, channels, embankments, moats, spillways, and ponds across Greater Angkor.

Historical context. Barays were built across several centuries, and they were not all contemporaneous. Their embankments are visible today as long raised lines, often with rice fields now occupying the former basin.

Why it matters. Barays are among the largest premodern earthworks in mainland Southeast Asia and central evidence for debate about Angkor's economy and cosmology.

Potential misunderstanding. "Reservoir" implies a function, storage for irrigation, that the evidence does not uniformly establish. Many scholars now see barays as multipurpose structures whose uses may have included water storage, flood regulation, ritual symbolism, and prestige, with the balance varying by baray and period. "Network" likewise should not suggest a single design: the system grew by accretion.

Treating Angkor's water works as a system, not a collection of monuments, is the key to Division III. Reservoirs, channels, embankments, and moats were connected to one another and to the natural streams. A change at one point, such as a diversion or the construction of a new reservoir, altered flows elsewhere. Researchers associated with the Greater Angkor Project have described the network as developed and repeatedly modified over centuries. The sections that follow examine its major elements in roughly chronological order, then turn to the smaller-scale facilities that served households.

Section 35.10

Early Reservoirs: Indratataka and the East Baray

The earliest of the large reservoirs on the plain is Indratataka at Hariharalaya, associated with Indravarman I and datable by inscriptions to the late ninth century. It is on the order of a few kilometers long and under a kilometer wide, a substantial work but smaller than the later barays. Its placement beside the Roluos temples shows that, from the outset, large-scale water storage was part of the urban and ceremonial landscape. The temple of Lolei, dedicated in 893, stood on an island within it.

The East Baray, called Yaśodharatāṭāka in inscriptions and associated with Yaśovarman I, is much larger: its commonly cited dimensions are roughly 7.5 kilometers by 1.8 kilometers. It was dug and embanked on the plain northeast of the new center and became a defining feature of Yaśodharapura. In the tenth century, the temple of East Mebon (dedicated in 952) was built on an island within it and Pre Rup (961) beside it, showing continued royal investment in the reservoir as a setting for state temples. Today the basin is largely dry and used for rice cultivation, and its embankments remain visible.

What was the East Baray for? Several possibilities are supported to differing degrees. The scale and the island temples point to ritual and symbolic significance: the baray as a sacred pool, associated in some interpretations with cosmological imagery and with the founding of the new city. Water storage is the practical function that its capacity suggests, but the way it was filled, by channels from the Siem Reap River, and the location of outlets, are matters on which archaeologists have worked in detail. Whether water from it was regularly released to irrigate fields, and how much, has been debated, a question taken up in Section 35.22. A baray could also have served as a buffer, absorbing surges, and as an amenity in a growing city, with fish and water for domestic use. Evidence for each is partial, and a defensible position acknowledges that more than one function probably applied.

The early reservoirs also relate to urban expansion. The East Baray was not simply built within an established city. Its construction coincided with the formation of the new center, and its embankments, channels, and associated roads shaped where later construction occurred. Infrastructure and settlement developed together, and the position of a reservoir influenced the placement of later districts, a pattern that recurs through the history of Angkor.

Section 35.11

The West Baray and Later Large-Scale Reservoirs

The West Baray, whose commonly cited dimensions are roughly 8 kilometers by 2.1 kilometers, is the largest of the Angkorian reservoirs. It is usually assigned to the eleventh century, though the dating and the sequence of construction and filling remain subjects of active research. On a central island lies the West Mebon temple. Unlike the East Baray, which is now largely a field, the West Baray still holds water in part, and it remains a visible and used feature of the modern landscape.

The scale of the work is a matter of engineering and labor. A reservoir of this size required moving enormous quantities of earth to build embankments several kilometers long, laying out the structure with enough accuracy that the embankments met and the interior was approximately level on slopes where small errors mattered, and providing inlets and outlets. We do not know the number of workers, the length of time required, or how labor was recruited, and any figures offered for these should be treated as speculation. What the structure does show is that the polity could organize large-scale, coordinated earthmoving over a long period, a theme taken up in Section 35.18.

The West Baray's role in the network changed. Studies of its inlets and outlets suggest that its connection to the surrounding channels was modified over time, and evidence of repair and alteration indicates it was not a single static construction. Its placement west of the central districts gave it a different relation to the streams and the lake than the East Baray had, and it has been argued that it could affect water flows over a wide area.

Later reservoirs show that the building of large water structures continued. The Jayatataka, the baray north of Angkor Thom associated with Jayavarman VII in the late twelfth and early thirteenth centuries, is smaller than the earlier two, with commonly cited dimensions of roughly 3.5 by 0.9 kilometers, and other basins, such as Srah Srang and the pool associated with Neak Pean, were built or modified. Chapter 38 treats Jayavarman VII's building program. For present purposes, the significance is that the network was not completed by a single act. Different rulers and communities continued to add and adapt reservoirs across several centuries, and it would be wrong to present the barays as one simultaneous project.

Section 35.12

Canals, River Diversions, and the Connected Water Network

Reservoirs stored water, but channels moved it. The Siem Reap River, the Puok River, and other streams descending from the Kulen foothills were integrated into the Angkorian system through diversion, channeling, and the excavation of canals. Some channels carried water into reservoirs. Others led out of them, or ran between districts, or drained toward the lake. By linking the foothills, reservoirs, urban centers, and agricultural lands, they turned a set of separate works into a connected network.

Archaeologists distinguish three kinds of watercourse, though in practice they grade into one another. Natural river courses follow the terrain and may have been modified by straightening or by embankment. Excavated canals were dug to a planned alignment, often straight, and recognized by their regular form, associated spoil banks, and relation to surrounding features. Diverted channels redirect a natural stream into a new course, for instance into a baray or around a settlement. Evidence from excavation and from mapping shows that many channels were modified over time: a channel might be cut, silt up, be cleaned, be rerouted, or be abandoned. The network as mapped today is therefore a palimpsest of different phases.

Canals did more than move water for agriculture. They connected temples and quarries, and archaeologists have argued that some channels served the transport of stone from Kulen to building sites. They linked areas of settlement for travel and goods by boat in the wet season. They also formed boundaries and alignments that shaped how neighborhoods were laid out. In the maintenance of the network, the channels were vulnerable points: a blocked or breached channel could affect reservoirs and settlements downstream, an issue examined in Sections 35.20 and 35.21.

Identifying and dating channels is challenging. Lidar shows linear depressions and embankments, but separating canals from natural gullies, old stream beds, or roads is not always straightforward, and dating a channel requires excavation or the identification of relationships to dated features. Where modern irrigation, roads, and fields have altered the terrain, interpretation is harder still. This is another instance in which the map displays more certainty than the evidence strictly allows.

Section 35.13

Moats, Embankments, Spillways, and Drainage

Structures that retained, redirected, released, or excluded water were as important as reservoirs. Embankments of earth, sometimes faced with laterite or sandstone, bounded reservoirs, lined channels, and raised roads above the wet-season water level. Moats surrounded major temple precincts and the city of Angkor Thom. The moat of Angkor Wat is on the order of two hundred meters wide, and Angkor Thom's is about a hundred meters wide. Spillways and outlet structures let excess water leave a reservoir or channel in a controlled way and prevented overtopping that could damage embankments. Drainage channels carried surplus water away from settlements and fields toward the lake.

Moats and reservoirs could also function as flood buffers. They could take and temporarily hold surges, reducing the peak flow reaching downstream areas. But their protective function depended on keeping them connected to inlets and outlets that worked. A moat that silted up or an outlet that became blocked altered the behavior of the system.

Hydraulic engineering and symbolism were closely entangled here. The moat around Angkor Wat and Angkor Thom is practical, in that it influences drainage and groundwater, and it also carries symbolic meaning: temple enclosures and their surrounding water have been interpreted as representing a cosmological ocean around a sacred mountain, an interpretation developed in Chapter 36. The embankments and roads often align with temple axes, tying movement through the landscape to ritual geography. It would be a mistake to assign each structure to a single category, "practical" or "symbolic." Recognizing that a structure could serve both purposes avoids the false choice, once common in the literature, between a purely utilitarian and a purely ritual reading.

The relationship among hydraulic structures, temple precincts, roads, and neighborhoods also shows how infrastructure shaped the city. Roads were raised on embankments that doubled as dikes. Neighborhoods were laid out between channels. Temple precincts were surrounded by water that was part of the drainage system of adjacent districts. A change to a major embankment could therefore affect circulation, drainage, and settlement at once, which is why modifications and repairs, evident from excavation and mapping, were consequential and why they are of such interest to researchers studying change over time.

Section 35.14

Household Ponds and Local Water Management

Beneath the level of the royal reservoirs lay thousands of small ponds. Lidar and ground survey show these rectangular depressions distributed densely across the landscape, in association with occupation mounds, small shrines, and paths. They are the commonest water facility at Angkor, and in many respects the most revealing, because they show how water was managed at the scale of the household and the neighborhood.

Their likely functions were domestic and communal. Water from them could serve drinking and washing where wells were not used, provide water for animals and gardens, support fish and aquatic plants as a food source, and hold water through the dry season. Some may have been dug to obtain earth for raising house mounds, with the resulting hollow filling with water, an example of a feature with more than one purpose. Excavated examples show repeated cleaning and re-excavation, evidence that ponds were maintained at the local level.

Primary Source Window

Zhou Daguan's Observations of Household Water Facilities, 1296–1297

Creator: Zhou Daguan, a Chinese envoy-observer who accompanied a diplomatic mission to the Khmer court and stayed at Angkor for roughly a year · Date: visit 1296–1297; account written afterward · Type: Eyewitness ethnographic and descriptive report

Context: Zhou's account of Cambodian customs, usually known in English as The Customs of Cambodia, is the only surviving detailed description of Angkor by an outside visitor during the period of its functioning. It was composed after his return to China, and it survives in later copies and compilations, so the transmitted text may contain errors and later changes. It describes the city, the court, daily life, trade, agriculture, and customs, from the point of view of a literate visitor who did not read Khmer and relied on what he saw and on interpreters and informants.

Paraphrase (not a verbatim excerpt):

Among other observations of daily life, Zhou notes that households had access to small ponds or cisterns for water, and that a single such facility might be shared by one to three families. He treats these as an ordinary feature of how people obtained water, not as a wonder, in contrast to his admiring comments about the royal buildings.

Analysis: Zhou's remark is valuable because it connects a late-thirteenth-century eyewitness observation with the physical features archaeologists have since mapped. A 2015 archaeological study discussed his comment alongside lidar evidence identifying more than 3,000 such ponds in the surveyed area, and argued that the remark and the physical pattern are consistent with each other. The convergence between the text and the landscape is real, but it should not be overstated. The source suggests how some ponds were used by some households around 1296–1297.

Limitations: The ponds mapped by lidar belong to different periods, and many were probably not in use simultaneously. Zhou describes the Angkor of the late thirteenth century, and his comment cannot be projected backward to the tenth century without further evidence. His observation also leaves open how many people lived in a household and how many ponds were in use at any moment, so it cannot by itself be used to convert a pond count into a population figure.

Perspective: Zhou represents a foreign, educated, male observer of the Yuan-era Chinese world, interested in customs and trade, with limited access to the lives of ordinary Khmer women and farmers. The perspectives of those who dug and used the ponds are not represented in the text.

  2. Who created the account, and under what circumstances did he observe Angkor?
  3. What purposes might have shaped what Zhou chose to record, and what he omitted?

Everyday Life and Social History

Ponds, Access to Water, and Residential Space

For most inhabitants of Greater Angkor, water was a local matter. A household lived on or beside a low mound of earth, probably in a house of timber and thatch, with a pond within a short walk. The pond supplied the water needed for cooking, washing, and animals, and perhaps fish. A neighborhood might share a path, a small shrine, and the labor of keeping ponds and mounds in repair. The pond is also evidence of obligation and cooperation: someone dug it, someone cleared it of silt, and where one pond served several families, those families had to agree on its use.

Social differences are likely to have shaped access, though the archaeological evidence cannot show them directly. Ponds near the central districts may have been more regularly maintained than those at the periphery. Households connected to temples may have had privileged access to larger water sources. Inscriptions record dependents attached to temples, including people performing work and service, and some of them presumably lived in these clusters. Chapter 39 treats these social relationships in more detail. Here the point is that the same landscape that supported kings' reservoirs also supported thousands of small, local arrangements for water, and that the daily lives of ordinary people depended more on these than on the great barays.

Students should also note the difficulty of using ponds to count people. Whether two ponds belonged to one household in successive generations or to two households at the same time cannot be determined from a lidar image alone. Estimates therefore require assumptions, and the more transparent a study is about those assumptions, the more useful it is.

The hydraulic division as a whole shows that Angkor's water works were a system of changing infrastructure, not simply a set of impressive individual reservoirs. At the top were barays and major channels, built under royal and temple patronage and visible in inscriptions. At the bottom were thousands of ponds, created and maintained locally. Between them lay moats, embankments, and drains. These levels interacted, and a full account of Angkor's water management must include all of them.

Division IV — Agriculture, Transport, and the Working Landscape
Section 35.15

Rice Cultivation and the Agricultural Landscape

Rice was the staple of Angkorian society, and inscriptions repeatedly record the endowment of temples with rice fields and with the people to work them. Understanding how rice was grown on the Angkor plain requires separating several distinct systems, which are often collapsed together in popular accounts.

Wet-rice cultivation grows rice in fields, or paddies, enclosed by low bunds that hold standing water during the growing season. Most paddy rice in the region has long been rain-fed, depending on monsoon rainfall and on water that accumulates in the field and in the local landscape. Farmers choose the location of fields and the layout of bunds to catch and retain water, and some fields lie in natural depressions or on the margins of streams and wetlands where water collects. Supplemental irrigation, in which water from a channel, pond, or reservoir is added to a field, can extend the growing season or buffer against a late start or early end of the rains. The two can be combined, and a landscape might include fields in several different situations.

Archaeologists have identified patterns of rectilinear embankments and field boundaries in aerial imagery and lidar, and have documented field systems in and around the monumental districts. Such patterns show where fields were laid out and imply the organization of cultivated land. They do not by themselves show how a given field was watered, in what period it was farmed, or what it yielded. Sediment and pollen evidence from cores can supplement the picture by showing the presence of cultivated plants and changing land use.

It is important to avoid the assumption that every rice field received water from a major reservoir. The barays were large, but the area of fields across Greater Angkor was far larger than any one reservoir could plausibly have served in a controlled fashion, and many fields lay at a distance from the barays or at elevations that reservoir water could not have reached without further channels. Local ponds, streams, and rainfall supplied much of the water. The best-supported statement is that agriculture drew on a range of water sources, with reservoir water an important possible supplement in some places, rather than the single foundation of the whole system.

Section 35.16

Agricultural Intensification and Food Supply

A population in the hundreds of thousands, concentrated in a district around the monumental centers, required a reliable food supply. Rice was central, but the diet also included fish from the Tonle Sap and from local ponds, vegetables and fruit from gardens and orchards, palm products, and animal foods, with exchange bringing goods from beyond the immediate area. Zhou Daguan's account mentions the abundance of fish and the importance of trade, and archaeological evidence for fishing, gardens, and market exchange supports this picture of a diversified food economy.

How intensively was rice produced? Here scholars disagree, and the disagreement is partly about evidence. One question is dry-season water. Reservoirs and ponds retained water into the dry months, and the question is whether it was used to grow a dry-season crop in addition to the monsoon crop. Zhou Daguan's account is often cited in this connection, because he reports that rice could be harvested several times a year. His statement shows that some form of multiple harvests was observed or reported in the late thirteenth century, but it is a traveler's generalization, and it leaves open what proportion of fields were involved, how reliable multiple harvests were, and whether the reports apply to earlier centuries. Some scholars treat the claim as evidence of widespread intensification; others consider that multiple annual crops were likely limited to favored locations or that the traveler may have been describing different kinds of rice or recession cultivation. The evidence does not establish that several annual harvests were universal.

A second question is vulnerability. Even where yields were high in good years, a rain-fed or partially irrigated system remained exposed to weather. A late monsoon, a dry spell during the growing season, or a flood that damaged a crop could produce a failed harvest. How large a buffer reservoirs and stored grain provided, and how the state and temples responded to shortfalls, are largely unknown. Inscriptions record endowments of rice to temples, but not general famine relief, and there is little direct evidence of famine in the Angkorian period. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, however, and the dependence of a large dispersed population on a monsoon-driven food system should be understood as a source of risk.

The overall picture is of an agricultural system that was productive enough to support a very large population, diversified across several food sources, and adapted to varied local conditions, with intensification in some places and periods. How much of that productivity depended on the reservoirs, as opposed to rainfall, local ponds, and the lake, remains debated, and the question connects directly to the hydraulic-city debate in Section 35.22.

Section 35.17

Roads, Causeways, Bridges, and Regional Connectivity

Greater Angkor was held together physically by routes. Mapping shows raised roads and causeways within the urban area, many following the lines of embankments, connecting temple precincts, reservoirs, districts, and fields. Raising a road above the surrounding terrain kept it usable in the wet season and, where it doubled as an embankment, helped control water. Gridded streets in the central districts and linear roads in the surrounding landscape formed a network that combined with canals and the lake shore to give the city its structure.

The monumental precincts have their own causeways, the paved or raised approaches to gates and temples, often lined with balustrades and crossing moats on bridges. These are the most visible features today, and they are significant because they integrated movement with ritual and display: the approach to a temple was also a procession route. The roads that radiated beyond the central area connected Angkor to other regions of the Khmer world, and this wider road system and the structures of Jayavarman VII's reign are treated in Chapter 38. The focus here is the physical integration of Greater Angkor.

Water transport complemented roads. Canals and the wet-season expansion of waterways allowed boats to carry goods, building materials, and people, and the lake gave access to the larger Tonle Sap and Mekong river systems and thereby to long-distance trade. Land and water routes together allowed the movement of rice, fish, stone, timber, and craft goods across the landscape and between Angkor and its hinterland. The interdependence of routes and water control is again apparent: a raised road might serve as a dike, a canal might serve as a transport route, and damage to one could disrupt the other.

Roads and bridges also required maintenance. Bridges, built with stone or laterite and sometimes with timber, needed repair, and embankments could erode. As with the water network, the road system was built and modified across centuries, and its form at any one time was the product of successive additions.

Section 35.18

Building and Maintaining an Engineered Landscape

An engineered landscape of this size required sustained labor. Digging basins and raising embankments meant moving very large volumes of earth by hand, with baskets, carrying poles, and simple tools. Clearing silt from channels, repairing breached embankments, reinforcing spillways, and maintaining moats and roads were recurring tasks, not one-off efforts. Feeding, housing, and organizing workers added to the logistics. We lack quantitative records of workforce size or time, and any specific numbers should be treated as speculative.

Sources suggest several overlapping ways in which labor was organized. Inscriptions record that temples held dependents, including people assigned to maintain temple lands and facilities, and that rulers and officials could direct people to work. Rulers' sponsorship of reservoirs and temples is recorded in royal inscriptions. Local communities presumably maintained their own ponds, paths, and small channels. This evidence should not be reduced to a single model. It is not safe to assume a uniform system of forced labor across centuries and regions; the arrangements probably included royal and temple obligations, dependent and enslaved labor of various kinds, local community effort, and paid or exchanged work, with proportions that varied by period and project. Chapters 34 and 39 discuss the institutions and social categories involved.

Maintenance was as important as construction. A reservoir or channel that was not kept clear and repaired would degrade, and a system built over centuries accumulated a long list of structures needing attention. Repair is visible archaeologically: excavations show recutting of channels, rebuilding of embankments, and modification of spillways. The relationship between the capacity to maintain the system and the political and social arrangements that supported it is an important issue for the later history of Angkor, discussed in Chapter 61.

Section 35.19

A Landscape Built and Rebuilt over Centuries

The preceding sections have repeatedly noted that Angkor's infrastructure was cumulative. Roads, reservoirs, neighborhoods, and canals were added over five centuries or more, and earlier features were reused, enlarged, bypassed, or abandoned. The result was an increasingly interconnected but complicated system. Interconnection brought benefits: water and goods could be moved widely, and districts were linked. It also brought complexity: a change at one point could have effects at others, and the system accumulated structures of different dates and designs whose interactions were not always planned.

Section 35.20

Sedimentation, Erosion, Drought, and Flood Risk

Environmental evidence for Angkor comes from several sources, and it is best treated as a set of partial records. Sediment cores from reservoirs, moats, rivers, and the lake record layers of deposition, which, combined with pollen, charcoal, and chemical signatures, can show changes in vegetation, erosion, and land use. River and channel evidence shows changes in watercourses and the buildup of sediment in structures. Climate reconstructions, notably tree-ring records from long-lived trees in Vietnam, give a regional picture of rainfall variability across centuries, with a dating precision to individual years.

The climatic record suggests that the fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries included extended droughts, one in the mid-fourteenth century and another in the early fifteenth century, alternating with years of intense monsoon rainfall. This is a pattern of variability and extremes, and it matters because a water system designed and maintained under one set of conditions could be stressed by another. Drought affects water supply and harvests. Intense rainfall raises flood risk and can damage embankments and channels. A sequence of both, within a few decades, is a particular challenge.

Human activity affected the system too. Clearing forest and expanding settlement and agriculture can increase erosion and sediment loads in streams. Sediment carried into channels, ponds, and reservoirs reduces capacity and can block inlets and outlets, requiring cleaning that is costly in labor. Geoarchaeological research published in 2019 found evidence for a gradual reduction in land-use intensity in Angkor's central area during the fourteenth century, and interpreted the pattern as a long adjustment, not a sudden collapse. These findings concern the central area and should not be generalized to the whole landscape or equated with abandonment, since other evidence documents continued occupation and religious use of parts of Angkor afterward.

The interpretation requires care. Environmental records show conditions and plausible stresses. They do not by themselves prove that any particular drought or flood caused a particular failure or a particular political or social change. Dating uncertainties, differences between local and regional records, and the complexity of human responses all limit what can be claimed. Environmental history here supplies context and mechanisms, not a single cause.

Section 35.21

Systemic Vulnerability: Could Complexity Become a Weakness?

A connected network has advantages: redundancy, flexibility, and the ability to move water to where it is needed. It also has a characteristic vulnerability. If key connections fail under stress, the failure can propagate: a breached embankment sends water into a channel not designed for it, which damages the next structure downstream, and so on. This is the concept of cascading failure, which engineers apply to power grids, transport systems, and water networks.

A 2018 study published in Science Advances developed a numerical model of Angkor's water network and examined how it would respond to extreme conditions. The model found that severe flooding, especially at a time when sediment and modifications had altered the system, could produce cascading failures, in which damage at crucial junctions disrupted flows through large parts of the network. This is important evidence for possible systemic vulnerability, and it connects the engineering of the network to the climatic record of intense monsoons in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries.

But the distinction between demonstrated physical changes and simulated vulnerabilities must be taught explicitly. A model shows that a failure mechanism is possible under specified assumptions. It does not show that the failure happened, when, or with what consequences for people. The actual historical sequence depends on evidence of physical damage, such as breaches, silting, and abandoned channels, on dating, and on evidence of how people responded, including repairs, changes in settlement, and relocation of activity. Some such evidence exists, notably for modification and repair of structures and for gradual change in land use, but it does not establish a single catastrophic event.

Division VI — Reinterpreting Angkor: The Hydraulic City and Low-Density Urbanism
Section 35.22

The Hydraulic City Debate: Irrigation, Ritual, and Urban Engineering

Bernard-Philippe Groslier, a French archaeologist who directed conservation work at Angkor, argued in the mid-twentieth century that Angkor's barays and canals were the foundation of a rice-based economy. In his view, the reservoirs stored monsoon water and released it to irrigate extensive paddies, supporting the population and the state, and the large-scale works reflected a society organized around agricultural production through water control. He also suggested that the strain of maintaining and extending the system contributed to Angkor's difficulties. The thesis is often called the hydraulic city, and it echoed broader twentieth-century theories linking irrigation to centralized states.

Historical Debate

Irrigation, Ritual, or Multipurpose Infrastructure? The Hydraulic-City Thesis

The question: Were Angkor's barays and canals designed and used chiefly to irrigate rice fields, as the hydraulic-city thesis proposed, or did they serve other functions, such as ritual, flood control, urban water supply, and display, with irrigation playing a lesser or varying role?

Interpretation A — The hydraulic city: Groslier's thesis held that the reservoirs and canals formed an irrigation system supplying large areas of rice land, making Angkor an agrarian, water-managing state. Evidence cited included the scale of the barays, the associated channels, and the extent of field patterns visible from the air.
Interpretation B — Critics: ritual and symbolic function: Later scholars, including Van Liere and Acker, criticized the thesis. They pointed to the lack of clear evidence for outlet channels distributing water from some barays to fields, to the placement of barays on sloping terrain unsuited to gravity irrigation of wide areas, and to the temples, island shrines, and cosmological imagery associated with the barays. They argued that the barays were primarily symbolic or ritual and that rice farming relied largely on rainfall and local sources.
Interpretation C — Multipurpose, changing infrastructure: Research associated with Fletcher, Penny, Evans, and colleagues, based on mapping of channels, flow studies, excavation, and sediment evidence, suggests a network developed and modified over centuries for several purposes, including water storage, possible agricultural distribution, flood control, and ritual. Different elements may have emphasized different functions at different times.

Evidence: Participants draw on the same body of material: reservoir dimensions and embankments, inlet and outlet structures, canal alignments, field patterns in imagery and lidar, sediment cores, and temple and inscriptional evidence. They differ in how they interpret the absence or presence of outlets, how they weigh symbolism against function, and how they handle the problem that structures of different dates are being compared.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation of function, the scale of irrigation relative to rainfall, and chronology: which elements operated together. It is less about whether the works were large or whether they had religious significance, on which most scholars agree.

Why it matters: If Angkor's economy depended on centrally managed irrigation, the water network is central to explaining state power, labor organization, and eventual vulnerability. If rainfall and local water supply were primary, the state's relationship to agriculture and the reasons for the network's construction look different. The evidence does not establish that every baray was designed principally for irrigation or that all fields relied on centrally controlled releases.

Student task: Using the evidence described in this chapter, construct the strongest case for each interpretation, then identify what additional evidence, such as datable outlets, sediment records downstream of a baray, or excavation of field systems, could help decide among them.

The debate has moved from a binary choice toward recognition of multiple functions, but disagreements remain about the balance. The criticism of the hydraulic thesis was valuable because it forced attention to evidence and to symbolism. The later work was valuable because it added mapping, dating, and environmental data. A student should treat "hydraulic city" as a significant historical hypothesis, not as a settled description and not as a discredited one.

Section 35.23

Was Angkor a City? Low-Density Urbanism and Urban Definitions

Angkor challenges a common picture of the city as a compact, walled, densely built settlement. Its monumental districts were concentrated, but its inhabitants lived across a wide landscape of dispersed neighborhoods interspersed with ponds, gardens, and fields. Does such a place count as a city? The answer depends on definition, and it shows how modern categories can mislead when applied to premodern settlements.

Historical Vocabulary
Low-density urbanism

Definition. Low-density urbanism, sometimes called agrarian-based or low-density agrarian urbanism, is a scholarly concept, developed in work by Roland Fletcher and others, describing very large settlement complexes in which a substantial population, concentrated and dispersed, lived in a landscape that combined residences, ceremonial centers, and extensive productive land, with relatively low population density per unit area.

Historical context. The concept was used to interpret Angkor and some other tropical settlements, where food production occurred within the settlement area, and where the population was spread across a large area rather than packed inside walls.

Why it matters. It explains how a settlement of enormous extent and large population could exist without the density of the compact city, and it focuses attention on the integration of agriculture, water management, and settlement.

Potential misunderstanding. "Low density" does not mean small, rural, or unplanned. It also does not mean that no part of Angkor was dense: the central districts were more concentrated. And the term is a modern analytical label, not a category used by the Angkorians themselves.

Reasons for calling Angkor urban include the scale of the settlement and population, the concentration of ceremonial, administrative, and religious institutions in central districts, evidence of planned grids, the specialized crafts and trade attested in inscriptions and by Zhou Daguan, and the connected infrastructure that integrated the whole. Reasons for caution include the fact that much of the area was agricultural and that the population was dispersed. The most useful approach is to compare concentrated ceremonial districts, dispersed neighborhoods, and productive land as parts of one system, and to recognize that the system's character was different from that of compact cities elsewhere.

Claims that Angkor was exceptionally large among premodern cities require the same care as population estimates. They depend on which area is measured, how density is treated, and how well other cities have been surveyed. The defensible statement is that Angkor was among the most extensive premodern urban complexes known, and that its extent reflects its low-density form. Superlatives about "the largest" city should be used only with these qualifications.

Section 35.24

Angkor as an Urban Ecosystem: Achievement, Adaptation, and Transformation

The evidence surveyed in this chapter supports an interpretation of Angkor as an urban ecosystem: a landscape produced by long-term human modification of a monsoon environment, in which settlement, agriculture, water, and infrastructure interacted. It was not a monument, and not the product of a single engineering project. Its parts were built by different rulers, communities, and workers over centuries, and their relationships changed as the system grew.

The achievement was real. People organized the movement of vast quantities of earth, created reservoirs and channels that shaped water flows across a plain of very gentle slope, integrated them with roads, neighborhoods, and fields, and sustained a large population. They adapted over time, adding, modifying, and repairing. Adaptation also had costs: accumulated structures brought complexity and maintenance demands, and the system operated in an environment of climatic variability.

The environmental and infrastructural evidence points to change in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, including gradual reduction in land-use intensity in the central area, evidence of repair and modification, and climatic extremes, and models suggest that network vulnerability was a possible factor. But the evidence does not support a story of sudden abandonment. Parts of Angkor remained occupied, and Angkor Wat continued as a religious site. The wider political, religious, economic, and military dimensions of transformation are the subject of Chapter 61, and it would be a mistake to read the environmental evidence as a complete explanation.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Settlement, Infrastructure, and Landscape Use at Angkor

What Changed

  • The focus of urban and ceremonial construction shifted from Phnom Kulen and Roluos to the Angkor plain, and then among successive centers.
  • The water network grew from a single large reservoir to a system of barays, channels, moats, and thousands of ponds.
  • Formal grids and denser residential districts developed around central monuments between the eleventh and thirteenth centuries.
  • In the fourteenth century and later, land-use intensity in the central area gradually declined, and structures were modified or repaired.

What Stayed the Same

  • The monsoon rainfall regime and the gentle southward gradient continued to frame water management.
  • Rice cultivation, fishing, and local ponds remained basic to the way people lived and fed themselves.
  • The intermixing of residence, farmland, and water facilities persisted across centuries.
  • Religious use and residence at parts of Angkor continued after the central area's land-use intensity declined.

When and how: Change was cumulative and uneven, not abrupt. Builders added over five centuries, and later change appears gradual in the central area according to current evidence. Who experienced it most directly: Residents of the central districts and communities dependent on repaired or failing channels experienced change most immediately, while many rural households experienced greater continuity in their local ponds, fields, and shrines.

Why the pattern matters: It shows that a landscape can be productive and adaptable while still accumulating vulnerabilities, and that transformation need not mean disappearance.

The institutions that gave Angkor's landscape religious meaning, its temples, priesthoods, endowments, and ritual practices, are examined in Chapter 36. They were not separate from the physical landscape described here, and the temples, reservoirs, roads, and neighborhoods of Angkor have to be understood together.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Angkor was an extensive, historically evolving urban-agricultural landscape and not merely a collection of temples supported by an irrigation system. The monuments that dominate popular and early scholarly images were the most durable part of a much larger inhabited world of neighborhoods, ponds, roads, fields, reservoirs, and canals, and understanding that world has required methods that go beyond epigraphy and architectural history.

The natural setting framed the problems Angkorians faced. The plain between Phnom Kulen and the Tonle Sap has a very gentle slope, and the monsoon concentrates rainfall in a single season. Water was therefore alternately abundant and scarce, and soils varied in fertility. These conditions created needs and risks, but they did not dictate any particular political or engineering outcome.

Archaeologists have reconstructed the landscape through field survey, excavation, aerial photography, radar, sediment coring, radiocarbon dating, and geographic information systems. Airborne lidar, in a survey flown in 2012 and published in 2013, revealed planned grids, ponds, mounds, and roads beneath forest. Its power is real but bounded: it reveals surface form, not date, function, or population, which depend on excavation and modeling.

The urban structure developed over centuries, from early settlement on Phnom Kulen and at Hariharalaya through Yaśodharapura and the later formal grids around Angkor Wat and Angkor Thom. Ordinary residents lived in clusters of perishable houses on low mounds, beside ponds and small shrines, interspersed with gardens and fields. Questions about size require care: the ceremonial core, the connected settlement landscape of roughly 1,000 square kilometers, and the roughly 3,000-square-kilometer Greater Angkor Region are different analytical areas, and the modeled population of roughly 700,000 to 900,000 in the thirteenth century is an estimate resting on assumptions.

Water engineering was the chapter's largest theme. Barays, canals, diversions, moats, embankments, spillways, and thousands of household ponds formed a network built and repeatedly modified over centuries. Reservoirs such as Indratataka, the East Baray, the West Baray, and the Jayatataka belong to different periods and probably served several purposes, including storage, flood regulation, ritual, and possibly agricultural supply. Zhou Daguan's late-thirteenth-century remark about shared household ponds connects an eyewitness text with the mapped landscape, though it cannot convert a pond count into a population.

Agriculture drew on rain-fed paddies, local ponds and streams, and, in some places, supplemental reservoir water. The evidence does not establish that all rice farming depended on centralized irrigation or that multiple annual harvests were universal. The food economy was diversified, with fish, gardens, and trade, and it remained exposed to monsoon variability. Roads, causeways, and canals integrated the landscape physically, and its construction and maintenance required sustained labor organized in several ways, not a single uniform system.

Environmental evidence points to a landscape under growing pressure in the fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries: sedimentation and erosion, droughts and intense monsoons, and gradual reduction in land-use intensity in the central area. A 2018 numerical model showed that severe flooding could produce cascading failure in the network. This is evidence of possible vulnerability, not proof of the actual historical sequence, and it cannot by itself explain Angkor's transformation, which belongs to the wider reassessment in Chapter 61.

Interpretively, the chapter traced a move from Groslier's hydraulic-city thesis, through critiques emphasizing ritual and symbolism, to multipurpose and changing-infrastructure interpretations. It also showed why Angkor can be understood as a low-density urban complex, extensive and populous but organized very differently from compact walled cities. Claims about its relative size must be qualified accordingly.

The overall lesson is that Angkor's achievements and vulnerabilities arose from the same source: a long-accumulated, interconnected human landscape in a variable monsoon environment. Parts of Angkor remained occupied and sacred afterward. The temples, priesthoods, and ritual institutions that gave this landscape religious meaning are the subject of Chapter 36.

MGU Library Research Project

Comparing Three Maps: The 2007 Archaeological Map, the 2013 Lidar Findings, and the 2021 Population Model

This project asks you to compare three landmark pieces of research on Greater Angkor and evaluate what each can and cannot establish.

  1. Locate the publications. Using library resources, find the 2007 comprehensive archaeological map of Angkor, the 2013 lidar publication, and the 2021 diachronic population study.
  2. Summarize each study's evidence and method. For each, identify what was observed, what dating was used, and what assumptions were made.
  3. Compare areas and scales. Record the areas each study covers and explain why they differ.
  4. Assess what each can establish. Identify which claims are direct observation, which rest on dating, and which are modeled.
  5. Write your findings. In a 3–5 page essay or equivalent presentation, explain how the three studies build on each other, where they leave questions open, and what further evidence would help, using careful language such as "this remains debated."

Library resources to use: Academic journals on archaeology and Southeast Asian history, published lidar and mapping datasets and their documentation, translations of Zhou Daguan's account, and the MGU History Library's reference materials on Angkor.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it changes what Angkor is understood to be. A site long known for temples is now also known as a landscape of homes, ponds, roads, fields, and engineered water, built by thousands of people over centuries. That shift illustrates a broader lesson of this textbook: Southeast Asian societies were active historical agents who reshaped their environments, not passive settings for outside influence or elite monuments.

It also shows how historical knowledge is made. The picture of Angkor changed because new tools and new questions exposed evidence that had always been there, and because scholars tested old theories against it. At each stage the discipline of distinguishing observation, dating, and inference has been essential, and students should carry it to every chapter, especially where striking images, large numbers, or compelling models invite more confidence than the evidence allows.

Later chapters build directly on this one. Chapter 36 examines the temples and religious institutions embedded in this landscape. Chapters 37 and 38 treat the reigns that added Angkor Wat, Angkor Thom, and the Jayatataka. Chapter 39 reconstructs the daily life of the people whose houses and ponds this chapter mapped. Chapter 61 reassesses Angkor's transformation, and it should be read with the understanding developed here that infrastructure, climate, and settlement interacted with political and religious change, and that parts of Angkor remained occupied and sacred.

Finally, Angkor's landscape reminds us that achievement and vulnerability can arise together. A system that grew by accumulation, connected many places, and sustained a large population was also complex to maintain in a variable environment. Understanding that dual character, without teleology and without reducing Angkor to either a triumph or a failure, is the purpose of this chapter and a model for how to approach the societies examined in the rest of the textbook.
