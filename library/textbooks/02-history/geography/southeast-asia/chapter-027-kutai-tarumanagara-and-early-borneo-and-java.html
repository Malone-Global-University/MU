Kutai, Tarumanagara, and Early Borneo and Java

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Seven weathered stone posts stand near the Mahakam River at Muara Kaman in eastern Borneo, each carved with Sanskrit verse in a script derived from South Asian models. A thousand kilometers to the west, inscriptions scattered across the hills and riverbanks of western Java record a king's name beside the carved outline of two human feet. Neither set of inscriptions describes a nation, a capital city, or a bureaucratic government in any detail a modern reader would recognize. Both describe something more specific and, in some ways, more revealing: rulers who had found, in a cosmopolitan language and an imported repertoire of ritual and symbol, new tools for declaring who they were and why their authority deserved recognition.

Along the middle reaches of the Mahakam River, where the waterway widens near the modern locality of Muara Kaman, boatmen, cultivators, and traders had long exchanged goods and labor within networks that linked Borneo's interior to its coasts and, through the Makassar Strait, to the wider archipelago, a world already examined in Chapters 17 through 19. Nothing about this riverine society required Sanskrit, Brahmanical ritual, or a genealogy inscribed in stone. And yet, at some point conventionally dated to the fourth or fifth century CE, seven stone *yūpas*, sacrificial posts of a kind associated with Vedic ritual in South Asia, were inscribed with Sanskrit verses commemorating the genealogy, sacrifices, and ritual gift-giving of a ruler calling himself Mūlavarman. The inscriptions themselves indicate that Brahmins erected or prepared the posts in commemoration of these acts, naming Mūlavarman's father and grandfather alongside an account of a massive ritual gift of cattle to Brahmin priests. These seven stones are among the oldest securely datable inscriptions found anywhere in the Indonesian archipelago, and they introduce, for the first time in this textbook's narrative of Borneo, a local ruler becoming visible in a permanent epigraphic record commemorating his rule and ritual acts, rather than only through the eyes of distant observers.

A comparable, though not identical, transformation unfolded in western Java. There, along the banks of the Citarum River and in the hills around what is now Bogor, a king named Pūrṇavarman commissioned a cluster of inscriptions, probably somewhat later than the Mahakam yūpas, that associated his rule with the Hindu god Viṣṇu, that carved the outline of his own footprints, or those attributed to him, into stone beside river crossings, and that commemorated the excavation of a substantial waterway completed, the inscription claims, in a remarkably short span of days. This western Javanese political world is directly attested in Pūrṇavarman's own epigraphic record: the Ciaruteun inscription describes him, in the Sanskrit phrase tārūmanagarendrasya, as the ruler of Tārumanagara, giving historians substantially firmer ground for treating this name as a contemporary political designation than for the Mahakam material, which never once mentions the word "Kutai," by which the eastern Bornean polity is conventionally known today.

These two bodies of evidence, separated by geography, by specific religious orientation, and very possibly by several generations, nonetheless pose a single shared historical problem, one this chapter treats as its central responsibility. Both sets of inscriptions show local rulers using Sanskrit language and Indic-derived script to express royal and religious authority, but they do so quite differently: the Mahakam material emphasizes genealogy, sacrifice, and ritual gift-giving, while Pūrṇavarman's inscriptions emphasize divine comparison, carved footprints, landscape-marking, and royal waterworks. In both cases, rulers drew on a cluster of ideas that originated, in a general cultural sense, from South Asia, and used those borrowed tools to build or declare authority within societies that were themselves locally rooted: riverine, agricultural, commercially connected, and historically embedded in the archipelago rather than imported wholesale from across the Bay of Bengal. Understanding how, and how far, this process of borrowing and adaptation actually proceeded, and understanding the severe limits of what seven stone posts or a handful of riverside inscriptions can and cannot tell historians about entire societies, is the task of this chapter.

It is tempting, when confronted with inscriptions this old and this evocative, to build confident narratives around them: a kingdom named Kutai, ruled from a capital, governing a defined territory; a Hindu-Buddhist kingdom of Tarumanagara engineering major public works across a settled countryside. Some such narratives appear in general surveys of Indonesian history, and earlier generations of scholarship sometimes advanced them with more confidence than the evidence can bear. This chapter asks students to read the inscriptions far more carefully than that, distinguishing what Mūlavarman and Pūrṇavarman's inscriptions actually say from the conventional labels, modern place names, and confident narrative scaffolding that later writers, including this textbook's own predecessors in the field, have sometimes constructed around them.

This chapter closes Part V of this textbook, which has traced the earliest documented political centers across mainland and maritime Southeast Asia: Funan and Chenla in the Mekong world, Champa along the central Vietnamese coast, the Red River delta under Chinese rule, the Pyu city-states of the Irrawaddy basin, Dvaravati and the Mon world of the Chao Phraya basin, and the early states of the Malay Peninsula. Borneo and western Java complete this survey by showing how the same broad process of selective adoption examined throughout Part V, and analyzed directly as a historiographical problem in Chapter 19, unfolded in two societies whose evidentiary base consists chiefly of royal inscriptions rather than extensive archaeological excavation or a continuous textual record.

The chapter also introduces, more directly than most earlier chapters in this textbook, a methodological lesson that will recur throughout the volumes ahead: royal inscriptions are not neutral windows onto entire societies. They were created to commemorate rulers and elite religious or political acts, using formulas and conventions inherited from wider ritual traditions. They tell historians a great deal about how certain rulers wished to be remembered, and comparatively little about the farmers, boat crews, artisans, and ritual specialists whose labor and resources made royal patronage possible in the first place. Learning to extract what inscriptions can responsibly tell us, while remaining honest about what they cannot, is itself one of the chapter's central objectives.

By the chapter's end, students should be able to explain what the Muara Kaman yūpas and the Tarumanagara inscriptions actually document, distinguish this documented evidence from the conventional scholarly labels built around it, and evaluate competing claims about the scale, nature, and historical significance of Borneo and Java's earliest inscriptionally attested polities.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain what the Muara Kaman yūpa inscriptions and the Tarumanagara inscriptions each actually state, as distinguished from later scholarly inference and popular retelling.
  2. Describe the geographic settings of the Mahakam River in eastern Borneo and the Citarum basin and surrounding hills of western Java.
  3. Trace the genealogy recorded in the yūpa inscriptions, from Kundungga through Aśvavarman to Mūlavarman, and evaluate what it can and cannot demonstrate about cultural change.
  4. Analyze the Tugu inscription's account of waterway construction as a commemorative royal claim rather than a straightforward engineering record.
  5. Distinguish the conventional scholarly label "Kutai" from the contemporary epigraphic record, which does not itself use that name.
  6. Identify the religious plurality of early western Java by comparing Pūrṇavarman's Vaiṣṇava inscriptions with the Buddhist archaeological remains at Batujaya.
  7. Evaluate competing interpretations of how large, how centralized, and how territorially defined these early polities actually were.
  8. Apply the concept of localization, introduced in Chapter 19, to the specific cases of Sanskritic kingship in Borneo and Java.
  9. Assess the limitations of royal inscriptions as historical evidence, including their bias toward rulers, ritual, and prestige.
  10. Connect the evidence examined in this chapter to the broader historiographical debate over "Indianization" versus localization developed across Part IV and Chapter 19.
  11. Avoid anachronistic claims about Indian colonization, ethnic conversion, or precisely bordered territorial kingdoms that the inscriptional evidence does not support.
  12. Articulate why the people who produced the resources and labor behind royal inscriptions remain largely invisible within the evidentiary record, and why this matters for historical interpretation.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What do the earliest inscriptions of Borneo and western Java reveal about the creation of new forms of kingship, and what do they leave unknown about the societies those rulers governed?
  2. Why should historians be cautious about equating the appearance of Sanskrit inscriptions with the arrival of a ready-made "Indian kingdom"?
  3. How does the three-generation genealogy recorded in the yūpa inscriptions illustrate, and also potentially overstate, a process of cultural transformation?
  4. Why does the absence of the word "Kutai" from the Muara Kaman inscriptions matter for how historians describe this polity?
  5. What can royal inscriptions tell historians about ritual, genealogy, and prestige, and what can they not tell historians about territory, administration, or population?
  6. How does the presence of Buddhist archaeological remains at Batujaya complicate a simple picture of a uniformly "Hindu kingdom" in early western Java?
  7. Why must the Tugu inscription's claim about a waterway completed in twenty-one days be read as a commemorative royal statement rather than a literal engineering record?
  8. What would it take, in terms of evidence, to determine confidently how large or how centralized the Muara Kaman or Tarumanagara polities actually were?
  9. Whose experiences and perspectives are largely absent from inscriptions that center on kings, Brahmins, and ritual gifts?

Historical Context

This chapter's two case studies do not emerge from nowhere. Chapters 17 through 19 established that, well before any Sanskrit inscription was carved in Borneo or Java, maritime exchange networks already linked coastal Vietnam, the Philippines, Borneo, and eastern Indonesia, and that expanding Indian Ocean and South China Sea trade had begun drawing merchants, religious specialists, and commodities into increasingly dense circulation across the archipelago and the mainland alike. Chapter 19 examined directly the historiographical shift away from older models of "Indianization," in which Indian civilization was imagined to have been transplanted more or less intact onto a passive Southeast Asian landscape, toward a model of selective adoption and localization, in which Southeast Asian rulers, merchants, and religious specialists chose which elements of Sanskritic political theory, Buddhist and Hindu religious practice, and associated artistic and architectural forms to adopt, and then transformed those elements according to local political and cultural needs.

Borneo and western Java, the subjects of this chapter, provide two of the clearest and earliest test cases for that model anywhere in the archipelago. Both regions had long participated in local and regional exchange before any inscription was carved. The Mahakam River basin in eastern Borneo connected upriver communities, likely engaged in forest-product collection, swidden and other forms of cultivation, and localized exchange, to coastal and maritime networks extending through the Makassar Strait toward Sulawesi and the wider archipelago. Western Java's river systems, particularly the Citarum, drained a landscape capable of supporting agriculture and linked an inland upland zone around modern Bogor to a coastal plain near what is now Jakarta Bay, itself positioned along routes used by ships moving through the Sunda Strait and along the Java Sea.

Nothing in the archaeological or geographic record of either region suggests that these societies were simple, isolated, or lacking in internal political organization before Sanskrit inscriptions began to appear. What changed, in the particular centuries this chapter examines, was not the existence of organized political life but the adoption, by specific rulers, of new technologies of commemoration and legitimation: writing in Sanskrit using an Indic-derived script, a repertoire of Brahmanical ritual centered on sacrificial gift-giving, and a vocabulary of divine association and dynastic genealogy that drew on South Asian models while serving distinctly local political purposes.

This chapter also inherits a specific evidentiary situation that differs somewhat from earlier chapters in Part V. For Funan, Chenla, Champa, and the Pyu city-states, historians can draw on a combination of foreign textual accounts, local inscriptions, and substantial archaeological excavation. For the Mahakam yūpas, by contrast, the evidentiary base is unusually narrow: seven inscribed stone posts, without an accompanying urban excavation of comparable scale, and without corroborating foreign textual accounts describing the same polity by a name that can be matched with confidence to the inscriptions. Western Java's Tarumanagara material is somewhat richer, including several additional inscriptions and a nearby complex of archaeological remains at Batujaya, but still falls well short of the kind of comprehensive urban and administrative documentation available for some later Southeast Asian kingdoms examined in Volume III. This chapter treats that evidentiary narrowness as a central interpretive challenge rather than an obstacle to be smoothed over through confident narrative reconstruction.

Major People, Groups, and Places

Reference — People, Groups & Places

Kundungga
The earliest named ancestor recorded in the Muara Kaman genealogy. His name is generally regarded by specialists as non-Sanskritic in form, which has led some scholars to treat him as a locally rooted figure preceding the fuller adoption of Sanskritic naming conventions, though this chapter cautions against turning that observation into a confident biographical claim about his religious identity.
Aśvavarman
Kundungga's son, described in the yūpa inscriptions as the founder or originator of the ruling lineage (vaṃśakartā) and bearing a fully Sanskritic name. The shift in naming convention between Kundungga and Aśvavarman is a central piece of evidence for cultural change across a single genealogy, though the inscriptions do not explain the circumstances of that change.
Mūlavarman
Aśvavarman's son, the ruler commemorated in the seven surviving yūpa inscriptions, which the texts indicate were erected by Brahmins and which record his genealogy and commemorate his substantial ritual gift of cattle to Brahmins at a sacred site called Vaprakeśvara.
The Mahakam River and Muara Kaman
The major river system of eastern Borneo along which the yūpa inscriptions were found, and the locality near which they were discovered, situated along routes connecting Borneo's interior to the coast and the Makassar Strait.
The conventional label "Kutai"
A modern scholarly designation, derived from the regional name of the area in which the yūpas were found, applied to the polity associated with Mūlavarman. The word "Kutai" does not itself appear in the fourth- or fifth-century inscriptions and should not be treated as the polity's own contemporary name.
Pūrṇavarman
The king named in the western Javanese inscriptions associated with the kingdom of Taruma, or Tarumanagara, whose reign is commemorated through inscriptions invoking Viṣṇu, carved footprints, and an account of major waterway construction.
Tarumanagara (Taruma)
The name of the kingdom recorded in Pūrṇavarman's own inscriptions, in contrast to the Muara Kaman material. Tarumanagara is associated with the Citarum River basin and surrounding areas of western Java.
The Citarum River basin and the Bogor uplands
The principal geographic setting of the Tarumanagara inscriptions, linking an inland upland zone to a coastal plain near present-day Jakarta Bay.
The Ciaruteun and Kebon Kopi inscriptions
Western Javanese inscriptions associated with Pūrṇavarman, notable for their carved footprint imagery, the Ciaruteun footprints linked to the king and the god Viṣṇu, and the Kebon Kopi elephant footprints linked to the god Indra's elephant mount, Airāvata.
The Tugu inscription
A Tarumanagara inscription recording the excavation of the Candrabhaga and Gomati waterways during Pūrṇavarman's twenty-second regnal year, accompanied by a ritual gift of cattle to Brahmins; the inscription's engineering claims require careful, non-literal historical reading.
Batujaya
An archaeological complex near the ancient Citarum delta containing numerous localities and substantial early Buddhist monuments, demonstrating religious diversity within or near the broader world associated with the Tarumanagara inscriptions.
Brahmin ritual specialists
South Asian-derived religious specialists who performed sacrificial and ritual functions for rulers such as Mūlavarman and who received substantial gifts recorded in the inscriptions; their presence attests to religious and ritual exchange without implying a wholesale population transfer from South Asia.

Section 27.1

Borneo and Java before the Sanskrit Inscriptions

Neither eastern Borneo nor western Java entered history as a blank landscape waiting to be inscribed. Chapters 17 and 18 traced expanding maritime exchange networks linking coastal Vietnam, the Philippines, Borneo, and eastern Indonesia by the early centuries of the first millennium CE, part of a wider system of South China Sea and Indian Ocean trade that drew merchants, commodities, and ideas into increasingly dense circulation. Archaeological evidence from across the archipelago, including bronze and iron-working traditions, burial practices, and exchange goods examined in Chapter 16, indicates that societies in Borneo and Java had long since developed organized communities capable of producing surplus, participating in exchange, and recognizing forms of local leadership well before any ruler commissioned a Sanskrit inscription.

This context matters because it is easy, when a chapter begins with inscriptions, to imply that writing itself created political order where none existed before. That implication should be firmly resisted. What this chapter traces is not the invention of political authority in Borneo and Java, but a specific and historically significant innovation in how certain rulers chose to represent, commemorate, and legitimate authority that, in some form, almost certainly already existed. The societies along the Mahakam River and the Citarum basin were riverine communities organized around agriculture, forest and sea products, craft production, and exchange, linked to wider networks extending across the archipelago and, increasingly, toward South Asia. The specific innovation examined in this chapter is the adoption, by rulers within these societies, of Sanskrit language, Indic-derived script, Brahmanical ritual, and a vocabulary of divine kingship as tools for expressing and reinforcing authority that had its own local foundations.

Students should therefore approach the yūpa inscriptions and the Tarumanagara material not as the first chapter of Borneo's or Java's political history, but as a documentary threshold: the point at which local rulers, for reasons this chapter can only partially reconstruct, began leaving behind permanent, legible, datable statements about themselves. What came before remains real, significant, and historically continuous with what follows; it simply left behind a different, and in most respects far less legible, kind of evidence.

Section 27.2

When Inscriptions Enter the Historical Record

This chapter rests almost entirely on a specialized kind of historical evidence: epigraphy, the study of inscriptions carved or engraved on durable materials such as stone, metal, or other permanent surfaces. Epigraphic evidence differs in important ways from the archaeological evidence, foreign textual accounts, and administrative records that inform other chapters in Part V, and understanding those differences is essential before examining the Muara Kaman and Tarumanagara material in detail.

Historical Vocabulary
Epigraphy and Paleography

Definition: Epigraphy is the scholarly discipline concerned with reading, translating, and interpreting inscriptions, texts carved or engraved on stone, metal, or other durable surfaces. Paleography, a closely related discipline, is the study of the historical development of handwriting and script styles, used by scholars to estimate the approximate date of an undated inscription by comparing its letterforms with other inscriptions of known or better-established date.

Historical context: Neither the Muara Kaman yūpas nor most of the Tarumanagara inscriptions carry an explicit calendar date in the way a modern document might. Their approximate placement in the fourth, fifth, or later centuries CE rests substantially on paleographic comparison with inscriptions elsewhere in South and Southeast Asia whose dates are more firmly established, supplemented in some cases by archaeological context.

Why it matters: Students should understand that even an inscription confidently described in survey literature as dating to "around the fifth century CE" typically carries a real margin of uncertainty, and that this uncertainty is a normal and expected feature of working with early epigraphic evidence rather than a sign of scholarly carelessness.

Potential misunderstanding: It is tempting to treat an inscription's date as being as precise as the publication date of a modern book. Historians instead treat most early epigraphic dates as considered estimates, generally expressed as a probable range or century, open to revision as new comparative material or analysis becomes available.

A second essential distinction concerns the purpose for which inscriptions such as these were created. The yūpas and the Tarumanagara inscriptions belong to a broad category sometimes called royal eulogy, or in Sanskrit terminology, praśasti: formal, often formulaic texts commissioned by or for a ruler, intended to commemorate a ritual act, a military achievement, a genealogy, or some combination of these, generally for an audience of contemporaries, religious specialists, and, implicitly, posterity. Such inscriptions were not intended to serve as comprehensive administrative records, population counts, or neutral historical narratives. They were political and religious statements, crafted to project a particular image of the ruler who commissioned them.

This does not make royal eulogies useless as historical evidence; quite the opposite. They provide direct, dateable, and often strikingly specific evidence of how rulers wished to present themselves, which religious traditions they patronized, which genealogical claims they wished to advance, and what kinds of resources they could mobilize for ritual and commemorative purposes. But students must read such evidence with an awareness of its genre: what an inscription states about a ruler's generosity, piety, or victories should be understood as a claim the inscription makes, not as an independently verified historical fact, unless corroborated by other evidence.

Section 27.3

The Mahakam River and Muara Kaman

Eastern Borneo's major geographic feature is the Mahakam River, a long waterway draining the island's interior highlands and emptying into the Makassar Strait, the body of water separating Borneo from Sulawesi. The Mahakam basin includes a mix of lowland floodplain, interior forest, and upland terrain, and the river itself has long served as the primary corridor connecting interior communities to coastal settlements and, through the strait, to maritime networks extending across the archipelago.

The locality of Muara Kaman, situated along the middle Mahakam, is where the seven yūpa inscriptions examined in this chapter were discovered. "Muara" is a term referring to a river confluence or mouth, and the locality's name reflects its position at the junction of the Mahakam with a smaller tributary. This riverine position would have allowed a political center based there to draw on both upriver resources, including forest products and agricultural surplus from interior communities, and downriver and coastal connections linking the Mahakam basin to broader exchange networks.

Geography of this kind created real opportunities for a riverine political center to accumulate resources, control movement along the waterway, and mediate exchange between interior and coastal zones. It is important, however, not to overstate what geography alone can explain. A river junction favorable to the concentration of authority does not, by itself, produce a kingdom; it creates a set of possibilities that specific historical actors, operating within specific social and political circumstances that remain only partially visible in the surviving evidence, chose to exploit in the particular way attested by the yūpa inscriptions. The geography explains why a political center might plausibly have developed along the Mahakam near Muara Kaman; it does not explain the specific decisions, rivalries, alliances, or resources that produced the particular ruling lineage commemorated in the inscriptions.

Section 27.4

The Yūpa Inscriptions

The central body of evidence for this chapter's first case study consists of seven stone inscriptions, each carved on a *yūpa*, conventionally dated by paleographic comparison to approximately the fourth or fifth century CE, making them among the oldest securely datable inscriptions so far discovered anywhere in the Indonesian archipelago.

Historical Vocabulary
Yūpa

Definition: In South Asian Vedic ritual tradition, a *yūpa* was a sacrificial post, typically of wood, to which an animal victim was bound during a ritual sacrifice. The term and the underlying ritual concept were borrowed into the Mahakam inscriptions, where the surviving objects are stone posts inscribed with Sanskrit verses rather than functional sacrificial implements in the original South Asian sense.

Historical context: By commemorating a ritual sacrifice and gift-giving ceremony on a permanent stone yūpa, Mūlavarman's inscriptions adapted a specific South Asian ritual vocabulary and object type to a monumental, commemorative purpose: creating a lasting, legible record of royal generosity and piety rather than, or in addition to, the yūpa's original ritual function.

Why it matters: The yūpa inscriptions demonstrate a clear case of localization: a South Asian ritual concept and its associated vocabulary were selected and adapted for a specific commemorative purpose within a Bornean political context, rather than being transplanted unchanged.

The surviving inscriptions are written in Sanskrit using a script related to scripts used in South Asia during the same general period, and their content centers on two closely related subjects: a dynastic genealogy naming three generations of rulers, and a commemoration of a substantial ritual gift of cattle made by the ruler commemorated in the inscriptions, Mūlavarman, to Brahmin priests at a place the inscriptions call Vaprakeśvara, apparently a site associated with the worship of Śiva. One of the inscriptions states that the gift included twenty thousand cows, a figure that, as this chapter discusses further below, should be understood as the inscription's own celebratory claim rather than an independently verifiable economic statistic.

The yūpas were not excavated as part of a larger, extensively documented urban site in the way that some other early Southeast Asian political centers have been. This means that, unlike chapters examining Funan's Oc Eo or other more extensively excavated early historic sites, this chapter cannot draw heavily on archaeological evidence of settlement patterns, material culture, or urban planning to supplement the epigraphic record. The yūpas themselves, and the Sanskrit verses carved upon them, constitute the chapter's primary direct evidence for the Muara Kaman polity.

Section 27.5

Kundungga, Aśvavarman, and Mūlavarman

The genealogy recorded across the yūpa inscriptions names three generations of rulers: Kundungga, his son Aśvavarman, and Aśvavarman's son Mūlavarman, the ruler commemorated by the surviving inscriptions, which were, as noted above, erected by Brahmins in his honor. This genealogy offers one of the clearest and most frequently cited pieces of evidence in the entire chapter for a process of cultural transformation occurring within a single ruling lineage, and it deserves careful, non-overreaching analysis.

Kundungga's name is generally regarded by specialists in Old Javanese, Old Malay, and related philology as non-Sanskritic in form, differing in structure from the names borne by his son and grandson. Aśvavarman and Mūlavarman, by contrast, both carry fully Sanskritic names: Aśvavarman incorporating a term associated with horses, and Mūlavarman incorporating a term suggesting origin or foundation. The inscriptions further describe Aśvavarman using the term vaṃśakartā, meaning founder or originator of the lineage, suggesting that the inscriptions themselves framed Aśvavarman, rather than his father, as the effective beginning of a Sanskritically legitimated dynasty.

This pattern, a non-Sanskritic founding ancestor followed by descendants bearing Sanskritic names and explicit dynastic titles, is genuinely suggestive of a process in which a locally rooted lineage adopted Sanskritic naming conventions and, with them, an associated vocabulary of legitimate kingship, across the span of a generation or two. This chapter treats that observation as a valuable and well-supported piece of evidence for cultural and political change.

At the same time, students must resist several tempting but unsupported extensions of this evidence. The inscriptions do not state that Kundungga practiced any specific religion that could be labeled "animism" in opposition to a later conversion to "Hinduism." They do not describe the circumstances under which Aśvavarman adopted a Sanskritic name, and they certainly do not describe a scene of Brahmin missionaries arriving to convert a previously unconverted ruler. Such narrative elaborations have appeared in some older popular and even scholarly accounts of the Muara Kaman inscriptions, but they go well beyond what the stones themselves record. The responsible historical claim is narrower and more precise: the genealogy documents a shift in naming convention and in the explicit vocabulary of dynastic legitimacy across three generations, a shift that most plausibly reflects some process of engagement with Sanskritic religious and political ideas, the specific mechanism, timing, and completeness of which remain unknown.

Section 27.6

Ritual Gifts, Brahmins, and Royal Authority

The yūpa inscriptions' second major subject, alongside the genealogy, is Mūlavarman's commemorated act of ritual generosity. The inscriptions describe a substantial gift, framed in the vocabulary of *dāna*, a Sanskrit term referring to religiously meritorious giving, made to Brahmin priests in connection with a ceremony at Vaprakeśvara. One inscription's often-cited figure of twenty thousand cows has become, in popular retellings, a kind of shorthand for the scale of Mūlavarman's wealth and piety.

This gift-giving pattern should be understood within a broader South and Southeast Asian framework in which rulers used large-scale ritual generosity, particularly toward Brahmin religious specialists, to accumulate religious merit, demonstrate material capacity, and reinforce claims to legitimate authority. The presence of Brahmins at Mūlavarman's court, performing ritual functions and receiving substantial gifts, indicates that some religious specialists trained in Brahmanical tradition, whether locally trained, visiting, or descended from earlier migrants, were active participants in the Mahakam polity's court life by the time the inscriptions were carved.

The specific numerical claim of twenty thousand cows requires particular caution. Inscriptions of this genre commonly employed large, rhetorically impressive figures to convey munificence and piety rather than to provide an auditable economic record. Historians have no independent means of verifying this figure, no corroborating administrative record, and no archaeological evidence of herding on a scale that could confirm or disprove the claim. The appropriate historical treatment is to report the inscription's claim clearly, identify it explicitly as the inscription's own rhetorical statement of royal generosity, and avoid treating it as a precise economic statistic suitable for calculations about the broader Mahakam economy, livestock population, or agricultural capacity.

More broadly, the ritual-gift evidence demonstrates that Mūlavarman's court commanded sufficient resources, whether through tribute, trade, agricultural surplus, or some combination, to support large-scale ceremonial activity and to patronize a specialized religious establishment. This is meaningful evidence of real political and economic capacity, even though it falls well short of enabling historians to reconstruct the Mahakam polity's overall economy or population.

Section 27.7

Was It Really a "Kingdom of Kutai"?

General surveys of Indonesian history frequently refer to the polity associated with Mūlavarman as the "Kingdom of Kutai," sometimes further elaborated as "Kutai Martadipura," distinguishing it from a later, better-documented Islamic sultanate of Kutai that developed in the same general region many centuries afterward. This chapter uses such labels only with explicit qualification, because the word "Kutai" does not appear anywhere in the fourth- or fifth-century yūpa inscriptions themselves.

"Kutai" is a conventional scholarly label, derived from the modern regional name of the area in which the inscriptions were found, applied retrospectively by later historians and archaeologists seeking a convenient way to refer to the polity. This is not, in itself, an illegitimate scholarly practice; historians frequently need working labels for societies whose own self-designation is unknown or unrecorded, and Chapter 1 of this textbook discussed a comparable situation regarding the modern label "Southeast Asia" itself. The problem arises only when a convenient modern label is mistaken for, or silently presented as, the polity's own contemporary name.

This chapter therefore uses the phrase "the Muara Kaman polity, conventionally called early Kutai," at points where precision matters, while permitting the shorter and more familiar label "Kutai" elsewhere in the chapter for readability, always with the understanding established here that this is a modern scholarly convention rather than an attested contemporary name. Students should likewise avoid treating "Kutai Martadipura" as though it were a name the fourth- or fifth-century rulers themselves used or recognized; that fuller name belongs to later tradition and to the subsequent, historically distinct Islamic sultanate of Kutai, not to the yūpa-era polity examined in this chapter.

This distinction matters for more than terminological tidiness. Confusing a modern scholarly label with a contemporary self-designation can lead, almost imperceptibly, to overconfident claims about the polity's identity, continuity, and territorial extent, precisely the kind of overreach this chapter's broader methodology seeks to avoid.

Section 27.8

What Kind of State Can the Yūpas Actually Demonstrate?

Having examined the yūpa inscriptions' content in detail, this section asks directly what kind of political organization that evidence can responsibly support. The inscriptions demonstrate, with reasonable confidence, the existence of a ruling lineage spanning at least three generations; the adoption of Sanskritic naming conventions and an explicit vocabulary of dynastic foundation by at least the second of those generations; the presence of Brahmin ritual specialists at the ruler's court; and the capacity to mobilize very substantial resources for large-scale ceremonial gift-giving.

This is meaningful evidence of organized political authority exercising real resource mobilization and ritual patronage. It falls well short, however, of demonstrating several things that popular accounts sometimes attribute to "the Kingdom of Kutai." The inscriptions do not establish fixed territorial borders; they do not describe an administrative structure, a system of provincial governance, or a network of subordinate officials; they do not provide population figures; and they do not describe a capital city or urban center comparable to the kind of archaeologically documented settlement found, for example, at Oc Eo in the Mekong delta, examined in Chapter 20.

Historians therefore describe the Muara Kaman polity cautiously: as a riverine political center capable of concentrating substantial ritual and material resources under a dynastically legitimated ruler, embedded within regional exchange networks connecting Borneo's interior to the Makassar Strait and the wider archipelago. Whether this center controlled a broad surrounding territory through direct administration, exercised a looser form of tributary influence over a wider but more loosely connected network of communities, or represented a more modest, localized center of authority whose significance the inscriptions themselves may somewhat inflate for commemorative purposes, cannot be determined with confidence from the yūpas alone. This uncertainty is itself an important historical conclusion, not a gap to be filled with confident speculation.

Section 27.9

Western Java before Pūrṇavarman

The chapter's second case study shifts from Borneo to western Java, a region whose geography differs in important respects from the Mahakam basin while sharing the broader archipelagic pattern of riverine connection between interior and coastal zones. Western Java's principal river system, the Citarum, drains a landscape that includes the upland terrain around present-day Bogor and a broad coastal plain extending toward what is now Jakarta Bay, historically a zone of agricultural potential, seasonal flooding, and access to maritime trade routes passing through the Sunda Strait and along the Java Sea.

As in eastern Borneo, nothing in the archaeological or geographic evidence suggests that western Java's riverine communities were historically static or lacking in organized social and political life before the appearance of Sanskrit inscriptions. Agricultural communities had almost certainly developed in the Citarum basin over an extended period, exploiting the region's river systems and coastal plain for cultivation, fishing, and exchange, and the broader currents of South China Sea and Indian Ocean trade, examined in Chapter 18, would have brought this region, like much of the archipelago, into increasing contact with merchants, religious specialists, and commodities moving along maritime routes by the early centuries of the first millennium CE.

What distinguishes the western Javanese evidence from the Mahakam material, and what justifies devoting a substantial separate portion of this chapter to it, is both the somewhat different religious orientation displayed in the surviving inscriptions, centered on Viṣṇu rather than the Śaiva associations suggested by Vaprakeśvara, and the fact that the Tarumanagara inscriptions, unlike their Bornean counterparts, do supply the kingdom's own contemporary name.

Section 27.10

Pūrṇavarman and the Tarumanagara Inscriptions

A cluster of Sanskrit inscriptions discovered across western Java, conventionally dated by paleographic comparison to approximately the fifth century CE, name a ruler called Pūrṇavarman and associate him with a kingdom called Tārumanagara, combining a base name, Taruma, with the Sanskrit-derived term *nagara*, meaning town, city, or realm. This is a significant point of contrast with the Muara Kaman material examined earlier in this chapter: where the word "Kutai" appears nowhere in the yūpa inscriptions, the Ciaruteun inscription explicitly describes Pūrṇavarman, in the Sanskrit phrase tārūmanagarendrasya, as the ruler of Tārumanagara. Unlike "Kutai," then, Tārumanagara is directly attested in Pūrṇavarman's own epigraphic record, giving historians substantially firmer grounds for treating it as a contemporary political name rather than a purely retrospective scholarly label.

The conventional fifth-century dating applied throughout this chapter to the Tarumanagara corpus remains the most common placement in the established scholarly literature and rests on paleographic comparison of the kind discussed in Section 27.2. Students should be aware, however, that the current DHARMA critical edition has tentatively dated the Tugu inscription specifically to about the sixth century CE, a placement that applies to that inscription rather than necessarily to the entire Pūrṇavarman corpus, and that illustrates how paleographic dating of this period remains an active and unsettled area of scholarship rather than a fixed, uncontested conclusion.

The surviving inscriptions associated with Pūrṇavarman include several found at different locations across western Java, each commemorating a distinct aspect of his reign: inscriptions invoking the god Viṣṇu and associating the king with divine protection and legitimacy; inscriptions bearing carved footprint imagery, discussed in detail in the following section; and the Tugu inscription, which records a major waterway construction project and is examined in its own dedicated section below. Taken together, this is a notably richer and more varied epigraphic corpus than the Muara Kaman yūpas, allowing historians to reconstruct a somewhat fuller, though still partial, picture of Pūrṇavarman's self-presentation as a ruler.

Scholars examining the script used in the Tarumanagara inscriptions have noted similarities with contemporary South Indian scripts, particularly those associated with the Pallava dynasty of the South Indian mainland. This observation has sometimes been extended, in older or more popular accounts, into claims of direct Pallava political influence or even colonization in western Java. Current scholarship explicitly cautions against this extension: script similarity reflects participation in a broader zone of shared writing conventions and cultural exchange across the Sanskrit cosmopolis, a concept introduced in Chapter 19, rather than evidence of direct political control exercised by any South Asian state over Java.

Section 27.11

Footprints, Viṣṇu, and the Landscape of Kingship

Among the most distinctive and frequently discussed features of the Tarumanagara inscriptions is their use of carved footprint imagery embedded directly into the natural landscape, a striking departure from the Mahakam yūpas' free-standing inscribed posts. The Ciaruteun inscription, found along a riverbank in the Bogor area, pairs a Sanskrit verse praising Pūrṇavarman with the carved outline of a pair of human feet, explicitly associated in the accompanying text with the footprints of the king and, by extension, with the god Viṣṇu, whose footprints in South Asian religious tradition could themselves serve as objects of veneration and symbols of pervasive divine presence and protection.

A related inscription found at Kebon Kopi depicts a different pair of footprints, this time explicitly identified in the accompanying text as belonging to an elephant and associated with Airāvata, the elephant mount of the Hindu god Indra. Together, the Ciaruteun and Kebon Kopi footprint inscriptions demonstrate a deliberate strategy of embedding royal and divine authority directly into specific, named points in the physical landscape: river crossings, prominent rocks, and other locations likely to be encountered by travelers moving through the kingdom.

Visual Source Study

Pūrṇavarman's Footprints: Reading a Sacred Landscape

Students examining images or descriptions of the Ciaruteun and Kebon Kopi inscriptions should resist the literal interpretation that these carvings record an actual physical footprint left by a king or an elephant passing through the area. The footprints are carved symbolic representations, not forensic traces. Their purpose was to declare, at a specific and likely well-traveled point in the landscape, that this territory lay under the protection of a divinely associated ruler.

Consider the following questions as you examine the footprint imagery:

  1. Why might a ruler choose a river crossing or a well-traveled natural feature, rather than a constructed monument, as the site for this kind of inscription?
  2. What does the explicit association between the king's footprints and the god Viṣṇu's footprints suggest about how Pūrṇavarman wished his authority to be understood?
  3. Why does pairing a human footprint inscription (Ciaruteun) with an elephant footprint inscription associated with a different deity's mount (Kebon Kopi) suggest a deliberate, coordinated program of symbolic landscape-marking rather than an isolated gesture?
  4. What are the limits of using carved symbolic imagery like this to reconstruct the actual geographic extent of a ruler's effective political control?

This landscape-marking strategy illustrates localization in a particularly vivid form. The underlying religious concepts, Viṣṇu, Airāvata, and the symbolic power of divine footprints, derive from South Asian religious tradition. The specific practice of carving them into river-crossing stones across a Javanese political landscape, as a tool for asserting a king's protective and divinely sanctioned authority over specific, named places, reflects a local political strategy adapted to local geography and local concerns about travel, boundary-marking, and visible authority.

Section 27.12

The Tugu Inscription and Royal Waterworks

The Tugu inscription, found near Jakarta Bay, provides this chapter's central case study in the careful, critical reading of a royal inscription's technical and infrastructural claims. Conventionally dated, like the rest of the Tarumanagara corpus, to approximately the fifth century CE, though the current DHARMA critical edition has tentatively proposed a sixth-century date for this inscription specifically, the inscription commemorates two waterways, called the Candrabhaga and the Gomati, and states that a channel measuring 6,122 *dhanus* in length, a unit of measurement whose precise modern equivalent remains subject to scholarly estimation, was excavated during Pūrṇavarman's twenty-second regnal year, with the work reportedly completed within twenty-one days. The inscription further records a ritual gift of cattle to Brahmins in connection with the project's completion, echoing the pattern of ritual generosity already examined in the Muara Kaman material.

This is a remarkable claim, and it has understandably attracted considerable scholarly attention and no small amount of popular enthusiasm, since a successfully completed waterway of substantial length, finished within three weeks, would represent an impressive feat of labor mobilization and engineering coordination for any pre-modern society. Precisely because the claim is so striking, however, it demands the same careful, genre-aware reading this chapter has applied throughout to royal commemorative inscriptions.

Several considerations should guide a responsible historical reading of the Tugu inscription. First, the inscription is, like the yūpas, a commemorative royal statement rather than a construction log or an engineering survey; its purpose was to celebrate Pūrṇavarman's achievement and piety, not to provide a verifiable technical account for later historians. Second, scholars have debated both the precise length represented by the stated measurement and the degree to which the twenty-one-day figure should be read literally, as opposed to representing a conventionalized or rhetorically rounded figure intended to convey extraordinary royal efficiency and resource command. Third, and perhaps most importantly for historical interpretation, scholars continue to debate the actual physical nature and purpose of the Candrabhaga and Gomati waterways. Possible functions proposed in the scholarly literature include irrigation and agricultural improvement, drainage and flood control for a low-lying coastal plain prone to seasonal flooding, improved river navigation and transport, or some combination of these purposes, and the physical course and remains of the ancient waterways have not been definitively reconstructed through archaeology in a way that resolves this debate.

The responsible classroom conclusion is therefore not that "Pūrṇavarman built an irrigation canal," a simplified claim that forecloses genuine scholarly uncertainty, but rather that the Tugu inscription commemorates a major waterworks project, of a scale and speed that the inscription itself presents as extraordinary, whose precise engineering details, physical course, and specific function remain actively debated by specialists working with an inscription that was never intended to serve as a technical record in the first place. This waterworks claim, read in this careful way, nonetheless remains valuable evidence: it demonstrates that Pūrṇavarman's court could mobilize substantial labor and resources for large infrastructural projects and wished this capacity to be remembered as a central achievement of his reign.

Section 27.13

Batujaya and a Buddhist West Java

A chapter built primarily around Pūrṇavarman's Vaiṣṇava inscriptions risks leaving students with the mistaken impression that early western Java was religiously uniform, a single "Hindu kingdom" of Tarumanagara. The archaeological complex at Batujaya, located near the ancient delta of the Citarum River, corrects that impression directly and should be treated as an essential, not optional, part of this chapter's account.

Batujaya consists of numerous distinct archaeological localities spread across a substantial area, among which several contain the remains of brick structures identified as Buddhist stupas or related ritual monuments, probably constructed over a period extending across several centuries. The scale of construction at Batujaya, including the number of distinct monumental localities identified by archaeologists, indicates sustained Buddhist religious activity and patronage in the broader region associated with the Tarumanagara inscriptions, operating alongside, rather than simply replaced by, the Vaiṣṇava royal tradition attested in Pūrṇavarman's own inscriptions.

The precise institutional relationship between the Batujaya Buddhist complex and Pūrṇavarman's royal court remains genuinely uncertain. It is plausible, given the sites' geographic proximity within the broader Citarum delta region, that Batujaya existed within or near the political sphere associated with the Tarumanagara inscriptions. It would be an overreach, however, to assume that every structure at Batujaya was directly commissioned, funded, or administered by Pūrṇavarman's own royal government; the archaeological evidence does not permit historians to assign specific monuments confidently to a particular ruler's patronage in the absence of inscriptional confirmation at the site itself.

What Batujaya demonstrates with greater confidence is the broader historical point this section exists to make: early western Java supported multiple religious traditions, Vaiṣṇava Hindu royal ritual on one hand and substantial, sustained Buddhist monastic and devotional activity on the other, operating within overlapping or adjacent social and political worlds. This religious plurality should inform how students understand every subsequent chapter in this textbook that discusses religious change in the archipelago, including the later Sailendra and Sanjaya rivalries in Central Java examined in Chapters 30 through 32.

Section 27.14

Rivers, Agriculture, Exchange, and the Sea

Comparing the Mahakam River environment of eastern Borneo with the Citarum basin of western Java highlights both shared patterns and important differences in how river geography shaped the two polities examined in this chapter. Both regions depended on river systems that linked inland resources and agricultural or forest production to coastal access and, through the sea, to wider regional and long-distance exchange networks. In both cases, a political center positioned along a significant river stood to benefit from control over the movement of goods, people, and resources passing along that corridor.

The two environments also differed in important respects. Western Java's Citarum basin, with its substantial coastal plain and documented later agricultural development, together with the major waterworks commemorated in the Tugu inscription, suggests an environment where water management, whether for irrigation, flood control, or navigation, played a significant role in the political economy of Pūrṇavarman's kingdom. The Mahakam basin's yūpa inscriptions, by contrast, emphasize ritual gift-giving and dynastic genealogy rather than infrastructural achievement, which may reflect genuine differences in the two societies' economic foundations, or may simply reflect differences in what each set of surviving inscriptions happened to choose for commemoration. Historians cannot be certain which explanation, or what combination of the two, best accounts for this difference in emphasis.

This comparison should reinforce, rather than undermine, a caution already raised in Chapters 2 and 3 regarding the physical geography of Southeast Asia as a whole: river valleys, deltas, and coastal plains created opportunities and constraints for human societies, but they did not mechanically determine particular political outcomes. The specific forms of kingship, ritual, and infrastructural achievement attested in the Mahakam and Tarumanagara inscriptions resulted from historical actors making choices within their respective environments, not from geography alone dictating those choices.

Section 27.15

Sanskrit, Scripts, and Localization

Chapter 19 introduced the historiographical shift away from older "Indianization" models toward a framework of selective adoption and localization, and this chapter's two case studies offer some of the clearest and earliest illustrations of that framework available anywhere in this textbook's treatment of the archipelago.

In both Borneo and Java, Sanskrit functioned not as a spoken vernacular replacing local languages, but as a prestige language of ritual, political declaration, and religious learning, selected by rulers for specific commemorative and legitimating purposes. Indic-derived scripts were likewise adopted as practical tools for rendering this prestige language in durable, legible form, rather than representing the wholesale importation of a foreign literate culture displacing local practice. Rulers in both regions adopted Sanskritic names, declared association with South Asian deities, Śiva in connection with the Mahakam material's Vaprakeśvara and Viṣṇu and Indra in the Tarumanagara material, and employed Brahmanical ritual specialists for ceremonies central to the inscriptions' content. None of this constitutes evidence of direct rule by a South Asian state, a mass migration of South Asian populations sufficient to transform the ethnic composition of either region, or a uniform, centrally directed process of religious conversion. What the evidence does show is two historically distinct local courts, operating in different parts of the archipelago, independently selecting broadly similar elements from a shared Sanskrit cosmopolis, examined in Chapter 19, and then deploying those elements in locally specific ways suited to each court's own political circumstances and physical landscape.

The contrast between the two case studies illustrates this local specificity clearly. The Mahakam material emphasizes genealogy, Śaiva ritual association, and a single spectacular act of gift-giving commemorated on free-standing stone posts. The Tarumanagara material emphasizes Vaiṣṇava divine association expressed through footprint imagery embedded directly in the landscape, together with a major infrastructural achievement commemorated in connection with ritual generosity. Both courts drew on the same broad cosmopolitan repertoire of Sanskritic kingship; each court applied that repertoire according to its own distinct local priorities, geography, and political needs. This is localization in concrete, comparative practice, not merely as an abstract historiographical claim.

Section 27.16

Were Kutai and Tarumanagara Territorial Kingdoms?

This section addresses directly a question raised implicitly throughout the chapter: how confidently can historians describe the Muara Kaman polity and Tarumanagara as "kingdoms" in any sense resembling a modern, or even a later pre-modern, territorially bounded state?

Historical Debate

How Large Were These Early States?

The question: Given that the surviving evidence for both the Muara Kaman polity and Tarumanagara consists chiefly of a modest number of royal inscriptions, together with limited corroborating archaeology, how confidently can historians describe either as a substantial territorial kingdom, as opposed to a more modest riverine center exercising looser or more localized influence?

Interpretation A — Substantial territorial kingdoms: Some historians argue that the scale of resources commemorated in the inscriptions, Mūlavarman's massive cattle gift and Pūrṇavarman's major waterworks project, implies a political center capable of mobilizing labor and tribute across a reasonably extensive and organized territory, since projects and gifts of this described scale would be difficult to sustain from a narrowly localized population alone.
Interpretation B — River-centered networks with tributary reach: Other historians argue that both polities are better understood as river-centered centers of authority exercising influence over a looser, more fluctuating network of allied or tributary communities along the Mahakam and Citarum basins respectively, resembling the overlapping, non-territorially-bounded mandala pattern discussed in Chapter 1 and examined further in later chapters on Angkor and other classical kingdoms, rather than a uniformly administered territorial state.
Interpretation C — Insufficient evidence to reconstruct political extent confidently: A third position holds that the surviving inscriptions, precisely because they are commemorative royal statements rather than administrative records, simply do not contain the kind of information, population figures, boundary descriptions, provincial administrative structures, needed to responsibly estimate either polity's territorial extent, and that historians should resist the temptation to fill this evidentiary gap with plausible-sounding but ultimately speculative reconstruction.

Evidence: All three interpretations draw on the same body of evidence, the yūpa inscriptions, the Tarumanagara inscriptional corpus, and the limited corroborating archaeology at Batujaya, but differ in how much inferential weight they are willing to place on indirect indicators of scale, such as the size of a ritual gift or the ambition of an infrastructural project.

Why it matters: How historians answer this question shapes broader narratives about the pace and scale of early state formation in the archipelago, and about how directly these fifth-century courts should be connected to the much larger, better-documented maritime and agrarian powers examined later in this volume, including Srivijaya and the Central Javanese courts of Chapters 28 through 32.

Student task: Using the specific evidence presented in this chapter, evaluate which interpretation you find most persuasive, or propose a position that draws selectively on more than one interpretation, explaining what additional evidence would most help resolve the remaining uncertainty.

This textbook does not resolve the debate presented above in favor of any single interpretation. Instead, it treats the debate itself as instructive: it illustrates, in a concrete case, the general caution this chapter has urged throughout regarding the limits of royal commemorative inscriptions as evidence for territorial extent, administrative structure, and population.

Section 27.17

The People the Inscriptions Barely Mention

Every inscription examined in this chapter centers a king or an elite commemorative act, celebrating a ruler's genealogy, piety, generosity, or infrastructural achievement, whether that ruler personally sponsored the inscription or, as with the Mahakam yūpas, was honored by Brahmins acting on his behalf. This concentration of attention is itself a significant historical fact, not a neutral feature of the evidence, and this section asks students to consider directly who and what the inscriptions leave out.

Mūlavarman's gift of cattle, whatever its precise scale, did not appear from nowhere; it depended on herders, land, fodder, and the accumulated resources of a wider agricultural and pastoral economy that the inscriptions never describe. The Brahmins who received that gift and performed the associated rituals were themselves supported by that same underlying economy, and their presence at the Mahakam court implies networks of religious training, travel, and patronage about which the yūpas say nothing directly. Pūrṇavarman's waterworks, whatever their precise engineering character, required substantial organized labor, diggers, haulers, and overseers, whose identities, status, whether free, dependent, or coerced, and working conditions remain entirely unrecorded in the Tugu inscription's celebratory language.

Everyday Life and Social History

Behind the Inscriptions: Labor, Resources, and Invisible Populations

Consider the kinds of people whose work and resources made the events commemorated in this chapter's inscriptions possible, even though none of them appear by name or description in the surviving texts: farmers and herders along the Mahakam and Citarum basins who produced the agricultural and pastoral surplus underlying royal wealth; boat crews and porters who moved goods and resources along the river systems connecting interior and coastal zones; laborers who dug and maintained the Candrabhaga and Gomati waterways, whatever their precise purpose; artisans and stoneworkers who carved the yūpas and the footprint inscriptions themselves; and local religious specialists, possibly practicing traditions that long predated or continued alongside the Brahmanical and Buddhist traditions documented in the inscriptions and at Batujaya.

None of these groups left behind inscriptions of their own. Historians can infer their presence and their essential contribution to the events the inscriptions celebrate, but cannot reconstruct their individual experiences, social status, or daily lives from the epigraphic evidence alone. This is epigraphic bias in concrete practice: royal monuments magnify the deeds and piety of kings because their commemorative purposes center royal and elite concerns, not the experiences of farmers or laborers, and this structural bias should inform how students read every inscriptional source introduced throughout this textbook.

Recognizing this bias does not mean abandoning epigraphic evidence as historically useless; the yūpas and the Tarumanagara inscriptions remain among the most important sources available for understanding early Borneo and Java. It does mean reading such evidence with a constant, disciplined awareness of whose voice is speaking, whose labor and resources are assumed but unacknowledged, and whose historical experience the surviving record, through no fault of the modern historian, simply cannot recover.

Section 27.18

From the Earliest Inscriptional Courts to a New Maritime Age

This chapter closes Part V of this textbook, which has surveyed the earliest documented states across mainland and maritime Southeast Asia: Funan and Chenla in the Mekong world, Champa along the Vietnamese coast, the Red River delta under Chinese imperial administration, the Pyu city-states of the Irrawaddy basin, Dvaravati and the Mon world of the Chao Phraya basin, the early states of the Malay Peninsula, and now the Muara Kaman polity and Tarumanagara in Borneo and Java. Across this entire survey, a consistent pattern has emerged: locally rooted societies, already engaged in regional and long-distance exchange, selectively adopted elements of Sanskritic or, in the Red River delta's case, Sinitic political and religious culture, and transformed those elements to serve distinctly local political purposes.

It would be a mistake to read this chapter's conclusion as establishing a direct, continuous developmental line running from Mūlavarman and Pūrṇavarman forward to the much larger maritime and agrarian powers examined in the chapters immediately following. Chapter 28 turns to the emergence of Srivijaya around southeastern Sumatra, a polity operating on a substantially different scale, with a far more extensive and better-documented network of inscriptions, foreign textual references, and control over strategic waterways including the Strait of Malacca. Chapters 30 through 32 examine the Sailendra dynasty and the broader political and religious transformations of Central Java, including Borobudur and Prambanan, societies whose scale of monumental construction and inscriptional documentation again exceeds what this chapter's evidence can support for either the Mahakam polity or Tarumanagara.

What this chapter's evidence does support is a more modest but historically significant claim: by the fourth, fifth, and following centuries CE, the archipelagic world already contained locally rooted political centers capable of using writing, Sanskritic political and religious vocabulary, large-scale ritual patronage, substantial labor mobilization for infrastructural projects, and connections to long-distance exchange networks. These capacities, developed independently and in locally specific forms in Borneo and Java, represent part of the broader foundation upon which the larger, more extensively documented powers of the following chapters would later build, without implying that any single, continuous political lineage connects the fourth-century Mahakam court, the fifth-century Tarumanagara court, and the seventh-century rise of Srivijaya examined next.

The Yūpa Inscriptions of Mūlavarman, Conventionally 4th–5th Century CE

Creator: Erected by Brahmins in commemoration of Mūlavarman, ruler of the Mahakam River polity, per the inscriptions' own statements · Date: approximate, based on paleographic comparison, examined further in Section 27.2 · Type: Sanskrit verse inscriptions carved on stone sacrificial posts (yūpas)

Context: Seven stone yūpas discovered near Muara Kaman on the Mahakam River record, across two related subjects, a three-generation royal genealogy and a commemorated act of ritual generosity. They are among the oldest securely datable inscriptions so far found in the Indonesian archipelago.

Paraphrase / Synthesis (not a verbatim excerpt):

The genealogical inscription identifies Kundungga as the earliest named ancestor, names his son Aśvavarman as the founder of the royal lineage, and names Aśvavarman's son Mūlavarman as a powerful and virtuous king. A separate inscription commemorates a great sacrificial ceremony performed by Mūlavarman at a place called Vaprakeśvara, during which he is said to have given a very large gift of cattle to Brahmin priests, an act the inscription presents as an expression of extraordinary royal generosity and piety.

Analysis: Read together, the genealogical and donative inscriptions allow historians to trace both a claimed shift in dynastic legitimation across three generations and a specific, richly commemorated act of royal religious patronage. The inscriptions demonstrate the presence of Brahmanical ritual specialists and Sanskritic literacy at a Bornean court by the period in question.

Limitations: The inscriptions provide no information about territorial extent, administrative structure, population, or the economic mechanisms underlying the described wealth. The specific numerical claim regarding the cattle gift cannot be independently verified and should be read as a rhetorical expression of generosity rather than an audited figure.

Perspective: The inscriptions represent the perspective of the royal court and its Brahmanical ritual specialists exclusively; no complementary local, non-elite, or foreign external account of the same events survives.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the genealogical inscription explicitly state, and what does it leave unstated about Kundungga's own religious or political identity?
  2. Why should the twenty-thousand-cow figure be treated as a commemorative claim rather than a verified economic statistic?
  3. What does the presence of Brahmin priests at Vaprakeśvara suggest about religious specialization at Mūlavarman's court?
  4. What kinds of information would historians need, beyond what these inscriptions provide, to confidently reconstruct the Mahakam polity's territorial extent or population?
Multiple Perspectives

Experiencing Sanskritic Kingship in Borneo and Java

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across the surviving evidence. They are not quotations from any named historical individual.

From the perspective of a Brahmin ritual specialist at Mūlavarman's court: Performing the rites at Vaprakeśvara and receiving the king's generous gift affirms both the king's piety and the specialist's own essential religious role, a role that depends on royal patronage and that, in turn, lends religious legitimacy to the ruler's authority.
From the perspective of a herder or cultivator supplying resources along the Mahakam: Whatever the inscriptions record of royal generosity, the cattle, labor, and surplus that made such generosity possible were drawn from the ongoing work of raising livestock, farming, and gathering resources within the wider riverine community, work that no inscription credits by name.
From the perspective of a laborer mobilized for Pūrṇavarman's waterway project: Excavating a major channel, whatever its precise purpose and timeline, required sustained, organized, and likely difficult physical labor performed by many hands, whose working conditions, status, and compensation the Tugu inscription's celebratory language does not describe.
From the perspective of a Buddhist devotee near Batujaya: Religious life in this part of Java encompassed devotional and monastic practice connected to Buddhist tradition, carried on alongside, rather than erased by, the Vaiṣṇava royal ritual commemorated in the king's own inscriptions elsewhere in the region.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Kingship in Borneo and Java across the Inscriptional Transition

What Changed

  • Rulers in both regions adopted Sanskritic names, Indic-derived script, and a vocabulary of divine association that had not previously been recorded in local evidence
  • Permanent, legible, commemorative inscriptions began to supplement, though not replace, whatever oral or less durable forms of commemoration may have existed previously
  • Brahmanical ritual specialists and Buddhist religious communities became visible participants in the religious life of both regions' elite and, at Batujaya, broader devotional contexts

What Stayed the Same

  • Riverine geography continued to structure exchange, resource mobilization, and the practical foundations of political authority in both regions
  • Local agricultural and pastoral economies remained the underlying material basis for royal wealth and ritual patronage, even as the vocabulary used to celebrate that wealth changed
  • Political authority in both regions continued to depend on the labor, resources, and cooperation of populations who remain largely invisible in the surviving elite-centered evidence

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents treating the appearance of Sanskrit inscriptions as either a sudden civilizational rupture imposed from outside or a purely cosmetic change with no real historical significance.

Cause and Effect

What Conditions Favored Early Inscriptional Kingship?

Long-term causes

Sustained participation in regional and long-distance exchange networks, examined in Chapters 17 and 18, brought riverine communities in Borneo and Java into increasing contact with merchants, religious specialists, and ideas circulating across the Sanskrit cosmopolis over an extended period.

Short-term causes

Specific rulers, such as Aśvavarman and Mūlavarman in Borneo and Pūrṇavarman in Java, made deliberate choices to adopt Sanskritic naming, ritual, and commemorative practices as tools for asserting or reinforcing their own authority within existing local political rivalries and circumstances that remain only partially visible in the evidence.

Effect

Permanent, Sanskrit-language inscriptions, employing South Asian-derived ritual vocabulary, divine association, and commemorative genres, began to appear in the historical record of Borneo and Java by the fourth and fifth centuries CE, providing historians with their earliest reasonably secure textual evidence for named rulers in either region.

This chapter's response

Read each inscription as a deliberate, locally produced political and religious statement, distinguish its explicit claims from later scholarly convention and popular elaboration, and remain attentive throughout to the people and processes the inscriptions do not describe.

Historical complexity: No single cause fully explains why Sanskritic kingship took the specific forms it did in Borneo and Java; the pattern reflects an interaction between long-term structural conditions of exchange and contact and short-term, contingent political choices made by specific rulers whose individual motivations remain largely unrecoverable.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the earliest documented instances of Sanskritic kingship in Borneo and Java through two carefully bounded case studies: the yūpa inscriptions commemorating Mūlavarman, found near Muara Kaman on the Mahakam River in eastern Borneo, and the cluster of inscriptions associated with Pūrṇavarman of Tarumanagara in western Java. Both bodies of evidence demonstrate local rulers using Sanskrit language, Indic-derived script, and Brahmanical or Vaiṣṇava ritual practice to express and reinforce authority, though the two corpora emphasize different elements of that shared repertoire: the Mahakam yūpas center on dynastic genealogy, sacrifice, and ritual gift-giving, while Pūrṇavarman's inscriptions center on divine comparison, landscape-embedded footprints, and royal waterworks. In both cases this authority was expressed within societies that were themselves locally rooted, riverine, and already embedded in regional and long-distance exchange networks well before any inscription was carved.

The chapter has emphasized throughout that these societies did not begin their political history with the arrival of Sanskrit; organized riverine communities existed in both regions long before the fourth and fifth centuries CE, and what changed with the yūpa and Tarumanagara inscriptions was a specific innovation in commemorative and legitimating practice, not the sudden invention of political order. The three-generation genealogy recorded in the Mahakam material, moving from the non-Sanskritic Kundungga through the Sanskritic Aśvavarman, explicitly named as founder of the lineage, to Mūlavarman, provides meaningful but carefully bounded evidence of cultural transformation, evidence this chapter has deliberately refused to extend into unsupported claims about religious conversion or ethnic change.

A central methodological theme running through the chapter concerns the appropriate use of conventional scholarly labels. The word "Kutai," though convenient and widely used in survey literature, does not appear in the fourth- or fifth-century yūpa inscriptions themselves, and this chapter has insisted on distinguishing that modern convention from the contemporary epigraphic record. Tarumanagara presents a markedly different and firmer evidentiary situation: the Ciaruteun inscription explicitly names Pūrṇavarman as ruler of Tārumanagara, so historians have direct contemporary epigraphic warrant for this name in a way they simply do not for "Kutai."

The chapter examined in detail the specific content of both inscriptional corpora: Mūlavarman's commemorated ritual gift of cattle at Vaprakeśvara, a figure this chapter treated as a rhetorical claim of royal generosity rather than a verifiable economic statistic; Pūrṇavarman's footprint inscriptions at Ciaruteun and Kebon Kopi, embedding royal and divine authority directly into the physical landscape at river crossings and other prominent natural features; and the Tugu inscription's account of waterway construction, examined as this chapter's central case study in reading a royal infrastructural claim critically rather than as literal engineering fact, given ongoing scholarly debate over both the waterways' precise physical nature and their intended function.

The chapter also introduced religious plurality as an essential corrective to any simplified picture of early western Java as a uniformly "Hindu kingdom." The substantial Buddhist archaeological remains at Batujaya, near the ancient Citarum delta, demonstrate that Buddhist devotional and monastic activity operated within or near the broader world associated with the Tarumanagara inscriptions, even as the precise institutional relationship between Batujaya and Pūrṇavarman's own court remains uncertain.

Methodologically, the chapter has applied and extended the localization framework introduced in Chapter 19, showing concretely how two historically distinct courts in Borneo and Java independently selected elements from a shared Sanskrit cosmopolis, Sanskrit language and script, Brahmanical or Vaiṣṇava ritual, divine association, and dynastic genealogy, and then deployed those elements according to each court's own distinct local priorities, geography, and political circumstances. Script similarities between the Tarumanagara inscriptions and contemporary South Indian scripts were explicitly addressed and explicitly distinguished from any claim of direct South Asian political control over Java.

The Historical Debate feature addressed directly the question of how large and how centralized these two polities actually were, presenting three plausible interpretations, substantial territorial kingdom, river-centered tributary network, and insufficient evidence for confident reconstruction, without resolving the debate in favor of any single position, since the surviving evidence genuinely does not permit confident resolution.

Finally, the chapter has insisted throughout on recovering, as far as the evidence allows, the presence of populations the inscriptions themselves do not name: the herders, farmers, boat crews, laborers, artisans, and local religious specialists whose resources and labor made the events commemorated in stone possible, even though their individual identities and experiences remain almost entirely absent from the epigraphic record. This methodological lesson, that royal inscriptions reveal rulers and obscure almost everyone else, applies well beyond this chapter and should inform how students approach epigraphic evidence throughout the remainder of this textbook.

Together, the Mahakam and Tarumanagara case studies close Part V of this textbook by demonstrating that, by the fourth and fifth centuries CE, the archipelagic world already contained locally rooted political centers capable of literate commemoration, substantial ritual patronage, and significant infrastructural ambition, developments that, while historically significant in their own right, should not be read as directly or inevitably leading to the larger maritime and agrarian powers examined in the chapters that follow.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Yūpa
A sacrificial post in South Asian Vedic ritual tradition; in Borneo, a stone post inscribed with Sanskrit verses commemorating Mūlavarman's genealogy and ritual gift-giving.
Epigraphy
The scholarly discipline concerned with reading, translating, and interpreting inscriptions carved or engraved on durable materials.
Paleography
The study of the historical development of script and handwriting styles, used to estimate the approximate date of an undated inscription through comparison with inscriptions of better-established date.
Vaprakeśvara
The site named in the Mahakam donative inscription where Mūlavarman's commemorated ritual gift of cattle to Brahmins took place, apparently associated with the worship of Śiva.
Dāna
A Sanskrit term referring to religiously meritorious giving, central to the ritual generosity commemorated in the Muara Kaman inscriptions.
Vaṃśakartā
A Sanskrit term meaning "founder of the lineage," applied in the yūpa inscriptions to Aśvavarman.
Brahmin
A South Asian-derived religious specialist trained in Brahmanical ritual tradition; Brahmins performed ceremonies and received substantial gifts at Mūlavarman's court.
Sanskrit cosmopolis
A concept, introduced in Chapter 19, describing the broad zone of shared Sanskritic language, script, religious vocabulary, and political ideas that connected South and Southeast Asian courts without implying direct political unification.
Localization
The process by which Southeast Asian societies selected and transformed Sanskritic and other outside elements to serve locally specific religious and political purposes, central to this chapter's interpretation of both case studies.
Taruma / Tārumanagara
The kingdom whose ruler, Pūrṇavarman, is directly named in the Ciaruteun inscription as "of Tārumanagara" (tārūmanagarendrasya), combining a base name, Taruma, with the Sanskrit-derived term for realm or city; unlike "Kutai," this name is directly attested in the contemporary epigraphic record.
Vaiṣṇava
Relating to the worship of the Hindu god Viṣṇu, the religious orientation most closely associated with Pūrṇavarman's inscriptions.
Airāvata
The elephant mount of the Hindu god Indra, invoked in the Kebon Kopi inscription's elephant footprint imagery.
Dhanus
A unit of measurement used in the Tugu inscription to record the length of the excavated waterway; its precise modern equivalent remains subject to scholarly estimation.
Regnal year
A year numbered according to the length of a particular ruler's reign, the dating system used in the Tugu inscription to situate the waterworks project within Pūrṇavarman's reign.
Conventional label
A modern scholarly name applied to a historical polity or period for convenience, as with "Kutai," which does not appear in the Muara Kaman inscriptions themselves.
Findspot
The location where an inscription or artifact was discovered, often used, as with "Kutai," as the basis for a conventional scholarly label.
Royal eulogy (praśasti)
A formal, often formulaic genre of inscription commissioned by or for a ruler to commemorate ritual, military, or dynastic achievements, generally not intended as a neutral administrative record.
Genealogy
A recorded sequence of ancestors and descendants; central to the Mahakam yūpa inscriptions' account of Kundungga, Aśvavarman, and Mūlavarman.
Riverine polity
A political center whose authority and economic foundation were organized substantially around control of, or access to, a river system connecting interior and coastal zones.
Tributary network
A looser form of political relationship in which subordinate or allied communities provide resources or recognition to a more powerful center without necessarily being directly administered by it.
Material culture
Physical objects and remains, such as the yūpas themselves or the brick structures at Batujaya, that provide evidence of past human activity.
Epigraphic bias
The tendency of inscriptional evidence to overrepresent the perspectives, achievements, and concerns of the rulers and elites who commissioned it, while underrepresenting or omitting the experiences of ordinary populations.
Indic-derived script
A writing system developed from, or closely related to, scripts used in South Asia, adopted and adapted for use in Sanskrit inscriptions across maritime Southeast Asia.
Batujaya
An archaeological complex near the ancient Citarum delta containing substantial early Buddhist monuments, demonstrating religious plurality in early western Java.
Kota Kapur inscription
A later inscription associated with Srivijaya, examined in Chapter 28, recording a military expedition against "bhūmi Jāwa," whose connection to Tarumanagara specifically remains an unproven scholarly possibility.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to do something more demanding than simply learning what happened in early Borneo and Java: it has asked students to practice reading a narrow, genre-bound body of evidence with the same discipline and caution that professional historians bring to any inscriptional source. The Muara Kaman yūpas and the Tarumanagara inscriptions are genuinely remarkable documents, among the oldest of their kind anywhere in the Indonesian archipelago, and they reward careful attention. But their very rarity and antiquity make them easy to overinterpret, and this chapter has tried, at every turn, to show students where the evidence ends and where convenient modern narrative begins.

The larger pattern this chapter reveals, local rulers selectively adopting a cosmopolitan vocabulary of Sanskritic kingship and adapting it to distinctly local political and religious purposes, connects directly to the historiographical argument developed in Chapter 19 and will recur throughout this textbook's treatment of maritime Southeast Asia, from Srivijaya in Chapter 28 through the Sailendra and Mataram courts of Central Java in Chapters 30 through 32, and onward into the later classical kingdoms of Volume III. Students who have learned, in this chapter, to distinguish a conventional scholarly label from a contemporary self-designation, to treat a royal eulogy's numerical claims as rhetorical rather than statistical, and to ask persistently who an inscription does not mention, carry forward an interpretive discipline that will serve them well throughout the remainder of this textbook's engagement with inscriptional evidence.

This chapter also closes Part V of the textbook, completing a survey that began with Funan in the Mekong delta and has moved, chapter by chapter, across the Red River delta, the Pyu city-states, the Mon world of Dvaravati, the early states of the Malay Peninsula, and now Borneo and Java. Across all of these chapters, the same central lesson has recurred in different regional forms: Southeast Asian societies did not passively receive outside civilization; they actively selected, adapted, and transformed what reached them, producing political and religious forms that were simultaneously connected to wider Asian networks and distinctly their own.

As the textbook turns, in Chapter 28, to the much larger and better-documented maritime world of Srivijaya, students should carry forward both the substantive evidence examined here, the Mahakam genealogy, the Tarumanagara footprints and waterworks, and Batujaya's Buddhist remains, and the methodological lessons this chapter has modeled: read inscriptions as deliberate political statements, distinguish evidence from inference, and never mistake the loudest voice in the historical record, the voice of the king, for the only voice that mattered.
