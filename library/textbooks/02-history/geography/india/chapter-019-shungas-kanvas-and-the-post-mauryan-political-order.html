Part V — Early Historic South Asia and the Connected Ancient World
19

Shungas, Kanvas, and the Post-Mauryan Political Order

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

When a great empire ends, the ordinary business of a society does not stop. Fields are still plowed, caravans still leave for the coast, monks still gather at their monasteries, and donors still pay for stone. The chapter that follows the Mauryan Empire is therefore not a story of darkness after brightness. It is the story of what a very large and very varied region looked like when no single court in Pataliputra could plausibly claim to speak for most of it.

Among the objects that survive from the century or two after the Mauryan collapse is an inscription from Ayodhya, in the central Gangetic plain, in which a damaged inscription conventionally attributed to a ruler called Dhanadeva contains wording often interpreted as making him the sixth descendant of a general called Pushyamitra, and credits Pushyamitra with two horse sacrifices. A few hundred kilometers to the southwest, at Besnagar near Vidisha, a stone column records that a Greek ambassador from a northwestern kingdom, a man who described himself as a devotee of a god called Vasudeva, set up the pillar in honor of that deity. In Odisha, a long Prakrit inscription cut into a cave-hill near Bhubaneswar narrates, year by year, the campaigns and public works of a king of Kalinga. And at Bharhut and Sanchi, in central India, donors of many kinds, kings, monks, nuns, guildsmen, and ordinary householders, paid for railings and gateways carved with scenes from the life of the Buddha and his earlier births. None of these objects belongs to the same political world as the great edicts of Ashoka. Together they show a subcontinent in which power, religion, wealth, and artistic energy were distributed among many centers.

This chapter examines that transformation. Chapter 18 explained why Mauryan authority fragmented: the problems of succession, the weakening of provincial control, the fiscal and military limits of the empire, and the overthrow of the last Mauryan king, Brihadratha, by his commander Pushyamitra. It also assessed the empire's legacies. The present chapter deliberately begins after that analytical work is done. Its subject is not the fall of the Mauryas but the political order that took shape in the Gangetic heartland and its neighboring regions once the fall had occurred.

The chapter's central argument is that the end of Mauryan supremacy did not create a vacuum or a civilizational collapse. It redistributed political power among smaller dynasties, regional rulers, military elites, religious institutions, cities, and commercial networks. The Shungas were important successors in the Gangetic heartland, and the Kanvas who replaced them were the heirs of that heartland's declining horizons, but neither dynasty simply inherited the Mauryan Empire. They were only two components of a wider, more plural, and more competitive post-Mauryan political landscape.

That argument requires careful use of evidence. The Shungas and Kanvas are known from a strikingly uneven documentary record: dynastic lists in the Puranas, composed centuries later in the form of prophecy; literary works written long after the events they describe; a handful of inscriptions; coins whose attribution is often disputed; and, above all, the physical remains of monasteries, stupas, and cities. Some of the most vivid stories associated with the period, especially those concerning Pushyamitra's relationship to Buddhism, come from texts composed much later and must be weighed against the archaeological record, not simply repeated. This chapter therefore treats evidence as part of the historical subject rather than as a background formality.

The chapter also resists two familiar simplifications. The first treats the post-Mauryan period as a dark interval between two great empires, the Mauryan and the Gupta. The second treats the Shungas as a Brahmanical reaction that reversed Ashoka's Buddhist policy. The evidence supports neither view. This was a period of thriving cities, expanding trade, new coinage, monumental religious construction, and competing patronage networks, and its religious life was plural, not polarized.

The chapter closes the Gangetic part of Part V's opening movement and prepares for what follows. Chapter 20 turns to the Indo-Greeks, Shakas, and Indo-Parthians of the northwest; Chapter 21 to the Kushans; Chapter 22 to the Satavahanas of the Deccan; Chapter 23 to Tamilakam; and Chapter 24 to Sri Lanka. This chapter supplies the pattern into which those chapters fit: an early historic South Asia of many political centers, connected more by trade, religion, art, and language than by any single imperial government.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain what historians can and cannot establish about Pushyamitra Shunga, and distinguish contemporary from later evidence for his reign.
  2. Describe the likely extent and character of the Shunga state, and explain why maps drawing a Mauryan-sized Shunga empire are misleading.
  3. Analyze post-Mauryan political fragmentation as the emergence of multiple political centers rather than simple disorder.
  4. Evaluate the role of Vedic royal ritual, especially the ashvamedha, in the legitimation of post-Mauryan rulers.
  5. Assess the evidence for the claim that Pushyamitra persecuted Buddhists, distinguishing later literary tradition from archaeological and epigraphic evidence.
  6. Interpret the monumental art of Bharhut and Sanchi as evidence of royal and nonroyal patronage under politically fragmented conditions.
  7. Explain why the poorly documented Kanva dynasty illustrates the shrinking of Magadha's political horizon.
  8. Identify major regional powers of the period, including Kalinga under Kharavela, and explain what they show about regionalization.
  9. Compare the Mauryan imperial order with the multipolar order that followed.
  10. Distinguish among dynasty, period, workshop, and donor in attributing post-Mauryan monuments.
  11. Contextualize the Gangetic post-Mauryan world within the wider Eurasian connections examined in Chapters 20 through 25.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. How much can historians reconstruct about the Shungas, given that most of the narrative about them was written centuries after the fact?

  4. How did new dynasties use ritual, patronage, and monument-building to establish legitimacy?

  6. How could artistic and commercial life flourish under conditions of political fragmentation?
  7. What does the obscurity of the Kanvas reveal about the limits of political chronology for this period?

  9. In what sense was the post-Mauryan order multipolar, and what connected its many centers?

Historical Context

The Mauryan Empire, examined in Chapters 15 through 18, was the first state to bring most of the subcontinent under a single government. It was built on the resources of the Gangetic plain and the political achievement of Magadha (Chapter 10), and it reached its greatest extent under Ashoka (Chapter 16). Chapter 17 showed that its administration rested on agrarian revenue, an army, and a network of officials and cities, and that its reach into forest and frontier regions was always uneven. Chapter 18 traced how, after Ashoka, succession problems, provincial autonomy, and fiscal and military strain weakened the center, and how Brihadratha was overthrown by Pushyamitra Shunga. Students should carry forward from that chapter one crucial distinction: the decline of a single imperial sovereignty is not the same thing as the decline of agriculture, cities, trade, religion, or local institutions.

Many of the structures that made post-Mauryan life possible were older than the empire and outlasted it. The second urbanization (Chapter 9) had created towns such as Kaushambi, Ujjain, Mathura, Vidisha, and Pataliputra itself. Coinage, first in the form of punch-marked silver, was circulating widely by the Mauryan period and continued to develop. Buddhist, Jain, and Brahmanical communities (Chapters 11 through 13) had established institutions that did not depend on Mauryan patronage. Merchant networks reached the northwest and the coasts. Such structures gave regional rulers something to govern and gave donors something to build with.

The geographic setting matters. The Gangetic plain remained the most productive agricultural region of the subcontinent, but the routes that carried goods and ideas ran in several directions: westward through Mathura and Ujjain to Gujarat and the Arabian Sea, southward through Vidisha and the Vindhyan passes toward the Deccan, eastward toward the Bay of Bengal, and northwestward toward Taxila and Central Asia. Rulers who controlled points along these routes could prosper without controlling the whole plain. The monsoon-dependent agricultural calendar described in Chapter 1 also continued to structure political life, since armies moved and revenue was collected according to its seasons.

Finally, students should understand the evidentiary situation, which shapes everything that follows. For the Mauryas, the Ashokan edicts supplied contemporary imperial voice. For the post-Mauryan Gangetic heartland there is no equivalent. Historians reconstruct the period by combining Puranic king lists compiled much later, Buddhist and Sanskrit literary works of various dates, inscriptions from a few sites, coins issued by many small authorities, and the material remains of cities and monuments. The result is a picture that is richer for religion, art, and regional life than for dynastic narrative, and readers should expect uncertainty in the latter.

Section 19.1

Pushyamitra Shunga

Chapter 18 examined the overthrow of Brihadratha as the endpoint of Mauryan history. This section begins after that event and asks a narrower question: what can actually be established about Pushyamitra Shunga as a ruler? The answer is less than the volume of later storytelling might suggest, and the reasons for that are instructive.

The principal source for Pushyamitra's rise is the Puranic tradition. In their dynastic lists, several Puranas describe him as the senapati, or army commander, of the last Mauryan king, who seized the throne and founded a line usually named the Shungas. The lists give him a reign of about thirty-six years and assign the dynasty a total duration of roughly a century or slightly more, with differences among the manuscripts. Modern historians usually place the beginning of Shunga rule in the 180s BCE and its end in the 70s BCE, while treating both dates as approximate. The Puranas, however, were compiled centuries after these events (see the Vocabulary feature below), and their dynastic lists were adjusted and abbreviated over time.

Historical Vocabulary
Puranic Dynastic Lists

The Puranas are a large body of Sanskrit texts concerned with cosmology, genealogy, religious practice, and sacred places (examined further in Chapter 28). Several of them contain sections that list the kings of the Kali age, the current era in Puranic reckoning, in the grammatical form of prophecy: the sage narrating the text foretells rulers who are, from the standpoint of the compilers, already in the past. These lists preserve the names of dynasties such as the Shungas, Kanvas, and Satavahanas, with reign lengths and sometimes brief comments on character.

Historians use them cautiously. Their versions differ from one another; their reign lengths sometimes add up inconsistently; they treat dynasties as though they ruled in strict sequence when several may have been contemporary; and they were compiled or revised centuries after the events, in a period (roughly the early centuries CE into the Gupta age) with its own religious and political interests. Where inscriptions or coins can be compared with them, the lists are sometimes confirmed and sometimes contradicted. They are best treated as a valuable, but retrospective and imperfect, framework.

A second body of evidence consists of literary works composed much later. The Sanskrit prose romance Harshacharita, written in the early seventh century CE by Bana at the court of Harsha (Chapter 30), alludes to Pushyamitra's killing of his master while reviewing the army. The Sanskrit play Malavikagnimitra, attributed to Kalidasa and usually assigned to the Gupta period (Chapter 29), is a courtly romance set in the household of Pushyamitra's son Agnimitra at Vidisha; it also refers to a military conflict with the neighboring kingdom of Vidarbha and to a clash with Greeks (Yavanas) over a sacrificial horse. Buddhist texts, especially the Divyavadana and related narratives, present Pushyamitra as a persecutor of monks (examined in Section 19.5). All of these are valuable evidence for how later authors remembered the Shungas. None is a contemporary account, and each has its own literary or religious purpose.

Contemporary evidence is much thinner. The clearest epigraphic reference to Pushyamitra is the Ayodhya inscription conventionally attributed to Dhanadeva, examined in the Primary Source Window in Section 19.4, which contains wording often interpreted as linking its ruler genealogically to Pushyamitra and credits Pushyamitra with sacrifices. The grammarian Patanjali, who wrote a commentary on Panini's grammar probably in the second century BCE, offers illustrative sentences that scholars have often connected to Pushyamitra, one suggesting that priestly rites were being performed on behalf of a ruler of that name and another referring to Greeks besieging cities in the region of Saketa and Madhyamika. Such passing references are suggestive but not straightforward historical narrative.

Taken together, this evidence supports a limited but useful picture. Pushyamitra rose from military command to kingship in the wake of Mauryan weakness; later traditions associate the Shunga family with Brahmanical lineages and Vedic ritual, but the details of his ancestry cannot be securely reconstructed; he ruled for several decades; he was remembered as a performer of Vedic royal rites; and his successors and descendants, or those who claimed descent from him, were established in more than one region. What the evidence does not support is a detailed political biography, a precisely delimited empire, or a confident account of his motives and religious policy. The rest of this chapter treats Pushyamitra accordingly: as a real and significant founder about whom later traditions say more than the surviving contemporary evidence can verify.

Section 19.2

The Shunga State

What kind of state did the Shungas rule? The question is easily distorted by the memory of the Mauryan Empire. Textbook maps sometimes show a Shunga territory covering most of northern and central India, as if the dynasty had taken over the empire's map along with its throne. The evidence does not support this. Shunga authority is best documented for a core in the middle and upper Gangetic plain and for the region around Vidisha in central India, and it is uncertain or absent in much of the rest of the former Mauryan domain.

Pataliputra remained the Shunga capital according to the Puranic tradition, and the city continued to be a major urban and commercial center. Vidisha, on the route between the Gangetic plain and the Deccan and Malwa, functions in the literary tradition as a second seat; in the Malavikagnimitra the prince Agnimitra governs from there while his father rules in the east. Whether this reflects a real division of authority between father and son or a later literary convention cannot be determined. The arrangement, if real, would resemble Mauryan practice, in which princes were sent to govern important provinces, but the scale is smaller.

Archaeological evidence gives a further sense of the state's reach. Kaushambi, Ayodhya, Ahichchhatra, Mathura, and other Gangetic sites show continued urban occupation, fired-brick construction in some places, and characteristic terracotta figurines from the second and first centuries BCE; the art historian's term "Shunga" is sometimes applied to these objects, but the label is chronological and stylistic more than political, and it is not evidence that a Shunga king controlled every place where such objects were found. Some local coinage carries names ending in -mitra (Agnimitra, Bhumimitra, and others in Panchala; a Mitra dynasty at Mathura), which has tempted some scholars to link these rulers with the Shungas. Since the ending was a common name-formation, such connections remain hypotheses.

Institutionally, the Shunga state appears to have relied on the inherited structures of Mauryan and earlier Magadhan government: revenue from agriculture and trade, garrisoned towns, provincial governors, and the army. But without an imperial inscription tradition, historians know little of the details. It is likely that the Shungas, like other successor rulers, exercised direct control over a core, paid allegiance or received tribute from subordinate rulers at a further distance, and competed with neighbors for the intermediate zone. This arrangement of a directly ruled center and an outer zone of shifting subordination is a common feature of early historic states in South Asia.

Geography Study

The Post-Mauryan Political Landscape

Using a historical map of the subcontinent in the second and first centuries BCE, students should locate and identify the following, remembering that all political boundaries in this period are approximate and that frontiers were zones of shifting influence rather than fixed lines:

  • Pataliputra and the Magadhan heartland on the lower Ganges
  • Ayodhya (Saketa) and the Kosala region on the Ghaghara river
  • Kaushambi, Mathura, and Ahichchhatra along the Yamuna and upper Ganges
  • Vidisha, Besnagar, Sanchi, and Bharhut in central India
  • Ujjain and the Malwa plateau
  • Kalinga on the coast of present-day Odisha and the Udayagiri-Khandagiri hills
  • Vidarbha and the northern Deccan, where the Satavahanas would emerge
  • The northwest, including Taxila and the routes toward Bactria, where Indo-Greek power was developing (Chapter 20)
  • The Rajasthan and Punjab zones inhabited by gana-sangha (republican) communities such as the Yaudheyas and Malavas

Section 19.3

Political Fragmentation

The word "fragmentation" is useful only if it is used precisely. It should not imply that a once-whole society broke into disorder. It refers to the loss of a single dominant political center and the growth of several. In the second and first centuries BCE the former Mauryan world contained many such centers, some new and some with older roots.

Three processes shaped this outcome. First, former Mauryan provinces and frontier zones acquired local rulers who no longer had a reason to defer to Pataliputra. In central India, in Kalinga, and in the Deccan, governors and local elites transformed delegated authority into inherited rule. Second, older political forms that the Mauryas had absorbed or suppressed, including gana-sangha communities governed by councils and the kinship-based polities of Rajasthan and the Punjab, revived or expanded. Their coins, issued in the names of communities such as the Yaudheyas, Malavas, and others, survive in large numbers. Third, external actors, above all the Greek-ruled states to the northwest, entered the political field, a development treated in Chapter 20.

The evidence for this multiplicity is largely numismatic and epigraphic. In the middle Gangetic and Yamuna region, small dynasties issued cast and die-struck copper coins bearing their names: rulers in Panchala with -mitra names, a series of Kosala kings with -deva names known from Ayodhya, local kings at Kaushambi, and Mitra and Datta rulers at Mathura. In central India, coins from Eran and Vidisha and local issues of Ujjain and Tripuri show similar diversity. The abundance of coinage itself indicates continuing commercial life. Regional coins were often modest in metal and design, and they served local exchange, but their proliferation is evidence for many authorities able to issue them.

Historical Vocabulary
Regionalization and Multipolarity

Regionalization refers to the process by which political, cultural, and economic life becomes organized around regional centers rather than a single dominant capital. Multipolarity describes a political landscape with several roughly comparable centers of power. Historians of South Asia use these terms for the centuries after the Mauryas, and again after the Guptas and after the Mughals, to describe periods when no single state controlled most of the subcontinent.

These terms are analytical, not moral. They do not mean decline, and they do not mean the absence of connection. A multipolar order can be commercially integrated, culturally dynamic, and religiously creative while remaining politically divided, as this chapter argues.

Fragmentation did not eliminate the ideal of a widely recognized ruler. The tradition of the chakravartin, the universal king, and the ritual of the ashvamedha (examined in Section 19.4) show that rulers could still claim precedence over their neighbors. What changed was the practical scale of such claims, and the fact that they now competed with numerous rival claims.

This section carries forward Chapter 18's conclusion that imperial decline and social continuity are different things. Fields continued to be plowed; guilds, monasteries, and temples continued to receive gifts; cities continued to be inhabited. What changed most directly was who collected revenue, who commanded soldiers, and who controlled the routes. The following sections show how, within this fragmented order, rulers and donors used religion and monument-building to establish their positions.

Section 19.4

Brahmanical Patronage

One of the best-known features of Pushyamitra's later reputation is his association with Vedic royal ritual. The Ayodhya inscription conventionally attributed to Dhanadeva, whose relationship to Pushyamitra is often interpreted genealogically, credits him with two horse sacrifices, and Patanjali's grammatical examples have been read as referring to priests performing rites for him. The Malavikagnimitra also depicts an ashvamedha connected with Pushyamitra, with his grandson guarding the horse against a Greek force. On this basis, historians often describe the Shungas as patrons of Brahmanical ritual.

Historical Vocabulary
Ashvamedha

The ashvamedha, or horse sacrifice, is among the most elaborate of the Vedic royal rituals, described in the Brahmana literature examined in Chapter 13 and in the ritual manuals. A consecrated horse is released to wander for a year, accompanied by a guard of warriors; the territory it traverses without challenge is symbolically claimed by the king who sponsors the rite, and the horse is finally sacrificed in a large public ceremony.

Historians treat the ashvamedha as a claim of paramount status rather than proof of actual imperial control. A king who performed it announced ambition and precedence, and often gained prestige and priestly support; but the rite could be performed by rulers of quite modest realms. It should not be read as evidence that any king who performed it ruled a large empire.

Two cautions are essential. The first concerns scale. Pushyamitra's ashvamedhas did not make the Shungas an empire on the Mauryan model. The rite was, in fact, associated with other figures of the period whose realms were much smaller. The fragmentary Ghosundi–Hathibada inscriptions, near Chittorgarh in Rajasthan, name Sarvatata, associate him with Gajayana and an ashvamedha, and refer to an enclosure or Narayana-vatika connected with Samkarshana and Vasudeva. Because the records are fragmentary, the reconstruction of the complete syntax, Sarvatata's precise status, and the meaning of Gajayana require caution. This was a different region and, apparently, a different family. It shows that ashvamedha performance and Vaishnava-related devotion were part of a wider culture of royal display that could be adopted by rulers without any connection to the Shungas.

The second caution concerns interpretation. The Ayodhya and Ghosundi–Hathibada records are among the important early examples of Sanskrit or Sanskritizing epigraphy, and this is significant. Ashokan edicts had used Prakrit, the vernacular of administration and Buddhist and Jain literature. The appearance of Sanskrit in royal epigraphy suggests that rulers, and the Brahmanical specialists who advised them, saw Sanskrit as a language of ritual prestige. But the process was gradual, and Prakrit remained the dominant language of inscriptions for centuries (Chapters 22 and 25).

Royal ritual served practical functions. A ruler who had come to power through the overthrow of a king, as Pushyamitra reportedly had, benefited from public ceremonies that established legitimacy. Priests gained patronage, gifts, and status. Such ritual also placed the ruler within an older Vedic tradition not tied to the Mauryan legacy, which had been strongly associated with Ashoka's dhamma and with Buddhist patronage. This need not imply hostility to Buddhism. It reflects the competitive patronage environment of the period, in which rulers, local elites, and religious communities sought support and recognition by different means.

The religious world visible in the epigraphy of this age was in fact plural. The Besnagar pillar, erected near Vidisha about the turn of the first century BCE, records that Heliodorus, son of Dion, of Taxila, sent by the Greek king Antialkidas as ambassador to the Indian king Bhagabhadra, set up a column in honor of Vasudeva, the god of gods, and described himself as a Bhagavata, a devotee. The Bhagabhadra of the inscription is often identified with a ruler named Bhadraka or Bhagavata in the Puranic Shunga list, though the identification is not certain. What the pillar demonstrates is that Vaishnava devotion, a religious current later associated with Vishnu and Krishna (Chapter 28), had followers in central India, and that a Greek official could adopt it. The religious history of this period cannot be reduced to a simple competition between Brahmanical and Buddhist camps.

Primary Source Window

The Ayodhya Inscription of Dhanadeva

Creator: A local ruler of Kosala in the central Gangetic plain, whose name survives only as "Dhana…" and is conventionally restored as Dhanadeva · Date: undated; approximately first century BCE–first century CE, palaeographic dating disputed · Location: Ayodhya (ancient Saketa), Uttar Pradesh · Language and script: Sanskrit, in Brahmi script · Type: Royal or dedicatory stone inscription

Context: The inscription is damaged, and the ruler's name is a conventional restoration. It commemorates a memorial or shrine (ketana) for his father, Phalgudeva. Because it is written in Sanskrit, it is among the earliest records of its kind. It is not dated by a regnal year or era; scholars place it in the post-Mauryan period on the basis of letter forms, and proposed dates range widely. The ruler is described as a lord of Kosala, one of the historic regions of the Gangetic plain, and connects himself to Pushyamitra.

Paraphrase: Dhana… (conventionally restored as Dhanadeva), lord of Kosala and described as Kausiki-putra, is called the "sixth" of the commander (senapati) Pushyamitra, often interpreted as the sixth descendant, who performed the ashvamedha twice. The record commemorates a ketana for his father Phalgudeva.

Analysis: This is the closest thing to contemporary epigraphic evidence for Pushyamitra's ritual reputation, though it was created several generations after him. It shows that a ruler in Kosala invoked a relationship to Pushyamitra as a source of status; under the usual genealogical interpretation, that relationship was descent, that the ashvamedha was remembered as a central achievement, and that Sanskrit and Brahmanical ritual language were used for royal self-presentation. It also shows the fragmented political landscape directly: Dhanadeva is a "lord of Kosala," not a king of Magadha or of a large empire. It does not, however, tell us the extent of Pushyamitra's realm, the circumstances of his rise, or his religious policy. Its claim of descent may also reflect the interests of a local dynasty seeking prestige, and its genealogy cannot be independently verified.

Questions for students:

  1. What does this inscription say or show about how Dhanadeva presented himself and his ancestry?
  2. Who created it, and for what purposes might a ruler have claimed descent from Pushyamitra?
  3. What can historians learn from it about Sanskrit, ritual, and legitimacy in the post-Mauryan period?
  4. What can historians not establish from this source alone about Pushyamitra or the size of his state?
Section 19.5

Buddhist Institutions

No question about the Shungas has drawn more attention than their relationship to Buddhism. A long tradition, reproduced in many older textbooks, presents Pushyamitra as a fierce Brahmanical restorer who persecuted Buddhists, destroyed monasteries, and reversed the policy of Ashoka. This section examines what that claim rests on and what the evidence allows historians to say about royal policy and patronage.

The claim comes primarily from Buddhist literary sources. The Divyavadana, a collection of Buddhist narratives compiled in the early centuries CE and incorporating the earlier Ashokavadana, describes a king named Pushyamitra who, wishing to destroy the Buddhist community, marched to the Kukkutarama monastery at Pataliputra, was turned back by a miracle, and then offered a reward for the head of every monk. The story is part of a narrative cycle about Ashoka and his successors, and it is preserved in a text with clear devotional and didactic aims. Later Buddhist authors, including Tibetan writers many centuries afterward, repeated and elaborated the tradition. None of these texts is contemporary, and none provides independent documentation.

The archaeological and epigraphic evidence points in a different direction, or at least complicates the story. Bharhut, Sanchi, and Bodh Gaya, all regions or sites that could plausibly have been affected by Shunga power, show expansion and embellishment of Buddhist monuments during the second and first centuries BCE (see Section 19.6). The Bharhut inscription mentions the rule of the Shungas in connection with a donor, and Buddhist donors at these sites include people with connections to local ruling families. At Sanchi, the stupa complex grew significantly in this period. There is no clear archaeological horizon of destruction that can be securely attributed to Pushyamitra, though the evidence for individual sites, such as Kaushambi and Pataliputra itself, is less complete than that for the great stupa sites.

Historical Debate

Did Pushyamitra Persecute Buddhists?

The question: Did the founder of the Shunga dynasty carry out a systematic persecution of Buddhists, as later Buddhist tradition claims, or does this tradition reflect later religious memory and sectarian rivalry more than events of the second century BCE?

Interpretation A — A real, if limited, hostility to Buddhist institutions: Some historians accept that the Buddhist accounts preserve a kernel of historical memory. On this view, Pushyamitra's support for Vedic sacrifice, and the possible loss of imperial Buddhist patronage after Ashoka, created tensions, and violent episodes directed at particular monasteries may have occurred, especially in and around the capital. The tradition, though exaggerated, reflects a real shift in royal patronage away from Buddhism.
Interpretation B — A later tradition not supported by contemporary evidence: Other historians argue that the stories are late literary and polemical constructions, written centuries after Pushyamitra in a period of sectarian competition, and that the archaeological record of continued and expanding Buddhist construction is difficult to reconcile with a sustained persecution. On this view, the evidence supports at most a preference for Brahmanical ritual and no demonstrable policy of suppression.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely on the same limited materials: Buddhist narrative texts of later date; the absence of contemporary royal edicts; inscriptions and monuments at Bharhut, Sanchi, and Bodh Gaya; and a small amount of excavation at other sites. They differ on how to weigh later literary testimony against archaeological silence.

Why it matters: If the persecution narrative were accepted uncritically, it would support a simple story of Brahmanical revival against Buddhism that historians have long questioned. If it is dismissed too quickly, students may miss the genuine competition for patronage that shaped this period. This textbook holds that the evidence does not support a demonstrated state policy of persecution, that Buddhism continued to flourish in Shunga-era regions, and that competitive patronage, not polarized conflict, best describes the religious environment.

Student task: Explain why a text composed several centuries after an event may preserve either genuine memory or later polemic. Then identify one kind of additional evidence, such as excavated monastic destruction layers with secure dating, that would help resolve the question.

This section's conclusion is deliberately modest. Historians can say that Buddhist institutions survived and grew in Shunga-era regions; that Buddhist authors later remembered Pushyamitra as hostile; that Brahmanical rites received royal support; and that a policy of persecution is not demonstrated. They cannot say how individual monasteries fared in the capital, or whether Pushyamitra personally treated Buddhist communities harshly at particular moments. That is a limit of the evidence and not a reason to choose one narrative over another.

Section 19.6

Art and Monumental Construction

Chapter 18 addressed the origins of Sanchi under Ashoka and the careful chronology of its early phases. This section turns to what happened next: the flowering of monumental Buddhist art in the second and first centuries BCE. This is one of the period's most important achievements, and it occurred not in spite of political fragmentation but in the context of it.

At Bharhut, in present-day Madhya Pradesh, a stupa was surrounded by a railing and gateway decorated with carved medallions, pillars, and coping showing the life of the Buddha represented by symbols such as the throne, the wheel, and the footprints, and numerous scenes from the Jataka stories of his earlier births. Many panels carry short inscriptions naming the scenes and donors. An inscription on the gateway, attributed to a donor named Dhanabhuti, opens with Suganam raje, commonly interpreted as "during the rule of the Shungas/Sugas." This is the closest contemporary link between the dynasty and a major monument. It places this major gateway phase of Bharhut in the post-Mauryan period commonly associated with Shunga rule, and suggests the donor used the dynasty's reign to date the work; the site as a whole should not be assigned to a single moment of creation. The standard donative formula at Bharhut is simply the donor's name followed by a word for "gift," sometimes specifying a pillar or rail. It does not tell us that the Shungas paid for it, and the donor inscriptions on the railing suggest that many individuals and communities, including monks and nuns, contributed.

At Sanchi, the brick stupa built in the Mauryan period was enlarged in the second century BCE, with a stone casing, a railing, and a harmika at the summit; a second and third stupa were also built or elaborated in this period. The great carved gateways (toranas) of Stupa 1 belong to a somewhat later phase, around the late first century BCE and early first century CE, when the region was associated with Satavahana rulers; one gateway inscription records a gift by artisans working under a Satavahana king. Thus Sanchi's monumental development spans several political regimes, a fact that underlines how little the monuments depend on a single dynasty. At Bodh Gaya, the site of the Buddha's awakening, a stone railing around the sacred tree and shrine was also erected in this era, with inscriptions naming donors that include the noblewoman Kurangi, whom some readings identify as the wife of a king Indragnimitra, along with other donors associated with names such as Brahmamitra. The readings differ, and whether these individuals belonged to a Shunga-related family is uncertain; neither ruler belongs securely to the Puranic Shunga genealogy.

Students should distinguish carefully among four analytical categories when attributing such monuments: dynasty (who ruled the region), period (when the work was carried out), workshop (the artisans and their traditions), and donor (who paid for individual elements). "Shunga art" is a useful chronological shorthand, but it should not be read as implying royal commissioning of every work. In fact, the inscriptions show donors of extraordinarily diverse status: monks and nuns, merchants, guild members, householders, and local officials. Such nonroyal patronage was a major source of funding and a sign of the wealth and religious commitments of urban and rural communities.

Artistically, these monuments show a new visual vocabulary. Early Buddhist art largely avoided showing the Buddha in human form, using symbols instead, and drew on older Indian traditions of sculpture in wood and ivory, translated into stone. Yakshas and yakshis, nature spirits, appear alongside Buddhist themes. Depictions of daily life, animals, plants, and dress provide rich evidence of material culture (see the Everyday Life feature). Painted and molded terracotta figurines from Gangetic and Bengal sites, including the Chandraketugarh region, show a parallel tradition in clay.

The main historical lesson is that monumental construction flourished under politically fragmented conditions. The resources for such works came from agricultural surplus, trade, and the religious commitments of local communities, and were mobilized by donors rather than a single imperial treasury. That is a real contrast with the Mauryan world, in which the state had been the principal patron of monumental stone works, and it points forward to the distributed patronage that characterizes later chapters.

Section 19.7

The Kanva Dynasty

According to the Puranas, the Shunga line ended when its last king, Devabhuti, was overthrown by his minister Vasudeva, who founded the Kanva dynasty. The Puranic lists give four Kanva kings, Vasudeva, Bhumimitra, Narayana, and Susarman, and a total duration of about forty-five years, traditionally placed from around the 70s to the 20s BCE. The story of Vasudeva's coup is elaborated in Bana's Harshacharita, where the last Shunga is portrayed as dissolute and betrayed by a plot involving a servant girl in disguise. This anecdote is a seventh-century literary account, not a contemporary report, and the moralizing tone is typical of later dynastic memory.

The Kanvas are, in practical terms, poorly documented. Few inscriptions or coins can be securely attributed to them, and the surviving information consists mainly of the Puranic names and reign lengths. Puranic tradition also says that the Kanva line ended with the rise of Satavahana power, though the relationship between the Kanvas, the later Shungas, and the Satavahanas is debated, and some historians question whether all these dynasties were successive rather than partly contemporary. The reader should therefore treat the Kanva period as a reminder of the limits of political chronology, not as a period whose events can be narrated.

The Kanvas' instructional value lies in what they show about the shrinking of Magadha's political horizon. Under the Nandas and Mauryas, control of Magadha, with its agricultural wealth, its iron and forest resources, and its position on the Ganges, had been the basis of imperial power (Chapters 10 and 15). By the first century BCE, holding Magadha meant something much smaller. The region remained productive, and Pataliputra remained a major city, but the ruler of Magadha was now one among many kings, and probably not the most powerful of them. This change did not reflect the loss of Magadha's resources so much as the appearance of alternative centers, whose rulers could draw on their own agricultural bases, trade routes, and military forces.

The Kanva period should also caution students about a tempting narrative habit: treating dynastic sequences as though they explained the whole of political life. A period known through four names and a few anecdotes was nevertheless a period of continuing agriculture, trade, and religious patronage. The region of Magadha remained a major Buddhist center, with Bodh Gaya and the monasteries of the area attracting pilgrims and donors, and it would regain imperial significance under the Guptas (Chapter 26).

Section 19.8

Regional Powers

The Shungas and Kanvas were only one part of the post-Mauryan political landscape. This section broadens the lens to other regional powers, not to narrate their histories in full, since later chapters treat several of them, but to show the pattern of regionalization. The examples are chosen for their evidence and for what they reveal.

Kalinga and Kharavela. The coastal region of Kalinga had been conquered by Ashoka in the war that Chapter 16 examined. By the first century BCE, and possibly earlier, it was the seat of an independent kingdom. Its best-known ruler, Kharavela, is known from the Hathigumpha inscription ("Elephant Cave"), a seventeen-line Prakrit record in Brahmi script cut into the rock of Udayagiri near Bhubaneswar. The text opens with an invocation of Jain holy figures, and it recounts, in a year-by-year format, the king's coronation, his public works, his military expeditions, and his religious gifts. It refers to an aqueduct or canal associated with an earlier Nanda king, to campaigns against neighboring rulers, to a raid or advance toward the Magadhan region, and to donations to Brahmins and to Jain monks. Some readings mention a king of Magadha named Bahasatimita, who has been identified by some scholars with Brihaspatimitra and, more speculatively, with Pushyamitra; other readings and identifications are contested. The date of the inscription, and the reading of several key passages, including a possible reference to a Greek king, remain disputed, and the inscription's weathered surface has allowed competing interpretations.

Kharavela's importance lies less in any single campaign than in what the inscription reveals. It shows a regional ruler using a royal biography to assert legitimacy, in a Prakrit closely related to the language of the Ashokan edicts; it shows Jain patronage by a king (compare Chapter 12) alongside gifts to Brahmins, which again illustrates religious plurality; it shows that Kalinga was a capable state with its own military and irrigation projects; and it shows that a ruler on the eastern coast could measure himself against the powers of Magadha. It does not show a Kalingan empire, and there is no evidence that Kharavela's achievements were institutionalized beyond his reign.

The Gangetic and Yamuna kingdoms. Beyond the Shunga core, small dynasties ruled in Kosala (the -deva rulers of Ayodhya, including Dhanadeva), Panchala (the -mitra rulers of Ahichchhatra), the Kaushambi region (where coins and the Pabhosa inscription attest figures including Bahasatimita/Brihaspatimitra, although his precise territorial position and his relationship to the ruler named, or possibly named, in the Hathigumpha inscription remain disputed), and Mathura (local Mitra and Datta rulers whose coinage is known). These rulers had modest domains, but their coinage, inscriptions, and continued urban life show functioning regional states.

Central and western India. Vidisha and its surrounding region, with the Besnagar pillar and the Sanchi and Bharhut monuments, formed a cultural and religious zone. Ujjain and Malwa were centers of trade and were to become contested between the Satavahanas and the Shakas (Chapters 20 and 22). In Rajasthan, Punjab, and Haryana, gana-sangha communities such as the Yaudheyas, Malavas, Arjunayanas, and others issued coins and defended themselves against larger powers.

Beyond the heartland. The northwest was passing to Indo-Greek and later Shaka and Parthian rule (Chapter 20), and would then be integrated into the Kushan empire (Chapter 21). The Deccan was the home of the emerging Satavahanas (Chapter 22). The far south and Sri Lanka had their own trajectories (Chapters 23 and 24). This chapter's task is to note the pattern, not to preempt those chapters: across the subcontinent, regional rulers were consolidating authority, often taking advantage of trade routes and local agricultural resources, and often drawing on a repertoire of religious patronage and royal ritual that was widely shared.

Section 19.9

From Empire to Multipolarity

This section draws together the chapter's interpretive argument by comparing the Mauryan political order with what followed. The comparison is not meant to rank one as better than the other, but to identify what actually changed.

Under the Mauryas, a single ruler and court at Pataliputra claimed sovereignty over most of the subcontinent, communicated with subjects through a network of officials and inscriptions, and mobilized the resources of a vast agrarian base for a large standing army and a ramified administration (Chapters 15 through 17). Imperial patronage was especially prominent in surviving Mauryan monumental stone projects. After the Mauryas, no ruler or dynasty could plausibly make such a claim. Instead, several kingdoms and communities coexisted, competed, and sometimes allied. Rulers relied more on personal loyalties, hereditary connections, and ritual display than on a large centralized bureaucracy, and military authority was distributed among regional courts and political communities.

Yet the period also shows striking continuities. Cities and trade continued to expand; coinage diversified; Buddhist, Jain, and Brahmanical institutions grew; and rulers everywhere drew on shared political vocabulary: royal titles, sacrifice, dedication of gifts, and inscription. Sanskrit and Prakrit coexisted as languages of state and religion. The Ashokan model of imperial dhamma had no direct successor, but the idea of the king as a patron of religion, and the practice of recording gifts in stone, spread widely.

The chapter's paradox can be stated plainly: political fragmentation coexisted with cultural, religious, artistic, and commercial connectivity. A donor at Bharhut, a Greek envoy at Besnagar, a Jain king in Kalinga, and a local ruler in Ayodhya belonged to different political worlds but shared a wider culture of patronage and display. Trade routes, monastic networks, and pilgrimage circuits linked regions more effectively than any government did. This pattern of a multipolar political order set within an integrated cultural and commercial world is the setting for Part V. The next chapters show it in the northwest, where transregional powers ruled over borderlands linking South Asia to Central Asia and the Mediterranean, in the Deccan and the far south, and in the maritime networks that carried goods to Rome and Southeast Asia.

Finally, this multipolarity should be understood as historically productive. It gave rise to new centers of patronage and innovation, allowed regional cultures to develop distinctive forms, and made possible the wide range of experiments in political and religious life that characterize the early historic period. Later historians, especially those who wrote when nationalism placed a premium on imperial unity, sometimes treated such periods as failures. A more careful account recognizes that the subcontinent's history has alternated, repeatedly, between periods of imperial integration and periods of regional multiplicity, and that each had its own creative and difficult dimensions.

Section 19.10

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has traced the transformation of the Gangetic heartland and its neighbors from Mauryan imperial predominance to a multipolar political landscape. Chapter 18 explained why the empire's authority fragmented; this chapter has explained what the resulting order looked like and how it worked. Several threads developed across the preceding nine sections come together here.

The first thread concerns evidence. Sections 19.1 and 19.7 showed that the Shungas and Kanvas are known largely through retrospective sources: Puranic lists, later literary works, and later Buddhist narratives. Contemporary evidence, in the form of a few inscriptions, coins, and monuments, is much thinner. This unevenness does not make the period unknowable, but it requires historians to reconstruct its political outlines cautiously and to give more weight to religion, art, and regional life, where the evidence is richer.

The second thread concerns political structure. Sections 19.2 and 19.3 argued that the Shunga state was a regional power with a core in the Gangetic plain and central India, not an empire, and that fragmentation meant the emergence of multiple political centers, including new dynasties, gana-sangha communities, and local kingdoms. This structure was not chaotic; it was organized around competing regional rulers and shared political vocabulary.

The third thread concerns religion and patronage. Sections 19.4 through 19.6 showed that royal ritual, especially the ashvamedha, served rulers seeking legitimacy; that Brahmanical, Buddhist, Jain, and emerging devotional traditions all received patronage; that later accusations of Shunga persecution of Buddhism are not supported by the archaeological record as a demonstrated state policy; and that monumental Buddhist art flourished under fragmented conditions through the donations of diverse patrons. The religious history of the period is best described as competitive and plural.

The fourth thread concerns scale and connection. Sections 19.7 through 19.9 showed that the Kanvas illustrate the shrinking of Magadha's political horizon, that regional powers such as Kalinga and the small kingdoms of Kosala, Panchala, Kaushambi, and Mathura formed a wider pattern of regionalization, and that this fragmentation coexisted with commercial, religious, artistic, and cultural connectivity.

Taken together, these threads support the chapter's central argument: the Shungas and Kanvas mattered, but the larger historical development was the transition from Mauryan imperial predominance to a multipolar South Asian political landscape. The next chapter turns to the northwest, where that landscape acquired a distinctive transregional dimension through the Indo-Greeks, Shakas, and Indo-Parthians.

Multiple Perspectives

Patronage in a Multipolar World: Reconstructed Perspectives

Note: the perspectives below are reconstructed analytical illustrations grounded in the types of evidence discussed in this chapter, not quotations from any named historical individual.

A regional ruler such as a lord of Kosala or Kalinga: Legitimacy depended on ritual display, inscriptions, coinage, and the support of priests and monastic communities. Claims of descent, as in the Ayodhya inscription, or narratives of conquest and public works, as in the Hathigumpha inscription, made a modest realm appear significant and secured recognition from rivals and subjects.
A Buddhist monastic community and its lay donors: Institutions depended on many small and large gifts, and railings and gateways at sites like Bharhut record the names of monks, nuns, and householders. Royal support was welcome but not the only source of resources, which made communities less vulnerable to changes in political regime.
A Brahmanical ritual specialist: Royal sacrifices offered prestige, gifts, and a role in legitimating new dynasties. Priests who performed or advised on rites such as the ashvamedha benefited from competition among rulers, though they too worked in an environment where other religious traditions attracted patronage.
A merchant or artisan in a Gangetic or central Indian town: Political fragmentation meant more local authorities, more coinages, and possibly more tolls, but also more markets and more potential patrons. Guild members appear among the donors at Buddhist sites, showing how commercial wealth was converted into religious and social standing.
A foreign visitor or official such as Heliodorus: Foreigners, whether ambassadors or traders, could adopt local religious forms and honor local deities. Such figures show that the multipolar landscape was open to outside participants, and that identities were more fluid than modern ethnic or religious categories suggest.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Imperial Center to Many Centers

What Changed

  • Sovereignty over most of the subcontinent passed from a single court to many regional rulers and communities
  • Monumental patronage shifted from mainly imperial to mainly donor-based and local
  • Royal ritual, including Vedic sacrifice and Sanskrit inscriptions, became more prominent as a source of legitimacy
  • Magadha's political weight shrank while other regions gained

What Stayed the Same

  • Agriculture, urban life, and trade continued on the foundations laid in the second urbanization
  • Buddhist, Jain, and Brahmanical institutions continued to receive gifts and to grow
  • The practice of recording royal and private gifts in inscriptions, popularized in the Mauryan period, continued and spread
  • Routes across the Gangetic plain, central India, and the coasts continued to connect regions

Who experienced change most directly? Ruling elites, officials, and the Magadhan capital's dependents felt the loss of imperial power most sharply. Who experienced greater continuity? Farmers, artisans, and most monastic communities continued with limited disruption, although local conflicts and changing tax collectors certainly affected them.

Why the pattern matters: It prevents students from equating the end of an empire with the end of a civilization, and helps them recognize that political and social continuity can diverge.

Cause and Effect

Why Regional Consolidation Followed Empire

Long-term causes

Regional diversity, uneven Mauryan penetration of frontier and forest zones, older regional traditions of kingship and gana-sangha government, and the growth of local agrarian and commercial resources gave regional elites a basis for autonomy.

Short-term causes

Succession problems, fiscal and military strain, and the overthrow of the last Mauryan king weakened the center at a time when local governors and elites had the means to act independently.

Event or transformation

A multipolar order emerged in which the Shungas, Kanvas, Kosala and Panchala dynasties, Kalinga, gana-sangha communities, and, later, the Satavahanas and northwestern powers coexisted.

Immediate effects

Political authority became more localized; rulers relied on ritual, coinage, and inscriptions to establish legitimacy; and donors funded monuments across the region.

Long-term consequences

The pattern of regional kingdoms set within a connected commercial and religious world shaped the early historic period and would recur after the Guptas and after the Mughals.

This textbook's response

The chapter avoids treating later regional states as rebel Mauryan provinces, and avoids reducing the transformation to one ruler's action or one religious policy.

Historical complexity: Not every regional power emerged from a former Mauryan province. Some were old political traditions, some were outside powers, and some grew from new commercial wealth. The end of Mauryan supremacy created opportunities; it did not determine their form.

Everyday Life and Social History

Towns, Donors, and Households in the Post-Mauryan World

The reliefs at Bharhut and Sanchi offer a rare window into everyday life, although they must be read as religious art, not documentary photography. They show townspeople in courtyards and streets, houses and gateways, carts and animals, musicians and dancers, ascetics and forest dwellers, and the dress and ornaments of men and women of varied status. Donor inscriptions record occupations and places of origin, including household heads, monks and nuns, and members of guilds, and they reveal that women appear frequently as donors in their own names. This suggests that at least some women controlled resources and participated in religious patronage, though such evidence cannot be generalized to all women.

Terracotta figurines from Gangetic and Bengal sites, many of them female figures with elaborate hairstyles and ornaments, and toys, ornaments, and household objects recovered from urban excavations, indicate a lively urban material culture. Most people, however, lived in villages and worked the land, and the evidence for them is thin. Farmers, herders, and forest-dwelling communities left few records, and historians must infer their lives from settlement patterns, tools, and later texts. The political changes of the period, including new rulers and tax collectors, would have affected them mainly through changes in revenue demands, military recruitment, and the security of roads and markets.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the political world of the Gangetic heartland and its neighbors after the collapse of Mauryan imperial authority. It began where Chapter 18 ended, with the overthrow of the last Mauryan king, and asked what kind of order emerged. Its central argument is that the end of Mauryan supremacy did not create a vacuum but redistributed power among smaller dynasties, regional rulers, military elites, religious institutions, cities, and commercial networks.

Section 19.1 examined Pushyamitra Shunga and showed that the evidence for him is largely retrospective. The Puranic dynastic lists, the Harshacharita, the Malavikagnimitra, and Buddhist narratives were composed centuries after his time, while contemporary evidence is limited to inscriptions such as the Ayodhya record and passing references in Patanjali. Historians can establish that he was a military man who became king and was remembered as a performer of royal sacrifices, but not much more.

Section 19.2 argued that the Shunga state was a regional power, with a core in the Gangetic plain and central India centered on Pataliputra and Vidisha, not a Mauryan-scale empire. Maps that suggest otherwise misrepresent the evidence.

Section 19.3 explained fragmentation as the emergence of multiple political centers, including new dynasties, revived gana-sangha communities, and local rulers, known from an abundance of coins and inscriptions, and it emphasized that political decentralization was compatible with continued urban and commercial life.

Sections 19.4 and 19.5 addressed religion and patronage. Vedic royal ritual, especially the ashvamedha, offered rulers legitimacy, and Sanskrit began to appear in royal inscriptions; but the period's religious life was plural, as the Besnagar pillar, the Ghosundi inscription, and Buddhist donor inscriptions show. The tradition that Pushyamitra persecuted Buddhists comes from later Buddhist texts and is not supported as a demonstrated state policy by the archaeological record, which shows Buddhist institutions continuing and expanding.

Section 19.6 showed that monumental construction flourished under fragmentation. Bharhut, Sanchi, and Bodh Gaya were built or enlarged through the gifts of many donors, from kings and queens to monks, nuns, merchants, and householders. Historians must distinguish dynasty, period, workshop, and donor, and avoid attributing every monument to "the Shungas."

Section 19.7 treated the Kanvas as a poorly documented dynasty whose main significance is the contraction of Magadha's political horizon. Control of Magadha no longer meant control of the subcontinent. Section 19.8 broadened the frame to regional powers, especially Kalinga under Kharavela, and to the small kingdoms and communities of Kosala, Panchala, Kaushambi, Mathura, Malwa, and Rajasthan, while reserving the northwest, the Deccan, the far south, and Sri Lanka for Chapters 20 through 24.

Section 19.9 compared the Mauryan and post-Mauryan orders and identified the chapter's paradox: political fragmentation coexisted with cultural, religious, artistic, and commercial connectivity. This multipolar landscape was productive and creative, and it sets the stage for the rest of Part V, in which transregional powers, maritime commerce, and regional kingdoms shape early historic South Asia.

Kanva
The dynasty that, according to Puranic tradition, replaced the Shungas in Magadha in the first century BCE; poorly documented outside the Puranic lists.
Senapati
An army commander; the office held by Pushyamitra before he became king.
Puranas
Sanskrit texts of cosmology, genealogy, and religion whose dynastic lists, composed centuries after the events, preserve the traditional chronology of the Shungas and Kanvas.
Dynastic list
A sequence of kings and reign lengths, as found in the Puranas, useful as a framework but often inconsistent, retrospective, and prone to presenting contemporary dynasties as successive.
Ashvamedha
The Vedic horse sacrifice, through which a king asserted paramount status; performance does not by itself prove control of a large empire.
Legitimation
The processes, including ritual, genealogy, inscription, and patronage, by which rulers established the acceptability of their authority.
Regionalization
The organization of political, economic, and cultural life around regional centers rather than one dominant capital.
Multipolarity
A political landscape with several comparable centers of power, as in the post-Mauryan subcontinent.
Sovereignty
The claim to ultimate political authority within a territory; under the Mauryas it was claimed by a single court over most of the subcontinent, while after them it was divided among many rulers.
Gana-sangha
A kinship-based or council-governed polity, often called a republic, that issued coins and defended its autonomy in regions such as Punjab and Rajasthan.
Stupa
A dome-shaped Buddhist monument containing relics or marking a sacred site, often surrounded by a railing and gateways.
Harmika
The square railing-like structure at the top of a stupa's dome.
Torana
A carved gateway at the entrance to a stupa enclosure, as at Sanchi.
Donor inscription
A short inscription recording a gift to a religious institution, typically naming the donor and sometimes occupation and place of origin.
Prakrit
Middle Indo-Aryan vernacular languages, used in Ashokan edicts, Buddhist and Jain texts, and most early inscriptions.
Palaeography
The study of ancient writing forms, used to date undated inscriptions by the shapes of letters.
Hathigumpha inscription
The seventeen-line Prakrit inscription of Kharavela of Kalinga at Udayagiri, a rare royal narrative of a regional power, with disputed date and readings.
Besnagar pillar
A column near Vidisha erected by the Greek envoy Heliodorus in honor of Vasudeva, evidence for cross-cultural religious participation.
Bharhut
A Buddhist stupa site in central India, known for its carved railing with Jataka scenes and donor inscriptions, one of which mentions Shunga rule.
Kharavela
King of Kalinga in the post-Mauryan period, known from the Hathigumpha inscription.
Patronage
The support of religious, artistic, or intellectual institutions through gifts, commissions, and protection; a key means of establishing status.
Punch-marked coin
An early Indian coin type struck with multiple punches; it continued to be used alongside cast and die-struck coins in the post-Mauryan period.
Bhagavata
A devotee of the deity Bhagavan, especially Vasudeva; the term the Greek envoy Heliodorus used for himself.

MGU Library Research Project

Investigating a Post-Mauryan Monument or Inscription

This project asks you to investigate how historians use material and epigraphic evidence to reconstruct political and religious life in a period with few contemporary narratives.

  1. Choose a subject. Select a monument or inscription discussed in this chapter, such as Bharhut, Sanchi, Bodh Gaya, the Besnagar pillar, the Ayodhya inscription, or the Hathigumpha inscription.
  2. Research the evidence. Using library resources and reliable academic sources, find out how the site or text was discovered, dated, and read, and what translations and interpretations exist.
  3. Identify disagreements. Identify at least one scholarly disagreement, such as dating, the reading of a passage, or the attribution of patronage.
  4. Distinguish categories. For a monument, separate the evidence for dynasty, period, workshop, and donor. For an inscription, separate what it states from what later scholars have inferred.
  5. Write your findings. In a 3–5 page essay or equivalent presentation, explain what the evidence shows, present differing interpretations fairly, and use careful language such as "the evidence suggests" or "this remains debated."

Library resources to use: South Asian art history and epigraphy collections, translated inscription corpora, historical atlases, and the MGU History Library's reference materials on early historic South Asia (see also Appendix A, Glossary of Regional and Historical Terminology).

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it corrects a common habit of thought: the assumption that political unity is the natural condition of South Asian history and that its absence signals decline. The centuries after the Mauryas produced some of the subcontinent's most important monuments, new forms of royal ritual and inscription, widening trade, and a religiously plural culture, all without a single imperial center. That a period so productive could be so poorly documented in dynastic terms is itself instructive about how historians build their pictures.

The chapter also reveals a larger pattern. Where Part IV showed how one state could bring most of the subcontinent under a single administration, Part V shows how power, wealth, and religious life were distributed among many centers connected by trade, pilgrimage, and shared political vocabulary. The Shungas and Kanvas were participants in this pattern, not its authors. Later rulers, from the Kushans to the Guptas, would draw on the same repertoire of ritual, patronage, and inscription, even as they built very different kinds of states.

Within the six-volume series, this chapter belongs to the long early historic phase of Volume I, in which regional kingdoms, transregional powers, and maritime and overland trade shape South Asia before the classical synthesis of the Gupta age. It also connects regions: Magadha, Kosala, and Kaushambi in the Gangetic plain, Vidisha and Malwa in central India, and Kalinga on the eastern coast, with the northwest, the Deccan, and the far south taken up in the chapters that follow.

Chapter 20 now turns to the northwest, where the Indo-Greeks, Shakas, and Indo-Parthians created political and cultural worlds that linked South Asia to Central Asia and the Mediterranean, extending the multipolar pattern examined here into a transregional setting.
