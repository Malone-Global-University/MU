Part X — The Mughal Empire

Babur, Humayun, and Sher Shah

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

On the morning of 21 April 1526, on a flat plain about ninety kilometers north of Delhi, a ruler from Kabul drew up a small army behind a line of wheeled carts and opened fire on the army of the Sultan of Delhi. Zahir al-Din Muhammad Babur, then forty-three years old, had spent nearly all of his adult life winning and losing small kingdoms in Central Asia. Ibrahim Lodi, the Afghan Sultan facing him, ruled one of the largest states in South Asia. By the afternoon Ibrahim lay dead on the field of Panipat. Yet what Babur won that day was an opportunity rather than an empire, and the thirty years that followed show how fragile that opportunity was.

This chapter studies the unstable first phase of Mughal expansion, from Babur’s accession as a boy-ruler in Ferghana in 1494 to the death of his son Humayun in Delhi in January 1556. It also studies the political innovations that preceded Akbar’s consolidation of the empire. Three overlapping political projects structure the narrative. The first is Babur’s conquest of northern India between 1526 and 1530. The second is Humayun’s loss of that inheritance in 1540, his long exile, and his restoration in 1555. The third is the competing Afghan empire founded by Sher Shah Suri and continued by his successors, which for roughly fifteen years contested or governed much of the same territory that the early Mughals struggled to hold.

The chapter’s central argument is that winning northern India in 1526 did not establish a stable imperial system. Babur had to secure his conquest against Afghan chiefs, Rajput rulers, and the restlessness of his own soldiers. Humayun inherited a realm whose institutions were thin, whose revenues were uncertain, and whose ruling family regarded territory as a shared dynastic property. Sher Shah built a powerful alternative regime out of the Afghan political world of Bihar. Humayun returned only after a long exile and in a changed political landscape, and he died within a year of his return, leaving his thirteen-year-old son another contested imperial inheritance.

The narrative is arranged in seven divisions. The first places Babur in his Timurid and Central Asian world and in the political crisis of the Lodi Sultanate. The second and third follow his campaigns from the Punjab to Panipat, Khanwa, Chanderi, and Ghaghra, and ask what he actually governed at his death in 1530. The fourth examines Humayun’s first reign and the defeats at Chausa and Kannauj. The fifth turns to Sher Shah and the construction of the Sur state, which receives the chapter’s most detailed institutional analysis. The sixth follows Humayun’s exile and restoration. The seventh compares the three regimes and asks what the first thirty years reveal about the challenges Akbar inherited.

The kinds of evidence available shape what can be known. Babur’s own memoir, the Baburnama, written in Chagatai Turkic, is a remarkable first-person source, but it is selective, has gaps, and presents its author’s own interpretation of events. Gulbadan Begum’s Humayun-nama, Jauhar’s memoir, and the great chronicles of Akbar’s reign were composed decades after the events they describe, under a dynasty that had its own reasons for shaping the story of its early years. Abbas Sarwani’s account of Sher Shah was likewise written at Akbar’s command, long after the Sur regime had fallen. Coins, inscriptions, and buildings provide a necessary check on all of these narratives. Cultivators, servants, ordinary soldiers, and most women outside the courts rarely speak in any of them.

Historians also face the problem of retrospective labels. The word "Mughal" is the Persian form of "Mongol" and was applied to Babur’s dynasty largely by outsiders, while the dynasty emphasized its Timurid descent. It is tempting to read the early period as a prelude to Akbar’s greatness, or to treat Sher Shah either as a mere interruption or as a flawless proto-Akbar. This chapter avoids both approaches. It also avoids reading conflicts of the period as simple confrontations between Hindus and Muslims: Babur fought Muslim Afghans and Hindu Rajputs, Rajput and Afghan leaders sometimes fought together, and Mughal princes fought one another.

The chapter connects directly to its neighbors. Chapter 43 examined the Lodi Sultanate, Chapter 47 the regional sultanates of Gujarat, Bengal, and Malwa, and Chapter 49 the Portuguese entry into Indian Ocean commerce. Chapter 51 begins with Akbar’s accession and the Second Battle of Panipat, and Chapter 52 treats his mature government and religious policy. Chapters 55 and 56 examine Mughal society and culture comprehensively. The present chapter therefore supplies only the context from those chapters that it needs, and it reserves for them what belongs to them.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Babur’s Timurid and Chagatai background, and his experience in Ferghana, Samarkand, and Kabul, prepared him for the invasion of northern India.
  2. Analyze the political crisis of the Lodi Sultanate and explain why Babur’s invasion succeeded when earlier incursions had not.
  3. Evaluate the military explanations for Babur’s victories at Panipat and Khanwa, including artillery, field fortification, mobility, and command.
  4. Distinguish between battlefield conquest and effective government in the years 1526–1530, and describe what Babur actually controlled at his death.
  5. Interpret the Baburnama as a historical source, identifying its strengths, silences, and perspective.
  6. Assess the causes of Humayun’s defeats at Chausa and Kannauj by weighing logistics, finance, dynastic rivalry, and Afghan resurgence against personal explanations.
  7. Trace Sher Shah’s rise from a Bihar jagirdar’s son to ruler of northern India and explain the political foundations of his regime.
  8. Analyze Sur administration, land revenue, coinage, roads, and communications, distinguishing demonstrated achievements from retrospective praise and from earlier precedents.
  9. Describe Humayun’s exile, his relationship with the Safavid court, and the reconstruction of his power in Kabul.
  10. Explain how Afghan fragmentation after 1545 enabled the Mughal restoration of 1555.
  11. Compare Lodi, early Mughal, and Sur institutions of nobility, military organization, and revenue.
  12. Connect the instability of 1526–1556 to the challenges Akbar inherited, without treating his later success as inevitable.

Historical Context

By the early sixteenth century the political map of northern South Asia was fragmented. The Delhi Sultanate, once the dominant power from the Punjab to Bengal, had been shaken by Timur’s invasion of 1398–99 and had never recovered its former reach (Chapters 42 and 43). In its place a number of regional states had taken form. Gujarat, Malwa, Bengal, Jaunpur before its absorption, Kashmir, and the Deccan sultanates each had its own court, army, and commercial base (Chapters 46 and 47). The Rajput kingdoms of Mewar, Marwar, and others occupied the arid and hilly country of Rajasthan and the borderlands of Malwa. Delhi and Agra, under the Afghan Lodi dynasty, formed the largest single political unit in the north, but even the Lodi state depended on the loyalty of Afghan chiefs who regarded the Sultan as first among equals.

Beyond the northwestern passes lay a different political world. The Timurid principalities of Central Asia and Khurasan had been the home of one of the most refined courts in the Persianate world, but in the decades around 1500 they were overrun by the Uzbek confederacy under Muhammad Shaybani Khan and squeezed by the rising Safavid state in Iran. Kabul and Kandahar sat between these worlds and the plains of Hindustan. Their importance lay less in their own revenues, which were modest, than in their position on the routes that carried horses, soldiers, and merchants between Central Asia, Iran, and the Indus plain. Horses from Central Asia were essential for northern Indian armies, and the route through Kabul was the principal channel by which they arrived.

Military technology was also changing. Firearms, in the form of both guns and handheld matchlocks, had been used in South Asia before 1526, in the Deccan, Gujarat, Malwa, and elsewhere, and the Portuguese were introducing new artillery on the western coast (Chapter 49). The Ottomans and Safavids were developing integrated systems of artillery, infantry firearms, and cavalry. Babur, who had observed Uzbek mounted tactics and recruited Ottoman-trained specialists, brought a combination of these methods to the plains of northern India. Historians debate how much weight to give technology in explaining the outcome, and this chapter takes up that debate directly.

The three regimes examined here relied on different sources of authority. The Lodis drew on Afghan tribal solidarity, the inheritance of Sultanate institutions, and revenue assignments. The Timurids invoked descent from Timur and from Chinggis Khan, an ideology that legitimated rulers but also encouraged every prince of the blood to claim a share. The Surs under Sher Shah combined Afghan military networks with administrative practices that drew on earlier Sultanate models, notably those of Ala al-Din Khalji and Sikandar Lodi. The decades between 1526 and 1556 were therefore a contest among competing ways of organizing military power, revenue, and kingship, and not simply a contest between a Central Asian dynasty and an Afghan one.

Babur’s Central Asian World and the Crisis of the Delhi Sultanate

Babur entered northern India as an experienced Central Asian ruler with an established political and military tradition, not as an unknown adventurer.

Section 50.1

Babur’s Timurid and Central Asian Background

Zahir al-Din Muhammad, known as Babur, was born on 14 February 1483 in the Ferghana valley, in what is now Uzbekistan. His birthplace is commonly identified as Andijan, although the historian Stephen F. Dale places it at Akhsi, another important Ferghana settlement. His father, Umar Shaikh Mirza, ruled Ferghana as a descendant of Timur (d. 1405) through Timur’s son Miran Shah. His mother, Qutluq Nigar Khanum, was the daughter of Yunus Khan, a ruler of the eastern Chagatai Mongol line, and thus descended from Chinggis Khan through his son Chagatai. Babur therefore combined the two most prestigious genealogies in Inner Asian politics. Timur himself had not been a Chinggisid and had governed through nominal khans of Chinggis’s line, so the Timurids valued marriage connections to that lineage. Babur’s own language was Chagatai Turkic, but he was also fluent in Persian, the language of administration, poetry, and high culture across the Timurid world.

The political geography in which he grew up was a patchwork of principalities. Ferghana lay along the upper Syr Darya. To its southwest were the rich cities of Transoxiana, Samarkand and Bukhara, which carried the memory of Timur’s capital. Farther south and west lay Khurasan with its cultural center at Herat, where the Timurid sultan Husayn Bayqara (r. 1469–1506) presided over a court that sheltered poets such as Jami and Navai and the painter Bihzad. Kabul and Badakhshan formed a mountain frontier to the southeast. To the north and northeast, nomadic and semi-nomadic Uzbek groups under the Shaybanid line were gathering strength.

Babur’s immediate family shows how personal and political ties overlapped in Timurid Central Asia. His paternal grandmother’s household and his maternal grandmother, Aisan Daulat Begum, a formidable woman who advised him in his early years, helped to hold his small court together after his father’s death. His maternal uncles, Sultan Mahmud Khan and Sultan Ahmad Khan, ruled the Mongol-descended lands around Tashkent and to the east, and they were at once protectors and rivals. His paternal uncles and cousins ruled Samarkand, Hisar, Badakhshan, and Kabul, so that nearly every neighbor was also a relative with claims of his own. A family quarrel in this setting was a political crisis, and a political crisis was usually a family quarrel.

Babur’s world was also divided by culture and way of life. The Chagatai Turks of the settled cities and the nomadic, pastoral Mongol (or “Mughul”) groups of the steppe frontier shared a language family and some institutions, but they looked at one another with suspicion. Babur, a city-raised Timurid prince, spoke with a certain disdain of the Mongol soldiers who followed him, though he depended on them. The religious landscape also mattered. In Ferghana and Samarkand the influence of the Naqshbandi Sufi order, associated with Khwaja Ubaydullah Ahrar (d. 1490), shaped the piety of rulers and townspeople, and Babur later translated a Sufi treatise by Ahrar into Turkic verse. This mix of dynastic ambition, nomadic military power, urban culture, and Sufi devotion was the background to the habits of rule that Babur later carried to India.

The Timurid system of succession shaped Babur’s career. Authority was understood as the collective property of the ruling house, and any prince with an army and a following could claim a throne. This produced constant competition among cousins, uncles, and brothers, a pattern that would later reappear in Hindustan among Babur’s own sons. It also made Babur’s early life one of recurrent flight and recovery, since a prince’s territory depended on the loyalty of begs and soldiers who could shift allegiance when circumstances changed.

Historical Vocabulary
Timurid, Chinggisid, Chagatai, and "Mughal"

Timurid refers to descendants of Timur (Tamerlane) and to the courts and culture they patronized in Central Asia and Iran. Chinggisid refers to descent from Chinggis Khan, which conferred a distinct form of legitimacy in Inner Asia. Chagatai names Chinggis’s second son, his appanage, and the Turkic literary language that developed there, in which Babur wrote his memoir. The word Mughal derives from the Persian and Arabic form of "Mongol" and was widely used by outsiders for Babur’s dynasty. The dynasty itself usually stressed its Timurid identity and sometimes took offense at the Mongol label. This textbook uses "Mughal" for the dynasty because it is the standard historical term, while recognizing that it was not the family’s preferred self-description and that Babur himself distinguished sharply between Turks and Mongols.

Section 50.2

Ferghana, Samarkand, and the Struggle against the Uzbeks

Umar Shaikh Mirza died in 1494 in an accident at Akhsi, and Babur, aged eleven, succeeded him in Ferghana. His territory was immediately threatened by relatives who wanted a share of it. Over the following years Babur made a series of attempts on Samarkand, the prize of Timurid Central Asia. He captured it in 1497 but held it for only about one hundred days, during which he lost Ferghana to rivals. He took Samarkand again in 1500 and lost it again within a short time. In 1501 he was defeated by Muhammad Shaybani Khan, who proceeded to conquer Samarkand, Bukhara, and eventually Herat (1507), establishing the Uzbek Shaybanid power across Transoxiana and Khurasan.

Shaybani’s rise ended Timurid rule in Central Asia and left Babur a dispossessed prince with a small band of followers. Babur’s sister Khanzada Begum was taken into Shaybani’s household in the aftermath of the defeat, an episode the Baburnama records with evident pain. In 1510 Shaybani was defeated and killed by the Safavid ruler Shah Ismail I of Iran, and Babur seized the opening. With Safavid support he retook Samarkand in 1511 and Bukhara briefly after, but he lost the region again in 1512 when the Uzbek commander Ubaydallah Khan defeated him at Ghujduwan. Babur’s dependence on the Shi’i Safavids may also have damaged his standing among the largely Sunni populations of Transoxiana. By about 1514 his Central Asian ambitions were effectively over.

The years of failure were not wasted. Babur learned how to hold together a mobile court, how to negotiate with kinsmen and rivals, and how to read the Uzbek system of mounted warfare, including the tulughma, a flanking and encircling maneuver. His memoir records the stubbornness and the miscalculations that accompanied these years. The experience also instilled in him a conviction that a ruler needed resources, not only descent, and that a base in the mountains could be a platform for conquest in the plains.

The collapse of the Timurid order was gradual but decisive. When Sultan Husayn Bayqara died in 1506, the Herat court that had given Timurid culture its brilliance passed to his sons, who could not defend it. Muhammad Shaybani Khan, a descendant of Chinggis Khan through a different line, gathered Uzbek nomadic clans and the support of Sunni religious leaders, presenting himself as the legitimate Chinggisid ruler against dissolute and divided Timurid princes. His conquests were not mindless raiding but a deliberate effort to build a state that controlled the oases, trade, and agricultural lands of Transoxiana. For Babur, the shock was personal as well as political: the entire world in which he had been trained to rule was dissolving.

Babur’s first attempts were also hindered by unreliable followers. In 1497 the city of Samarkand came to him after a long siege, but he fell ill, his supporters at home in Andijan rebelled, and in leaving to recover Ferghana he lost both places. In 1500 he surprised Samarkand again with a small party that scaled the walls by night, but the city’s notables had not committed to him and Shaybani’s army soon appeared. After his defeat in 1501 at Sar-i Pul, Babur left Samarkand in the night, and his sister Khanzada Begum was handed over to Shaybani in marriage to end the siege. Babur then wandered for some years among the hills and the Tashkent region, supported by his Mongol uncles, until a defeat at Archian in 1503 ended his realistic hopes in Ferghana. He had been a king, an exile, and a prisoner of fortune, and he had learned that a prince without a secure revenue base could not keep an army.

The last attempt, in 1511–1512, depended on the Safavid victory over Shaybani Khan at Merv in 1510. Safavid support enabled Babur to enter Samarkand in 1511, but the Safavid commander Najm-i Sani, with Babur beside him, was defeated and killed by Uzbek forces at Ghujduwan in 1512. The connection to the Shi’i Safavids had brought troops but also unpopularity among Sunni townspeople, and the attempt ended in disaster. From this point Babur’s Central Asian career was over. It had lasted nearly twenty years and had produced no durable state, but it had trained a body of loyal officers, taught Babur the strengths and limits of Uzbek mounted warfare, and given him a style of leadership that would serve him on the plains of India.

Section 50.3

Kabul and the Construction of a New Power Base, 1504–1525

In 1504 Babur crossed the Hindu Kush with a small following and took Kabul, which had come under the control of Muqim Arghun after the death of Babur’s uncle Ulugh Beg Mirza. The Kabul region was not wealthy. Its revenues were modest, and its population included Turkic and Persian-speaking communities, Afghan tribes in the surrounding hills, and Hazara groups of Mongol descent. But it controlled important routes, and its markets served as a clearing point for caravans moving between Central Asia, Iran, and the Indus plain. Babur governed Kabul for over twenty years, and he came to regard it with great affection, as his descriptions of its gardens and fruits show.

From Kabul he built a following of soldiers drawn from Central Asian exiles, local Turkic and Afghan groups, and other recruits. In the decade after 1512 he sought to expand by raiding and by diplomacy. He contested Kandahar with the Arghun rulers, who eventually moved to Sindh, and by about 1522 Kandahar was in his hands, soon to be entrusted to his son Kamran. Dealing with the Afghan hill tribes, Babur combined raiding with marriage ties and subsidies. As early as 1505 he had crossed the Khyber and conducted a raid into the Indus frontier, and he later spoke of the territories once ruled by Timur in the Punjab as belonging by right to his house, citing Timur’s conquests of 1398–99 as the basis of that claim.

The base at Kabul did not by itself provide the resources for conquest. It supplied a core of soldiers and officers and a secure route to the Punjab, but Babur understood that a decisive campaign required the revenues and manpower of the plains. His interest in India was thus a strategic judgment as well as an ambition: the Punjab and Gangetic plains offered what the mountains could not, and the political fragmentation of the Lodi realm made the opportunity real.

Babur’s Kabul was a frontier state made up of different peoples and a few valuable resources. The city sat in a valley fed by snowmelt and was famed for its orchards, gardens, and fruit, which Babur described with great fondness. The surrounding country included the cultivated valleys of Kabul and Ghazni, the pastures of nomadic and semi-nomadic Afghan tribes, the highland communities of the Hazaras, and passes that tied the region to Badakhshan, Balkh, Kandahar, and the Indus. Revenue came from agriculture, from customs on the caravan trade in horses, textiles, slaves, and goods exchanged between Central Asia, Iran, and India, and from tribute and raids. By Babur’s own account these resources barely sufficed to feed his followers, and raids into the plains of India were part of the economy of the state.

Governing Kabul meant constant bargaining. Babur took the Timurid officers and their followings who had joined him after the fall of the Timurid courts, local Turkic and Persian-speaking notables, and Afghan chiefs who might be won over by gifts or marriages and punished by raids. Rebellions occurred, including mutinies by Mongol begs, and he met them by a mixture of clemency and force. His expeditions against the Afghan hill peoples, such as those in Bajaur and the Khyber region, were meant to secure the roads and to demonstrate that he could protect travelers and merchants. His occupation of Kandahar in 1522, after years of negotiation and siege with the Arghun rulers, gave him the second great city of the southern route and a base for his son Kamran. Together, Kabul and Kandahar formed the bridgehead from which the invasion of India could be launched.

Babur’s interest in Hindustan grew gradually. In the early years it consisted of raids to seize cattle, grain, and booty. Later he came to see the plains as the source of the revenue he lacked, and the claim derived from Timur’s conquests provided a language in which to express the ambition. An important point is that the invasion of India was not the abandonment of Central Asian ambitions for the sake of an easy substitute. It was a decision by an experienced ruler, taken after the Uzbek rise had shut the door in the north and after the Safavid and Ottoman powers had made the west impossible, that the only open direction lay to the southeast.

Section 50.4

Timurid Kingship, Political Culture, and the Baburnama

Babur’s court was a military household organized around a ruler, his family, and a body of begs, who held command over troops and were rewarded with revenue assignments and gifts. Decisions were taken in councils at which senior officers gave advice, and Babur routinely consulted them, though he did not always follow their counsel. Hospitality, feasts, poetry, and hunting were means of binding followers to the ruler. The ideal ruler was a skilled warrior, a patron of learning, and a person of cultivated taste. Babur wrote poetry in Turkic and Persian, composed a treatise on prosody, and prepared a verse treatise on Islamic law and practice.

His memoir, usually called the Baburnama (Babur’s book, called in Turkic the Vaqa’i), is the most extraordinary source for this period and one of the most remarkable autobiographies of the premodern world. It covers events from 1494 to 1529 with notable gaps, including a long interval between the early 1500s and 1519 and further breaks in the 1520s. Babur wrote in Chagatai Turkic, in a candid and observational style, describing battles, landscapes, plants, animals, and people, including judgments about their characters. His grandson Akbar had it translated into Persian in 1589 by Abd al-Rahim Khan-i Khanan, and illustrated manuscripts were produced for the court. Modern English translations include that of Annette Beveridge (1922) and that of Wheeler Thackston (published in the 1990s and 2000s).

The memoir must be read with care. It is a first-person account, but the text as transmitted was revised, and Babur wrote with an audience in mind, including his sons and the Timurid tradition of royal memoirs. He presents himself as a figure of resolution and cultural refinement. He interprets opponents through his own categories. He says little about the thousands of ordinary soldiers and cultivators whose labor sustained his campaigns. Where the Baburnama can be checked against other evidence, including Afghan chronicles, Rajput traditions, coins, and inscriptions, it often proves reliable on events but contestable on motives and numbers.

Babur’s memoir is only one of several sources for his world. Other contemporary and near-contemporary accounts help to place his career in context, including the Persian history of Khwandamir, the Habib al-siyar, composed at Herat, and the Tarikh-i Rashidi of Babur’s cousin Mirza Muhammad Haydar Dughlat, written in the 1540s. These texts are written from different vantage points, sometimes with strong biases, and where they overlap with the Baburnama they allow historians to check it. The Baburnama itself survives in a few Turkic manuscripts, none of which is an autograph, together with the Persian translation prepared for Akbar and illustrated copies produced in Mughal workshops, so that even the transmission of the text involves layers of copying and revision.

The Baburnama’s literary character also matters. Babur shaped his narrative on the model of Timurid royal memoirs, with set descriptions of regions, towns, rulers, and notable persons, but he added a personal, sometimes humorous candor that sets his work apart. He recounts failures and embarrassments as well as victories, and he draws character sketches of kinsmen and opponents that read as fair-minded but are also acts of judgment. Such passages, whatever their literary merit, serve a political purpose: they explain why his own conduct was right and why others’ was not. Where the Baburnama describes numbers of soldiers, the size of enemy armies, or the casualties of battles, historians are well advised to treat the figures as impressions rather than counts.

Section 50.5

The Lodi Sultanate and the Political Crisis of Northern India

The Lodi dynasty had come to power in Delhi under Bahlul Lodi in 1451 and continued under his son Sikandar (r. 1489–1517), who extended Delhi’s authority east toward Jaunpur and Bihar and founded the city of Agra in 1504 as a military center. These Afghan rulers governed through chiefs who commanded their own followings and expected to share in decisions. The relationship between sultan and nobles was the central political issue of the dynasty, as Chapter 43 explained. Sikandar had maintained reasonable control through personal prestige. His son Ibrahim, who succeeded in 1517, tried to assert greater royal authority by humbling powerful nobles and, according to hostile accounts, by arresting or eliminating some of them.

These efforts alienated a number of Afghan leaders. Ibrahim’s brother Jalal Khan had been given a separate territorial base in Jaunpur, and the effort to remove him produced a rebellion. Dilawar Khan, a son of Daulat Khan Lodi, the governor of the Punjab, was reportedly mistreated at court, and Daulat Khan came to fear for his own position. Alam Khan Lodi, an uncle of Ibrahim and a rival claimant, also looked beyond the frontier for support. The Punjab, which lay on the main invasion route, was thus governed by a man who had reason to distrust the Sultan.

Other powers added to the pressure. Rana Sanga of Mewar had expanded across eastern Rajasthan and into Malwa, and in clashes with Lodi forces he had gained territory and confidence. Bengal, under the Husain Shahi dynasty, controlled the lower Ganges, while Gujarat and Malwa pursued their own ambitions. In this setting, Babur’s decision to invade did not rest simply on opportunism. It reflected a calculation that the Lodi state was weaker than its size suggested, that its leaders could be divided, and that the Punjab, already under raid from Kabul, could be taken with local assistance. The conditions that made invasion possible were created by Afghan, Rajput, and regional politics as much as by Babur’s own initiative.

The structure of Lodi politics rested on relations among Afghan clans and their leaders. The Lodi, Lohani, Sur, Farmuli, Sarwani, Niyazi, and other groups provided soldiers, officers, and governors, and each group had its own leaders, rivalries, and expectations. Since the middle of the fifteenth century Afghan chiefs had been rewarded with grants of revenue and with governorships over regions such as Bihar, Jaunpur, Kalpi, and the Punjab. They accepted the Sultan’s overlordship but expected consultation and a share in the spoils. Sikandar Lodi had managed this system through personal prestige and a record of success. His son faced a different problem: he inherited a state that had already stretched from the Punjab to Bihar but had no matching institutions for bringing its great nobles to heel.

Ibrahim’s reign shows the difficulty. At his accession in 1517 the kingdom was divided, with his brother Jalal Khan ruling at Jaunpur, a split that Ibrahim soon reversed by force, having Jalal Khan killed. The powerful noble Azam Humayun Sarwani rebelled, and was defeated, and the harsh treatment of such leaders reportedly deepened suspicion. Darya Khan Lohani had made himself nearly independent in Bihar, and after his death his son Bahar Khan took the title Sultan Muhammad and refused obedience. In Gwalior, Ibrahim captured the Tomar fortress in 1518 and made its ruler Vikramajit his vassal. The Sultan also suffered defeats against Rana Sanga in the years around 1517–1519, losing prestige and territory in eastern Rajasthan and the Malwa borderland. The sources that describe these events were written under later dynasties, and some, such as the Afghan chronicles of Akbar’s time, are sympathetic to the nobles and critical of Ibrahim, so the picture of a tyrant must be treated with some caution.

Beyond Afghan politics there was an economic dimension. The Delhi region and the Doab produced the grain and revenue that fed the court, while the Punjab supplied cavalry horses and the road to Kabul. Control of the road mattered because it was the route by which horses came into India, and a governor of the Punjab who chose to deal with an outside power could threaten the Sultanate at its source of military supply. Daulat Khan Lodi, who had governed the Punjab for years, understood this and weighed his options, which is why his messages to Kabul were more than treason; they were an exercise in the politics of the frontier. The conditions for invasion thus lay in the relationship between the Sultan and a frontier governor who held the key to the northwest.

Division II

The Conquest of Northern India, 1519–1527

Conquest was a combination of military organization, competing political ambitions, and mobilization of resources; neither Panipat nor Khanwa was inevitable.

Section 50.6

The Punjab Campaigns and the Road to Delhi

Babur’s incursions into the Punjab began in earnest in 1519 with an attack on Bajaur, where he used matchlock firearms against a fortified position. The ruthlessness of the assault was meant to intimidate other frontier communities. He then moved into Bhera, receiving submissions and extracting supplies, but turned back when events in Kabul required his presence. A second expedition in 1520 reached Sialkot and the region of Saidpur. Saidpur (later Eminabad) is also associated with the Sikh tradition, since verses attributed to Guru Nanak are commonly read as a response to the violence that accompanied Babur’s capture of the town (see Chapter 48 for the early Sikh Panth).

The turning point came in 1524, when Daulat Khan Lodi and Alam Khan sent messages inviting Babur to intervene against Ibrahim. Babur advanced to Lahore and seized it, but the Lodi nobles soon discovered that he intended to rule the Punjab, not to serve as their instrument. Daulat Khan turned against him, and Alam Khan’s own attempt to take Delhi with Babur’s support failed. These setbacks showed that the Lodi opposition was divided and that the invasion of Hindustan could not rely on easy alliances.

In November 1525 Babur set out from Kabul on his fifth expedition, with Humayun joining him from Badakhshan. Daulat Khan Lodi submitted, and the Mughal army advanced down the Punjab toward Delhi. The invasion proceeded in the cooler season, and Babur’s sources provided information on Ibrahim’s preparations and numbers. In April 1526 the two armies converged near the old town of Panipat, a site that has repeatedly decided the political fate of northern India.

The sequence of Babur’s incursions shows a patient process of testing and learning. The attack on Bajaur in early 1519 served several purposes: it secured the northern approaches to the Khyber, demonstrated the new weapons, and sent a message to the Afghan tribes of the region. The campaign into Bhera, between the Indus and Jhelum, brought him to an area where the Lodi governors had limited control, and he left Afghan officers to maintain order. After these expeditions Babur sent a message to Ibrahim Lodi demanding the territories that he claimed as Timur’s heritage, a request that was never answered because Daulat Khan detained the envoy. He later claimed that the Punjab belonged to him by right of conquest and inheritance, a claim that carried weight in his own political culture.

The invitation of 1524 reflected the conflicting interests of the Lodi opposition. Daulat Khan sent his son Dilawar Khan to Kabul with a message offering submission, while Alam Khan Lodi, the Sultan’s uncle who had fled to Kabul, came with a promise to take the throne with Babur’s help. The two did not trust each other, and when Babur’s army entered the Punjab, Daulat Khan Lodi first resisted and then submitted. Lahore was taken and plundered, and Babur kept the Punjab for himself, passing a few districts to Alam Khan. Alam Khan’s attempt to seize Delhi, supported by a Mughal force, failed ignominiously, and Babur returned to Kabul to prepare for a larger effort. These events taught Babur that his Lodi allies were self-interested and that a successful invasion would need to be undertaken in his own name.

In the last campaign, Babur crossed the Indus in late 1525 and moved through Sialkot and the Punjab plains. Humayun, with the army of Badakhshan, joined him. Daulat Khan, who had again turned against Babur, was forced to surrender. At Ambala, Humayun’s vanguard defeated a Lodi advance force under Hamid Khan, a first success that the Baburnama notes with satisfaction. Babur then advanced to the Yamuna and followed it south to Panipat, where he began to prepare his position. He sent out scouts and spies, gathered information about the Lodi army’s size and morale, and made his choice of ground. The invasion that ended at Panipat was thus the culmination of seven years of preparation, intelligence-gathering, and political maneuver.

Section 50.7

The First Battle of Panipat, 1526

Babur reports that Ibrahim Lodi commanded about 100,000 men and a thousand elephants, while his own force numbered perhaps 12,000. Modern historians doubt the larger figures, since medieval authors regularly inflated enemy forces to magnify victories, but there is little doubt that the Lodi army was substantially larger. Ibrahim’s force included Afghan cavalry, infantry, camp followers, and war elephants, and its coherence was weakened by uncertain loyalties among his nobles.

The battle was brief. Babur’s flanking detachments, using the tulughma, swung around the Lodi wings and attacked from the rear, while firearms and archery contributed to the disruption of the advance. Some accounts describe gunfire frightening the war elephants and causing them to trample their own troops, but the extent of such panic is not securely established. Humayun commanded the Mughal right in his first major battle. By midday the Lodi army was encircled and broken. Ibrahim Lodi was killed in the fighting, as was Raja Vikramajit of Gwalior, who had fought on the Lodi side. Babur’s claim that many thousands of Afghans died is plausible in general terms but not verifiable in numbers.

Babur sent Humayun to Agra and another detachment to Delhi to secure the capitals and their treasuries, and the Friday prayer sermon (khutba) was read in his name. This asserted sovereignty but did not end the war. Many Afghan chiefs remained at large with their followings, and those who had stood aside from Ibrahim’s cause were not necessarily Babur’s allies. Panipat removed a Sultan; it did not create a state.

The battlefield at Panipat offered natural advantages for a smaller army. The Mughal right rested on the edge of the town, where houses and gardens made a cavalry attack from that direction difficult. The left was protected by a ditch and by felled trees. The center consisted of a line of roughly seven hundred carts, according to Babur’s account, joined together by ropes of rawhide, with gaps between them closed by portable wooden shields or screens behind which matchlockmen could fire. At intervals, wider openings allowed a few hundred horsemen to ride out in a charge and withdraw. The whole arrangement was a flexible fortress that could resist a cavalry assault while permitting a controlled counterattack, and it reflected Ottoman field practice as Ustad Ali Quli and Mustafa adapted it.

Ibrahim’s approach and Babur’s decision-making followed a recognizable pattern. For about a week the two armies faced each other at some distance, and Babur used the time to improve his preparations and to try to lure the Sultan’s army into a premature advance. A night attack on the Lodi camp failed to cause much damage but demonstrated that the Lodi forces were hesitant and loosely controlled. When Ibrahim finally advanced, he found the Mughal position more compact than expected. His troops, in the process of deploying, pressed against one another, and the narrow front prevented the large army from using its numbers effectively. As the Lodi vanguard approached, it recoiled from the fire of the guns and the matchlocks, and the army’s own mass of men and animals impeded its maneuver.

Section 50.8

Artillery, Mobility, and the Military Explanation of Babur’s Victory

Babur’s victory at Panipat is often attributed to gunpowder. The firearms were indeed important. The matchlock, a handheld gun fired by a slow-burning match, gave infantry a weapon that could disrupt cavalry and intimidate elephants, and field artillery supplied noise and shock. But the battle was won by the coordination of weapons with other elements. The carts and breastworks protected the gunners and provided a fixed center. The tulughma gave the Mughals a mounted force capable of enveloping the enemy. Discipline and unified command enabled Babur’s officers to execute a plan on the battlefield, while the Lodi army suffered from divided command and from a crowding of its own men.

Firearms were not new to India. Gujarat, Malwa, the Deccan sultanates, and Vijayanagara had used cannon in sieges and field battles, and chronicles record the use of guns in several earlier campaigns. What was distinctive about Babur’s army was not simply possession of firearms but a trained team of specialists, field tactics integrating guns with cavalry, and a tradition of mobile mounted archery that Afghan and Indian armies often lacked. His own cavalry, mostly horse archers, remained central; the guns made it possible to hold a position until the flanks closed.

A closer look at the weapons clarifies what firearms could and could not do. The matchlock of the early sixteenth century was heavy and slow to load, and its fire was inaccurate, so its value lay in massed volleys behind protection rather than in individual aimed fire. The artillery of the period was equally limited. Field guns fired at a very slow rate, were difficult to move, and could be fatally exposed if the enemy reached them before they were ready. For this reason Ali Quli’s guns were placed behind the carts and defended by matchlockmen and infantry, not used as mobile batteries. They could disrupt an advancing formation through noise, casualties, and shock; the precise battlefield effect on cavalry and elephants remains debated.

The wider military setting also mattered. In 1514 the Ottomans had defeated the Safavids at Chaldiran with the aid of artillery and disciplined infantry armed with firearms, a victory that impressed observers across the Islamic world and prompted rulers to recruit gunners from Ottoman lands. Ustad Ali Quli and Mustafa came from this tradition, which combined wagon lines, trenches, and firepower with the use of cavalry. Babur’s contribution was to adapt it to the mixed Central Asian and Afghan forces he commanded, and to synchronize it with the Uzbek-style flanking maneuver that his cavalry had long practiced. The result was an army whose parts supported each other, in contrast to an army of forces which acted without a unified plan.

Indian armies of the early sixteenth century were not primitive. They combined cavalry, infantry, elephants, and, in many cases, artillery in sieges, and some regional rulers employed European or Ottoman gunners. Their weaknesses lay in command and cohesion: tribal and clan contingents loyal to individual chiefs, large camp followings, and a tendency to rely on the weight of numbers. Panipat and Khanwa exposed these weaknesses, and later rulers, including Sher Shah, adapted by recruiting artillery specialists and building forts designed for gunpowder warfare. Thus the battles of 1526 and 1527 marked a stage in a longer process of military adaptation across the region, not a sudden revolution produced by a single invention.

Finally, the limits of gunpowder should be recognized. Weather, especially rain, could neutralize matchlocks and cannon, as the Mughals would later discover. Gunners needed carts, draught animals, and secure supply of powder and shot. Guns dominated sieges only when the attackers had time and resources, and Babur’s successes at Chanderi and elsewhere depended on those conditions. At Chausa in 1539, fighting in the rainy season, and at Kannauj in 1540, Humayun’s superior weapons did not prevent defeat. Technology was valuable when combined with sound command, supply, and morale, and the early Mughals’ later difficulties show how quickly its advantage could be lost.

Historical Debate

Did Gunpowder Technology Explain Babur’s Victory?

The question: Was Babur’s conquest of northern India primarily the result of firearms technology, or of tactics, organization, and the political weakness of his opponents?

Interpretation A: Technological explanation: Some historians, drawing on the "gunpowder empires" thesis developed by scholars such as Marshall Hodgson and William McNeill, argued that the Ottomans, Safavids, and Mughals owed their rise to the effective use of gunpowder weapons, which allowed centralized states to overcome older military elites. On this view Babur’s artillery and matchlocks gave him decisive superiority over Afghan cavalry armies.
Interpretation B: Organizational and political explanation: Other historians, including Iqtidar Alam Khan, have emphasized that firearms were already in use in India and that Babur’s success depended on combined-arms tactics, effective leadership, and the disunity of his enemies. Gunpowder was a contributing factor, but not an automatic guarantee of victory, as shown by the very different outcomes of later campaigns in which Mughal guns did not prevent defeat.

Evidence:

The Baburnama describes the use of guns and carts at Panipat and Khanwa. Indian and Persian chronicles document earlier firearms in Gujarat, Malwa, and the Deccan. The numbers, deployment, and effect of the weapons at specific battles are only partly recoverable from sources that were written by the winning side.

Why it matters:

If technology was decisive, the early Mughal state looks like a product of a military revolution. If tactics and politics mattered more, the rise of the dynasty appears contingent on specific circumstances and on the quality of leadership, and the failures of Humayun appear less surprising.

Section 50.9

Delhi, Agra, and the Limits of the Initial Conquest

The capture of Delhi and Agra gave Babur their treasuries and the symbolic authority of the Lodi capital. He distributed substantial gifts to his begs, to the members of his family, and to people in Kabul, where he reportedly sent presents to every inhabitant of the region. The treasure helped him reward his followers, but the money was quickly spent. The revenue system of the conquered territories had to be reconstructed, since local officials, including Afghan chiefs and zamindars (local landholders), had not necessarily accepted his rule and often retained control of collection.

His soldiers presented a further problem. Many of them had come from the cool uplands of Central Asia and Kabul, and they found the heat of the northern plains oppressive. They also regarded the campaign as a successful raid whose profits should be taken home. Babur gathered his officers and told them that the conquest could not be abandoned after so much effort and that a large and wealthy country was theirs for the taking. Some chose to leave, and he let them go. The decision to remain in Hindustan, rather than withdrawing to Kabul with the spoils, was a choice with major consequences.

Babur’s attitudes toward the land were mixed. He described its climate, gardens, and public buildings with some disappointment and found little to admire in local social customs, but he appreciated its wealth and the variety of its craftspeople. The distinction between a battlefield victory and effective government was central. Local communities, Afghan chiefs, and Rajput rulers had not been conquered simply because the Sultan was dead. Babur needed to control the Doab, the roads, the forts, and the revenue-collecting intermediaries, and he lacked an administrative apparatus of his own to do so.

Agra and Delhi fell into Mughal hands without a siege, but the process of taking control was slow. Babur sent Humayun to Agra, where the family of the late Raja Vikramajit of Gwalior presented a large store of jewels and valuables, among them a famous diamond that tradition associates with the later history of South Asian gems; the identification is uncertain, but the episode illustrates the scale of the treasure that came into Mughal hands. Babur distributed this wealth among Humayun, his officers, his family, and followers in Kabul, Badakhshan, Samarkand, and elsewhere, partly as a reward and partly as an assertion of generosity that was a core duty of a Timurid ruler. The gifts were extensive enough to deplete the treasury and leave the government short of ready money within a few months.

Beyond the capitals, the Afghan landscape of Hindustan remained intact. Nizam Khan of Bayana, Hasan Khan Mewati in Mewat, the Afghan chiefs of the Doab and of Kannauj, and the large Afghan following in the east all had to be dealt with. Babur sent detachments to Jaunpur, Kalpi, Dholpur, and other towns, and offered terms to chiefs who submitted. Some came in and received assignments, others waited to see how the new ruler fared, and a few maintained a hostile stance. The conquest was therefore an accumulation of local settlements, each of which depended on a bargain between the new regime and a local power-holder. The difference between submission and loyalty was large, and Babur knew it.

The environment also played a role. The summer of 1526 was extremely hot, and the Mughal soldiers suffered from heat and illness. Babur, who was used to cool air, running water, and fruit, found Agra unpleasant and soon began to build gardens with wells and water channels on the Yamuna, an effort to create a livable space and to assert a sense of order. The monsoon brought relief and flooding, and the pattern of Indian seasons, with the best campaigning weather in the cooler months, shaped the timing of his operations. A conquest that began in April thus faced its first test in the hottest months, before it had any administrative structure to support it.

Section 50.10

Rana Sanga, Mewar, and the Rajput Confederacy

Rana Sanga (Sangram Singh) had ruled Mewar since about 1508 and had expanded its power across eastern Rajasthan and into the Malwa borderlands. In clashes with Lodi forces he had gained a reputation for martial skill and political acumen, and by the 1520s he was arguably the most powerful ruler in the region west and south of Agra. Rajput chiefs from many parts of Rajasthan acknowledged his leadership, though such alliances were often personal and conditional. His ambitions included a role in the politics of Delhi, which the collapse of Lodi authority made newly plausible.

The Baburnama claims that Sanga had sent an envoy to Kabul offering to attack Agra while Babur moved against Delhi. Whether this offer was made, and what Sanga meant by it, is debated. Sanga may have believed that Babur would behave like Timur, who had raided and withdrawn, rather than remain as a ruler of Hindustan. Once it became clear that Babur intended to stay, Sanga’s calculation changed, and the two became rivals for the inheritance of the Lodi position.

The coalition that formed against Babur was not limited to Rajputs. Hasan Khan Mewati, the Muslim ruler of Mewat, joined Sanga, as did Mahmud Lodi, a brother of Ibrahim whom the Afghan chiefs recognized as Sultan. Afghan leaders in the Doab and the east, with their own scores to settle, also considered joining. The result was a broad opposition that included Hindu and Muslim leaders united by a shared interest in preventing the consolidation of a new Central Asian regime. The conflict that followed is therefore better understood as a contest for political predominance in northern India than as a religious war.

Rana Sanga’s rise rested on military success and shrewd politics. He had won the throne of Mewar after a contested succession, and in his struggles he lost an eye and suffered other wounds, which later tradition remembers as marks of his courage. He fought and defeated the Sultans of Gujarat and Malwa, capturing Mahmud II of Malwa at Gagron in 1519, and then released him, a mark of the political confidence of a ruler who did not need to destroy his rival to profit by defeating him. Marriage alliances with other Rajput houses and the loyalty of chiefs from Marwar, Amber, and other regions gave him a following that extended beyond Mewar. His forts, above all Chittor, were central to a political order built on lineage, kinship, and personal service.

This history shows why Sanga should not be seen merely as a “Hindu” champion against a “Muslim” invader. His opponents in the 1510s were Muslim sultans, but he also negotiated with them and sheltered refugees from their courts. Rajput rulers regularly served Sultans and Mughals as vassals, allies, and officers, and they entered into conflict with other Rajput houses, for reasons of land, honor, and succession. The Rajput polities of this period, discussed in Chapter 35, were bound by shared warrior traditions, but their political interests were not uniform. Sanga’s success consisted in making a wide coalition of such interests behind a single leader, a feat that had no precedent in recent memory.

The alliance with Afghan leaders was therefore logical. Hasan Khan Mewati, who ruled the territory south of Delhi, had long been a semi-independent chief and saw Babur as a threat to his position. Mahmud Lodi offered the prestige of a Lodi claimant, and a few other Afghan officers joined, expecting to recover lost status. Together these forces constituted a plausible challenge. Their strength was in numbers and courage, their weakness in coordination, and in the absence of any plan for a unified government if they won. The fall of Bayana to Sanga in early 1527 and the defeat of a Mughal vanguard sent to meet him made the coalition seem formidable and shaped the crisis that preceded Khanwa.

Section 50.11

The Battle of Khanwa, 1527

Early in 1527 Babur’s position was precarious. Mughal outposts around Bayana fell to Sanga’s advance, and a vanguard was defeated. The troops, already unsettled by the heat and by stories of Rajput strength, were demoralized, and reports of unfavorable omens circulated in camp. Babur responded with a series of striking moves. He made a public vow to abstain from wine, had his gold and silver drinking vessels broken and distributed to the poor, and canceled the tamgha, a commercial tax on Muslims. He then addressed his officers, reminding them that death was certain and that a fight to the finish was preferable to disgrace, and they swore on the Qur’an to stand and fight.

The battle took place on 16 March 1527 near Khanwa, about sixty kilometers west of Agra. Babur once more arranged his army behind carts, chains, and trenches, with guns and matchlocks in the center and cavalry on the flanks. Sanga’s charge, delivered by heavy cavalry with great courage, was initially hard to contain, and the fighting continued for several hours. As at Panipat, flanking units encircled the Rajput army, and its coordination broke down. Sanga was wounded and carried from the field. Hasan Khan Mewati was killed. Babur took the title of ghazi, a warrior for the faith, and ordered a tower of skulls to commemorate the victory, a Central Asian practice meant to terrify opponents.

The political significance was substantial. Sanga’s defeat ended the prospect of a Rajput-led successor to the Lodis, and Sanga himself died in 1528 while reportedly preparing a new campaign. The battle did not destroy Rajput power, but it forced the Rajput states to reckon with a new northern power and weakened Mewar’s claim to wider leadership. For Babur it removed the most serious obstacle to establishing his control over the Doab and Agra region.

The Mughal camp at Sikri, west of Agra, was the scene of preparation. Babur ordered the construction of earthworks, trenches, and a line of carts chained together, and he waited for the enemy to come to him. He also brought up reinforcements, including contingents from the Doab and, at the last moment, a body of Afghan troops who had changed sides. The astrologer Muhammad Sharif, who had been with Babur for some years, predicted ill fortune, and many in the camp were disheartened. The reported change in Babur’s behavior, the renunciation of wine and the breaking of the cups, was thus part of an effort to restore morale and to present the coming battle as a trial of faith. Such public gestures were meant for an audience of soldiers who needed to believe in the commander’s cause.

The sequence of the battle was disciplined but uncertain. Rajput cavalry attacked the Mughal right and left with great force, and the fighting at times threatened the line. Babur held his reserve until the critical moment, then ordered his flanking units to wheel against the enemy while the guns and matchlocks in the center continued to fire. Tradition among later Rajput chroniclers says that one of Sanga’s allies, Silhadi of Raisen, deserted at a crucial point, an account that Persian sources do not confirm and that might reflect an attempt to explain a defeat that had been unexpected. The Rajput army, with its leader wounded and carried from the field, could not regroup. The pursuit continued for some distance, and the Mughal victory was complete.

In the weeks after the battle, Babur’s forces took advantage of the rout, entering Mewat and capturing Alwar, and the Baburnama emphasizes the death of Hasan Khan and the end of the Mewati ruling house’s independence. The Mughals also gained access to large numbers of horses, elephants, and prisoners. Historians have debated how to interpret Khanwa in the broader history of northern India: as the end of Rajput hopes of imperial leadership, as the moment when Babur’s conquest became permanent, or as one stage in a longer process. Mewar was weakened but not destroyed, and in later generations its rulers would resist Mughal expansion, as discussed in Chapter 51. The memory of the battle was later reshaped in Rajput, Persian, and modern nationalist narratives for different purposes, which is why the contemporary Baburnama and the later traditions must be read in tandem.

Section 50.12

Chanderi, 1528, and the Consolidation of Rajput Frontiers

After Khanwa, Babur turned to the Rajput and Afghan holdouts of the central and southern frontier. In January 1528 he besieged Chanderi, a fortress in the Malwa borderlands held by Medini Rai, a Rajput chief who had risen to prominence as a minister in the Malwa sultanate and had joined Rana Sanga’s coalition. Babur offered him another territory in exchange for the fort, an offer Medini Rai refused. The assault, supported by artillery and by an escalade of the walls, took the fort in a single day.

According to the Baburnama, the defenders did not surrender. The Rajput men performed jauhar, the ritual in which, when defeat appeared certain, women and children were killed or immolated to prevent capture, and the men then rode out to die in the fight. The practice reflected a martial code of honor among Rajput warrior groups and its memory endured in later traditions. Chanderi’s fall demonstrated the effect of artillery against even strongly held positions, and it frightened other garrisons. It did not, however, bring Malwa under stable Mughal control.

Chanderi also illustrates a persistent pattern. Babur could take fortresses but had few means of administering the territories behind them. He assigned Chanderi to a local figure, as a kind of subordinate ally, rather than building a provincial government. Over the following years the Rajput states of Rajasthan, though weakened, were neither integrated nor eliminated. They would be dealt with on very different terms by Akbar, whose Rajput policy is examined in Chapter 51.

Chanderi’s position explains its importance. Perched on a hill near the passes between Malwa and Bundelkhand, the fortress guarded a route to central India and the Deccan. Medini Rai had made himself indispensable at the court of Malwa, where he served as minister to Sultan Mahmud II against the wishes of a faction of Muslim nobles, and he gained control of Chanderi and nearby districts. Under him the town became a refuge for those who opposed Babur and a base for continuing Rajput influence in Malwa, and he sent a contingent to fight with Sanga at Khanwa. Babur’s campaign was therefore an extension of the war that had begun on the Doab.

The march to Chanderi in early 1528 covered difficult country. Babur’s army moved with its artillery through ravines and scrub, and the assault was prepared with the care that the Mughal style of siege required. The defenders, outnumbered, were reportedly overwhelmed when the Mughal troops scaled the walls at one point. The Baburnama describes the fort’s fall as swift, which reflects the author’s pride and the real effect of the siege guns. The jauhar, as Babur reports it, deprived the conquerors of captives. Historians of Rajput culture caution that accounts of jauhar come largely from the conquerors’ narratives or from later bardic traditions, and that although the practice is attested in several contexts, its details are often shaped by memory and by ideas of honor.

After the capture, Babur did not plan to hold Chanderi directly. He assigned it to a claimant from Malwa’s former ruling line, a decision that allowed him to avoid the expense of a permanent garrison and to enlist a local figure as a client. The arrangement was typical of his method and revealed its weakness: the larger the area he conquered, the more he depended on clients whose loyalty was uncertain. In the following decades Chanderi and Malwa were disputed among Mughal, Afghan, Gujarat, and Rajput interests, and the territory never became a stable provincial base for Babur himself.

Section 50.13

Ghaghra, 1529, and the Continuing Afghan Challenge

The eastern frontier proved harder to resolve. After Panipat and Khanwa, Afghan leaders in the Doab and Bihar had submitted conditionally, and others never submitted at all. Mahmud Lodi, the brother of Ibrahim, was proclaimed Sultan by Afghan chiefs in Bihar, where regional magnates retained substantial forces. Babur led a campaign east in 1528–1529, moving through Awadh and the Gangetic plain, relying on river routes and heavy artillery.

The decisive encounter took place on 6 May 1529 near the confluence of the Ghaghra and the Ganges. Babur’s artillery covered the crossing of the river against an Afghan force that had positioned itself to oppose him. The battle was a mixed land and river engagement in which Mughal firepower and flanking moves again prevailed. The Sultan of Bengal, Nusrat Shah, who had initially stood on the opposite bank, avoided a further confrontation and made terms with Babur. Mahmud Lodi fled and later died in 1532.

The victory broke the cohesion of the eastern Afghan opposition but did not eliminate it. Powerful Afghan leaders remained in Bihar, and some, such as Sher Khan, a rising figure whose career is traced in Division V, studied Mughal methods while serving or observing them. Later Afghan tradition states that Sher Khan spent time in Babur’s camp and concluded that Mughal power was less formidable than it looked. This anecdote comes from a retrospective source and cannot be taken as established, but it points to the dangerous afterlife of an incomplete conquest.

The campaign in the east was not a single expedition but a series of moves over some eighteen months. Babur first sent forces to Awadh, where Afghan leaders had reasserted themselves, and in 1528 his officer Mir Baqi was established at Awadh, a post that would later become famous for reasons treated in Chapter 99. Babur’s own march in 1529 followed the Ganges downstream, using boats to move supplies and artillery. The Afghan chiefs of Bihar, notably Biban and Bayazid, had no common strategy, and they fell back before the Mughals. Mahmud Lodi, proclaimed Sultan by some of them, provided a rallying point but lacked the resources to unify them.

The battle at the confluence of the Ghaghra and the Ganges in May 1529 was unusual because it involved forcing a river crossing against opposition. Babur’s gunners shelled the enemy bank, while troops crossed in boats and by fords upstream, and the Afghans, outflanked and under fire, withdrew. A short pursuit followed. The Bengal ruler Nusrat Shah, whose troops were close to the field, concluded a treaty by which he agreed not to harbor Babur’s enemies, an arrangement that reduced the threat from Bengal for the moment. The victory thus served as much to confirm Mughal supremacy on the Ganges as to destroy a specific army.

The aftermath showed the persistence of Afghan power. Afghan chiefs in Bihar who submitted received revenue assignments in return for service, a pattern that left their military resources intact. Babur recognized the arrangement as a compromise and returned to Agra with his health failing. The Bihar Afghans, who had in effect been absorbed into the Mughal system on loose terms, would later flock to Sher Khan when he rebelled against Mughal authority, and the political geography of the eastern Gangetic plain remained one of Afghan local power under a Mughal overlord. That Babur’s victory at Ghaghra did not remove this foundation of Afghan strength is the central reason for the later events narrated in Division IV.

Section 50.14

Governing Babur’s Conquests: Nobles, Military Service, and Provincial Authority

Babur’s government in Hindustan was an extension of his military household. He rewarded followers with grants of revenue, in the form of districts, parganas, and local rights, rather than salaries, and expected his commanders to maintain troops from the assigned income. His principal officers included the minister Nizam al-Din Ali Khalifa (Mir Khalifa), the Timurid cousin Mirza Muhammad Zaman, and other begs of Central Asian origin, together with Afghan and Indian officers who had joined the Mughal side. The family was distributed across the new territories and across Kabul, Kandahar, and Badakhshan, which Babur kept as the home base.

Administration relied on existing local structures. Revenue collection continued through hereditary and local intermediaries, such as qanungos (record-keeping officials of a pargana) and village headmen, who were not easily replaced. Some districts were farmed out to officers who collected what they could. The new rulers' limited knowledge of local conditions made them dependent on these intermediaries, and the regime lacked the surveys and fiscal records that would later characterize Akbar’s government.

Financial strain was constant. Armies had to be paid and rewarded, the treasuries captured in 1526 were soon depleted, and new revenues were difficult to extract from areas not yet pacified. Babur’s own letters and the Baburnama refer to the problem of keeping his soldiers in the field. His nobles were loyal in the military sense, but their loyalty was tied to opportunities for rewards, and the system gave them considerable autonomy. The limits of centralized governance were thus built into the structure of the conquest.

The territorial structure of Babur’s holdings is a useful way to see how the regime worked. In the northwest, Kabul, Ghazni, Kandahar, and parts of Badakhshan stayed attached to the family but were governed by princes and officers with their own resources. In the Punjab, Mughal officers held Lahore, Sialkot, and other towns, with large areas still under Afghan or local chiefs. In the Doab and around Delhi and Agra, the Mughals held the core districts, and in places such as Sambhal, Bayana, Jaunpur, Kalpi, and Gwalior they placed garrisons under trusted commanders. East of Jaunpur and in Bihar, Babur’s authority was mostly nominal. This was less a continuous administrative territory than a network of strongpoints joined by roads and communications along the great rivers.

Revenue arrangements are only partly known. Babur had his officers compile lists of districts with reported revenues, which are preserved in the Baburnama and which reproduce, in part, the Lodi accounts. These lists were not a record of what the Mughals collected but an estimate of what might be collected, and the distance between the two was large in years of conquest and disruption. Much of the income came from the assignments given to officers, who collected what they could and used it to support troops and households. The result was a fiscal system in which the crown depended on the success of its commanders in extracting revenue, with few controls beyond the ruler’s personal oversight.

The social composition of the ruling group also shaped government. Central Asian begs and their retinues, Afghan officers who had joined the Mughals, Indian Muslim officers, and some Hindu officials and clerks formed an elite that was not yet unified by a common institutional framework. Persian was the language of administration, and Hindu Khatri and Kayastha clerks, who had served Sultanate governments, continued to keep accounts, but the sources say little about them. Local religious leaders, including Sufi shaikhs and ulama who held revenue-free grants, had to be conciliated, and Babur confirmed some of their holdings. The regime thus rested on a series of accommodations, each of which worked only so long as the military situation remained favorable.

Section 50.15

Babur’s Court, Gardens, Observations, and Cultural Legacy

Babur brought with him a taste for formal gardens, which he considered an essential feature of civilized life. In Agra he laid out a garden on the bank of the Yamuna, known as the Ram Bagh or Aram Bagh, with watercourses, pavilions, and planted beds on the charbagh (four-part garden) model that would become a signature of later Mughal landscapes. He also ordered wells, stepwells, and baths, and he commissioned buildings from stonecutters and masons he recruited from Hindustan. Mosques are associated with his reign at Panipat, Sambhal, and Ayodhya, the last of which later became the subject of intense controversy and is treated in Chapter 99.

His memoir reveals an acute observer of nature. He describes Indian animals, plants, and seasons, comparing them to those of Kabul and Central Asia. He recorded the rhinoceros, the monsoon rains, and the mango, which he praised, and he measured distances and listed districts with their reported revenues. These descriptions are of great historical value for environmental and economic history, though they reflect the interests of a ruler who was recording useful information as well as satisfying curiosity.

The distinction between Babur’s literary reputation and his institutional achievement is important. His memoir secured his place among the great writers of the Turkic world, and his gardens set a pattern for Mughal elite taste. But the empire he left behind lacked the administrative structure to keep it together. Culture could survive where institutions failed: the Timurid-Persianate tradition that Babur carried, and that Humayun and Akbar developed, shaped the court long after the political survival of the dynasty seemed in doubt. Chapter 56 examines Mughal culture comprehensively; here it is enough to note its formative beginnings.

The Baburnama is valuable as environmental and social evidence precisely because Babur was an unusually curious observer. In his descriptions of Hindustan he lists its rivers, its seasons, and the animals and trees he found strange or useful, and he records the density of the population, the arrangement of villages and the dependence of cultivation on monsoon rain and wells. He notes that the people of the country did not make much use of running water in their gardens and that the crafts and trades were organized in hereditary groups. He measured the roads from Agra to the distant parts of his territories, and he commented on the structures of Indian towns and the work of Indian masons and carvers. Such passages give historians a picture of the country as an educated outsider saw it in the 1520s, with all the limitations of that vantage point.

His building program, though small, was meaningful. The garden at Agra and similar gardens in other towns were not merely places of leisure but statements of dynastic presence, designed to make a space in which the court could live according to Timurid ideals. Babur also had wells, stepwells, and baths constructed, and he employed large numbers of stonecutters, some from outside the region, on his buildings. His governors constructed mosques in several towns. The Babri mosque at Ayodhya, built by his governor Mir Baqi in 1528–1529, later became the focus of a major dispute, and its history, the competing claims about it, and the political movement it produced are the subject of Chapter 99. For the present chapter it is enough to note that the episode is not mentioned in Babur’s surviving memoir.

Babur’s work was a literary and intellectual enterprise as well as a political one. He composed poetry in both Turkic and Persian and a treatise on prosody, and his Turkic verse was admired for its simplicity. He prepared a versified summary of Islamic law, and he transmitted to his sons and officers the Timurid ideal of the prince as warrior and man of letters. This ideal strongly influenced the court that Humayun and Akbar maintained, and it gave the Mughal dynasty a lasting cultural identity. At the same time, the contrast between this cultural achievement and the weakness of the administration he left is a reminder that literary fame and institutional durability are different measures of a ruler’s legacy.

Section 50.16

Babur’s Death in 1530 and the Problem of Dynastic Succession

Babur died at Agra on 26 December 1530, at about forty-seven. Later tradition tells that when Humayun fell gravely ill, Babur walked around his bedside offering his own life in exchange, after which the son recovered and the father sickened. This story, preserved in later sources, cannot be verified, but it conveys the emotional framing of the succession. Humayun was named heir and was enthroned on 30 December.

The succession was less simple than the enthronement implied. Timurid custom did not give a single heir sole right to the whole inheritance, and Babur had already given Kamran control of Kabul and Kandahar. Humayun received the Indian conquests, which were richer but much less secure. One tradition relates that the minister Mir Khalifa considered supporting another candidate, Mahdi Khwaja, though the evidence is limited and the story is told in later narratives. Whatever the truth, the succession went to Humayun without a civil war, a sign of Babur’s personal authority.

Babur was first buried in Agra and later, according to his wishes, reburied in Kabul in a garden that bears his name, Bagh-i Babur. The geography of his tomb is telling: Kabul, not Agra, remained in some sense his home. At his death he ruled a territory stretching from Badakhshan and Kabul across the Punjab to the Doab and eastern Hindustan, held together by his personal command of followers, by a handful of fortified positions, and by the destruction of his chief rivals. He had not created an administration that could outlive him, and he had not resolved the question of how to share authority within his family.

Babur’s last years were marked by declining health. He had spent much of his adult life in hard campaigning, in a climate that affected him adversely, and in 1529–1530 he was often ill. Humayun, who had been governor of Badakhshan and Sambhal, was summoned to Agra when his father’s condition worsened. The reported story that Humayun fell ill and Babur sought to take the illness upon himself belongs to the family tradition and is told with sentiment in later Mughal chronicles. Whatever its truth, it reflects a perception that father and son were united in affection, and it served the dynasty’s later interest in portraying the succession as harmonious.

Conditions at court after Babur’s death show the tensions that the succession created. Officers who had served the old ruler wondered who would control the revenues and the forts, and they looked at the other princes, who were absent. Kamran in Kabul and Kandahar, Askari with commands in the Indian provinces, and Hindal in Badakhshan each commanded troops and resources of their own. There were also senior Timurid relatives, such as Muhammad Zaman Mirza, who had their own claims and followers. The new ruler’s first task was to reward officers, confirm assignments, and hold the army together. That he did so without an immediate rebellion says something about the personal loyalty that Babur had inspired, and also about the fact that no rival had a sufficient force on the spot.

The consequences of the succession plan were quickly visible. Humayun was formally enthroned in Agra and ceremonial distributions of money and titles were made, but he inherited the territory only on the eastern side of the Indus, while Kamran’s authority in Kabul and Kandahar became the basis for demands that would soon be extended to the Punjab. Babur’s own wishes about the division of his realm cannot be known with certainty, since the sources are retrospective and reflect the interests of the brothers and their partisans. What is clear is that the dynasty entered its second generation with a divided inheritance, a thin treasury, and Afghan resistance in the east, and that these conditions framed the challenges of the 1530s.

Section 50.17

Humayun’s Accession and the Inheritance of Empire

Humayun, born in Kabul in 1508, was twenty-two at his accession. He had commanded troops in his father’s campaigns, served as governor of Badakhshan, and played a visible part at Panipat. Contemporary descriptions emphasize his learning, his interest in astronomy and astrology, his courtesy, and his generosity. The Persian historian Khwandamir, who joined his court, composed for him the Qanun-i Humayuni, which describes the ordering of ranks and ceremonies at the new court, and records Humayun’s fondness for classifying his officials and his schedule by planetary and elemental symbolism.

The inheritance was less valuable than it appeared. Babur’s conquests in Hindustan had not been consolidated, and much of Humayun’s early reign was occupied by campaigns to subdue rebellious Afghan chiefs in the Doab and east. Revenue was insufficient to maintain the army at the level of Babur’s mobilizations, and the treasury, built on the capture of 1526, was by then depleted. Humayun’s legitimacy rested on his father’s name, his Timurid descent, and his ability to reward followers, but each of these was fragile. Challenges came from kinsmen with their own claims, from Afghan magnates in Bihar and Jaunpur, and from regional rulers such as Bahadur Shah of Gujarat.

In 1533 Humayun founded a new capital called Din-panah ("refuge of the faith") on the Yamuna near Delhi, on or near the site later developed by Sher Shah as Purana Qila. The foundation conveyed ambitions of dynastic permanence and cosmic order. In practice the king spent most of his time in the field, and the settled administrative apparatus that a capital normally implies never fully developed.

Humayun’s first campaigns after his accession reveal both his energy and the unfinished business he had inherited. In 1531 he besieged the fortress of Kalinjar, held by a Rajput ruler, but he accepted terms and withdrew when news arrived of an Afghan rising in the east. In 1532 he met a force led by Mahmud Lodi, who had gathered Afghan followers in the east to claim the Lodi legacy, and defeated it at Dadrah on the Gomti, an engagement that effectively ended Mahmud’s efforts. He then besieged Chunar, the fortress held by Sher Khan, for several months. Sher Khan came to terms, accepting Mughal suzerainty and sending a contingent under his son, and Humayun, anxious about developments in Gujarat, accepted the settlement. In retrospect, this decision to leave Sher Khan in possession of Chunar appears a serious error, but at the time it was a reasonable choice for a ruler with multiple urgent problems.

The court that Humayun built around himself had a distinct character. His interest in astronomy and astrology led him to organize ceremonial life on planetary principles, as Khwandamir records, assigning days of the week to different classes of business and arranging courtiers in categories associated with the state, the learned, and the pleasures of the court. Later writers have sometimes treated this as eccentricity, but it expressed a Timurid and Persianate conception of the ruler as the center of a cosmic order. The foundation of Din-panah, with its palace and walls, belonged to the same impulse. Such ideas could unite a heterogeneous court, but they could not by themselves pay soldiers, secure roads, or defeat Afghan chiefs.

Political challenges also came from outside the court. In the east, Bihar and Jaunpur were still dominated by Afghan families who owed only conditional loyalty. In the west, Gujarat under Bahadur Shah was growing stronger and giving shelter to Humayun’s rebellious relatives. In Rajasthan and Malwa, Rajput and Afghan rulers kept their own counsel. Humayun’s response was to move between these theaters of action, winning victories in one while problems accumulated in another. His difficulty was that the Mughal state, without a standing army paid from a regular treasury, could not be in several places at once, and the loyalty of its officers depended on continuing success.

Section 50.18

The Timurid Brothers: Kamran, Askari, Hindal, and Divided Authority

Humayun had three half-brothers who played central roles: Kamran, who held Kabul and Kandahar; Askari; and Hindal, the youngest. Following the Timurid custom, each expected a territorial command and an independent role. Early in the reign Kamran marched from Kabul to Lahore and seized the Punjab, and Humayun, wishing to avoid conflict with his brother, ratified the arrangement, adding territories in the northwest. This concession gave Kamran control of the principal route to Kabul and the Punjab’s revenues, and it deprived Humayun of the recruiting grounds and communications that had sustained Babur.

Askari and Hindal received commands in the Indian territories, and from time to time the brothers took independent political positions. Hindal at one point declared himself king in Agra during Humayun’s eastern campaign, and Kamran moved to take advantage of his brother’s difficulties. Cousins such as Muhammad Zaman Mirza and Muhammad Sultan Mirza also plotted and rebelled, and Humayun’s usual response was forgiveness, in line with Timurid norms of family honor. This leniency has been criticized as weakness, but it also followed conventions that treated kinship as a bond to be preserved.

The consequences were serious. Divided command meant that Humayun could not concentrate his full resources on any single threat. Nobles and soldiers calculated their loyalties by watching which brother seemed most likely to win. The Mughal ruling family functioned as a collective enterprise whose members could undermine one another, and it was an easy target for a determined adversary.

The incentives of the individual brothers help to explain their behavior. Kamran, who received Kabul and Kandahar, controlled the military recruiting grounds and the passes, and the possession of the Punjab doubled his power. He had good reason to regard himself as an independent ruler with an equal claim to the empire’s resources. Askari, given Sambhal and later other commands, was ambitious but less capable, and his record in Gujarat, discussed below, was poor. Hindal, the youngest, was given Mewat and Alwar and later served in various posts, and he was caught between loyalty to Humayun and the pressures of surrounding nobles. Each brother commanded troops and gathered followers who expected reward, so that the family resembled a confederation of small courts rather than a single household.

Their rivalries had direct consequences. In 1534–1535 a cousin, Muhammad Zaman Mirza, rebelled against Humayun, and other Timurid princes such as Muhammad Sultan Mirza and his sons plotted to seize power. Humayun suppressed the rising and, in keeping with the conventions of his house, pardoned the leaders, only to see them rebel again. In 1538, while Humayun was in Bengal, Hindal declared himself ruler at Agra and, according to the sources, caused the death of the respected Sufi Shaikh Phul (Buhlul), a deed that damaged the family’s reputation among religious communities. Kamran, instead of coming to his brother’s aid, moved into the Doab. Humayun learned of these developments while far from home, and they constituted one reason for his hasty return from Bengal.

A comparison with Babur’s own experience suggests why Humayun’s approach was costly. Babur had also faced rebellious kin, but he had used force when necessary and he held most of the resources directly. Humayun, by contrast, created territorial grants for his brothers that effectively put resources beyond his own control. This was a political decision as well as a personal one, and it reflected a belief that the family was stronger if each member had a stake. The result was the opposite: a ruler who could not count on his brothers’ armies at a decisive moment, and who also could not move against them without further dividing the dynasty. Gulbadan Begum’s account illustrates the role of the royal women, particularly Khanzada Begum, as mediators who tried to keep this fragile family together.

Section 50.19

The Gujarat Campaign and Bahadur Shah, 1535–1536

Bahadur Shah, who became Sultan of Gujarat in 1526, was an energetic ruler who annexed Malwa in 1531 and in 1535 attacked Chittor, the capital of Mewar. Gujarat controlled some of the richest ports on the western coast and drew revenue from the Indian Ocean trade (Chapters 47 and 49). It was also a refuge for Afghan and Mughal dissidents, and Bahadur’s power and ambition made him a natural rival. A legend, often retold, says that the queen mother of Mewar appealed to Humayun for protection against Bahadur, but historians doubt this tale, and it is sometimes treated as later embellishment.

In 1535 Humayun marched against Gujarat. Bahadur Shah, who had just taken Chittor, was defeated and his army scattered, and Humayun captured Mandu in Malwa and then the fortress of Champaner, which his men took by night with iron spikes driven into the rock. Bahadur fled to the coast and then to Diu, where he negotiated with the Portuguese, who had already obtained concessions at Bassein in 1534 (Chapter 49). Humayun’s occupation of Gujarat appeared complete.

It did not last. Humayun’s army, after the quick victories, was unable to administer the large and wealthy province, and he left its government to Askari and other commanders. Revolts broke out, Gujarat slipped from Mughal control, and Humayun’s attention was drawn eastward by Sher Khan’s growing power. Bahadur Shah was killed in 1537 in a scuffle with the Portuguese near Diu. The episode demonstrates a recurrent pattern of Humayun’s reign: brilliant campaigns, followed by an inability to consolidate. It also shows the extent to which the Portuguese presence had begun to complicate the politics of the western coast.

The economic and geographic importance of Gujarat explains why Humayun took the risk of campaigning there. The sultanate controlled the ports of Cambay and others on the Gulf of Khambhat, which handled a large trade in textiles, indigo, and other goods bound for the Red Sea and the Persian Gulf, as well as the commerce of Malwa and the northern interior. Its revenues and its artillery, partly in the hands of experienced gunners such as Rumi Khan, who had come from the Ottoman world, made it among the most powerful states in western India. Bahadur Shah’s support for Mughal rebels and his expansion into Malwa and Mewar were direct challenges to Humayun’s authority, and a successful campaign promised access to large resources.

The campaign was well conducted at the operational level. Humayun moved into Malwa, severed Bahadur’s supplies, and forced him to break camp. When Bahadur retreated, Mandu, the great hill fortress of Malwa, was stormed, reportedly by a small band of Mughal troops who climbed its walls at night. Champaner, the old Gujarati capital, was then taken by a similar climb up a steep rock face, with iron spikes driven into the stone for footholds, and its treasury yielded a large amount of wealth. The speed of these achievements impressed contemporaries, and Humayun was able to occupy the heart of Gujarat. But the victories were achieved by moving quickly with a select force, which is why they were hard to hold.

The loss of Gujarat reveals the problem of governance. Humayun left Askari with a force in Gujarat and returned to Malwa, then moved eastward. Askari proved unable to hold Ahmadabad and other centers, and his own thoughts of independence added to the instability. Local Gujarati nobles and commanders, who had been dispossessed or had not been given a role in the new order, organized resistance, and Bahadur Shah, returning from Diu, retook much of the sultanate. The failure was not that Humayun’s army could not win battles but that the Mughal state had no mechanism for turning a victory into administration. It lacked a body of officials to manage revenue, a garrison system, and a political settlement with the local elite. These deficiencies would be even more damaging in the East.

Section 50.20

Bihar, Bengal, and the Rise of Sher Khan

In the east, Afghan political recovery proceeded under Sher Khan, who had risen from local jagirdar to the dominant figure in Bihar by about 1534. After defeating the Sultan of Bengal’s forces at Surajgarh in 1534, he attacked Gaur, the Bengal capital, in 1537 and forced a large payment. Humayun’s campaign to the east began in 1537 and opened with a long siege of the fortress of Chunar, which lasted several months and diverted resources from the wider contest. Sher Khan meanwhile took the fortress of Rohtas, an important stronghold, by stratagem, and extended his influence in Bengal.

Humayun reached Gaur in 1538 and took possession of the Bengal capital. He remained there for several months, a delay that hostile accounts attribute to indolence and pleasure but that was partly a consequence of the monsoon season and the need to reorganize the territory. In the meantime, Sher Khan cut his lines of communication and consolidated his hold on Bihar, while Hindal’s revolt in Agra and Kamran’s maneuvers in the Punjab compelled Humayun to hasten west.

By the time Humayun left Bengal in 1539, he was weakened by lost supplies, by disease and desertion among his soldiers, and by the loss of the initiative. Sher Khan had pursued a patient strategy of avoiding open battle until Humayun’s army was exhausted, and the Mughal advance back along the Ganges carried it into a prepared adversary’s territory. The sieges of Chunar and the long stay in Gaur reveal how a campaign could be won in the field and lost through lines of supply and time.

The rise of Sher Khan was gradual and cumulative. After his submission at Chunar in 1532, he used the interval to strengthen his position in Bihar, to win the loyalty of Afghan nobles, and to build up his treasury. In 1534 his victory at Surajgarh over the forces of the Sultan of Bengal, and the tribute exacted from that kingdom in following years, brought him wealth and prestige. His siege of Gaur in 1537 and his possession of Rohtas, which he acquired by sending armed men into the fortress hidden in litters, according to Sarwani’s account, gave him a powerful base. Although the details of the Rohtas story belong to a tradition that may have been embellished, the capture itself is not doubted.

Humayun’s campaign showed the strains of the logistics of the eastern route. His army marched from Agra down the Ganges, with its heavy artillery and its large camp, and spent the months from October 1537 to the spring of 1538 at the siege of Chunar, which fell after a long effort in which the gunners of Rumi Khan, who had changed sides to Humayun, played a part. The delay allowed Sher Khan to complete his conquest of Bengal. When Humayun reached Gaur in the monsoon of 1538, he found it a ruined city, possibly affected by disease, and took it with little resistance. The decision to remain there for several months allowed the monsoon to pass but gave Sher Khan the opportunity to cut the Mughal communications at Patna and Monghyr, and to take control of the passes on the return route.

The consequence was that Humayun’s victory in Bengal was undercut by the loss of the country behind him. As he marched back, the army struggled with rain, sickness, and shortages, and some officers deserted. News of Hindal’s revolt and Kamran’s maneuvers reached him. Sher Khan, in the meantime, avoided battle and pursued a policy of delay, parallel to the movements of the Mughal army, so that he could choose the moment of engagement. The contrast with Humayun’s rapid advance in Gujarat is instructive: here a defeated enemy was not a fleeing army but a determined adversary operating on interior lines in a region where he commanded the loyalty of local chiefs and the cultivators who supplied grain.

Section 50.21

The Battle of Chausa, 1539

Chausa, near the confluence of the Karmanasa and the Ganges, was the setting for the first of the two decisive defeats of Humayun’s reign. In the early summer of 1539 the two armies faced one another across fortified positions. Sher Khan opened negotiations, which Humayun, short of supplies and eager to return west, took seriously. Reports suggest a truce was nearly agreed. On the night of 25–26 June, as the rains began, Sher Khan attacked Humayun’s camp before dawn, catching the Mughals unprepared.

The Mughal army broke. Many soldiers were killed or driven into the Ganges, and Humayun himself, fleeing, reached the river and nearly drowned. Tradition says that a water carrier ferried him across on an inflated skin, and that Humayun later rewarded him, in some versions by granting him a short reign on the throne. Women of the royal household were captured, among them Humayun’s wife Bega Begum, whom Sher Shah, according to Mughal sources, treated honorably and later returned.

The consequences were political as well as military. Sher Khan took the royal title Sher Shah, had the khutba read and coins struck in his name, and presented himself as an independent Afghan sovereign. For Humayun the defeat meant the loss of much of his army and treasury and a serious blow to his legitimacy as a ruler capable of protecting his followers. It also tested the loyalty of his brothers, who were now able to bargain from a position of strength.

The geography of Chausa shaped the engagement. The Mughal camp was located between the Ganges and the Karmanasa, a small river that has historically marked a boundary between Bihar and the lands to the west, on ground that had been softened by the first monsoon rains. Humayun had reached this point in the hope of making a settlement with Sher Khan, and the commanders on each side exchanged messages. At the same time, the Mughal army suffered from shortages and from the effects of a long campaign. The Afghan camp, a short distance away, was well provisioned, its soldiers rested, and its leader skilled at deception.

The Afghan attack at dawn was carefully planned. Sher Khan reportedly divided his army into three, sending forces to strike at different parts of the Mughal camp, so that confusion would spread before the defenders could organize. The rain and mud interfered with the use of artillery and matchlocks, and the Mughal cavalry could not form up. In the confusion, the army broke and its soldiers sought escape by the river, where no sufficient means of crossing was available. Many were drowned. The loss of the army’s baggage, treasury, and many officers, as well as the capture of women of the royal household, made the defeat extremely severe. The details vary among sources, but the main outline is consistent: the battle was won by surprise, timing, and use of terrain and weather.

The political results unfolded quickly. Humayun reached Agra, where he gathered his remaining followers, while Sher Khan took Bengal and Bihar into his own hands and declared himself king. His assumption of the title Sher Shah was both a statement of independence and a claim to Afghan sovereignty in Hindustan. Persian sources that reflect the Mughal viewpoint emphasize the treachery of the night attack, but Afghan sources saw it as a masterful stratagem. Both perspectives show that Chausa was not just a military defeat but a turning point in the contest for legitimacy, since it gave the Afghan ruler the chance to present himself as the restorer of Afghan rule.

Section 50.22

The Battle of Kannauj, 1540, and the Loss of Northern India

After Chausa, Humayun returned to Agra and tried to rebuild his strength. His brothers, who had been invited to join him, produced contingents of uneven quality, and Kamran, whose troops were important to the effort, was reluctant to risk them. Hindal and Askari were present but not united in purpose. By spring 1540 the Mughal army had assembled on the Ganges near Kannauj. Sher Shah’s forces, experienced and unified, approached from the east.

The battle was fought on 17 May 1540 and ended quickly. Much of the Mughal army dissolved after a short encounter, and the Afghans pursued the fleeing troops. Humayun escaped across the Ganges, hurried through Agra and Delhi, and made for Lahore. Sher Shah occupied the Mughal heartland and began to establish his own government. By the end of 1540, the Mughals had lost Hindustan.

Kannauj ended the first phase of Mughal rule in India. It also settled the question of whether Humayun could unite the family: Kamran withdrew to Lahore with his forces, and the brothers' disagreements worsened as Sher Shah pressed northwest. Within a few years Humayun would be a fugitive without a base. The next division examines what Sher Shah did with his victory.

The year between Chausa and Kannauj was one of frantic effort but diminishing options. Humayun, back in Agra, tried to reassemble an army, and Kamran, Askari, and Hindal came with their forces, although their cooperation was uneven. Despite a degree of reconciliation among the brothers, the underlying distrust persisted, and councils were marked by arguments over strategy. Revenue was short, soldiers had to be recruited, and the Mughal commanders knew that their opponent was moving steadily west. Sher Shah’s own preparations included consolidating his hold on Bengal and Bihar, bringing up his Afghan contingents, and assembling the artillery that he had begun to collect.

The two armies met on opposite banks of the Ganges near Kannauj in the spring of 1540. For about a month they faced each other, and during this time the Mughal army suffered a steady loss of men through desertion. Soldiers slipped away at night, and the Mughal camp became increasingly demoralized. Humayun decided to cross the river and attack rather than wait for his army to dissolve. On 17 May, as the Mughals were arranging their formation on the bank, the Afghans attacked. Parts of the Mughal line, including some of Askari’s and Hindal’s contingents, gave way, and others failed to coordinate. Reports suggest that the Mughal artillery made little difference to the outcome. The Afghan victory was a result of unified command, steady discipline, and a sound reading of the enemy’s weakness.

The pursuit and its consequences shaped the next years. Humayun escaped across the Ganges, in some accounts on an elephant, and made his way to Agra. Sher Shah sent forces after him, and the Mughals did not stop to defend the capital. Humayun fled to Lahore, where the brothers’ quarrels deepened, and then to Sindh. Many officers and soldiers who had followed him abandoned his cause and entered the service of the Afghan victor. For Sher Shah, the victory opened the door to the heartland and made him the effective ruler of northern India. For the Mughal dynasty, the most important consequence was that it was now without a territorial base, depending on the goodwill of others and on its own capacity to survive.

The deeper lesson of Kannauj concerns the relation between battle and structure. The Mughal army was no smaller than the Afghan, and it possessed artillery and trained cavalry. But the cohesion of an army depends on pay, supplies, and the confidence of its soldiers in their leaders. Humayun’s army had marched long distances, lost much of its equipment, and been commanded by a family whose members did not trust one another. The Afghan army, though made up of clan contingents, was bound together by the combination of Sher Shah’s authority, the prospect of rewards, and the momentum of success. The battle thus illustrates, in compact form, the argument that the chapter develops: that the early Mughal regime was far more vulnerable than its victories in 1526 and 1527 suggested.

Cause and Effect

The Collapse of Mughal Rule, 1540

Long-term causes

An incompletely consolidated conquest, thin administrative structures, and a dynastic culture that treated territory as shared property. Afghan resentment and the survival of Afghan military networks in the east and Bihar.

Short-term causes

Humayun’s overextension in Gujarat and Bengal, depleted resources, lost supplies and communication lines, the rebellion of Hindal, the ambivalence of Kamran, and Sher Khan’s strategy of patience and surprise.

Event

Defeats at Chausa (26 June 1539) and Kannauj (17 May 1540), followed by the retreat of Humayun and the occupation of Agra and Delhi by Sher Shah.

Immediate effects

Loss of the Mughal heartland, dispersal of the court, and the beginning of Humayun’s exile. Sher Shah proclaimed himself ruler and began to organize an administration.

Long-term consequences

Fifteen years of Sur rule in northern India, an exile that exposed Humayun to the Safavid court, and the shaping of the political world that Akbar would later inherit.

Historical complexity

The defeat cannot be reduced to a single cause. Personal failings, structural weakness, the skill of an opponent, and chance each played a part.
Historical Debate

Was Humayun’s Failure Personal or Structural?

The question: How much of Humayun’s defeat should be attributed to his character and decisions, and how much to the political and military circumstances he inherited?

Interpretation A: Personal and strategic failings: Many Mughal-era writers, followed by some modern historians, emphasized Humayun’s indecision, his lengthy halts at Gaur and elsewhere, his leniency toward brothers, and his lapses in vigilance. On this view a more determined ruler would have prevented the collapse.
Interpretation B: Structural conditions: Other historians stress that Humayun faced a conquest only partly consolidated, a family that treated territory as shared, revenue shortfalls, and the skill of Sher Khan. They argue that Humayun’s decisions were not unusual for a Timurid ruler and that Babur himself would have struggled under the same conditions.

Evidence:

The Mughal sources that emphasize Humayun’s flaws were composed under Akbar, who had reason to portray his father sympathetically, and they also had reason to explain defeat. Afghan sources emphasize Sher Shah’s talents. Modern interpretations weigh these accounts alongside evidence of revenue, logistics, and the geography of the campaigns.

Why it matters:

Whether the early Mughal state collapsed because of a man or because of its structure affects how the achievements of Akbar are understood: as a restoration of an interrupted success or as the first real solution to problems that earlier rulers could not solve.

Section 50.23

Farid Khan, Sher Khan, and the Afghan Political World of Bihar

Sher Shah was born Farid, probably around 1486, into a family of the Sur clan of Afghan military immigrants. His father, Hasan Khan, held a jagir (revenue assignment) in the Sasaram region of Bihar and in the neighboring pargana of Khawaspur Tanda under a Lodi-era noble. According to Sarwani, Farid quarreled with his father over his treatment in the household, left for Jaunpur to pursue an education, and returned to manage the family estates, where he is said to have introduced fair practices toward cultivators and firmness toward headmen. The accuracy of such details is uncertain, because they are told from the vantage of later admirers, but they show how his reputation as an administrator was constructed.

He entered the service of Bahar Khan Lohani, who ruled Bihar as Sultan Muhammad, and received the title Sher Khan after allegedly killing a tiger. After his patron’s death he became guardian of the child ruler Jalal Khan and effectively controlled Bihar. His power increased through the capture of the fortress of Chunar, which came to him through marriage to the widow of its holder, and through victory at Surajgarh in 1534 over the Bengal forces. Chunar and Rohtas gave him strongholds, treasure, and access to the Ganges.

His career belongs to the Afghan political world of eastern India, in which chiefs commanded personal followings, formed alliances through marriage, and sought the patronage of sultans. Sher Khan combined tribal connections with an ability to manage larger resources. He watched Mughal methods, adopted elements of them, and avoided direct confrontations until he was ready. The eastern Afghan elites who followed him did so because he offered leadership, security, and a plausible claim to continue the Afghan sultanate that Babur had overthrown.

The Afghan political world in which Sher Khan grew up was characterized by a mixture of clan solidarity and individual ambition. Afghans who migrated into northern India from the highlands of Roh and the Sulaiman range found employment as soldiers under the Delhi Sultans, and over generations they acquired land assignments, built networks of kin, and rose to positions of command. Clan identity, as Sur, Lohani, Niyazi, or Sarwani, gave a basis for trust, but alliances also crossed lines of clan, and success was measured by ability to attract followers by gifts and leadership. A leader without resources could not keep his following. This culture could produce loyal, mobile fighting forces, but it also produced rivalry, because status was always open to competition.

The sources for Sher Shah’s life reflect this complexity. Abbas Sarwani’s Tarikh-i Sher Shahi, written under Akbar, is the fullest narrative, and it was probably based on family and clan memories. Other Afghan histories, including the Tarikh-i Daudi of Abdullah, the Waqiat-i Mushtaqi of Rizqullah Mushtaqi, and the Tarikh-i Salatin-i Afaghina of Ahmad Yadgar, were also composed in the Mughal period and sometimes tell different stories. All these narratives contain moral lessons about the education and character of a ruler and tend to enlarge the idea of Sher Shah’s wisdom. Material evidence offers a check: inscriptions on his buildings, notably at Sasaram and at the Rohtas fortress in the Punjab, and his coins confirm his titles and aspirations but say little about his early life. Historians therefore reconstruct his career from texts composed generations later, supplemented where possible by documents in stone and metal.

Sher Khan’s career in Bihar shows the application of these qualities. He supported Afghan officers who needed patrons, he took advantage of the quarrels among Lohani and Lodi chiefs, and he used marriage and diplomacy to extend his influence. His acquisition of Chunar, with its large treasury, was a turning point, since it gave him resources to pay troops. He also learned from his enemies, for he understood the Mughal use of artillery and field tactics, and he understood the weaknesses of the Bengal sultanate. By 1537, when he challenged Humayun, he was a ruler of experience who had already assembled a political and military machine.

Section 50.24

The Sur Empire, 1540–1545: Conquest and Territorial Ambition

Having defeated Humayun, Sher Shah moved to consolidate. He occupied Agra and Delhi, pursued Humayun’s remnants through the Punjab, and fortified the northwestern marches. To contain the Gakhars, a hill people who had assisted the Mughals and controlled the road to Kashmir and the Indus, he began construction of the great fortress of Rohtas in the Punjab, begun in the early 1540s and today recognized by UNESCO. It was intended to protect his northwestern frontier and hem in the Gakhars.

His later campaigns extended the realm in other directions. In 1542 he moved into Malwa, and in 1543 he besieged Raisen, where a Rajput chief, Puranmal, surrendered on terms that were later broken, an incident described differently in Afghan and Rajput traditions and remembered as a breach of faith. Multan and Sindh were brought under Sur authority. In 1544 he marched against Maldeo Rathore of Marwar, a Rajput ruler who had built a powerful state, and defeated him at Giri-Sumel after a hard-fought battle in which Rajput commanders such as Jaita and Kumpa fought with distinction. A later tradition has Sher Shah saying that he had nearly lost Hindustan for a handful of millet. The remark is probably apocryphal, but it expresses the severity of the contest.

The empire he created stretched from Bengal to the Indus and from the Himalayan foothills to Malwa, but it did not include Gujarat, Kashmir, or the Deccan. Its limits show the practical constraints on expansion in a sixteenth-century state: large armies, difficult terrain, and the need to garrison numerous strongholds. He died during the siege of Kalinjar in 1545, leaving the work of consolidation unfinished.

The first years of Sur rule were devoted to consolidation. In Bengal, Sher Shah faced the rebellion of the governor, Khizr Khan, in 1541, who had taken advantage of the distance from the center and acted as an independent ruler. He removed him and divided the province into districts under separate officers, with a civilian official, Qazi Fazilat, supervising them, in order to prevent any one officer from becoming too powerful. This administrative solution, reported in Sarwani, shows the awareness of the danger that provincial governors would follow a path similar to his own. Elsewhere he maintained garrisons in important forts and placed trusted officers in the regions most prone to disorder.

The campaigns of 1542–1544 extended Sur power toward the west and south. In Malwa, he took over from the former Khalji officer Qadir Shah (Mallu Khan), who had controlled the province after the fall of the sultanate. At Raisen, in 1543, he besieged the fortress held by Puranmal, who surrendered under a guarantee of safety. The Afghan sources say the surrender was followed by the killing of the Rajputs after clerics declared that the agreement was not binding, while Rajput traditions remember the event as treachery. The case is a reminder that the same incident might be understood as a matter of law, as a breach of faith, or as a lesson about the cost of resistance. In Multan and Sindh, Sur officers established authority over regions that had been outside Delhi’s control for decades.

The war with Marwar tested the new regime. Maldeo Rathore of Marwar had built a strong state, with forts and cavalry, and Sher Shah advanced with a large army, taking care to fortify his camp at each stage. For weeks the armies faced one another near Giri-Sumel. A ruse, according to later tradition, caused Maldeo to doubt the loyalty of his own officers, and some of his commanders, notably Jaita and Kumpa, attacked with a small force and nearly won. The Afghan victory was narrow, and Sher Shah proceeded to occupy Marwar’s forts, but the territory was never wholly pacified. The campaign illustrates a pattern: the Surs could defeat and occupy, but rapid administrative absorption of independent Rajput states remained beyond their reach.

Section 50.25

Afghan Kingship, Nobility, and the Organization of Sur Rule

Sher Shah faced the problem that had troubled the Lodis: how to build a strong monarchy among Afghan chiefs who valued independence and an egalitarian ideal of leadership. The Afghan political tradition made the ruler something of a first among equals, who ruled by consultation and by managing the tribal and clan loyalties of his followers. Sher Shah’s response was to assert royal authority and to reduce the dependence of the army on tribal chiefs, while avoiding the confrontation that had destroyed Ibrahim Lodi.

According to Afghan chroniclers, he maintained a standing force paid from the treasury and supervised through descriptive rolls of soldiers and the branding of horses, practices known from the time of Ala al-Din Khalji (Chapter 41). He also distributed commands among nobles of different tribes, transferred officers between districts, and mixed contingents so that no single chief controlled a large block of troops. He used non-Afghans as well, including Hindu officers such as Brahmajit Gaur, a Brahman commander. The mix of tribal recruitment, centralized payment, and political management gave him greater control than the Lodis had enjoyed.

Legitimacy rested on military success, patronage of religious scholars, and the claim to justice. Sher Shah is remembered for personal attention to government, including an itinerary of inspection and quick decisions. He built a mausoleum for his father and eventually for himself at Sasaram and constructed the Qila-i Kuhna mosque in Delhi, whose architecture blended Lodi and later forms. The monarchy he fashioned still depended on the loyalty of Afghan nobles, and his successors would find that his personal authority could not be easily passed on.

Sher Shah’s attempts to alter the character of Afghan kingship are visible in his treatment of leading nobles. One of the most important was Khawas Khan Marwat, who served as his principal commander and who enjoyed great authority. Others included Isa Khan Niyazi, Haibat Khan, and members of the Sur family. Sher Shah relied on them but also watched them, and the transfers and rotations that were reported were meant to prevent the growth of personal followings. After his death, the success of these measures was tested, and the revolts of the Niyazis under Islam Shah indicated the strength of the old tradition that Afghan nobles could oppose a king whom they judged unjust.

At the same time, the Sur regime was not exclusively Afghan. Hindu officials, scribes, and military officers served in important positions, and the revenue and record-keeping staff included Hindus who had served earlier regimes. Brahmajit Gaur is mentioned as a Brahman commander, and other non-Afghans appeared in the narratives, although the evidence does not allow a precise picture of their number or influence. The regime also continued the Sultanate practice of patronizing Sufi saints and scholars with grants of land, and it supported religious leaders whose approval conferred legitimacy. In these respects Sher Shah’s government resembled earlier Sultanate governments more than it differed from them.

His mausoleum at Sasaram, a monument built on a platform in the middle of an artificial lake and completed in 1545, is a powerful statement of the kingship that he projected. Its form, an octagonal tomb of sandstone surmounted by a great dome, draws on Sultanate precedents but is expressed on a larger scale and with a confidence that signaled the arrival of a new dynasty. The building suggests that Sher Shah thought of his rule as lasting, and it is among the most visible and securely dated physical remains of the regime. Together with the Qila-i Kuhna mosque at Delhi and the great fort at Rohtas, it shows that the Surs were builders as well as warriors.

Section 50.26

Provincial and District Administration under Sher Shah

Later chroniclers, especially Abbas Sarwani, describe an administrative structure in which the empire was divided into units called sarkars, each subdivided into parganas. A sarkar was supervised by two principal officers, one responsible for military and police matters and one for civil and judicial affairs. At the pargana level, a small team of officials included a revenue supervisor, a treasurer, and two clerks, one writing in Persian and one in a local script. Qanungos, who kept records of local revenue and custom, remained essential intermediaries. Sher Shah is said to have moved frequently among the provinces, inspecting officers and punishing offenders.

Policing relied on collective responsibility. Village headmen (muqaddams) were held accountable for crimes committed in their localities, and they were obliged to find the culprits or compensate the victim, a measure intended to make rural communities guardians of the roads. Justice was administered by qazis and by officials who heard petitions, with Sher Shah himself acting as a final court of appeal. The aim was to make the countryside safe for travelers and traders and to ensure that revenue reached the treasury.

It is important to distinguish what is documented from what is retrospectively attributed. Sarwani wrote at Akbar’s command, decades after Sher Shah’s death, and presented an idealized picture that suited a Mughal court interested in showing continuity of good government. Many features of the structure had earlier precedents under the Delhi Sultans, and the sarkar and pargana were in use before him. His distinctive contribution was a degree of supervision and mobility that made existing structures work more effectively, and a program that consciously sought to make officials answerable to the center. Local intermediaries continued to hold considerable power, as in every premodern South Asian regime.

The functions of the officials described by Sarwani can be understood as an effort to separate different kinds of power. The military and police officer of the sarkar, who commanded troops and maintained order, was distinguished from the officer who handled civil disputes and revenue. By dividing authority between the two, the king could use each to check the other and make it harder for any single officer to build a local following. At the pargana level, the officers worked as a team: the executive, who managed security and the collection of dues; the revenue assessor, who measured and recorded; the treasurer, who held the funds; and the clerks, who kept accounts in the two languages. The qanungo, a hereditary local expert, was valuable because he knew the customs and the revenue history of the district, and he was therefore both indispensable and potentially dangerous.

Transfers and inspections enforced discipline. According to the sources, officers were moved from district to district at intervals so that they would not form ties with local magnates, and the king’s own tours and his reported willingness to hear complaints gave cultivators and merchants a reason to expect redress. Information moved through the dak system, and officials were required to report. Justice was administered at several levels, and the qazi held authority in matters of Islamic law while customary and local procedures governed disputes among other communities. The picture that emerges from the sources is of a regime that had grasped the importance of supervision, even if the actual achievement fell short of the ideal.

What the evidence leaves unclear is how much of this structure actually existed in every region. The Sur state was in existence for only five years of central rule, and the administrative reforms described by Sarwani and later authors would have needed more time to become uniform. Some provinces, such as Bengal and the Punjab, were governed in distinct ways. Historians such as K. R. Qanungo accepted most of Sarwani’s account, while others, including scholars who have studied Mughal revenue records, doubt that the Surs could have created a system as comprehensive as described. A cautious conclusion is that the Surs experimented with a stricter and more supervised form of the Sultanate system, and that Akbar’s administration inherited both the experience and the memory of this experiment.

Historical Vocabulary
Sarkar, Pargana, and Qanungo

Sarkar names a district-level administrative unit in the Sultanate and Sur systems; a pargana was a smaller territorial and revenue unit comprising villages. A qanungo maintained local revenue records and knowledge of customary assessments. These terms predated Sher Shah and varied in institutional detail by time and place. Their importance lies in the interaction between central directives, local recordkeepers, and the agrarian resources that supported armies and courts.

Section 50.27

Land Revenue, Measurement, and the Agrarian Foundations of Power

Land revenue was the principal source of state income, and Sher Shah’s government sought to make its assessment more systematic. According to later accounts, officials surveyed cultivated land using a standard measuring unit derived from the Sikandari gaz, the yard introduced in Sikandar Lodi’s reign, and recorded the area under each crop. A schedule of rates (sometimes called the rai) assigned a standard yield for each crop, and the state’s claim was set as a share of this estimated produce, commonly reported as one third. Cultivators could pay in cash or grain, and the officials were to record the agreement in a written undertaking (kabuliyat), with the state issuing a document of title or terms (patta).

Additional provisions are reported. The state set aside reserves for famine relief, offered remissions in cases of crop failure, and sometimes advanced loans to peasants. Officials were to be paid reasonable salaries and punished for extortion. Surveyors and tax collectors received small fees. These practices, if implemented as described, could reduce arbitrary exactions and increase predictability.

How fully these measures were carried out is uncertain. Surviving documentation from the Sur period is limited, and historians have debated how much of the system described by later writers existed in practice. K. R. Qanungo, in his biography of the ruler, presented a favorable and detailed picture, while later scholars, including Irfan Habib and Shireen Moosvi, have stressed the limited reach of systematic measurement, the continuing role of local intermediaries, and the likelihood that the state’s actual practice combined measurement in some areas with traditional sharing arrangements in others. Tradition also states that Todar Mal, later Akbar’s finance minister, began his career under the Surs, but the evidence is thin. The best-supported conclusion is that Sher Shah’s government developed an ambitious program of revenue assessment that provided a model, and that Akbar’s later reforms drew on both Sultanate and Sur precedents. Chapter 52 examines the mature Mughal system.

The cultivator’s perspective is the hardest to recover. The villages of the Gangetic plain were organized around headmen, accountants, and caste and kin groups, and many were dominated by landholders who collected revenue for the state while retaining a share. The Sur government is reported to have encouraged cultivators to enter agreements directly, which would reduce the role of intermediaries, but the existence of zamindars and local chiefs meant that in practice the state continued to depend on them. Sarwani tells a story, which is probably meant to illustrate a principle rather than record a particular event, in which Sher Shah punished soldiers who damaged crops during a march, and he reportedly insisted that cultivators be treated with consideration because their labor supported the state.

Records and documents were essential. A revenue system based on measurement requires surveys, accounts of assessed and collected revenue, and registers of rates, and the sources suggest that the Surs expected their officials to keep such records. But very little of this documentation survives, and the later Mughal records, such as the Ain-i Akbari, mix earlier information with new calculations. It is therefore difficult to know how far Sur measurement extended and how often rates were revised. The distinction between an administrative intention and a verified practice is critical, since praise for Sher Shah’s revenue system may rest more on what later writers wished to attribute to him than on what his officials actually achieved. Chapter 52 returns to the comparison with Akbar’s system.

Revenue-free grants and the support of religious institutions formed another part of the agrarian landscape. Sher Shah is said to have confirmed and extended grants to Muslim scholars, Sufi lodges, and some Hindu religious figures, reducing the income of the state but securing the goodwill of influential groups. The Sur government also had to deal with famine and shortage, and the provision for relief is described in the sources as a duty of the state. In regions such as Bengal, where rice cultivation and riverine communication differed from the wheat economy of the Doab, the methods of assessment were necessarily different, a reminder that a single system could not be applied uniformly across the entire territory.

Section 50.28

Currency, Coinage, Markets, and Commercial Integration

Sher Shah’s monetary policy is among the best-documented aspects of his reign, since the coins survive in large numbers. His mints issued a silver coin of about 178 grains, around 11.5 grams, with high silver content, together with copper coins (dams) and gold issues in smaller quantities. The silver coin is often called the rupiya, and the name became the foundation of the rupee, though the word and coins of similar weight had earlier antecedents in the Sultanate period. Sher Shah did not invent the silver coin of this weight, but he standardized it, enforced a consistent quality, and issued it from mints across the realm.

Surviving specimens carry Arabic legends giving the ruler’s name and titles, the confession of faith, the names of the four early caliphs, and the mint and date. Some issues include legends in the Nagari script, reflecting the multilingual character of the realm and the practical need for coins to be recognized by a diverse population. The consistency of weight and metal made payment easier, tax collection more predictable, and market exchange more reliable. The system survived his regime, since the Mughals adopted the rupee standard.

Commercial integration depended on more than coins. The security of roads, the removal of internal tolls, and the encouragement of trade, all of which Sarwani emphasizes, aimed at making goods flow more freely. The Surs' policies were consistent with a long Sultanate effort to build a stable currency, but the measure of Sher Shah’s contribution was in extending the standard over a vast territory in a few years.

The contrast between literary sources and numismatic evidence is especially clear in the study of Sur coinage. Sarwani and later writers praised Sher Shah for replacing a confusing variety of debased coins with a uniform silver currency, and Mughal historians treated his coinage as the basis of their own. Coins provide a way to test that claim, because they survive in large numbers and can be weighed, assayed, and read. They show that the Sur mints indeed issued silver coins of fairly consistent weight, though not of identical weight, and that the standard was maintained across mints in different parts of the realm. They also show that the coinage of the early Sur period was not the start of a new monetary order, since it built on a long Sultanate tradition of silver and billon coinage.

Specific coins make the point concrete. A silver rupee catalogued in the Fitzwilliam Museum, Cambridge (CM.IN.3681-R), struck at Gwalior in 1543–1544, weighs 11.39 grams, which is close to the reported standard of about 11.5 grams and shows that a mint in the Mughal-Afghan borderland, a place of strategic importance, was operating to the same standard as mints elsewhere. Such a single specimen cannot prove the system, but taken with the many other known examples, it confirms that the regime issued a standardized silver coinage over a wide area and that the rupee as a coin of account has its origin in this period. The coinage also had a political function: it announced the king’s name and status in a medium that moved through every market and every tax collector’s hands.

The regime’s monetary policy supported the commercial economy as well. Merchants who used silver coins of known weight could trade across long distances with greater confidence. The government’s insistence on payment of revenue in coin in certain areas stimulated the circulation of money, though many transactions in villages continued in grain and other goods. The relationship between silver, copper, and gold coins was managed by official rates, and the later Mughal system preserved these arrangements. The Sur period thus contributed a durable element to the economic structure of northern India, even if its creators did not invent the underlying institutions.

Section 50.29

Roads, Sarais, Postal Communications, and Internal Security

Sher Shah is widely credited with building or improving a network of roads. The most famous ran from Sonargaon in eastern Bengal through Bihar and the Doab to Delhi and on to the Indus, along a corridor that had long been used for travel and trade and that later came to be known to Europeans as the Grand Trunk Road. Other routes linked Agra with Burhanpur and with Marwar and Chittor, and Lahore with Multan. Many of these routes followed older paths, and some had been maintained or improved by earlier states. The Surs' achievement was in repairing, protecting, and equipping them on a larger scale and over a short time.

According to Sarwani, rest houses (sarais) were built at intervals of about two kos (a kos is roughly three kilometers), with wells, shade trees, and mosques, and separate quarters and provisions for Hindu and Muslim travelers. Officials supervised each sarai, and travelers received food and fodder. The post system, called the dak, relied on relays of couriers and horses stationed at the sarais to carry official messages, and it also supported intelligence-gathering, since news-writers and spies reported on local conditions. The roads were policed through the same principle of collective local responsibility.

The result was greater speed of communication and reduced risk for merchants, officials, and ordinary travelers. These measures also served the state’s interest by enabling troop movements and the swift transmission of orders. Their effects can still be seen in the surviving sarais, wells, and mile-markers (kos minars), though many of the latter were built under later Mughal rulers, and they are not all attributable to the Surs.

The effectiveness of the communication system depended on how its parts connected. Roads provided the routes, but they were maintained by the villages through which they passed, whose headmen were responsible for repairs and security. The sarais provided fixed points at which travelers, couriers, and officials could find food, water, and shelter, and the staff of each sarai, which included a keeper, a watchman, and in some cases cooks and a water supplier, was financed by the state. Horses were kept at certain posts to carry messages in relays, so that the king could receive news from distant provinces in days rather than weeks. These elements, none of which was new, formed a coherent system when they were linked by an official policy and an effort to enforce it.

Earlier precedents are well documented. The Mauryas had a royal road in the north, and the Delhi Sultans used a postal system in which runners and horsemen carried letters and news. The fourteenth-century traveler Ibn Battuta described the Tughluq postal service, with its relays of runners at short intervals and horsemen at greater ones, and he noted that it carried both letters and goods. Sher Shah’s contribution was to revive and extend this tradition and to link it with a program of road-building and sarai construction on a broader scale. Sarwani’s statement that sarais were built at regular intervals reflects the intention, and surviving buildings and ruins along the old Delhi–Lahore road offer some evidence, though many have been dated to the later Mughal period.

The security dimension needs emphasis. A route without protection was a route that merchants and officials avoided, and bandit groups and rebellious local chiefs had long taken advantage of the instability of Indian politics. Collective responsibility, backed by punishment, was intended to turn the villages into guardians. Rohtas, in the Punjab, was meant to hold the Gakhars, who controlled a key section of the northwestern road, in check. The resulting network connected the Gangetic plain with the northwest, and the movement of horses, merchants, and armies along it helped to knit together the economy of northern India. Such networks were also vulnerable, as Humayun’s return in 1555 would show, since the same corridors that carried Sur messengers and merchants could also carry an invading army.

Everyday Life and Social History

Travel, Safety, and Daily Life on the Roads

Travel in this period meant long journeys on foot, with pack animals or carts, exposed to weather, bandits, and uncertain food supplies. Merchants moved grain, cloth, salt, and horses, while pilgrims, soldiers, couriers, and migrating laborers shared the same routes. The sarai and its well offered shelter and water, and the provision of cooked food and fodder, as reported by Sarwani, mattered most to poorer travelers. Villages along the road bore the burden of providing guides, supplies, and security, and their headmen risked punishment if travelers were robbed. For the villagers, then, the benefits of safe roads had a cost, and the experience of the roads differed between a merchant with an escort and a peasant answerable for a crime committed near his fields.

Division VI

The Sur Succession, Humayun’s Exile, and the Mughal Restoration

Humayun’s exile was a consequential period of political survival, diplomacy, and military reconstruction, and the Safavid connection was not an uncomplicated alliance.

Section 50.30

Sher Shah’s Death at Kalinjar and the Sur Succession, 1545–1554

Sher Shah was besieging Kalinjar, a fortress held by the Chandela ruler Kirat Singh, in May 1545 when a shell or gunpowder charge exploded near him. He died of his injuries on 22 May, reportedly after learning that the fort had fallen. The Afghan nobles chose his younger son Jalal Khan, who took the title Islam Shah, over the elder son Adil Khan, reflecting the importance of noble consent in Afghan succession.

Islam Shah (r. 1545–1553/54) maintained and in some ways extended his father’s centralizing policies. He issued detailed regulations (zawabit) governing the army and administration and moved against powerful nobles who threatened royal authority. Several Afghan leaders rebelled, including factions of the Niyazi clan, and he suppressed them by force. His treatment of the Mahdawi movement, a religious community led by Shaikh Alai, was severe, and Alai died following a public punishment under Islam Shah, dated to 1549 or 1550 in surviving accounts. These tensions reveal the limits of the political settlement that Sher Shah had built.

After Islam Shah’s death, dated to 1553 in some studies and 1554 in others, the dynasty fragmented. His young son was killed within days by Muhammad Mubariz Khan, who took the title Muhammad Adil Shah, and he in turn relied on his minister Hemu (Hemchandra), an able administrator of mercantile background. Rival Sur leaders, including Ibrahim Sur in Delhi and Agra and Sikandar Sur in the Punjab, claimed the throne. A struggle between nobles, kinsmen, and regional commanders replaced the unified state, and the empire’s military potential dissipated. These divisions opened the opportunity for the Mughal recovery.

Islam Shah’s accession shows how the Sur regime handled the succession it had never planned for. Sher Shah’s elder son, Adil Khan, was absent, and the nobles at Kalinjar summoned Jalal Khan, who was nearer and had a record as a commander. The consent of the leading Afghan chiefs mattered more than primogeniture, and Jalal Khan, taking the title Islam Shah, rewarded the chiefs who supported him while preparing to restrict their independence. Adil Khan was offered a province but was soon defeated and removed, and his supporters were absorbed or eliminated. The speed of the settlement indicates that the Sur regime had a sufficient institutional core to survive its founder, but at the cost of antagonizing the nobles who had backed the loser.

His policies followed his father’s but with greater rigidity. The regulations (zawabit) attributed to him prescribed procedures for the army, for the payment of soldiers, and for the conduct of officers, and sought to bring more of the Afghan forces under direct royal supervision. He built the fort of Salimgarh on the Yamuna at Delhi, which later became part of the Mughal complex, and he strengthened the forts of the Punjab. These measures provoked resistance. Khawas Khan, Sher Shah’s leading general, was killed at his order in 1547, and the Niyazi chiefs, led by Haibat Khan and others, rebelled in the Punjab, until they were defeated near Ambala in 1548–1549. The suppression of these rebellions strengthened the king’s hand but reduced the reservoir of experienced Afghan commanders.

Religious controversy added to the tensions. The Mahdawi movement, which followed the teaching of Sayyid Muhammad of Jaunpur and had gained supporters among Afghans, was led by Shaikh Alai, who attracted notice and made enemies among the ulama. Islam Shah, under pressure from his scholars, had him examined and punished, and Alai died in 1551. The episode shows that the ruler’s attempt to create a more powerful monarchy also involved defining correct religion and suppressing dissent, a pattern found in other regimes of the time. After Islam Shah’s death in 1553 at Gwalior, his young son Firuz was murdered within days by Muhammad Mubariz Khan, a relative who ruled as Muhammad Adil Shah, and the struggle among the competing Sur claimants began. The effective governance of Adil’s realm passed to his minister Hemu, a man of modest origins who rose through ability, and the Surs divided into rival power centers: Ibrahim Sur at Delhi and Agra, Sikandar Sur in the Punjab, and Adil in the east.

Section 50.31

Humayun’s Exile in Sindh and Rajasthan, 1540–1544

Humayun fled from Kannauj to Lahore, where his brothers and officers debated what to do. Unable to establish a base in the Punjab against Sher Shah’s advance, he moved to Sindh, where he hoped to find support and a route to Kandahar or Gujarat. Sindh was controlled by Shah Husain Arghun, who proved unwilling to accept him as an overlord and who provisioned him inadequately. Hindal and others began to leave him.

During this period, in 1541, Humayun married Hamida Banu Begum, a young woman of a Persian family who had been in Hindal’s household, at Patri in Sindh. Seeking help from Maldeo Rathore of Marwar, Humayun moved into Rajasthan, but he learned that Maldeo had changed his mind about welcoming him and turned back across the Thar desert, suffering heavy losses from thirst and exhaustion. At Umarkot, he found shelter with the Hindu Rajput ruler Rana Prasad.

Akbar was born at Umarkot on 15 October 1542. According to a well-known tradition, Humayun, with little to give his followers on the occasion, distributed pods of musk and expressed the hope that the child’s fame would spread like its scent. The story comes from later narratives and may be embroidered, but it conveys the emotional setting of an exiled ruler whose fortunes had reached their lowest point. In 1543 Humayun left for Kandahar and then Iran, leaving the infant and his wife in the care of others.

The uncertainties of Humayun’s position in 1540–1541 are apparent in the choices available to him. From Lahore he could have turned to the Punjab hills, to Kashmir, or to Kabul, where Kamran had already gone, but each of these options carried risk. Sindh seemed to offer an independent base, but its ruler, Shah Husain Arghun, treated Humayun as a threat and starved him of support. A long siege of the fortress of Bhakkar produced no result, and as the army’s supplies dwindled, desertions mounted. Hindal, who had been among Humayun’s more reliable supporters, drifted away, and Askari too left. The marriage with Hamida Banu at Patri, in the midst of this crisis, was an act of both personal and political significance, since it connected Humayun with a family that had ties to the court, and it produced the son who would become his successor.

The decision to turn to Rajasthan reflects the hope that Rajput rulers who feared Sher Shah might give shelter. Maldeo of Marwar had invited Humayun, but as Sher Shah’s strength became evident, he withdrew the offer. Humayun’s party, now small, was traveling through the Thar desert in the heat of summer, and the sources describe terrible hardships, with a shortage of water and the death of animals and people. In this setting, the hospitality of the Rajput ruler of Umarkot, Rana Prasad, was crucial. He provided shelter, and the birth of Akbar in October 1542 occurred under his protection. It is worth noting that Hindu and Muslim rulers alike both aided and refused him in these years, further evidence that the political landscape was too complicated to be described in terms of a religious divide.

Humayun’s efforts to find support in Sindh in 1543 failed, and he decided to move to Kandahar and then to Iran. The infant Akbar was left behind and was later seized by Askari and brought up in Kandahar and Kabul, in the household of his uncles, a fact that shaped his childhood. The exile in Sindh and Rajasthan produced little in the way of military success, but it ensured that the dynasty would survive. It also gave Humayun a long period in which to reflect on his failures and to recognize that restoration depended on securing a base and the support of a powerful ally.

Section 50.32

The Safavid Refuge and Shah Tahmasp

In 1543–1544 Humayun crossed into Safavid territory in Sistan, was received with ceremony at Herat, and made his way through Mashhad to the court of Shah Tahmasp I at Qazvin. His situation was delicate. He was a refugee king seeking military help from a ruler who regarded himself as the leader of Twelver Shi’ism and whose court expected deference. The negotiations continued over many months and involved ceremonial occasions, hunting parties, and the question of religious allegiance.

The Persian sources of the period report that Tahmasp pressed Humayun to adopt the Shi’i confession, and the Mughal narratives, which were written in Akbar’s reign, treat the episode with delicacy. It appears that Humayun accepted some outward signs of association with the Safavid cause, such as wearing the red Qizilbash headgear, but the extent to which he concealed his own Sunni affiliations remains debated. Tahmasp eventually agreed to supply a contingent of several thousand troops under his son Murad Mirza and the commander Budaq Khan, with an understanding that Kandahar would be given to Iran if the campaign succeeded.

The arrangement was therefore not an equal alliance. It involved dependence, bargaining, and mutual suspicion, and Safavid and Mughal accounts do not give identical pictures. Yet the stay at Tahmasp’s court also exposed Humayun to Safavid administration, military practice, and artistic traditions, and he later recruited Iranian officers and painters. The cultural effects of this contact, including the arrival of artists such as Mir Sayyid Ali and Abd al-Samad, are examined in Chapter 56.

The Safavid dimension of Humayun’s exile must be understood in the context of Safavid politics. Shah Tahmasp had ruled since 1524 as a young man, and he claimed religious as well as political leadership over a state that was committed to Twelver Shi’ism. He had fought the Uzbeks in the northeast and the Ottomans in the west, and the status of Kandahar, which lay on the eastern frontier of his realm and had passed between Safavid and Mughal control, was a matter of concern. Offering shelter to a Timurid ruler gave Tahmasp a client who might serve as a counterweight to the Uzbeks and the princes at Kabul, and a claim to Kandahar. The Safavid court’s hospitality was therefore not a simple act of generosity.

The records of the visit demonstrate a mixture of ceremony and pressure. Humayun was met by officials as he traveled through Sistan, Herat, and Mashhad, and he was entertained at the shrines of Shi’i saints and in the royal gardens. His reception at Tahmasp’s court in 1544 involved banquets, gifts, hunts, and long discussions. Safavid chroniclers presented the arrangement as a matter of the Shah’s benevolence, while Mughal writers, preparing their histories under Akbar, softened the account of Humayun’s dependence and the religious demands made on him. The surviving Persian and Mughal narratives agree on the outline but differ on details, and modern historians have used them with careful attention to the interests of their authors.

The long-term effects of the connection exceeded the immediate military aid. Humayun’s stay brought him into contact with the Safavid administrative elite, with architects and painters, and with a court culture that blended Timurid and Turkmen traditions. Iranian nobles and officers who joined him after the recovery of Kandahar became an important group in the Mughal nobility, bringing skills in administration and finance. The Safavid artists of Tahmasp’s workshop who joined Humayun at Kabul, notably Mir Sayyid Ali and Abd al-Samad, trained a generation of painters who contributed to the emergence of the Mughal style. These effects were gradual and not part of Humayun’s immediate calculations, but they were among the most significant consequences of the exile.

Section 50.33

Kandahar, Kabul, and the Recovery of Timurid Power, 1545–1553

With Safavid troops, Humayun besieged Kandahar, which was held by his brother Askari, and took it in 1545. When Prince Murad died soon afterward, Humayun kept the city instead of handing it to the Safavids, an act that required diplomacy with Tahmasp. In November 1545 he entered Kabul, where he was reunited with his son Akbar. The recovery of Kabul and Kandahar restored a base in the region that had sustained Babur.

Securing it proved difficult. Kamran, who had held Kabul under his own authority, drove Humayun out again in 1546, and Humayun recovered the city the next year. For years the brothers struggled for control of Kabul and the surrounding regions, with shifting allegiances among nobles and local tribes. Hindal was killed in 1551 while serving Humayun. In 1553 Kamran, who had been captured by the Gakhar ruler Sultan Adam and delivered to Humayun, was blinded, an act that his brother reluctantly sanctioned under pressure from his officers. He was then permitted to go on pilgrimage to Mecca, where he died in 1557. Askari also traveled on pilgrimage, dying in 1558.

These years restored Humayun’s authority within the dynasty and rebuilt an army and court. They also re-established the cultural character of his household, with Persianate scholars, poets, and artists joining it in Kabul. Whatever the personal cost, the elimination of the brothers as rivals removed the most persistent source of Mughal instability. When the opportunity to return to Hindustan came, Humayun was far better placed to take advantage of it.

The recovery of Kandahar involved a siege that lasted from the summer to the early autumn of 1545, ending with its surrender to Humayun and the Safavid forces. Askari was captured and, after a period of confinement, brought along in Humayun’s camp. The question of the city’s ultimate disposition was delicate, since Humayun had promised it to Iran in return for assistance, and when Prince Murad Mirza died soon afterward, Humayun kept the city under his own control, to the Shah’s displeasure. Tahmasp did not press the matter at once, perhaps because of other concerns. Keeping Kandahar gave Humayun a southern base from which he could threaten Kabul.

The struggle for Kabul was long and complicated. Humayun entered Kabul in November 1545 and reunited with his son, but Kamran, who had fled to the hills, returned with support from Afghan tribes and in 1546 retook the city. In 1547 Humayun defeated him again, only to lose and regain Kabul more than once during the following years. In 1548 Humayun campaigned in Badakhshan, where he was wounded, and in 1549–1550 he led a failed expedition against the Uzbeks of Balkh. These years were hard on his army and finances, and the fluctuating loyalties of Mughal and Afghan officers, who changed sides when they thought their interests demanded it, kept his position uncertain. The final settlement came when Kamran, defeated and in flight, was captured by Sultan Adam Gakhar and handed over to his brother in 1553.

The decision to blind Kamran, taken under pressure from officers who regarded his repeated betrayals as unforgivable, was a grim demonstration of the dynasty’s priorities. Kamran’s later pilgrimage to Mecca, where he died in 1557, and Askari’s, where he died in 1558, removed the brothers from the political scene. Hindal had been killed in 1551. With his brothers gone, Humayun had no rivals within the family, and the Mughals’ ability to act as a unified force was greater than at any time since Babur’s death. Through these years Humayun also gathered Persian scholars, poets, and artists at Kabul, rebuilt a library, and recovered something of the cultured court life that had been lost in the retreat from Hindustan.

Section 50.34

Afghan Fragmentation and the Reconquest of Northern India, 1554–1555

In 1554 Humayun, informed of the confusion in the Sur state, crossed the Indus with a force of Central Asian, Iranian, and Afghan soldiers and began his advance into the Punjab. His commanders included Bairam Khan, a noble of Turkmen origin from a family long in Mughal service, who directed much of the campaign. Lahore was occupied in February 1555. Afghan commanders, divided among competing claimants, were unable to mount a unified defense.

The reconquest depended less on overwhelming force than on exploiting disunity. The Sur regime, built around Sher Shah’s personal energy and the cooperation of Afghan chiefs, had come apart in the struggle for succession. Humayun’s army was well led, had more cohesion than the divided Afghan forces, and benefited from the experience of a long period of campaigning in Kabul and Afghanistan. The speed of the recovery stands in contrast to the lengthy process of Mughal conquest under Babur, and it shows the importance of the political context.

Humayun’s decision to invade Hindustan in 1554 rested on intelligence that the Sur state was fracturing. Islam Shah’s death had led to civil war among his relatives, with several Afghan commanders aspiring to the throne. Humayun assembled a force of about several thousand men, drawn from his Central Asian followers, Iranian nobles, and Afghan and other local allies, and he crossed the Indus in the winter of 1554. The numbers in the sources are inconsistent, and modern historians treat them cautiously, but there is agreement that the Mughal army was small compared with the forces that Sher Shah had commanded. Its advantage lay in its cohesion and in the disorder of the Surs.

Bairam Khan led the advance in the Punjab. After the occupation of Lahore in February 1555, the Mughals moved southeast along the Sutlej. In May, at Machhiwara, a Mughal force defeated an Afghan detachment under commanders including Tatar Khan who had been sent by Sikandar Sur to block the crossing, and this success opened the way to the plains. Sikandar then advanced in person with a larger army, and the two forces met near Sirhind on 22 June. The Mughals, drawing on the defensive tactics that they had used under Babur, fought from a prepared position, and the Afghan army, which outnumbered them, was defeated after a hard fight. Sikandar retreated toward the Siwalik hills, leaving his future submission and the contest for the Punjab to the next reign.

The path to Delhi was now open, and the recovery of the city did not require a siege. Within the Sur camp, rivalries had undermined the capacity to resist: Ibrahim Sur had briefly contested Delhi but was displaced by Sikandar, and their rivalry deprived the Surs of an opportunity to unite. Humayun’s entry into Delhi in July 1555 was accompanied by the usual ceremonies, and the khutba was read in his name. But the Afghans had not been eliminated, and Hemu, acting for Adil Shah, was still powerful in the east. The Mughal restoration was therefore the result of a combination of Mughal military professionalism and the weakness of an opponent distracted by civil war.

Section 50.35

Humayun’s Restoration in 1555 and Death in 1556

After the recovery of Delhi and Agra, Humayun faced the same problems that had undermined him in 1540. Afghan leaders had not been eliminated, Hemu and others remained active in the east, and revenue, supplies, and loyalties had to be reorganized. He appointed governors, including Akbar in the Punjab under Bairam Khan’s guidance, and began to plan campaigns against the remaining Afghan forces. Many of the officers who had followed him from Kabul expected rewards, and the redistribution of territories proceeded under pressure.

On 24 January 1556, while descending the stairs of the Sher Mandal, an octagonal pavilion in the Purana Qila complex whose original patronage remains disputed between Sher Shah and Humayun, and which Humayun used as a library, he heard the call to prayer, hurried to kneel, and slipped. He died a few days later. His mausoleum, developed in the 1560s and traditionally associated with the patronage of his senior widow Bega Begum (Haji Begum), with Akbar also playing a role in the dynastic project, is treated in Chapter 56.

The succession was uncertain. Akbar, thirteen years old, was in the Punjab with Bairam Khan, and he was enthroned at Kalanaur on 14 February 1556. His accession, the immediate challenge from Hemu, and the Second Battle of Panipat belong to Chapter 51. What needs emphasis here is that the restoration of 1555 was brief and incomplete. Humayun had reestablished Mughal claims but had little time to build institutions, and the empire passed to a minor under a regent, with Afghans still powerful and the dynasty’s survival in doubt.

The first months of the restoration were occupied by the practical problems of government. Humayun dispatched officers to hold the captured provinces, to collect revenue, and to deal with the Afghans who remained in the Doab and the east. Revenue was needed to pay the army, and the officers who had come from Kabul, including many Central Asians and Iranians, expected rewards. The region around Delhi and Agra was reported to be suffering from scarcity in this period, which complicated the effort to collect revenue and to feed an army. Surviving Persian chronicles mention few specific measures, which suggests that the regime was still improvising.

Humayun’s death and its aftermath were handled with caution. The accident that occurred in January 1556 came when the emperor was at the height of his recovery and was planning further campaigns, and the Mughal nobles faced a delicate task of securing order while the heir was far away in the Punjab. Some later accounts say that the news of his death was withheld for several days to prevent disturbances, a report that cannot be verified. Bairam Khan acted quickly to arrange for Akbar’s formal accession, which took place on 14 February 1556 at Kalanaur. The speed of this arrangement is a reminder that the Mughal leadership understood how dangerous an interregnum could be.

The situation Akbar inherited was perilous. Hemu, who had won victories over Afghan rivals in the east, remained a formidable potential opponent of the restored Mughals, while the Surs still held areas of the Punjab, Rajasthan, and Bengal. The Mughals’ power consisted of a limited area around Delhi and Agra, the Punjab, and the supply line to Kabul. How the new regime survived this crisis, through the second battle of Panipat and the assertion of Bairam Khan’s regency, is the subject of Chapter 51. For this chapter, the essential point is that the restoration of 1555 was a precarious beginning that depended on the continued disunity of the Afghans and the ability of a boy and his guardian to hold the army together.

Section 50.36

Comparing Lodi, Early Mughal, and Sur Institutions

The three regimes that ruled northern India between 1500 and 1556 shared more than is sometimes recognized. All relied on revenue assignments to maintain military followings, all used Persian as the language of administration, all depended on local intermediaries such as qanungos and village headmen, and all legitimized themselves through Islamic kingship adapted to the Indian setting. Their differences lay in the degree to which they centralized authority, standardized procedures, and integrated their nobility.

The sources for institutional comparison are uneven, which affects what can be said. For the Lodi period, historians depend on later Afghan chronicles and on the Sultanate tradition of histories and manuals, as well as on a few inscriptions and coins. For the early Mughals, the Baburnama gives unusual detail on the conquest but little on administration, while the chronicles of Akbar’s reign describe the earlier period through the lens of later achievements. For the Surs, Sarwani and the other Afghan chroniclers provide the main narrative, with coins and buildings as the crucial material evidence. Each regime is thus seen through sources that were produced for or under a successor, which means that the portraits of Lodi weakness, Mughal vision, and Sur competence all bear traces of later purposes.

With that caution, several patterns emerge. In the relationship between ruler and nobility, the Lodis operated within an Afghan tradition in which chiefs commanded their own followers and expected consultation. Ibrahim’s attempt to override that tradition provoked rebellion, which suggests that the tradition was too strong to be overturned by command. The early Mughals brought a different tradition, in which the ruler was the head of a dynasty with a charismatic claim, and officers were bound to him by personal service and gifts. But the family’s collective claims limited the ruler’s freedom, and the officers’ loyalties were conditional. Sher Shah tried a third solution: to draw on Afghan military networks while limiting the autonomy of the chiefs by transfers, mixed contingents, and direct payments, a strategy that worked while he lived but depended on his personality.

In military organization, the three systems rested on revenue assignments to maintain troops, but they differed in the balance between private retinues and central control. The Lodi army was built around chiefs and their contingents, with elephants as a prominent arm. The early Mughal army combined mounted archers, matchlockmen, and artillery and used field fortification, but it relied on officers who recruited and paid their own men. The Sur army, as described in the sources, included a standing force paid directly from the treasury and subject to inspection, with descriptive rolls and the branding of horses, which had been used in the Khalji period and which were revived. Direct payment gave the king greater control but required more cash revenue, which in turn depended on the monetary and assessment reforms already described.

In relation to local society, all three regimes depended on landholders, headmen, and record-keepers whose authority rested on local knowledge. The Lodis left much to these intermediaries, the Mughals under Babur and Humayun relied on them because they had no alternative, and the Surs tried to supervise them by means of the sarkar and pargana officers and the principle of collective responsibility. None of the regimes had an apparatus capable of eliminating them, and a further stage of development, in which the center would define the rights of landholders and the rules of assessment and rank, would come under Akbar. The comparison thus shows a continuum rather than a break, in which the Surs moved furthest toward a more bureaucratic structure but remained within the limits of the Sultanate heritage. Chapter 51 examines the mansabdari system, which belongs to Akbar’s reign, and Chapter 52 treats his revenue reforms.

Section 50.37

The Unstable Foundations of the Mughal Empire, 1526–1556

Between 1526 and 1556 political control in northern India changed hands repeatedly. A Central Asian invader overthrew the Lodi Sultan, an Afghan leader expelled the Mughal heir, a Sur dynasty collapsed in civil war, and the exiled Mughal returned, only to die within a year. These shifts show that the institutions of the Delhi region did not provide automatic continuity. Revenue systems, intermediaries, and commercial networks persisted beneath the political changes, but the sources of military power and the principles of dynastic authority were unstable.

Several features explain the instability. First, rulers depended on military followings whose loyalty was conditional on rewards, and each regime had to find revenue to sustain them. Second, succession rules were either contested, as in the Timurid and Afghan practices, or poorly regulated, which meant that each ruler’s death threatened a crisis. Third, the fusion of territory and kinship made family members potential rivals. Fourth, the large and varied geography of northern India, with its rivers, forests, hills, and deserts, gave local powers refuge and resources. Finally, no regime had yet built an administrative apparatus that could extend royal control into the villages and bind elites to the center in ways that outlasted the ruler.

The environment imposed its own discipline on the politics of the period. The monsoon, with its heavy rains from June to September, made campaigning difficult in the plains, and rivers such as the Ganges and Yamuna became barriers when in flood. The battles of Panipat and Khanwa took place in the dry spring months, when the ground was firm and armies could move. Chausa, in the first rains of June 1539, and the long delay at Gaur in the monsoon of 1538 show the costs of campaigning at the wrong season. Armies also depended on the harvest cycle for supplies, so that a campaign that ran into a poor crop or a period of scarcity could collapse. The geography of the Gangetic plain, with its great rivers, ravines, and forests, helped local chiefs to resist and enabled commanders such as Sher Khan to use rivers and forts to wear down their opponents.

The stakes were also international. Kabul and Kandahar linked the Mughals to Central Asia and Iran, which meant that Mughal rulers had to consider the Uzbeks, the Safavids, and the Ottomans as well as the Afghans and Rajputs. Humayun’s exile demonstrated that the Mughals could use these connections, but it also showed how dependent they could be on foreign powers. The creation of a dynasty with two centers of gravity, one in Kabul and one in Hindustan, became a feature of the Mughal state for the next century. In this respect the period between 1526 and 1556 laid foundations that the later empire would build upon, even though at the time these seemed to be signs of weakness.

Historians have offered different explanations of why the Mughals returned. One emphasizes the collapse of the Sur regime, whose institutions could not survive the loss of Sher Shah and the rivalries that followed. Another stresses the military and political development of the Mughals themselves during the exile, including the elimination of family rivals, the acquisition of Iranian and Afghan recruits, and the experience of command. A third points to the persistence of Mughal legitimacy among a portion of the nobility, who preferred a Timurid ruler to a divided Afghan leadership. The most persuasive accounts combine these factors, recognizing that the Mughal restoration was both an opportunity seized and an outcome that was by no means certain.

Institutions did survive. Persian as an administrative language, the system of revenue assignments, the use of local intermediaries, standard silver coinage, and the main roads outlasted individual rulers. The next generation inherited these continuities, together with the memory of how fragile political success could be. Akbar’s reign would depend on both. Chapter 51 shows how he used the lessons of the preceding thirty years.

What changed?

  • Military technology and tactics: artillery, matchlocks, and field fortifications became more important.
  • The standardization of coinage and the development of road and postal networks under the Surs.

What remained continuous?

  • Reliance on local intermediaries to collect revenue and maintain order.
  • The use of Persian as an administrative language and the use of revenue assignments to maintain armies.
  • The agrarian economy of the Gangetic plain and the revenue it supplied.
  • The importance of Delhi, Agra, and the Doab as the heart of political competition.

Who experienced change most directly?

Military elites, whose fortunes rose and fell with each regime, and the communities of towns and fortresses that bore the direct brunt of sieges. Rulers and nobles experienced the political changes most acutely.

Who experienced greater continuity?

Many cultivators, artisans, and village communities, for whom the main continuity lay in the obligation to pay revenue to whichever authority controlled their area, though they bore the costs of marching armies and shifting demands.

Why the pattern matters:

Recognizing both elements prevents the period from being read as simple chaos or as a prelude to Akbar. It was a time in which an unsettled political order rested on durable social and economic foundations.

Section 50.38

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has followed three overlapping political projects across the years 1494 to 1556. Babur’s conquest drew on his Timurid inheritance, his long experience of loss and recovery in Central Asia, and the weakness of the Lodi state. It was achieved by combining firearms, field fortifications, mounted maneuver, and political opportunism, and it was extended through the victories at Khanwa, Chanderi, and Ghaghra. By his death in 1530, however, Babur had conquered more than he had governed. Humayun inherited a thin administrative structure, a family accustomed to shared authority, and persistent Afghan resistance. His campaigns in Gujarat and Bengal achieved rapid victories that he could not consolidate, and he lost his kingdom at Chausa and Kannauj to Sher Khan, whose political skill and patient strategy made the most of Mughal weaknesses.

Sher Shah’s regime was the period’s most developed state, and the most analytically significant. It combined Afghan military networks with the more systematic exercise of royal authority, a standardized silver currency, an ambitious program of land revenue assessment, and a network of roads, sarais, and communications. Its achievements must be distinguished from earlier Sultanate precedents, from retrospective praise in Sarwani’s chronicle, and from the later work of Akbar’s government. The regime’s fragility, evident after Sher Shah’s death, shows that sound institutions could not substitute for a settled order of succession. Humayun’s exile and return illustrate the opposite lesson: that a dynasty could recover its position by exploiting a rival’s divisions, by drawing on the resources of Kabul and Safavid Iran, and by eliminating the family rivals who had weakened it.

Source limitations shape every part of this account. Babur’s memoir is a precious first-person record, but it presents his own perspective. Gulbadan, Jauhar, and the Akbarnama tradition were written under Akbar, and Sarwani wrote at his command. Rajput, cultivator, and ordinary soldier perspectives are known mostly through the filter of elite or later texts. Coins, inscriptions, forts, mosques, and sarais are indispensable checks, but they provide limited answers to questions about daily life. The political geography of the chapter, running from the Hindu Kush and Kabul through the Punjab, the Doab, Rajasthan, Malwa, and Gujarat to Bihar and Bengal, also reminds students that the modern borders of South Asia did not exist, and that the region was a field of shifting alliances.

The chapter prepares for what follows. Akbar inherited a realm in which Mughal rule had been twice established and once lost, in which Afghan power remained formidable, and in which institutions existed only in fragmentary and competing forms. His reign, examined in Chapters 51 and 52, would draw on Mughal, Afghan, and Sultanate precedents to build a more durable order. Questions for further study include: How might the period look if told from the perspective of Afghan chiefs or Rajput rulers? How much of Sher Shah’s administration can be confirmed independently? What did the experience of exile contribute to the later cultural outlook of the Mughal court? And how should historians weigh contingency and structure in explaining why the Mughals returned?

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the unstable first phase of Mughal expansion, from Babur’s accession in Ferghana in 1494 to Humayun’s death in 1556, and has argued that the conquest of northern India in 1526 did not by itself create a stable imperial system. Three overlapping political projects shaped the period: Babur’s conquest, Humayun’s loss and recovery of his inheritance, and Sher Shah’s Afghan empire.

The chapter began with Babur’s Timurid and Chagatai background, his repeated failures to hold Samarkand against the Uzbeks, and his construction of a new power base at Kabul. These experiences gave him military and political skills and a pragmatic understanding of the value of resources. The Baburnama, his Turkic memoir, is a vivid first-person source but a selective one. The Lodi Sultanate he confronted was weakened by the tension between Ibrahim Lodi’s efforts to assert royal authority and the traditions of Afghan chiefs, and the Punjab’s governor and Lodi relatives looked for outside support.

Babur’s invasion proceeded through a series of Punjab campaigns and the First Battle of Panipat on 21 April 1526, where field fortification, matchlocks, artillery, and flanking maneuver defeated a larger Lodi army. Historians debate how much weight to give to gunpowder weapons, and the best-supported view treats them as one element in a combination of tactics, leadership, and enemy disunity. The capture of Delhi and Agra gave Babur treasuries and legitimacy, but not an administration, and the choice to stay in Hindustan was a major decision for an army reluctant to remain.

The battle of Khanwa on 16 March 1527 defeated a coalition led by Rana Sanga of Mewar and including Muslim Afghan and Mewati leaders. The religious language Babur used before the battle was a tool of mobilization within a contest for political predominance, and the episode illustrates the danger of reading the period through simple Hindu-Muslim confrontations. The later capture of Chanderi and the victory at Ghaghra extended Mughal reach but did not eliminate Rajput or Afghan power. At his death in 1530 Babur ruled a vast area held together by personal authority and a few fortified positions, lacking a stable administrative system or clear rules for sharing power within his family.

Humayun’s reign showed the cost of this inheritance. His half-brothers held independent commands, Afghan chiefs in Bihar and Jaunpur remained powerful, and revenue was insufficient to maintain a large army. His conquest of Gujarat in 1535–1536 revealed a pattern of quick military success without consolidation, and his eastern campaign allowed Sher Khan to cut his communications and defeat him at Chausa in 1539 and Kannauj in 1540. Historians debate whether personal failings or structural conditions were more important, and the strongest explanations combine the two.

Sher Shah’s rise from a Bihar jagirdar to ruler of northern India rested on Afghan political networks, the capture of strongholds, and patient strategy. His regime, from 1540 to 1545, built the period’s most developed state: a standing army paid and supervised from the center, a sarkar and pargana administration, a reported program of land measurement and revenue assessment, a standardized silver rupiya, and a network of roads, sarais, and communications. Many of these measures had precedents in earlier Sultanate government, and the detailed picture of the regime comes largely from Sarwani’s retrospective chronicle, so historians distinguish what is well documented, such as the coinage and certain fortifications, from what is idealized or uncertain.

After Sher Shah’s death at Kalinjar in 1545, Islam Shah maintained and centralized the regime, but the succession dispute that followed Islam Shah’s death fragmented the Sur state. Meanwhile Humayun had survived exile in Sindh and Rajasthan, the birth of Akbar at Umarkot in 1542, and a difficult association with Shah Tahmasp of Iran. With Safavid help he recovered Kandahar and Kabul, eliminated his brothers as rivals, and rebuilt an army and court. He reconquered northern India in 1555 by exploiting Afghan disunity, defeating Sikandar Sur at Sirhind and entering Delhi.

The restoration was brief. Humayun died in January 1556 after a fall, and his thirteen-year-old son Akbar inherited an empire that had been twice established and once lost, with Afghan power still formidable. A comparison of Lodi, early Mughal, and Sur institutions shows significant continuities, including reliance on revenue assignments, local intermediaries, and Persian administration, along with real differences in the degree of centralization, supervision, and standardization. Sher Shah’s practices influenced Akbar’s government, but the Mughals were also heirs to Sultanate and Timurid traditions.

The chapter has emphasized that this period was not simply a prelude to Akbar. It was an era in which several ways of organizing military power, revenue, and kingship competed, and in which the outcome was uncertain. Understanding that uncertainty is essential to understanding why Akbar’s later consolidation was a significant achievement, which Chapters 51 and 52 examine.

Key Terms

Timurid
A descendant of Timur, or the court culture associated with his heirs in Central Asia and Iran; Babur’s primary dynastic identity.
Chinggisid
Descended from Chinggis Khan; a form of Inner Asian legitimacy that Babur held through his mother.
Chagatai Turkic
The Turkic literary language of Central Asia in which Babur wrote his memoir.
Baburnama
Babur’s memoir, an observational first-person record of 1494–1529 with significant gaps.
Matchlock
A handheld firearm ignited by a slow-burning match, used by infantry in Babur’s army.
Araba
A wheeled cart linked with others in a defensive line, used by Babur at Panipat and Khanwa.
Tulughma
A flanking and encircling cavalry maneuver, associated with Uzbek and Central Asian warfare.
Khutba
The Friday sermon in which the ruler’s name is proclaimed, a mark of sovereignty.
Ghazi
A warrior for the faith, a title Babur adopted after Khanwa.
Jauhar
A Rajput practice of mass self-immolation when defeat seemed certain, recorded at Chanderi in 1528.
Jagir
A revenue assignment given to an officer in return for military service.
Qanungo
A local official who kept records of revenue and custom at the pargana level.
Muqaddam
A village headman, made responsible under Sher Shah for crime within his village.
Sarkar
An administrative unit comprising several parganas under Sur and Mughal government.
Pargana
A subdivision of a sarkar and a basic unit of revenue administration.
Rai
A schedule of crop rates used in estimating revenue assessments in the Sur system.
Kabuliyat and patta
Written undertaking by the cultivator and state document stating revenue terms.
Rupiya
The standardized silver coin of about 178 grains under Sher Shah, the basis of the later rupee.
Dam
A copper coin used for small transactions.
Sarai
A rest house for travelers along main roads, with a well and supplies.
Dak
The postal and courier system using relays of messengers and horses.
Sadak-i Azam
The great road from eastern Bengal to the Indus, later called the Grand Trunk Road.
Din-panah
The capital city Humayun founded in 1533 near Delhi.
Qizilbash
Red-capped Turkmen warriors who formed the military backbone of the Safavid state.
Zawabit
Regulations issued by Islam Shah Sur governing the army and administration.
Mahdawi movement
A religious community in northern India led by Shaikh Alai, persecuted under Islam Shah.
Qanun-i Humayuni
Khwandamir’s account of the ceremonial and administrative classification of Humayun’s court.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to see the beginning of Mughal history as a period of risk rather than as the opening of a golden age. The dynasty that would later rule much of South Asia for centuries entered the region as one contender among several, lost its position within a decade of winning it, and returned to find the empire it had hoped to restore transformed by the work of a rival. That uncertainty is essential to understanding what followed.

The period also reveals processes that extend beyond its three rulers. The use of firearms and field fortification altered warfare, though it did not decide it. The standardization of coinage, the development of roads, and the elaboration of revenue procedures prefigured the institutional work of later regimes. The tensions between central authority and the claims of nobles, kinsmen, and local intermediaries recurred in different forms through the subcontinent’s history, and the response to them shaped state formation.

The chapter also connects regions that later chapters examine separately. Kabul, Iran, and Central Asia remained vital to the Mughals, and Gujarat, Bengal, Rajasthan, and Malwa shaped the course of events as much as Delhi did. Chapter 51 turns to Akbar, who would inherit this unsettled legacy and, by combining and extending its institutional lessons, create a more durable imperial system. Chapter 52 will show how that system defined government, religion, and kingship, while Chapters 55 and 56 will examine the society and culture of an empire whose first thirty years were far less secure than its later reputation suggests.

Students who understand the instability of 1526–1556 are better prepared to appreciate the later empire as a historical achievement rather than as a natural outcome, and to ask, of every polity in this textbook, what held it together, what threatened it, and who bore the costs.
