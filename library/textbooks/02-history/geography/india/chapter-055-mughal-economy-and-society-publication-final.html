Mughal Economy and Society

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

In the early seventeenth century a cultivator in the Gangetic plain, a weaver in a Bengal village, a Jain merchant carrying bills of exchange between Agra and Jaunpur, and a noblewoman who held commercial interests tied to ships sailing from Surat to the Red Sea were all, in different ways, participants in the same economy. None of them appears in the grand narrative of emperors, battles, and succession wars that occupied Chapters 50 through 54. Yet without them the empire could not have existed. Their labor, credit, and consumption produced the revenue that paid the nobility, fed the armies, and built the monuments that later generations remember.

Chapters 50 through 54 followed the Mughal Empire through its political history: Babur's conquest, Akbar's consolidation, the courts of Jahangir and Shah Jahan, and the long reign of Aurangzeb. This chapter changes the question. Instead of asking who ruled and how, it asks who produced, who traded, who paid, who worked, and who went hungry. Its subject is the material and social foundation of one of the largest states of the early modern world, a state whose territory at its greatest extent, in the late seventeenth century, held a population that modern scholars estimate, with wide margins of uncertainty, at well over one hundred million people.

Most of those people lived in villages and worked the land. The countryside was therefore the base of the imperial order, supplying food for cities and armies, raw materials for manufacture, and the great bulk of state revenue. But Mughal South Asia was not simply an agrarian society with a few courtly cities. It contained one of the world's largest manufacturing sectors, above all in cotton textiles; dense networks of merchants and bankers; busy ports linked to the Persian Gulf, the Red Sea, East Africa, Southeast Asia, and Europe; and a monetary system in which silver rupees circulated widely. Rural and urban economies were closely connected, and even remote villages often sold produce, borrowed money, and paid taxes in cash.

This chapter also insists on a second point: economic strength and popular well-being were not the same thing. The empire could sustain extraordinary wealth at its top while most cultivators, laborers, and poorer townspeople lived close to the margin. Social hierarchies of caste, class, gender, religion, and legal status shaped who gained from economic activity and who bore its burdens. When harvests failed, as in the terrible Gujarat and Deccan famine of 1630-1632, the unequal capacity of households to survive became painfully visible.

The evidence for these subjects is both rich and uneven. The A'in-i Akbari, compiled under Abu'l Fazl around 1595, preserves remarkable statistical and descriptive information, but it was written from the standpoint of the imperial administration. Persian chronicles focus on the court. European merchants and travelers left detailed accounts of ports, workshops, and famines, though they saw only parts of the empire and sometimes misunderstood what they saw. Surviving legal documents, merchant papers, and a remarkable Hindi verse autobiography by the Jain merchant Banarasidas open windows onto ordinary lives. Peasants, women, laborers, and enslaved people rarely wrote for themselves, so historians must read the records of others against the grain.

This chapter is organized in five divisions. The first examines agriculture, peasants, zamindars, and the ecological frontiers of rural society. The second turns to commercial crops, textiles, artisans, and cities. The third follows merchants, credit, internal and maritime trade, European companies, and the silver that circulated through the Mughal monetary system. The fourth reconstructs caste, labor, slavery, women, property, and household life. The fifth assesses living standards, famine and vulnerability, and competing historical interpretations of Mughal prosperity, before the synthesis in Section 55.20. Technical questions of revenue administration belong to Chapter 52, and the fiscal strains of Aurangzeb's wars to Chapter 54; the artistic and intellectual life of the period belongs to Chapter 56.

  4. Trace the connections among commercial crops, textile manufacture, artisan labor, and urban demand.
  5. Distinguish early modern karkhanas, household workshops, and independent artisan production from modern industrial factories.
  6. Assess the roles of Indian merchants, bankers, and brokers in internal and maritime commerce, and the position of European companies within existing Asian trade.

Historical Context

The Mughal economy rested on foundations older than the dynasty. By the sixteenth century, the Delhi Sultanate and regional sultanates had established currency systems, revenue practices, market towns, and merchant networks (Chapters 40-47). Gujarat's ports had long connected the subcontinent to the Arabian Sea; Bengal's rice and textiles were exported across the Bay of Bengal; and the Coromandel coast produced cottons sought in Southeast Asia. Mughal rule, beginning with Babur's victory in 1526 and consolidated under Akbar, unified a very large part of northern and central South Asia under a single fiscal and monetary framework. Chapter 51 examined mansabdari and jagirdari, and Chapter 52 the revenue system; this chapter assumes that background and asks how those arrangements looked from below.

The monsoon governs the economic geography of South Asia. Summer rains support the main autumn harvest (kharif), while winter crops (rabi) depend on residual moisture and irrigation. Rainfall varies enormously: the Bengal delta and the Western Ghats receive heavy rain, while much of Punjab, Rajasthan, Sindh, and the Deccan interior is semi-arid. River systems, wells, and canals extended cultivation, but a delayed or failed monsoon could bring famine. Environmental diversity meant that no single agrarian model could describe the whole empire.

The Mughal period also coincided with a transformation of global commerce. The Portuguese reached the Indian Ocean in 1498 (Chapter 49); the Dutch and English East India Companies were founded in 1602 and 1600 respectively; and silver from the Americas and Japan flowed into Asian markets. Chapter 61 treats the European companies and the competition among them in detail. Here they matter as customers and competitors within an Asian commercial world that was already large and sophisticated.

Finally, this chapter depends on a distinctive body of scholarship. Irfan Habib's The Agrarian System of Mughal India and Shireen Moosvi's studies of Mughal economy and population established a quantitative and documentary approach emphasizing revenue extraction and social inequality. Tapan Raychaudhuri, Satish Chandra, Om Prakash, and others have stressed commercial growth, regional variation, and the agency of Indian merchants and producers. Readers should treat all estimates of population, output, and wages as reasoned approximations rather than established totals, because the Mughal state collected data for its own purposes and much has not survived.

Agriculture, Rural Society, and the Foundations of Economic Life

How food production, environment, cultivators, and rural authority supported the Mughal Empire.

Section 55.1

Agrarian Production, Crops, Irrigation, and the Agricultural Calendar

Agriculture employed the great majority of the population and generated most of the empire's wealth. The staple grains varied by region. Wheat and barley dominated the winter harvest of Punjab and the upper Gangetic plain; rice was the main crop of Bengal, Bihar, eastern Uttar Pradesh, the Malabar and Konkan coasts, and irrigated tracts elsewhere; and millets such as jowar and bajra, together with pulses and oilseeds, sustained the drier regions of Rajasthan, Gujarat, Malwa, and the Deccan. Because dry-land millets needed little water, they allowed cultivation in places where wheat or rice could not be grown.

The agricultural calendar followed the monsoon. Kharif crops, sown with the summer rains and harvested in autumn, included rice, millets, cotton, sugarcane, indigo, and some pulses. Rabi crops, sown after the rains and harvested in spring, included wheat, barley, gram, and mustard. The A'in-i Akbari records both harvests for each province, and the existence of two harvests helped many regions spread labor and risk across the year. The success of both depended on rainfall and, increasingly, on irrigation.

Irrigation took several forms. Wells, lifted by animal-driven devices such as the Persian wheel and the simple bucket-and-rope, were the commonest source of water in the Gangetic plain and Punjab. Tanks and reservoirs stored rainwater in the Deccan and the south, canals diverted river water in parts of Punjab and Sindh, and inundation channels watered fields in the delta. Mughal rulers sponsored some canal works, but most irrigation was built and maintained by cultivators and local landholders. Oxen supplied traction for plough, well, and cart, so ownership of a pair of oxen was often the dividing line between secure and precarious cultivation. Implements remained simple, usually an iron-tipped wooden plough, a seed drill in some regions, and the sickle.

Regional differences were large. Punjab combined well irrigation with canal and flood water and produced wheat, cotton, and sugarcane for markets. The Gangetic plain supported dense settlement and a diverse crop base. Bengal's rainfall and rivers made multiple rice harvests possible and supported a large export economy. Gujarat grew cotton, indigo, and grains. Much of the Deccan rested on black cotton soils suited to cotton and millets but vulnerable to drought. Students should avoid imagining a single "Mughal agriculture."

Historians have also moved away from older descriptions of Indian villages as isolated, self-sufficient units. Cultivators paid revenue in cash in many regions, which required them to sell part of their produce. Crops such as cotton, indigo, sugarcane, and oilseeds were grown largely for sale, and even grain moved through local markets. At the same time subsistence mattered: most households first secured their own food and sold what remained, and sales were often forced by revenue and debt rather than freely chosen. The distinction between subsistence and commercial production was therefore one of degree, not a dividing wall.

The agricultural calendar also illustrates the problem of reconstructing an empire from a single administrative source. A tax assessment could classify the expected yield of a crop, but it did not establish what every household actually harvested, how much seed it retained, or how labor was divided within the family. Cropping intensity changed with soils, access to wells, and the availability of cattle and hired labor. A household able to irrigate a winter field had more options after disappointing summer rains than one cultivating only rain-fed land. Even where two harvests were possible, cultivating both required seed, traction, and enough family or hired workers at the appropriate season. Historians therefore use the A'in's figures as evidence of imperial fiscal knowledge, comparing them with regional documents and environmental history rather than converting them directly into an exact measure of peasant prosperity.

Primary Source Window

The A'in-i Akbari on Provinces, Crops, and Land

Source type: Persian administrative and descriptive compendium · Author: Abu'l Fazl · Date: completed around 1595-1598 · Place: Akbar's court

Context: The A'in-i Akbari is the third volume of Abu'l Fazl's Akbarnama and was written to record the institutions of Akbar's empire. For each province (suba) it provides statistical tables, descriptions of crops, and information on land classification, revenue, and local resources; elsewhere it describes imperial workshops, prices, and wages.

Editorial paraphrase (not a verbatim quotation): Abu'l Fazl classifies cultivated land according to how regularly it was cropped and records assessment rates for individual crops, listing the crops of each harvest and describing regional produce and revenue. Land that was cultivated every year was assessed differently from land left fallow for periods.

Analysis: The A'in is indispensable: it allows historians to estimate cropped areas, revenue demands, and prices that survive nowhere else. But it is a state document, produced by a high official for an imperial purpose, and it tends to present administration as orderly and just. It records what the state wished to measure and tax, not the household economy of the cultivator, and its figures reflect some provinces and periods better than others.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the A'in-i Akbari reveal about the crops and agricultural cycles of Mughal India?
  2. Why would a high imperial official compile such a work, and how might that purpose shape what it includes?
  3. What can historians learn about agricultural production from this source?
  4. What can historians not establish from it, especially about the lives of cultivators themselves?
Section 55.2

Peasants, Cultivating Households, and Village Communities

The basic unit of rural production was the cultivating household, which supplied most of its own labor. Persian sources use terms such as raiyat and muzari'an for those who farmed; regional vernaculars used many others. Cultivators were not a uniform class. Some, often from locally dominant castes, held substantial plots, possessed several oxen, and employed laborers; many held only small plots; and others, including agricultural laborers, held no land at all. The economic distance between a prosperous farmer and a landless worker could be as great as that between the farmer and a petty noble.

The village community was a social and administrative body. The headman, called muqaddam or by other regional terms, represented villagers to revenue officials and often helped collect revenue, for which he received privileges. The village accountant, the patwari, kept records of fields and payments. Village artisans, such as carpenters, blacksmiths, potters, and leatherworkers, served the community and were commonly paid in shares of grain or customary dues, while other service groups included barbers, washermen, and watchmen. Village councils, often built around caste or kin groups, settled disputes, regulated customs, and organized collective obligations, including the upkeep of wells and tanks.

Differences among villagers mattered greatly. Landholding rights were usually tied to hereditary occupancy, but in many regions they also reflected caste and dominance within the village: higher-status groups typically held better land and larger shares. Resident cultivators, who belonged to the village and held hereditary claims, differed from non-resident or migrant cultivators who farmed land in a village without belonging to it. Migrants could be important when land was abundant and labor scarce, and landholders competed for them by offering favorable terms. The ability of discontented cultivators to move was one of the few checks on oppressive demands, although flight meant leaving fields, kin, and shelter.

For the household, the demands of subsistence, rent, and taxation had to be reconciled each year. After setting aside seed and food, a family paid the revenue demand, often in cash, and settled debts to moneylenders; the remainder might be sold. When revenue was set high, as scholars such as Irfan Habib have argued it often was, little remained, and poorer households borrowed to meet payments, falling into debt that could become dependency. Family labor was crucial: women, children, and the elderly all worked in tasks from weeding and harvesting to threshing, tending cattle, and processing grain. This work was rarely recorded by officials, but it was essential to cultivation.

The village should not be imagined as a single corporate household whose members shared resources equally. Landholders and those without secure holdings faced different obligations; dominant lineages could influence how taxes were divided and which common resources remained accessible. Services provided by artisans and village functionaries might be compensated in customary shares of grain, cash, land assignments, or mixtures of these, according to local practice. A headman could negotiate collectively with an official and still distribute burdens unequally among neighbors. Nor was rural settlement motionless: marriage, debt, service in armies, market opportunities, and flight from excessive demands all moved people between villages and towns. The contrast between resident and incoming cultivators mattered particularly on agricultural frontiers where local elites needed to attract labor to bring new land under the plough. These relationships made village society resilient in some circumstances but capable of intense internal conflict in others.

Section 55.3

Zamindars, Land Rights, and Rural Power

Zamindars were among the most important and most variable figures in Mughal society. The word, from Persian, means holder of land, but it covered a wide range: from village-level proprietors who held a share of the village's produce, to powerful hereditary chiefs whose territories were as large as districts and who commanded armed retainers. Their rights were usually hereditary and could be bought, sold, or divided, and Mughal authorities generally recognized them. In return, zamindars were expected to assist in collecting revenue, keeping order, and providing troops when required. Where Chapter 52 examined the revenue system as administration, this section examines zamindars as a social and economic power.

There was consequently no single chain of ownership linking emperor, jagirdar, zamindar, and peasant in the manner of a later modern land registry. Different actors could hold distinct claims over cultivation, collection, customary dues, local authority, and military service at the same time. A cultivator might possess a recognized right to continue farming a plot without owning every revenue right attached to it; a zamindar might receive hereditary dues without directly tilling the fields; and a jagirdar's assignment normally concerned a revenue claim rather than an outright grant of every local proprietary right. Where such claims collided, resistance could take the form of litigation or negotiation, concealment of cultivation, withholding payment, or armed conflict. Reading Mughal land relations through these layered rights better explains both the empire's dependence on local intermediaries and the limits of its ability to command village life from a distant capital.

Historical Vocabulary
Zamindar

A zamindar was a person holding hereditary rights over land and its produce, usually including a claim to a share of the revenue collected from cultivators. The term covered village headmen with limited rights and territorial magnates with fortified strongholds and followers. In the Mughal period the zamindar was not the same as the nineteenth-century landlord created under the Permanent Settlement (Chapter 63), who held stronger and more exclusive property rights; reading later colonial arrangements back into Mughal practice is a common error.

Imperial officials and zamindars occupied overlapping but competing positions. Jagirdars (Chapter 51), who received temporary assignments of revenue in lieu of salary, collected revenue from lands whose zamindars lived there permanently and held local influence. Because jagirdars were transferred frequently, zamindars often possessed far more local knowledge and authority than the officials who taxed them. This gave zamindars leverage. They could assist assessments, shield the cultivators or exploit them, and, when displeased, withhold revenue or rebel.

Zamindars' relations with cultivators were unequal and contradictory. They claimed a share of the produce and often exercised judicial and customary authority, and some extracted additional dues. But they also had an interest in maintaining a settled, productive peasantry, and many founded villages, dug tanks and wells, and encouraged settlers. Many cultivators looked to local zamindars for protection against revenue officials, which helps explain why zamindars could mobilize rural support. Caste ties frequently connected zamindars to the cultivators of a locality: in many regions the zamindars and a large share of the cultivators belonged to the same caste or clan, which gave resistance to imperial demands a social basis.

Zamindars were also regional political figures. Some, especially in Rajasthan, Bundelkhand, the hill borderlands, and parts of Bengal, were chiefs of considerable standing, and the Mughals incorporated many into the imperial system as mansabdars or tributaries, as discussed in Chapters 51 and 54. Others resisted taxation and imperial authority. Historians such as Irfan Habib have argued that the demands of the imperial system and of intermediaries contributed to agrarian unrest in several regions in the seventeenth century, and that zamindar leadership often gave such unrest organization. Others emphasize that these conflicts were shaped by local politics, caste solidarity, and religion as well as by revenue pressure. Both interpretations agree that the zamindar was neither a mere agent of the state nor an independent lord, but a negotiator between them.

Section 55.4

Forests, Pastoral Communities, Land Reclamation, and Agrarian Expansion

Large areas of Mughal South Asia were not densely cultivated. Forests, grasslands, marshes, and semi-arid tracts supported people who lived differently from settled cultivators: foragers and hunters; shifting cultivators who cleared and burned patches of forest; and pastoral groups who moved livestock seasonally. The forests supplied timber, bamboo, lac, honey, wax, and medicinal plants, and they sheltered game and elephants, which the Mughal court prized, so imperial agents captured wild elephants for its stables. Pastoral communities such as the Gujars, Ahirs, Rabaris, and Banjaras supplied animals, dairy products, and transport. Banjaras in particular, as mobile carriers of grain and salt, were important in moving goods between regions.

Relations between settled and mobile peoples were varied. Pastoralists and forest dwellers traded with villages, supplied labor and goods, and were sometimes taxed; the Mughal state and zamindars also campaigned against communities regarded as resistant to authority. Some groups moved between pastoralism, cultivation, and military service. Expanding cultivation often reduced pastures and forests, and the extension of fields into formerly wild land affected the livelihood of the people who used it. The ecological diversity of South Asia meant that frontiers between these ways of life were frequently contested.

The most notable agrarian expansion occurred in the Bengal delta. Over the Mughal centuries the zone of rice cultivation moved steadily eastward, as rivers shifted their courses and settlers cleared forests and drained marshes in what is now eastern Bengal. Richard Eaton has argued that Muslim holy men, shrines, and religious leaders played a significant role in this process, organizing settlement and obtaining land grants for local communities, and that the spread of Islam in eastern Bengal was linked to the agrarian frontier. This interpretation has been debated, but it shows how religious institutions and agricultural expansion could interact. The Mughals strengthened the process by establishing provincial government at Dhaka and encouraging cultivation.

Expansion had human costs as well as benefits. Clearing forests increased food production and revenue but reduced the resources of forest communities. Access to land depended on caste, kin, connections, and power, so the benefits of new land often went to those already advantaged. In Bengal, raids by Arakanese and Portuguese pirates on the lower delta captured people for sale as slaves and discouraged settlement in the south, a reminder that frontiers could be dangerous. The central point is that South Asia's ecological diversity prevented a single agrarian model from applying everywhere: the same empire included settled irrigated plains, expanding delta frontiers, dry-land millet zones, hill forests, and pastoral grasslands.

The Bengal case also brings the changing landscape into the history of religion and settlement. As the Ganges-Brahmaputra river systems altered their channels, older western centers and new eastern tracts could experience different opportunities. Cultivating new wetlands required labor, embankments or drainage, access to seed and animals, and some guarantee that pioneers could enjoy the product of their work. Shrines and locally respected religious figures could function as institutions of settlement as well as devotion; Eaton's argument is about historically specific processes rather than the assumption that an entire population converted at once. The lower delta was also a region of coercion: armed groups operating from the Arakanese frontier, at times with Portuguese participants, raided settlements and took captives. The Mughal conquest of Chittagong in 1666 affected this frontier, but it did not erase the economic and ecological diversity of Bengal. The example makes agrarian expansion a history of institutional bargaining, environmental change, and conflict rather than simply the opening of supposedly empty land.

Textiles, Manufacturing, Labor, and Urban Economic Life

How rural raw materials became manufactured goods and how skilled labor, urban demand, and commerce supported a large non-agricultural economy.

Section 55.5

Agricultural Surplus, Commercial Crops, and Rural Markets

Agriculture supported manufacturing and towns because cultivators produced more than they consumed. This surplus, however, was partly taken by the state and intermediaries and partly sold. The grain that fed cities, armies, and artisans came from villages, usually through local traders. Armies on the march and the imperial camp required enormous supplies; Banjara carriers and grain merchants moved grain to them and to urban markets, and rulers recognized that supplying the camp was a major commercial opportunity.

Several crops were grown mainly for sale. Cotton, the basis of the textile industry, was widely cultivated in Gujarat, Malwa, the Deccan, Punjab, and Bengal. Indigo, a blue dye, was grown in Gujarat near Sarkhej and Broach, and in the Agra region around Bayana, and was exported to Europe and West Asia in large quantities. Sugarcane, processed into raw sugar and jaggery, was important in Bengal, Uttar Pradesh, and Punjab. Oilseeds supplied cooking and lamp oil. Opium was produced in Bihar and Malwa and used in India and abroad, though its great expansion as an export crop belongs to the colonial period (Chapter 66). Silk was produced in Bengal, notably around Kasimbazar. The A'in-i Akbari lists many such crops, often at higher revenue rates than grains, which indicates that the state expected them to bring cash returns.

Cultivators of cash crops depended on merchants and moneylenders. Merchants and brokers advanced money to cultivators or artisans before the harvest, to be repaid in produce, a practice that gave merchants control over prices and supply. Village moneylenders, often traders from local trading castes, lent seed, food, and money for revenue, at interest. Credit made commercial agriculture possible, but it also bound poorer households to creditors. The revenue demand, which often required payment in cash soon after harvest, forced many cultivators to sell when prices were lowest, a point emphasized by Habib. Other scholars note that cultivators of cash crops sometimes did well when demand was high, so regional experience varied.

Rural markets completed the system. Local bazaars and periodic markets, held on fixed days in market villages, allowed cultivators and artisans to exchange goods, and larger market towns (qasbas) drew produce from surrounding villages. This network linked even small cultivators to commercial circuits, and its existence is one of the main reasons that historians reject the picture of an isolated village economy.

Commercialization had several possible meanings for a cultivator. In favorable circumstances, a grower able to choose cotton or indigo in response to demand might use the proceeds to purchase food, livestock, tools, or land rights. Under pressure, the same grower might have to market grain immediately after harvest to pay a fixed obligation and then repurchase food at a higher price during the lean season. An advance could provide capital before production, but it might also restrict the recipient's choice of buyer. This tension discourages a simple division of villages into a subsistence sector and towns into a market sector. Currency, credit, and commodity exchange linked the two, while unequal property, labor, and bargaining power determined whose participation yielded gains. The history of rural markets is therefore also a history of distribution: the existence of more transactions does not show, by itself, how much of the benefit remained in producing households.

Section 55.6

Textile Production: Cotton, Silk, Weaving, and Dyeing

Textiles were the most important manufactured goods of Mughal South Asia and among the most important in the world economy. Cotton cloth clothed most of the subcontinent's population and was exported in large quantities to West Asia, East Africa, Southeast Asia, and Europe. Production involved a long chain of skilled work: ginning and carding raw cotton, spinning thread, weaving, bleaching, dyeing, printing, and finishing. Spinning was done chiefly by women in their homes, often as a supplement to household income; weaving was usually carried out by men in weaver households, though women assisted in preparation. Because it required many spinners to supply one weaver, textile production offered employment to very large numbers of rural households.

Production was regionally specialized. Gujarat, with centers at Ahmedabad, Cambay, Broach, and Surat, produced plain and printed cottons, silks, and brocades, and exported them through its ports. Bengal was famous for very fine muslins, of which Dhaka's were the most celebrated, and for silk from Kasimbazar. The Coromandel coast, around Masulipatnam and Pulicat, produced painted and printed cottons, known to Europeans as chintz, which were prized for their colorfast dyes. Lahore, Multan, Benares, and Burhanpur were also textile centers, and Benares was known for brocades with gold and silver thread. Dyeing was a specialized craft: indigo supplied blues, and other dyes included madder, lac, and turmeric. Block printing and hand painting created patterns, and local water and mordants affected quality.

The organization of production was largely household-based. Weavers usually worked at home with their own looms, often on credit advanced by merchants or their agents, who supplied money or thread and collected the finished cloth. The merchant's advance gave him a claim on the weaver's output and, in practice, often tied weavers to particular merchants, though weavers could sometimes shift between buyers when demand was high. European companies, from the seventeenth century, adopted the same system of advances, which is why they depended on Indian brokers and merchants to organize supply. Much later, in the colonial period, such arrangements were used to impose stricter control (Chapter 66).

The economic gains from textiles were unevenly shared. Skilled weavers of fine cloth might earn a reasonable living, and weavers' guilds and caste organizations protected their interests, but spinners were often poorly paid, and many weavers were indebted. Women's labor in spinning was an essential but poorly paid and rarely recorded part of the process. Scholars therefore emphasize both the large scale of Indian textile manufacture and the need to avoid exaggerated claims about its output or its dominance of world markets: it was an extremely important sector, but one whose exact size cannot be measured.

Descriptions of 'Indian cotton cloth' conceal a commercial world of carefully differentiated goods. Cloth was sold by dimensions, weave, finish, color, and intended market; the requirements of consumers in Southeast Asia were not necessarily those of customers in the Persian Gulf or Europe. Merchants communicated orders, provided patterns or samples, advanced funds, and inspected the cloth before shipment. The apparent independence of a household loom could coexist with strong commercial control over specifications, deadlines, and prices. A defective dye, a late delivery, or a change in demand could shift losses onto weavers and their creditors. Textile history must also distinguish the great production zones from political boundaries: much of the Coromandel coast lay outside the Mughal Empire, even as its commerce interacted closely with Mughal Bengal, Gujarat, and Deccan markets. Its inclusion in this chapter concerns regional economic connections, not a claim that all textile centers were governed directly from Delhi.

Section 55.7

Artisans, Workshops, and Non-Textile Manufacturing

Beyond textiles, Mughal artisans produced an enormous variety of goods. Metalworkers made agricultural tools, weapons, utensils, and, on a large scale, guns and cannon; jewelers and goldsmiths worked gold, silver, and precious stones for the court and wealthy patrons; potters, leatherworkers, papermakers, and carpenters supplied everyday needs. Sugar was refined in many regions, saltpetre was produced in Bihar for gunpowder and for export, and construction required masons, brick makers, and lime burners whose work built forts, mosques, tombs, and cities. Shipbuilding flourished at Gujarat, Bengal, and on the Malabar coast, where teak and skilled labor supported vessels that sailed across the Indian Ocean.

The practical division between state and private production was less rigid than a modern distinction between public and private industries. Court establishments could purchase materials from merchants, hire skilled people already connected to outside workshops, and commission goods that entered wider networks of exchange. Merchants might organize temporary concentrations of labor for a large order without owning all the tools or premises used in production. The payment system also varied: cash wages, piece rates, rations, advances, and obligations attached to service could coexist. In his study of Mughal labor, Irfan Habib emphasizes that wage earners, dependent workers, self-employed artisans, and family producers formed different categories, even when they made similar goods. This helps explain why a city full of skilled craftspeople should not automatically be treated as a center of modern factory employment, and why analysis of output alone can disguise differences in control over working time and earnings.

Historical Vocabulary
Karkhana

A karkhana was a workshop. The term is used both for imperial and noble establishments that produced goods for the court and household, such as arms, textiles, jewelry, tents, and stable and kitchen supplies, and for private workshops. It should not be translated simply as "factory." Imperial karkhanas combined skilled craftsmen working under the supervision of officials and were organized to meet the needs of the court, not primarily to sell on the market. They differed from modern factories in power sources, division of labor, and the relationship between worker and owner.

Production took several forms, and the distinctions matter. Most artisans worked independently in household workshops, owning their tools and selling to local customers or to merchants. Others worked within karkhanas, where they received wages or rations and where the products belonged to the patron. The imperial workshops at the major cities employed large numbers of skilled specialists, and nobles maintained smaller ones to supply their households. Some craftsmen were attached to patrons as dependent service providers, and in the towns some worked for wages in the workshops of merchants. Family labor was central in all of these settings, and skills were generally passed down within caste or kin groups, which gave crafts a hereditary character, though craft communities also admitted newcomers and moved between occupations in some regions.

Artisans lived in a market in which the court, the nobility, and the army were the largest consumers. Demand from nobles, who spent their incomes in cities, supported luxury crafts, and imperial patronage attracted skilled workers from Persia and Central Asia as well as from India. Merchants connected artisans to wider markets and supplied raw materials. Craft communities were organized in caste and trade groups that regulated entry, standards, and disputes and negotiated with authorities. Not all artisans benefited equally. Master craftsmen working for the court could do well, but many ordinary workers earned very little, and their wages are discussed further in Section 55.17.

It would be a mistake to describe Mughal workshops as industrial factories. They did not rely on mechanical power, did not use the machinery of later centuries, and operated through hand skills, household labor, and patronage. Nevertheless, the scale of production, the specialization of skills, and the organization of supply through merchants indicate a sophisticated manufacturing economy, which scholars term proto-industrial or simply artisanal.

Section 55.8

Cities, Qasbas, and the Organization of Urban Economic Life

The Mughal Empire was more urbanized than many contemporary societies, though most people still lived in villages. Scholars have estimated that a significant minority of the population, perhaps around fifteen percent, lived in towns, though such figures are uncertain. Cities differed in function. Agra, Delhi, and Lahore were imperial and administrative capitals with large courts and military populations. Ahmedabad and Surat were commercial and manufacturing centers, and Surat was the empire's chief western port. Dhaka was the center of Bengal's muslin trade and the seat of provincial government for much of the period, and Patna was a hub of the river trade of Bihar and a center for saltpetre and opium. Smaller qasbas served as market and administrative towns, while religious centers such as Benares, Mathura, and Ajmer drew pilgrims and merchants.

Towns performed several functions at once. They were centers of manufacture, with workshops and artisans' quarters; of trade, with bazaars, warehouses, and caravanserais; of finance, with bankers and moneychangers; and of administration, with governors, judges, and revenue officers. Urban life was organized by neighborhoods (mohallas, katras), often associated with particular occupations or communities, so that weavers, dyers, goldsmiths, and merchants lived and worked in distinct quarters. European travelers were struck by the size and bustle of Agra, Lahore, and Delhi, which they compared with the great cities of Europe. Shahjahanabad, built by Shah Jahan in Delhi between 1639 and 1648, illustrates the scale of imperial urban planning, with its fort, mosque, and great market street, Chandni Chowk.

Cities depended on their rural hinterlands. Grain, cotton, indigo, fodder, and fuel moved from villages to towns, and urban merchants supplied cash and goods to the countryside. The concentration of nobles in cities, who spent the revenue of their assignments there, created strong demand for luxuries and services, so that the movement of revenue from the countryside to the towns fueled urban employment. Nobles' households required cooks, grooms, tailors, soldiers, servants, and entertainers, and the markets that served them employed many others. The Cambridge Economic History of India emphasizes that Mughal towns served simultaneously as centers of manufacture, trade, finance, and administration, and that their economies were closely tied to imperial service and revenue.

Urban life was also precarious. Large populations of poor laborers, porters, servants, and petty traders lived on small and irregular earnings, and they suffered most from rising prices, fires, floods, and epidemics. When a noble died or was transferred, the dependents of his household lost their livelihood, and when famine drove villagers into cities, as in 1630-1632, the towns could not always feed them. Cities, in short, concentrated both the wealth and the vulnerability of the Mughal economy.

The often-cited urban population share of about fifteen percent is an estimate rather than a count from a census. Shireen Moosvi derived a figure of roughly that order for the economy around Akbar's reign using administrative revenue information and economic assumptions; its precision should not be extended uncritically to every decade or every region. A large capital could grow when the court resided there and lose demand when imperial households moved away, whereas an export port could expand for different reasons. The distinction between a great city and a qasba also matters. Market towns connected village producers to creditors, workshops, officials, and itinerant traders; they were essential to the economic structure even when they lacked monumental architecture. Religious fairs and pilgrimage routes offered additional occasions for buying and selling. Thus urbanization should be evaluated through the functions of settlements and their links to rural exchange, not by counting imperial capitals alone.

Section 55.9

Internal Trade, Market Networks, and Transportation

Internal trade connected villages, towns, and ports. At the base were local bazaars and periodic markets; above them stood wholesale markets in larger towns, where merchants collected grain, cotton, cloth, and other goods for shipment elsewhere. The commodities that moved were bulky and varied: grain from surplus regions to deficit ones, cotton from Gujarat and the Deccan to weaving centers, textiles from Bengal and Gujarat to northern and overseas markets, salt from Sambhar, Punjab, and the coasts, and metals, horses, and timber. Horses from Central Asia and Persia came overland through Kabul and Qandahar, and were among the most valuable imports, since the army depended on them.

Transport used roads, rivers, and coasts. Caravans of pack oxen, led by Banjara carriers, moved enormous quantities of grain and salt over long distances, and bullock carts and camel trains served the main routes. The Grand Trunk road between Lahore, Delhi, and Agra and eastward toward Bengal carried heavy traffic, and rulers maintained caravanserais and inns along routes. Rivers were cheaper than roads for heavy cargo: the Ganges, Yamuna, Indus, and their tributaries, and the rivers of Bengal, carried goods by boat, and coastal shipping linked Gujarat, Malabar, Coromandel, and Bengal. Transport costs, however, were high, and travel was seasonal, since monsoon rains made many roads impassable. Political security mattered as well. Tolls levied by local authorities raised costs, and travelers and merchants were vulnerable to robbery where zamindars or local chiefs were hostile. Mughal governors sought to control highway robbery, and Akbar and later rulers abolished some tolls, though the practice persisted locally.

A merchant's decision about how to transport a cargo depended on its value, bulk, season, urgency, and the political conditions along the route. High-value items could bear the expense of pack animals, while grain and building materials were more economical to move by river wherever navigable channels were available. Boats also depended on water levels and currents, and river transport was not automatically possible in every season. A caravan required fodder, drivers, guards, and places to rest; its total costs included tolls, losses, and the time during which capital remained tied up. Merchants therefore organized trade through overlapping land and water routes rather than one fixed imperial road system. A disruption in one corridor might raise prices in a city even where harvests remained satisfactory elsewhere. This capacity to move goods, and its practical limits, is essential background for understanding both regional specialization and famine.

Section 55.10

Merchants, Moneylenders, Banking, and Commercial Credit

Trade and credit were dominated by Indian communities. Along the Gujarat coast and across northern and western India, Hindu Bania and Jain merchants, Khatris in Punjab and the northwest, Multanis, Bohra and Khoja Muslim communities, Parsis, and later Armenians formed commercial networks that reached from Agra and Lahore to Persia, Central Asia, the Red Sea, and Southeast Asia. On the Coromandel coast, Telugu- and Tamil-speaking merchants, including Balija, Komati, and Chetti trading groups, were active in cloth procurement and long-distance exchange; their positions varied among ports and political jurisdictions. Several merchants possessed enormous fortunes: Virji Vora of Surat was reputed one of the wealthiest merchants in the world, and shipowners such as Abdul Ghafur of Surat commanded fleets. Merchants were not simply wealthy individuals; they were members of kin and caste networks, which facilitated trust, credit, and the transmission of information over long distances.

Credit made long-distance trade possible. Bankers and moneychangers, known as sarrafs (the origin of the English "shroff"), exchanged coins of different metal and weight, took deposits, made loans, and issued bills of exchange. The hundi, a bill of exchange, allowed a merchant to deposit money in one town and have it paid in another, which avoided the danger and cost of transporting coin. Merchants kept careful accounts, formed partnerships, and used brokers (dalals) to arrange sales and purchases, and insurance on shipments existed at some ports. The great banking houses had agents in many towns and could move large sums, so that nobles and the state itself depended on them to remit revenue from the provinces to the capital, a role that grew more significant later (Chapters 57 and 60).

The hundi was important because it combined a method of payment with relationships of confidence among merchants and financiers. Its practical terms, including who was expected to honor it, differed with place, transaction, and commercial custom; it should not be equated mechanically with a modern bank transfer. A broker's reputation, a family's branch houses, and the standing of a sarraf could be as valuable as the coin itself. Credit financed not only overseas voyages but local purchases, textile advances, the remittance of state revenues, and the needs of aristocratic households. Commercial actors also had diverse legal and cultural affiliations. It would be misleading to imagine one united merchant class or a single monopoly held by any caste or religious community. Networks competed, collaborated, and sometimes sought protection from rival authorities. Their experience demonstrates how substantial commercial institutions could operate without a unified modern banking system or direct European control.

Historical Vocabulary
Hundi and Sarraf

A hundi was a written order to pay a stated sum, usually at another place or after a stated time, functioning as a bill of exchange or credit instrument. A sarraf was a moneychanger and banker who assessed coins, took deposits, made loans, and issued and honored hundis. Together they formed a credit system that moved money over long distances without moving coin, and they were essential to commerce, revenue transfer, and the finance of noble households. Their significance shows that the Mughal economy was financially sophisticated, though the instruments were based on personal trust and reputation within communities, not on formal state banks.

Merchants' relations with the state were reciprocal. Officials and nobles borrowed from bankers, invested in trade, and sometimes participated in it directly, and imperial women also engaged in commerce. Merchants paid customs duties and tolls and needed political protection, which they obtained by paying officials and cultivating powerful patrons. Governors might also exploit merchants, and complaints of extortion appear in the sources. Customs rates and administrative practices varied, but imperial farmans, port officials, coinage, road security, and commercial privileges could encourage exchange even as individual officials imposed unlawful exactions.

Section 55.11

Indian Ocean Commerce and the Participation of European Trading Companies

Mughal India was part of the Indian Ocean trading world, which had long linked the subcontinent to the Persian Gulf, the Red Sea, East Africa, and Southeast Asia (Chapter 49). Monsoon winds governed the voyages: sailing westward from the Gujarat coast toward Arabia and East Africa was generally favored by the northeasterly winter monsoon, while eastward return passages used the southwesterly summer monsoon, subject to regional winds, currents, and Red Sea conditions. Indian textiles were the principal export, paid for with horses, pearls, dried fruit, and wine from Persia and Arabia, coffee from the Red Sea, ivory and gold from East Africa, and spices from Southeast Asia, and above all with precious metals. Surat, called the "gate of Mecca" (Bab al-Makka) because pilgrims embarked there for the hajj, was the chief western port, while Bengal's trade centered on Hooghly and other river ports and the Coromandel coast's on Masulipatnam, Pulicat, and Nagapattinam, under different regional powers, including Golconda and the successor states of Vijayanagara; these ports were not uniformly within the Mughal Empire. Imperial women participated: Jahanara Begum owned a ship, the Sahibi, which traded to the Red Sea, and held rights to revenue from Surat.

European merchants entered this system as newcomers, not rulers. The Portuguese seized Goa in 1510 and sought to control shipping by requiring passes (cartazes), but they could not dominate Mughal ports, and Mughal rulers tolerated them as long as their ships did not harm pilgrim traffic. The Dutch East India Company (VOC), founded 1602, traded in Gujarat, Coromandel, and Bengal, where it purchased textiles for the spice trade of Southeast Asia, while the English East India Company, founded 1600, obtained permission for a factory at Surat in 1613, following the English naval success off Swally in 1612 and earlier negotiations at court; Sir Thomas Roe's embassy of 1615–1618 later sought broader trading privileges (Chapter 53). The French company followed later in the century. All depended on permission from Mughal governors, on payment of customs, and on Indian brokers, merchants, and bankers who arranged supply and credit. Chapter 61 follows their competition in detail.

Their significance in the seventeenth century should be neither dismissed nor exaggerated. European companies were important buyers who increased demand for Indian textiles and brought silver, but they handled only a fraction of India's overseas trade, much of which remained in the hands of Gujarati, Armenian, Persian, and Arab merchants. The English Company was not in control of the Indian economy; it was one trader among many, vulnerable to Mughal power, as shown during the Anglo-Mughal conflict of 1686–1690: Mughal authorities seized several English factories and besieged Bombay, while English forces attacked Mughal shipping; the Company ultimately sought peace and permission to resume trade. Treating the seventeenth-century Company as a dominant power projects later colonial conditions back onto an earlier age.

Shipping combined commercial and religious movements. The Red Sea route carried pilgrims as well as textiles, bullion, and merchandise, making interference with pilgrim traffic especially sensitive to Mughal rulers. Ships had to coordinate sailing seasons, port fees, local brokers, and credit arrangements, while merchants faced the possibility of piracy, detention, or war between coastal powers. Surat's importance was consequently both economic and political, but overseas exchange did not depend on Surat alone: Gujarat, Bengal, the Coromandel coast, and Malabar participated through different commercial and political systems. The clash between the English Company and the Mughal authorities in the late 1680s is particularly revealing. English naval action could damage shipping, yet the Company could not simply compel the emperor to grant favorable terms on land. The outcome of the conflict underlines the need to distinguish the seventeenth-century merchant corporation from the territorial power it would become in the eighteenth century.

Section 55.12

Global Silver Flows, Currency, Prices, and Monetization

South Asia produced little silver of its own, so the coin that circulated in Mughal markets came largely from abroad. Demand for Indian textiles and other goods, with little corresponding European or Asian demand for imports in return, meant that buyers paid chiefly in precious metals. Silver mined in the Americas reached Europe and was shipped to Asia by the Dutch, English, and others, and some came across the Pacific through Manila; silver from Japan was exported by the Dutch and others, and gold and silver also arrived through the Persian Gulf and Red Sea. It is important not to imagine that all silver came to India directly or that it was the sole cause of Mughal prosperity: inflows varied by period, and they affected different regions differently.

The Mughal monetary system was well developed. The silver rupee, standardized under Sher Shah and Akbar and issued from imperial and provincial mints, was the principal coin, with a weight and fineness maintained over long periods. Gold coins, the mohur, were used mainly for large payments and presentation, and copper coins, the dam, for small transactions. The state permitted anyone to bring bullion to the mint to be coined, for a fee, which encouraged the conversion of imported silver into coin. Money-changers assessed coins of varied age and wear, applying discounts that changed their value in different markets.

Silver supported monetization, the spread of money payments. Because the state demanded revenue in cash in many regions, cultivators had to sell produce for coin, and nobles' salaries were calculated in money terms, so that coin circulated widely between countryside and town. Prices in silver rose during the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, a movement that scholars such as Irfan Habib have linked to silver inflows, though prices also responded to harvests, wars, and local conditions. Monetization was uneven: coastal and commercial regions such as Gujarat and Bengal were heavily monetized, while remote or hill regions relied more on barter and customary payments.

The connection among trade, money, revenue, and credit was close. Exports brought silver; silver was minted into rupees; rupees paid revenue and salaries and financed purchases from artisans; and bankers' credit multiplied the effect. For the state this meant that commercial prosperity enlarged the money base from which revenue was drawn. But it also created dependence on external demand: a decline in overseas demand or in silver imports would affect commerce and revenue, a vulnerability that became visible in later decades (Chapters 57 and 60). Silver, then, helps explain the Mughal economy but does not by itself explain its dynamism.

The financial consequences of metal inflows were mediated by institutions. A quantity of foreign silver was not useful to a revenue-paying household until it was exchanged, weighed, coined, or accepted in payment, activities that gave moneychangers and mint officials considerable importance. Currency circulated through cash payments in markets and through credit accounts that transferred claims without physically moving every coin. Even the same nominal rupee could be valued differently according to age, local mint, weight, or condition. Price history therefore cannot be read as a single smooth rise across the empire. A grain shortage could raise local food prices independently of long-term monetary change, while regional markets and administrative assessments responded at different speeds. Evidence from imperial price lists must be interpreted with attention to location, date, measure, and the difference between an official rate and an observed market transaction. These qualifications preserve the importance of silver without turning it into an automatic explanation of growth or hardship.

Section 55.13

Caste, Jati, Varna, and Social Organization

Social hierarchy in Mughal South Asia was organized through several overlapping systems, which should not be confused. Varna refers to the fourfold ideal division of Brahmanical texts into priests (Brahmins), warriors and rulers (Kshatriyas), traders and cultivators (Vaishyas), and servants (Shudras), with outcaste groups placed outside. Jati refers to the far more numerous, locally defined birth groups, typically endogamous and associated with occupations, which were often more immediate units of social identification and marriage practice than the idealized fourfold scheme. Occupation, wealth, political power, religion, and region also determined status. Normative texts, such as the Dharmashastras, describe an ideal order; actual communities were far more complex, and the same varna category could include groups of very different standing.

Social organization varied by region and religion. In Hindu communities, jatis regulated marriage, commensality, and occupation, and councils (panchayats) enforced norms; Muslim communities included hereditary status groups, such as Sayyids and Shaikhs, who claimed foreign or religious descent, and groups of converts who often retained local caste-like distinctions, so that hierarchy existed within Muslim society as well. Jain and Sikh communities had their own patterns, and merchant groups of several religions were often organized as castes. Concepts of purity and pollution marked the position of the lowest groups, whose work, such as handling dead animals, leatherwork, and scavenging, was labelled polluting, and who suffered exclusion from wells, temples, and neighborhoods.

Caste was not completely static. Groups could rise by acquiring wealth, land, or military position, and many castes claimed higher status through imitation of higher-caste practices and genealogies; occupational change occurred, particularly in times of economic expansion or political disruption. The Mughal state cooperated with existing hierarchies, appointing nobles of diverse origins, including Rajput, Persian, Central Asian, and Maratha, and it did not impose a uniform caste order, though it generally recognized caste councils' authority over local disputes. The reconstruction of caste as a rigid, uniform, all-India system owes much to later colonial classification, discussed in Chapter 73, and should not be read back into the seventeenth century.

The relationship between caste and economic position was close but not exact. Brahmins, Rajputs, and other high castes often held land and offices, while the lowest castes supplied agricultural labor, but there were poor Brahmins and wealthy Banias, and peasants of middle castes sometimes held considerable land. Studying caste in this period therefore requires attention to local evidence, not only to normative texts.

The relationship between social rank and economic position was complicated by regional politics. A locally dominant cultivating group might control village headmanship and armed followers without fitting neatly into the role prescribed for it by a Sanskrit legal text. Merchant communities could gain standing through control of capital, temples, or patronage, while an occupational label might conceal considerable differences in wealth among its members. Religious community did not erase distinctions of descent, locality, or occupation. At the same time, caste-related exclusion could make access to wells, agricultural land, schooling, and particular forms of work unequal even among people living in the same settlement. Historians must reconstruct these relationships from a variety of records rather than use later colonial tables as descriptions of all earlier periods. The important analytical point is that hierarchy was both durable and historically changing: everyday practices, economic incentives, political patronage, and customary rules shaped its actual operation.

Section 55.14

Labor, Service, Slavery, and Economic Inequality

The Mughal economy depended on many forms of labor. Agricultural laborers, often drawn from the lowest castes, worked for cultivators and landholders, receiving grain or small payments, and many were tied to employers by debt or custom. Craftworkers worked independently or under merchants and patrons. Wage labor existed in towns, in construction, transport, and the workshops, and the A'in-i Akbari lists wages for different occupations. Servants and attendants filled noble and wealthy households, and soldiers, porters, boatmen, and carriers supplied the military and trade.

Dependence took many forms, and legal status, occupation, and economic dependence did not coincide. A free worker might be as poor as a bonded one, while some dependent people held privileged positions. Bonded and hereditary service existed where debt or custom tied a family to a landholder or patron, and some groups owed unpaid labor (begar) to officials and zamindars. Slavery was also present. Enslaved people, including household servants, concubines, eunuchs, and some military and agricultural workers, were bought and sold in markets and often acquired through war, raids, debt, or famine; Habshis (Africans) held notable positions at the Deccan courts, and in Bengal raiders captured people for sale. Slavery in Mughal India was largely domestic, not plantation-based, and enslaved people could sometimes gain freedom or rise, but they were property, and women and children were especially vulnerable.

Not all dependent labor was legally or socially the same. A household servant who could leave an employer, a debtor compelled to work until an obligation was repaid, a war captive sold as a slave, and an artisan bound by an advance occupied different positions, even if all lacked equal bargaining power. Courtly records often reveal servants and enslaved people primarily as members of elite establishments rather than as individuals with independent life histories. Soldiers, porters, transport workers, construction laborers, and agricultural workers likewise moved between cash earnings, customary service, and temporary employment. These distinctions are important when assessing inequality. The existence of markets for labor did not automatically confer freedom of contract, and dependence could be reinforced by caste, household authority, indebtedness, or the threat of violence. Conversely, the presence of slavery should not obscure the many other forms of work on which production and state service depended. Historians cannot infer the numerical distribution of these arrangements from the surviving examples alone.

Multiple Perspectives

Experiencing the Mughal Economy

A mansabdar holding a jagir: For a noble, the economy meant assigned revenue that financed a household, troops, and consumption, but the assignment was temporary and uncertain, which could encourage rapid extraction from the land before transfer.

The sources on laboring people are fragmentary. Chronicles rarely mention them except as numbers, and wage and price information comes chiefly from the A'in and from European and other accounts. Historians reconstruct their lives from legal documents, village records, and the occasional literary reference, and must remember that most of those who did the work left no account of it. The inequalities among these groups were large, and Section 55.17 returns to them.

Section 55.15

Women, Marriage, Households, and Property Rights

Women worked in nearly every sector of the economy. In rural households they took part in sowing, weeding, harvesting, threshing, tending animals, and processing food; in textile production they did most of the spinning and assisted in other tasks; in towns they worked as domestic servants, midwives, entertainers, and petty traders, and in some regions as sellers of produce and cloth. Much of this work was unpaid or poorly paid and is rarely recorded. Women of wealthy households managed property and servants, and some invested in trade.

Marriage, family organization, and property followed religious law, custom, and region. In Muslim law a bride was entitled to mahr, a sum or property agreed at marriage and owed to her by her husband, and women could inherit shares of family property, though smaller than men's; in practice, local custom sometimes reduced these rights. In Hindu communities women's property typically consisted of stridhana, gifts and property received at and after marriage, which they could control to varying degrees, though schools of law differed and daughters generally did not inherit land alongside sons. Widows' circumstances varied: Hindu high-caste widows were often expected not to remarry, and in some communities sati, the self-immolation of widows, occurred, a practice that Akbar and later rulers discouraged without being able to abolish it (Chapter 67 returns to its history under colonial rule), while widows of lower castes more often remarried.

Surviving documents show that women exercised more economic agency than normative prescriptions suggest. Legal deeds, sale and gift records, and petitions from the Mughal period record women buying, selling, mortgaging, and bequeathing property, and holding rights in land and revenue; the research of historians such as Shireen Moosvi has used such records to show that women's rights and agency must be investigated through actual documents, not only through religious prescriptions. Their experiences differed sharply by class: royal and aristocratic women, such as Nur Jahan (Chapter 53) and Jahanara, controlled large resources, estates, and trade, while artisan, peasant, and laboring women worked long hours with little property, and many were subject to the authority of male relatives and, in elite households, to seclusion (purdah) that restricted their movement but not always their economic role.

Elite women are better recorded than others, so they risk standing in for all women. It is a mistake to infer the lives of all women from the experience of the court or from elite legal texts. Gender intersected with class, caste, religion, and region, and it shaped access to property, work, and protection in ways that differed from one household to another.

Ownership and control of property are not always identical. A woman might have a formally recognized claim to a marriage settlement or inheritance yet face practical obstacles in collecting income, litigating, traveling, or resisting pressure from relatives. Conversely, wives, widows, and daughters could use legal institutions, gifts, commercial partnerships, and family negotiations to exercise agency that did not appear in prescriptive accounts of proper conduct. The evidence is uneven: many surviving deeds concern families with enough property to generate legal records, while the work of landless and poor women remained largely outside formal documentation. It is also necessary to distinguish norms from practices. Rules associated with particular Muslim or Hindu legal traditions did not operate uniformly across region, status, or community, and one should not assume that later colonial codifications reproduce every earlier local custom. A comparative history of women's property must hold legal entitlement, social constraints, and observed transactions in view at the same time.

Section 55.16

Family Life, Consumption, Community Institutions, and Everyday Experience

Ordinary living conditions differed sharply by class. Most people ate grain-based diets: wheat flatbread in the north and west, rice in the east and south, millets in drier regions, with pulses, vegetables, and little meat or fat; wealthier households ate more varied foods, including meat, ghee, sugar, and fruit, and the nobility had elaborate cuisines. Clothing for the poor was a few pieces of coarse cotton; the wealthy wore fine muslins, silks, and brocades. Houses of peasants and the poor were mud and thatch, and those of wealthier townspeople brick and stone, while nobles lived in mansions with gardens, and the emperor in forts and palaces.

Material culture provides another way to study households that left little written testimony. Earthenware cooking vessels, hand-mills, wells, dwellings, and clothing production help reconstruct routines of fetching water, preparing grain, storing food, and caring for children. Such work consumed time and energy even when it did not appear as paid employment in an account book. The experience of the household also changed across the agricultural year: food stocks, debts, and workloads were not evenly distributed between harvest and lean seasons. Life in a trading town differed from life in a dry-land village, and neither can be represented adequately by the diet or buildings of an imperial city. For students, the central methodological lesson is to use descriptions of elite domestic life cautiously and to seek evidence from prices, local documents, material remains, and vernacular writing whenever possible. 'Everyday life' was not one uniform Mughal experience.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Day in Different Households

Reconstructing daily life requires piecing together fragments. A cultivating household probably rose before dawn, with men tending oxen and fields and women grinding grain, fetching water, and cooking, and children helping with animals; evenings might involve village gatherings and songs. An urban weaver family would have worked at the loom through the day, with women and children preparing thread, and merchants' agents calling to collect cloth. A merchant household kept accounts, received visitors, and attended to religious observance, while a noble household employed hundreds of servants. These reconstructions are illustrative generalizations from evidence, not records of particular individuals, and they leave out much that surviving sources do not show.

Household structures varied by means, locality, and life cycle; joint or extended arrangements occurred among some landholding and merchant families, while many other households were smaller or changed composition as members migrated, married, or died. Marriage was arranged by families, often at young ages for girls, and served to ally kin groups; childhood for the poor meant early work, while education was limited. Boys of some communities attended village or town schools (pathshalas and maktabs), learning reading, accounting, or religious texts, and merchant families trained sons in bookkeeping; literacy was rare among the rural poor and among women, though elite women were sometimes educated.

Community institutions provided support and regulation. Caste and village councils settled disputes and enforced norms; religious institutions, including temples, mosques, shrines, and monasteries, offered worship, charity, and sometimes food and shelter; and endowments (waqf, and grants to temples and Brahmins) supported schools and charities. Sufi shrines and Bhakti communities (Chapter 48) provided religious and social spaces open to many groups, and pilgrimage fairs combined religious and commercial purposes. In hard times, such institutions and kin networks were often the only safety net, though their resources were limited. Social experience thus varied with class, caste, region, religion, gender, and household resources; the cultural productions of the period, including painting, literature, and architecture, are the subject of Chapter 56.

Section 55.17

Wealth, Poverty, Elite Consumption, and Standards of Living

The Mughal Empire's wealth was concentrated at the top. The imperial nobility, a few thousand mansabdars of high rank, received very large assignments of revenue, maintained large households, retinues, horses, and elephants, and spent lavishly on clothing, jewels, buildings, and gardens. Wealthy merchants and bankers also lived in great comfort, and the court's consumption supported entire sectors of luxury manufacture. These elites commanded a large share of the surplus drawn from the countryside, and their spending shaped urban markets.

Most people lived far more modestly. Cultivators, laborers, and the urban poor spent most of their income on food, mainly grain, and had little security against bad harvests or price rises. Estimating living standards is difficult. The A'in-i Akbari and other sources record prices and wages, and scholars such as Irfan Habib and Shireen Moosvi have used them to compare wages with the cost of grain, concluding that unskilled laborers earned little more than enough for basic subsistence, though such calculations rest on limited and uneven data and depend on assumptions about family size, employment days, and the combination of income sources. Skilled artisans and middling cultivators did better; the evidence does not permit precise comparisons of income across groups, and this chapter does not offer them.

The central point is the gap between the wealth of the empire and the prosperity of its population. Economic historians have emphasized large differences among nobles, middling groups, peasants, and the urban poor. A state could be rich, a source of impressive monuments and exports, while the typical household remained poor, and rising total output did not necessarily mean rising living standards for most people. Foreign travelers contrasted the splendor of the cities with the poverty of the villages, though their impressions depended on what they saw and their own expectations.

A useful measure of economic well-being is the purchasing power of a day's work rather than the number of coins named as wages. To calculate it, a historian must establish which workers actually received the recorded wage, how often they found employment, how much grain cost in that place and year, and how many other dependents the worker supported. If laborers also received food, lodging, or payments in kind, these arrangements change the comparison. The same problem affects attempts to infer the welfare of cultivators from aggregate agricultural revenue: the state demand does not by itself reveal the distribution of land, the price at which produce was sold, or the cost of seed and animals. Quantitative reconstructions can illuminate large inequalities when their assumptions are stated openly, but a precise figure calculated from a narrow source does not become an exact average for the entire population. The most defensible conclusion concerns uneven opportunity and exposure to risk, not a single standard of living for everyone.

Historical Debate

How Can Historians Reconstruct Inequality and Living Standards from Elite Sources?

The question: Since most surviving sources were produced by or for elites, how far can historians reconstruct the economic condition of cultivators, laborers, and the poor?

Interpretation A, quantitative reconstruction: Using price and wage data from the A'in-i Akbari and other records, together with revenue statistics, scholars have attempted to estimate wages relative to grain prices, the share of output taken by the state, and the distribution of income, concluding that surplus extraction was heavy and that most households lived near subsistence.
Interpretation B, caution about the evidence and local variation: Other historians stress that the data are fragmentary, drawn from particular regions and periods, and reflect official purposes; they argue that living standards varied widely by region, crop, caste, and local conditions, that peasants adapted and bargained, and that firm all-India generalizations are not possible.

Evidence: Both rely on the same A'in data, European travelers' accounts, merchant records, and documents of sale and revenue, but weigh them differently.

Why it matters: The answer affects whether the Mughal economy is understood primarily as prosperous, extractive, or unevenly both. Scholars increasingly combine the approaches, using quantitative estimates while acknowledging their limits.

Section 55.18

Famine, Disease, Environmental Disruption, and Economic Vulnerability

Because most households lived close to subsistence and depended on the monsoon, crop failure could be catastrophic. Drought, flooding, locusts, and war all reduced harvests; epidemic diseases such as plague and smallpox, alongside other infections imperfectly identified in the sources, spread along trade routes and through crowded towns; and disruption of markets by war or unrest raised grain prices. Famines recur in the sources, and the Mughal state, which cared about the stability of revenue, sometimes responded by remitting revenue, distributing food, or opening kitchens, though relief was limited by distance, transport, and resources.

The famine of 1630-1632, which struck Gujarat and the Deccan, was one of the worst. After the monsoon failed in successive years, grain prices soared, and cultivators abandoned villages, migrating toward cities, where the survivors found little food. Both Persian and European sources record vast mortality. The court history of Shah Jahan's reign, the Padshahnama tradition (Abdul Hamid Lahori, and the Shah Jahan Nama of Inayat Khan), describes the suffering and states that the emperor ordered relief, including the distribution of food and remission of revenue. The English traveler Peter Mundy, who was in the region, describes the dead and dying and the abandonment of fields. The two kinds of source agree on the scale of the disaster but differ in emphasis, the court chronicle stressing imperial benevolence and Mundy the scale of death.

The detailed court account of the 1630–1632 crisis illustrates both the value and the limitations of official testimony. The Shah Jahan Nama describes severe scarcity and epidemic suffering and attributes to the emperor daily charitable distribution, payments for relief in Gujarat, and tax remissions. These are reported imperial measures, not independent proof that relief reached every afflicted district or that the announced amounts were fully delivered. Peter Mundy's account is valuable because he traveled through affected areas and observed the human consequences directly, but his itinerary offers neither a census nor a complete map of the disaster. The famine also intersected with military demands for supplies in the Deccan, which could worsen access to grain at particular places. Reading the sources together reveals an environmental shock amplified by distribution failures, war, inequality, and limitations of relief. It does not justify treating vivid descriptions as a reliable numerical death toll.

Peter Mundy and the Famine of 1630-1632

Households survived unequally. Landholders with stored grain, wealthy merchants, and nobles with assured income could buy food at high prices, while landless laborers, artisans with no stocks, and small cultivators had nothing to fall back on. Many sold cattle, tools, land, or even family members, borrowed at high interest, or migrated; debt and enslavement often followed. The poorest were especially exposed, and women and children could face acute risks, although surviving records do not support a precise ranking of mortality by gender or age. Famines could also reshape the economy by killing workers, depopulating villages, and reducing revenue and trade for years.

Cause and Effect

The Famine of 1630-1632

Long-term causes

Dependence on monsoon rain, limited irrigation in the Deccan, low household reserves, and high revenue demands left cultivators with little buffer.

Short-term causes

Repeated failure of rains in successive seasons, together with disruption from war in the Deccan, sharply reduced harvests and raised prices.

Immediate effects

Mass mortality, abandonment of villages, migration to towns, and loss of land, cattle, and family members through distress sales.

Long-term consequences

Depopulated regions, reduced revenue and trade, and enduring debt, with some relief efforts by the state but limited capacity.

Historical complexity: Weather triggered the crisis, but who died and who survived depended on land, stocks, credit, caste, and gender, and on markets and governments, so the famine cannot be explained by drought alone.

Section 55.19

Economic Dynamism, Structural Inequality, and the Foundations of Mughal Power

Taken together, agriculture, manufacturing, commerce, revenue, urban consumption, labor, and social hierarchy formed a single system. Cultivators produced a surplus, partly taken as revenue and rent and partly sold; revenue paid the nobility, who spent it in cities, supporting artisans, merchants, and servants; manufactured goods, especially textiles, were exported in return for silver, which was coined and returned to circulation, enlarging the monetary base from which revenue was drawn. This circuit explains how the Mughal state could support a large army and court, and why control of revenue was so central to imperial politics (Chapters 51 and 52).

The interaction between economic capacity and political power did not have a single outcome. Revenue could sustain troops and administration, but also draw cultivators into cash markets and make imperial dependents sensitive to changing prices and harvests. A large noble household offered employment to artisans, guards, transporters, and domestic workers while directing substantial resources to people whose income depended on royal service. In contrast, some merchants possessed credit and regional connections that allowed them to adjust to shifts in political centers. The pressures of the late seventeenth century were consequently unevenly distributed; a fiscal difficulty for the court was not necessarily the immediate disappearance of all commercial vitality. This distinction is essential for the following chapter on Mughal culture and for Chapter 57's analysis of eighteenth-century political reorganization. It prevents the chapter from treating an expanding economy and an increasingly strained imperial government as mutually exclusive observations.

Historical Debate

Was Mughal Economic Expansion Broadly Prosperous or Substantially Extractive?

The question: Did the growth of agriculture, manufacture, and trade benefit the population broadly, or was it primarily sustained by heavy extraction of surplus from cultivators and artisans?

Interpretation A, extractive: Irfan Habib and others argue that the state and its nobles took a very large share of agricultural output, leaving cultivators little above subsistence, and that the pressure of revenue demands contributed to agrarian unrest and ultimately to the strains of the late seventeenth century.
Interpretation B, dynamic and commercial: Other historians, among them Tapan Raychaudhuri, Satish Chandra, and scholars of trade such as Om Prakash, emphasize growth in markets, manufacturing, and overseas commerce, the agency of merchants and producers, and regional variation, and caution against assuming uniform extraction.

Evidence: The two sides interpret the same revenue statistics, price and wage data, trade records, and travelers' descriptions, differing over how representative these are and what they imply for living standards.

Why it matters: It shapes whether Mughal decline is attributed to an agrarian crisis, to political factors, or to other causes, a question taken further in Chapter 57. Most current scholarship accepts both commercial dynamism and heavy extraction as real and treats their relative weight as regional.

By the late seventeenth century, several pressures converged. Costly campaigns, especially in the Deccan, raised demands for revenue; competition among jagirdars for productive assignments intensified, and the number of nobles seeking assignments outstripped the available revenue (Chapter 54). Agrarian unrest in several regions reflected local grievances over revenue, and the resources of the empire were stretched. The structures described in this chapter help explain these strains: a system dependent on surplus extraction and a mobile, rent-collecting elite became more fragile when expansion ended.

It would nevertheless be wrong to treat economic decline as inevitable after 1707. Commerce, manufacture, and banking continued to flourish in many regions, and the eighteenth century saw the rise of regional economies under the successor states and the Marathas, Sikhs, and others; these transformations are treated in Chapters 57 through 61, and the question of "decline" versus "transformation" is taken up in Chapter 57.

Section 55.20

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has reconstructed the material and social foundations of the Mughal Empire. It has argued that the empire rested on a productive and diverse rural economy, in which cultivators, working within village communities and under the authority of zamindars and imperial officials, produced both food and commercial crops; that this agricultural base supported a large manufacturing sector, above all in textiles, based on household and workshop production and on the labor of men and women; and that a dense network of Indian merchants, bankers, and brokers, using credit instruments such as the hundi, linked villages, cities, and ports into regional, imperial, and oceanic markets. European companies entered as buyers within this system, not as its masters, and silver flowing into the subcontinent supported coinage, monetization, and revenue.

The chapter has also emphasized that these achievements existed alongside deep inequality. Caste, class, gender, and legal status shaped access to land, work, credit, and property; women's labor and property rights were significant but constrained; enslaved and bonded people performed essential work without freedom; and the wealth of nobles and great merchants contrasted with the narrow margins of most households. The famine of 1630-1632 showed how thin those margins were, and how unequally survival was distributed.

The core synthesis is this: the Mughal Empire was capable of sustaining extraordinary wealth and extensive commercial activity, but those achievements existed alongside significant social inequalities and recurrent economic vulnerability. Chapter 56 turns from this material world to the cultural and intellectual life that elite wealth and patronage helped sustain, and Chapter 57 considers how these economic and political structures were transformed after 1707.

A further conclusion follows from the nature of the evidence. A courtly fiscal treatise can explain what rulers sought to count and collect, a travel narrative can document particular journeys and impressions, a merchant's autobiography can reveal the uncertainties of one commercial life, and regional studies can recover patterns of settlement and landholding. None by itself provides a complete social census. Taken together, however, these records show why historians must assess Mughal power from both the imperial center and the diverse communities that sustained it. Economic integration was real, yet political authority, labor relations, and standards of living remained regionally differentiated. The transition to Chapter 56 is therefore not a departure from economic history: cultural patronage depended on the circulation and concentration of resources described here. Nor does the death of Aurangzeb in 1707 mark the end of those networks, whose changing forms belong to the next Part.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the economy and society of Mughal South Asia, approximately from the sixteenth century to 1707, shifting attention from rulers to producers, traders, workers, and households. It has argued that Mughal power rested on material foundations that were productive, commercially connected, and deeply unequal.

Agriculture was the base. Cultivators grew wheat, rice, millets, pulses, and commercial crops such as cotton, indigo, sugarcane, and oilseeds, following the rhythm of the monsoon and the two harvests, and relying on wells, tanks, and canals. Regional diversity prevented any single agrarian model, and villages were connected to markets through cash revenue, credit, and trade.

Rural society was layered. Cultivating households differed in landholding, oxen, and caste; village headmen, accountants, artisans, and laborers made up the village community; and zamindars, from village proprietors to regional chiefs, mediated between cultivators and the imperial state, sometimes supporting and sometimes resisting it. Forest and pastoral communities lived differently from settled farmers, and the expansion of cultivation, especially in eastern Bengal, brought both new production and new pressures.

Manufacturing was extensive. Textile production, spread across spinning, weaving, dyeing, and printing, employed vast numbers of households, with women doing much of the spinning, and regions specialized in cottons, muslins, silks, and chintz. Other crafts, shipbuilding, and imperial and noble karkhanas supplied needs from household goods to arms and luxuries. Workshops were artisanal, not industrial, and production was organized through household labor and merchants' advances.

Cities and qasbas combined manufacture, trade, finance, and administration, and drew on their rural hinterlands. Merchants, bankers, and brokers from many Indian and Asian communities dominated trade and credit, using hundis and partnerships to move goods and money over great distances. European companies participated in this system as buyers, dependent on Indian intermediaries and Mughal permission, and were not yet powers over the economy.

Silver from the Americas and Japan reached India through global trade, paying for Indian goods and supporting a well-developed coinage dominated by the silver rupee. Monetization spread unevenly, linking revenue, prices, and credit, though silver alone does not explain Mughal economic development.

Society was hierarchical. Varna, jati, occupation, class, and religion overlapped but were distinct, and caste was neither uniform nor static. Laborers, bonded workers, and enslaved people performed essential work, often without recognition. Women worked in agriculture, textiles, and trade; held rights such as mahr and stridhana that documents show they exercised actively; and experienced the economy very differently depending on class and community.

Living standards varied enormously. The empire's wealth lay with its nobility and great merchants, while most households lived near subsistence, and the famine of 1630-1632 revealed how unevenly the burdens of crisis fell. Historians debate whether the economy is best described as prosperous or extractive, and how inequality can be reconstructed from elite sources.

The chapter's central conclusion is that the Mughal Empire sustained extraordinary wealth and commerce alongside significant inequality and recurrent vulnerability. These structures help explain the strains of the late seventeenth century without making later political and economic change inevitable.

Reconstructing a Mughal Commodity Chain

This project asks you to trace one commodity, such as cotton cloth, indigo, sugar, or saltpetre, from producer to consumer.

  1. Choose a commodity and region. Select, for example, Bengal muslin, Gujarat indigo, or Coromandel chintz.
  2. Map the chain. Identify cultivators or raw-material producers, artisans, merchants and brokers, transport routes, ports, and consumers.
  3. Find the sources. Use library resources to locate Persian, vernacular, and European evidence, and note each source's perspective and limits.
  4. Assess who gained. Discuss how profits, risks, and obligations were distributed among the participants.
  5. Write up findings. Produce a 4-6 page essay or annotated map, using careful language and noting what evidence cannot show.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to see the Mughal Empire from the fields, looms, counting houses, and households that sustained it. The monuments and chronicles of the court are impressive, but they rested on millions of people whose work seldom appears in the record, and learning to recover their presence from tax tables, merchant accounts, legal deeds, and travelers' notes is a central skill of social and economic history.

The economy described here reveals processes that extended well beyond the empire's political life. Commercial networks, credit practices, and textile manufacturing created connections across South Asia and the Indian Ocean that survived the empire's political changes, and these shaped the eighteenth-century regional states and the later encounter with European power. At the same time, the patterns of inequality, dependence, and vulnerability examined here persisted through later centuries.

Within the six-volume series, this chapter provides the social and material ground for the political narrative of Part X. It helps explain how the Mughal state could mobilize resources, why the fiscal strains of Aurangzeb's reign mattered, and why the later eighteenth century saw reconfiguration rather than collapse. It also reminds students that economic strength and human well-being are different things, a distinction that recurs throughout the history of South Asia.

Chapter 56 now turns to the art, architecture, literature, and intellectual life of the Mughal world, much of it supported by the wealth and patronage whose foundations this chapter has traced.
