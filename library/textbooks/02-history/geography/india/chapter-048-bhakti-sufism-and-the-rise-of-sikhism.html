Part IX — Regional Kingdoms, Devotional Worlds, and the Indian Ocean

Bhakti, Sufism, and the Rise of Sikhism

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

In 1604, at the new and growing town of Ramdaspur, soon to be known as Amritsar, the fifth Sikh Guru, Arjan, oversaw the compilation of a large volume of sacred poetry. It gathered the compositions of the first five Gurus together with verses attributed to a number of earlier devotional poets, among them Kabir, a weaver associated with Banaras; Ravidas, associated with leatherworking; Namdev, a Marathi poet; and Sheikh Farid, linked to the Chishti Sufi tradition of the Punjab. The book was one object, but it held voices from many regions, languages, occupations, and religious worlds. It is hard to find a single artifact that better illustrates both the richness of medieval South Asian devotional life and the care historians must take in describing it.

This chapter studies how devotion, in several distinct forms, reshaped religious life across much of South Asia between roughly the sixth and early seventeenth centuries. It follows the Tamil Alvar and Nayanar poets, the great Vaishnava theologians and regional Krishna and Rama traditions, the Shaiva and Virashaiva communities, the Sant poets of northern India, the Sufi orders and their shrines, and finally the early Sikh community from Guru Nanak to Guru Arjan. Chapters 45 through 47 examined states and political systems: Vijayanagara, the Bahmani and Deccan Sultanates, and the regional sultanates. This chapter turns from courts and armies to teachers, poems, pilgrimage, shrines, households, and the communities that formed around sacred words.

A familiar textbook story describes a single "Bhakti movement" that began in the Tamil south, traveled northward across the centuries, and softened caste hierarchy and religious boundaries, sometimes with Sufism as its companion. Historians have increasingly questioned that account. Devotion to a personal deity has deep roots in the Bhagavad Gita, the Puranas, and temple worship (Chapters 13, 28, and 37). Tamil devotional poetry, Kannada vacanas, Marathi abhangs, Braj and Awadhi verse, Bengali and Assamese devotional literature, Persian Sufi conversations, and Punjabi hymns arose in different languages, social settings, and institutional contexts. They interacted, but they were not simply branches of one tree. This chapter therefore treats the traditions separately before comparing them, and it asks throughout what is gained and what is lost when they are placed under a single label.

The chapter also insists on the distinction between teachers and communities. Many of the figures studied here, including Kabir, Ravidas, Mirabai, Ramananda, and even Guru Nanak, are known through compositions, biographies, and memories that were collected, edited, and transmitted long after their lives. A saint's poem may be an early voice or a later attribution. A saint's biography may preserve a social memory without being a reliable record of events. Historians must work carefully between the surviving texts, the institutions that preserved them, and the later communities that claimed their authority.

Third, the chapter treats devotional religion as social history. Poets were weavers, tailors, potters, cultivators, merchants, courtiers, women, and priests; their audiences included temple worshippers, pilgrims, artisans, rulers, and the households that sang their songs. Devotional teachers criticized caste pretension, ritual display, and inherited religious authority with varying force, yet the communities that preserved their words often developed their own boundaries, lineages, and hierarchies. Recognizing both the real critique and the real limits of reform is one of the chapter's central tasks.

Fourth, the chapter treats Sufism as a set of substantial religious communities within Islam, with their own teachers, institutions, texts, and disagreements, not merely as a cultural bridge between Hindus and Muslims. And it treats the early Sikh tradition as a distinctive religious path with its own teachings, line of Gurus, scripture, and sacred centers, not as a blend of Hindu devotion and Islam. The chapter ends its Sikh narrative in 1606, with Guru Arjan's death. The Khalsa of 1699 and later Sikh political history belong to Chapters 54 and 59.

By the end of the chapter, students should be able to explain how multiple, interacting devotional traditions, each with its own theology, language, social critique, and institutions, changed South Asian society, and why historians now speak of devotional worlds rather than a single movement.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why historians question the idea of a single, south-to-north "Bhakti movement," and describe the alternative regional picture.
  2. Trace the development of Tamil Alvar and Nayanar devotion and the collection of their hymns by temple institutions.
  3. Distinguish the theological positions of Ramanuja, Madhva, Vallabha, and Chaitanya, and the regional traditions of Pandharpur, Assam, western India, and the Hindi belt.
  4. Analyze the Virashaiva-Lingayat tradition and the vacana poets as religious, literary, and social phenomena.
  5. Evaluate the evidence for Kabir, Ravidas, and Dadu Dayal, distinguishing surviving textual traditions from later biography and sectarian memory.
  6. Describe the principal Sufi orders in South Asia, including the Chishti, Suhrawardi, Firdausi, Qadiri, Shattari, and Naqshbandi traditions, and their institutions.
  7. Explain the life and teachings of Guru Nanak and the development of the early Sikh Panth through Guru Arjan and the Adi Granth of 1604.
  8. Assess how sources such as hymn collections, malfuzat, janamsakhis, hagiographies, and manuscripts shape what historians can know.
  9. Compare the critiques of caste and ritual offered by different devotional teachers and the extent to which they changed everyday social life.
  10. Analyze the participation of women, artisans, cultivators, and merchants in devotional communities.
  11. Evaluate the claim that devotional traditions shared a common religious synthesis, using evidence of both interaction and distinctiveness.
  12. Connect devotional developments to the political settings of the Delhi Sultanate, regional kingdoms, and the early Mughal empire without reducing religion to politics.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Was there one Bhakti movement, or many devotional traditions that later came to be described as one?
  2. How do historians distinguish what a poet-saint may actually have composed from what later communities attributed to him or her?
  3. What difference did writing devotion in vernacular languages make to who could participate in religious life?
  4. How did temples, shrines, monasteries, and congregations turn individual teachers' words into lasting institutions?
  5. In what ways did devotional teachers challenge caste and ritual authority, and why did hierarchy so often persist within devotional communities?
  6. How did Sufi orders differ from one another, and why is it inadequate to describe Sufis simply as intermediaries between Hindus and Muslims?
  7. What was distinctive about Guru Nanak's teaching, and how did the early Sikh community acquire its own institutions and scripture?
  8. How did women, artisans, and cultivators participate in devotional life, and what limits did they encounter?
  9. How should historians describe interaction among Bhakti, Sant, Sufi, and Sikh traditions without erasing their differences?

Historical Context

Devotion did not begin in the medieval period. The Bhagavad Gita presents loving devotion to a personal God as one path to liberation, and Puranic literature (Chapter 28) developed elaborate narratives, ritual forms, and sacred geographies around Vishnu, Shiva, and the Goddess. Temple worship, which expanded enormously in the early medieval centuries (Chapters 32, 33, and 37), gave devotion a durable architectural and economic base. What changed in the centuries covered here was not the invention of devotion but the range of languages, social groups, and institutions through which it was expressed.

The political setting varied widely. In the Tamil south, Pallava and Chola kings patronized temples and, in some cases, devotional hymn traditions. In the Deccan, Kannada-speaking regions saw the rise of new religious communities in an urbanizing world of merchants and artisans. From the thirteenth century, the Delhi Sultanate (Chapters 39 through 44) created a Persianate political order in the north, within which Sufi lodges flourished. The regional sultanates and Vijayanagara (Chapters 45 through 47) provided still other contexts. By the sixteenth century, the early Mughal empire (Chapters 50 through 52) shaped the Punjab and the Gangetic plain in which the Sikh Gurus lived and the Hindi poets composed. Rulers sometimes patronized religious teachers, sometimes distrusted them, and often ignored them. Devotional communities were rarely simple extensions of state policy, and they were never fully independent of the political and economic conditions around them.

The evidence is unusual in its form. Much of it is poetry, composed to be sung and often transmitted orally before being written down. Other evidence consists of hagiographies, hymn anthologies, temple inscriptions, Persian conversation records (malfuzat) and biographical dictionaries, manuscript collections, shrine records, and sectarian histories. These sources are indispensable, but each reflects the viewpoint of the community that preserved it. Dating is often uncertain; many saints have traditional dates that scholars treat with caution. Readers should therefore expect to encounter phrases such as "traditionally dated," "attributed to," and "later tradition holds" throughout this chapter.

Finally, the chapter draws on earlier discussions of caste (Chapters 7, 37, and 44), the early temple economy (Chapter 37), the Delhi Sultanate's society and culture (Chapter 44), and the early Indian Ocean networks of Muslim merchants (Chapter 38). Chapter 49 turns next to the Indian Ocean and the arrival of the Portuguese, and Chapters 54 and 59 return to the Sikh community in later periods.

Foundations of Devotional Religion in Medieval South Asia

Section 48.1

Bhakti, Sufism, and Sikh Origins: Historical Questions and Sources

The terms that organize this chapter did not always mean what they now suggest. Bhakti, from a Sanskrit root associated with sharing, devotion, and attachment, names loving participation in the divine, and it appears in texts long before the medieval period. Sant is a term used especially for northern Indian poets who spoke of a God beyond form and who criticized ritual display; it is a modern scholarly and sectarian category as much as a self-description. Sufism, or tasawwuf, refers to Muslim traditions of inner discipline, ethical training, and devotion, organized through teaching lineages. Panth, meaning path or community, designates a following that gathers around a teacher's teaching. None of these terms is a neat container, and historians use them carefully.

The sources are as varied as the traditions. Tamil hymns were sung in temples before being written and collected. Marathi, Kannada, Hindi, Bengali, and Punjabi poetry circulated orally and in manuscript, often with additions and changes by later singers. Persian malfuzat, records of a teacher's conversations, preserve the voices of Sufi masters as remembered by disciples. Biographical dictionaries (tazkiras) and hagiographies relate saints' lives, often through miracle stories. The Sikh janamsakhis narrate Guru Nanak's life, and the Adi Granth preserves his own compositions. Inscriptions, temple records, land grants, and shrine documents add evidence of patronage and property.

Three problems recur. The first is dating: many poets' lives can be placed only within a century or two, and the earliest manuscripts of their poems often date long after their deaths. The second is attribution: poems were signed with a poet's name by later composers, a practice that makes some collections a record of a tradition rather than of one author. The third is perspective: nearly every account of a saint was written by a community with an interest in remembering him or her in a particular way. A good historian reads such sources not only for what they say about the saint but for what they reveal about the communities that preserved them.

For that reason, this chapter distinguishes among three kinds of historical object: a teacher (the historically recoverable person, where evidence allows), a body of compositions (what survives in manuscripts, with its textual history), and a community (the institutional and social formation that claims the teacher's authority). Often the third is far better documented than the first.

The layering of sources can be illustrated concretely. A Persian hagiographical dictionary such as Mir Khurd's Siyar al-Auliya, composed in the Chishti community around the later fourteenth century, and Abd al-Haqq Dehlawi's Akhbar al-Akhyar, written in the Mughal period, describe thirteenth-century Sufis from the standpoint of later institutions that needed founding saints. A Tamil hymn recited in a temple today may preserve an old melody, but its written form passed through palm-leaf copying, editorial arrangement, and, from the nineteenth century, printing, each of which tended to fix one version of what had been a more fluid performance tradition. A Sikh janamsakhi composed in the seventeenth century answers questions that seventeenth-century Sikhs were asking about their founder. In every case the document is evidence first of the community that produced it and only secondarily, and with caution, of the earlier person it describes.

The very concept of a "Bhakti movement" has its own history. Late nineteenth- and twentieth-century scholars and literary historians, including George Grierson, R. G. Bhandarkar, and, in Hindi criticism, Ramchandra Shukla and Hazariprasad Dwivedi, shaped an influential picture of devotion spreading across India and shaping a national culture. John Stratton Hawley's A Storm of Songs (2015) argues that this picture reflects the concerns of its authors, and that medieval poets and communities had few ways of speaking about themselves as members of one movement. Students should therefore learn the term as part of the historiography as well as part of the history, a point developed in §48.5.

Finally, the chapter's chronological reach, from the Tamil hymns of the first millennium to the Adi Granth of 1604, means that the evidence changes sharply along the way. Early centuries depend heavily on temple inscriptions and later hagiography; the Sultanate centuries add Persian malfuzat and tazkiras; the sixteenth century adds dated manuscripts, anthologies, and the first sectarian histories. Readers should notice, section by section, which kind of evidence is carrying each argument.

Historical Vocabulary
Bhakti, Sant, Nirguna, and Saguna

Bhakti is devotion to a personal deity or to the divine, expressed through worship, song, remembrance, and service. Sant names poets and teachers, especially in northern India, who emphasized inner realization and the divine name over ritual and sectarian boundary. Nirguna describes the divine as without attributes or form, while saguna describes the divine as possessing qualities and appearing in forms such as Rama or Krishna. These contrasts are useful but unstable: many poets move between them, and some communities hold both at once.

The danger is to treat these terms as watertight categories. Kabir is often called nirguna, yet his poems frequently use the language of Rama; Tulsidas is saguna, yet his poetry speaks of a God beyond all attributes. The terms organize discussion, but they do not describe every poem.

Section 48.2

Devotion before the Medieval Movements: Scriptural, Ritual, and Regional Foundations

Medieval devotional traditions inherited a long history. The Bhagavad Gita, part of the Mahabharata (Chapter 28), teaches that the devotee who offers love to Krishna reaches him, and it offers devotion as one among several paths to liberation. The Puranas narrate the deeds of Vishnu, Shiva, and the Goddess, prescribe temple worship and pilgrimage, and teach that sincere devotion is open to people of many social ranks, although the texts themselves are written within a Brahmanical frame and prescribe social hierarchy. The Bhagavata Purana, perhaps composed in the south in the first millennium CE, gave Krishna's childhood and love for his devotees a literary richness that later poets drew on repeatedly.

Ritual and place mattered as well. Temples, whose growth Chapter 37 traced, employed priests, musicians, dancers, and cooks, held land, and organized festivals. Pilgrimage routes tied villages to major shrines. Domestic worship, vows, fasting, and seasonal festivals brought devotion into households. Women performed rites, sang songs, and commissioned donations, and village shrines to local deities carried devotional meaning that never entered Sanskrit texts. What the written evidence preserves is therefore only the visible part of a much larger religious practice.

Buddhist and Jain communities also developed devotional forms, including worship at stupas and temples, hymns of praise, and the veneration of Buddhas and Tirthankaras (Chapters 25, 28). The Alvars and Nayanars of the Tamil country composed in an environment shaped by competition and exchange among Brahmanical, Buddhist, and Jain communities. Medieval devotion did not arise in a religious vacuum but within a dense field of existing practices.

For these reasons there was no single founding event. Different devotional traditions drew on different earlier materials: the Gita and Puranas for Vaishnava theology; the Agamas and temple ritual for Shaiva worship; Sufi texts and institutions brought from West and Central Asia for the Muslim traditions; and the speech of artisans, cultivators, and traders for the Sant poets. When a later tradition claims descent from a single source, historians treat that claim as part of the tradition's own history.

Several earlier texts and practices deserve closer attention because later devotional writers drew on them constantly. The twelfth chapter of the Bhagavad Gita describes the qualities of the devotee dear to God, a passage commentators from Ramanuja to Dnyaneshwar returned to. The tenth book of the Bhagavata Purana, with its narrative of Krishna's childhood and the gopis' love, supplied the images that Surdas, Chaitanya's followers, Vallabha's community, and Shankaradeva each reworked. Temple ritual manuals, the Pancharatra and Vaikhanasa texts for Vishnu and the Shaiva Agamas for Shiva, regulated image installation, daily service, and festival, and so provided the ritual frame within which hymns could be sung.

Devotional expression also existed in forms beyond Sanskrit and Tamil. The Sangam-era anthology tradition of Tamil includes the Tirumurukarruppatai, a poem guiding pilgrims to the shrines of Murugan, which shows that devotional poetry and pilgrimage geography were already linked in the early centuries CE. In the twelfth century Jayadeva, who worked in the Bengal and Odisha region, composed the Sanskrit Gitagovinda, a lyric poem of Radha and Krishna that was sung in temples and influenced later Bengali, Odia, and Braj poetry. Kerala tradition associates the Alvar Kulashekhara with the Chera court. These examples show that devotion was already regionally diverse before the better-known vernacular traditions took shape.

The wider religious setting also matters. Village and caste deities, goddess shrines, ancestor and hero stones, and local spirit cults formed a large substratum of worship that rarely appears in learned texts. Devotional teachers sometimes absorbed these practices, sometimes criticized them, and sometimes stood in tension with them. When later hagiographies describe saints defeating rival ascetics or local cults, they compress complicated processes of competition and accommodation into stories of triumph.

Section 48.3

The Tamil Alvars and Nayanars: Early Vernacular Devotion

The earliest large body of vernacular devotional poetry in South Asia comes from the Tamil country. The Alvars, traditionally twelve in number, composed hymns in praise of Vishnu. The Nayanars, traditionally sixty-three, composed or are remembered for devotion to Shiva. Their dates are conventionally placed between roughly the sixth and ninth centuries CE, though the traditional chronology comes from later hagiography and is approximate. They lived in a world of Pallava and early Chola kingdoms (Chapters 32 and 33), within which temples and Brahmanical learning were expanding.

Their poetry is intensely personal. The hymns describe the poet's love, longing, humility, wonder, and joy before a deity who dwells in particular temples. The poets travel from shrine to shrine, singing a poem at each, and so create a sacred geography of Tamil lands. Sambandar, Appar, and Sundarar, the three most celebrated Shaiva poets, are remembered for hymns now gathered in the Tevaram. Among the Alvars, Nammalvar is associated with the deeply philosophical Tiruvaymoli, and Periyalvar and his adopted daughter Andal with poems of Krishna's childhood and of a bride's love for God. Manikkavacakar, whose dates are debated, composed the Tiruvacakam.

Women's voices are visible here. Andal, whose Tiruppavai and Nachchiyar Tirumoli remain central to Sri Vaishnava worship, speaks as a young woman who longs to marry Vishnu. Karaikkal Ammaiyar, traditionally among the Nayanars and usually placed in the early centuries of the tradition, composed hymns on the transient world and on Shiva's dance; her tradition depicts her as a radical ascetic who rejected conventional life. These women are known through poems and later hagiography, and the biographies deserve caution; the poems nevertheless show that women could be recognized religious authors.

The relationship with Jain and Buddhist communities was often one of rivalry. Hagiographies describe Appar's return from a Jain environment to Shaivism and Sambandar's debates with Jains, narratives that probably reflect real competition for royal and popular support, but that were composed in later centuries and should not be read as neutral reports. The poems themselves criticize rival ascetic groups in sharp terms. This is another reason to read Alvar and Nayanar literature as the voice of a community engaged in religious argument, not only as private devotion.

The traditional lists of Alvars clarify the range of social positions the tradition remembers. The earliest three, Poykai, Bhutam, and Pey, are said to have met at Tirukkovalur. Tirumalisai, Nammalvar, Madhurakavi, Kulashekhara, Periyalvar, Andal, Tondaradippodi, Tiruppan, and Tirumangai complete the traditional twelve. Nammalvar, whose Tiruvaymoli is treated as the Tamil equivalent of the Vedas by Sri Vaishnavas, is remembered as belonging to a cultivating community. Tiruppan Alvar is traditionally linked to the Panar, a community of minstrels regarded as low-ranking, and Tirumangai Alvar to a chieftain family who is said to have turned from raiding to devotion. Whether these identifications are historically exact is uncertain, but they show how the community wished to remember the poets.

The Nayanars similarly include figures of varied status in the hagiography of the Periya Puranam. Kannappar, a hunter, is remembered for an act of extreme devotion; Nandanar, from a community considered untouchable, is said to have longed to enter the Chidambaram temple and to have been permitted only through a miraculous passage through fire. The story is revealing in two ways: it honors a low-status devotee as a saint, and it also assumes that ordinary entry to the temple was closed to him. Later centuries tell it differently depending on the teller's position, which is a reminder that saintly biographies carry social messages.

The poetry itself shows how Tamil devotional language moves between registers. Alvar poems adopt the conventions of Tamil love poetry, in which the poet takes on the voice of a woman longing for her beloved, to speak of the devotee's longing for God. Nayanar hymns often alternate descriptions of a shrine's landscape with brief theological assertions and a closing verse promising the benefit of singing the hymn. Historians use such features both to study literature and to understand how the poems were sung. Hymns were performed with music, and the Tevaram tradition associates individual hymns with specific melodic types (pan), suggesting that musical specialists were central to transmission.

Section 48.4

Temples, Pilgrimage, Hymn Collections, and the Institutionalization of Devotion

Hymns composed by individual poets became institutional property through a process of collection and ritual use. The Vaishnava hymns were gathered in the Nalayira Divya Prabandham, the "four thousand sacred compositions," a collection traditionally attributed to a teacher named Nathamuni and associated with Srirangam. The Shaiva hymns of Sambandar, Appar, and Sundarar were arranged in the Tevaram, and with other works became part of the twelve books of the Tirumurai. Later Chola tradition associates the arrangement of the Shaiva corpus with Nambi Andar Nambi during the age of Rajaraja I, and Sekkizhar's twelfth-century Periya Puranam retold the lives of the Nayanars. These attributions come from later tradition and cannot all be verified, but the broad pattern is secure: between the tenth and twelfth centuries, temples and patrons fixed, ordered, and ritualized hymn collections.

Temple institutions shaped the hymns' meaning. Reciting hymns became part of temple ritual; singers were appointed; inscriptions record endowments for the recitation of the Tevaram and of the Vaishnava hymns. Kings who gave land and gifts to temples tied themselves to the poets' prestige, and the poets' memory strengthened royal legitimacy. At the same time, institutionalization brought hierarchy. Brahmanical priests and learned teachers acquired control over interpretation, and the social range of the original poets, who by tradition included both Brahmins and members of other communities, was not matched by the ritual positions people could hold in the temples that preserved them.

Inscriptions from Chola temples illuminate this process by recording grants for singers and for musical accompaniment. They show that certain hereditary groups, associated with singing the hymns, could hold positions of recognized honor. They also reveal the temple as an economic institution, as Chapter 37 described. The sacred poem entered ritual time, and ritual time in turn made the poem permanent.

Inscriptions provide the clearest evidence for the institutionalization of hymn singing. At the Brihadisvara temple in Thanjavur, inscriptions of the early eleventh century from Rajaraja I's reign record the appointment of a group of singers, traditionally numbered at forty-eight, to sing the Tiruppadiyam hymns, along with arrangements for their support and for accompanists. Similar endowments at other Chola temples show that hymn recitation had become part of the temple's regular service and that hereditary families of singers, the oduvars in Shaiva temples, held recognized offices. Royal patronage thus gave the hymns a stable ritual and economic base, in line with the temple economy described in Chapter 37.

On the Vaishnava side, the Nalayira Divya Prabandham was organized around the major Alvar works, and the tradition of Araiyar Sevai at Srirangam and other temples, in which hereditary performers recite and enact the hymns in front of the deity, developed as a specialized ritual art. Teachers such as Nathamuni, remembered as the compiler, and his descendant Yamunacharya (Alavandar), are the link between the Alvars and Ramanuja's generation. The Tamil hymns thus became both an object of ritual performance and a subject of commentary, with later teachers writing extensive Tamil and Sanskrit commentaries on them.

Institutionalization had mixed effects. It preserved the poems and enlarged their audience, but it also placed them under the control of hereditary specialists and temple managers, and it encouraged a canonical order, with the Shaiva corpus eventually arranged in twelve books and Sekkizhar's Periya Puranam providing an authoritative biographical frame. What began as a poet's personal outpouring was thus converted into a liturgical resource, an example of a recurring pattern throughout this chapter.

Section 48.5

Vernacular Languages, Religious Mobility, and the Problem of a Single Bhakti Movement

From the twelfth century onward, devotional poetry appeared in many languages: Kannada vacanas, Marathi abhangs and ovis, Braj, Awadhi, Rajasthani, and Punjabi verse, Gujarati and Bengali poems, and Assamese songs and plays. Writing in a regional language did not simply make religion accessible. It also created new literary standards, drew on local song forms, and linked religious teaching to the political rise of regional courts and mercantile communities. Vernacular literature often coexisted with Sanskrit learning rather than replacing it, and many poets were themselves learned in Sanskrit.

The conventional account describes Bhakti moving from the Tamil country to Karnataka and Maharashtra, then to the north, a story often linked to a verse in the Bhagavata Mahatmya, a text associated with the Bhagavata Purana tradition, which describes Bhakti as born in the Dravida land. Historians such as John Stratton Hawley argue that this narrative took its modern form through a particular interplay of devotional memory and twentieth-century scholarship, and that the idea of a single "movement" travelling northward reflects a retrospective vision rather than a documented chain of influence. The traditions certainly overlapped, and some teachers, like Ramanuja and the Sri Vaishnavas, had wide influence. But Kabir, Shankaradeva, and Guru Nanak, for example, cannot be explained as disciples of a southern teacher. Each worked out of a specific regional environment.

This chapter therefore uses "devotional traditions" in the plural. It reads the Tamil poets, the Vaishnava theologians, the regional Krishna and Rama communities, the Sant poets, the Sufis, and the Sikhs as parallel and sometimes intersecting developments, each with a distinct origin and its own sources.

The regional picture is richer than any single movement narrative. In Maharashtra, alongside the Varkaris, the Mahanubhava community, associated with Chakradhar in the thirteenth century, produced early Marathi prose such as the Lilacharitra and rejected caste and image-worship in ways that set it apart from other traditions. In Bengal and Mithila, the lyrics attributed to Vidyapati in Maithili and to Chandidas in Bengali, whose dating is debated, treated the love of Radha and Krishna. In Odisha, Sarala Dasa composed an Odia Mahabharata in the fifteenth century, and the later Panchasakha poets of the sixteenth century linked Jagannath devotion to Odia literature. In Assam, Madhava Kandali's Ramayana in the fourteenth century preceded Shankaradeva's work. In the Telugu country, Annamacharya (1408–1503) composed thousands of devotional songs for Venkateshwara at Tirupati, many preserved on copper plates. In Kerala, Ezhuthachan's sixteenth-century Malayalam Ramayana became a foundational text of Malayalam literature.

These examples show that vernacular devotional literature arose from regional courts, temples, and communities, often without any demonstrable link to the Tamil south. Some regional poets knew each other's work, and later compilers linked them together, but the primary explanation for most traditions lies in local conditions: the growth of regional kingdoms, the spread of temples and pilgrimage, the rise of merchant and artisan communities, and the prestige of vernacular literary forms.

The notion of a single movement therefore needs revision in two directions. It overstates uniformity of teaching, because Sri Vaishnavas, Mahanubhavas, Virashaivas, Sants, and Sikhs disagreed on essential matters. It also understates connection, because poets, singers, pilgrims, and manuscripts did travel, and later compilers such as Nabhadas in the Bhaktamal deliberately created lineages that bound disparate figures together. A historically responsible account treats both the diversity and the connections as part of the record.

Historical Debate

Was There a Single Bhakti Movement?

The question: Should historians describe the devotional poets and communities of the sixth to seventeenth centuries as one Bhakti movement, or as multiple traditions?

Interpretation A — A connected movement: Supporters point to shared themes: personal devotion, vernacular expression, criticism of empty ritual, openness to women and lower-status communities, and the use of song. They note actual connections, such as the influence of Alvar poetry on later Vaishnavism and the appearance of overlapping poets in the Sikh scripture. The idea of a movement helps students see common features across a vast region.
Interpretation B — Many traditions: Critics argue that the "movement" narrative flattens differences in theology, language, social setting, and institutions, and depends on a south-to-north story that the evidence does not clearly support. They emphasize that Virashaivas, Varkaris, Sants, Gaudiyas, Sufis, and Sikhs formed distinct communities, sometimes in conflict, and that the idea of a single movement took shape in part through modern nationalist and literary-historical writing.

Evidence: Both views draw on the same texts: manuscripts of devotional poetry, biographical traditions, and temple and shrine inscriptions. They differ in how much weight they give to similarity and to difference, and in whether they treat later memory as evidence of shared origins or as a construction.

Why it matters: If there was one movement, it can be described as a broad social reform sweeping across the subcontinent. If there were many traditions, then historians must explain each in its own context and ask why they came to be linked. This textbook adopts the second approach while acknowledging real connections.

Section 48.6

Ramanuja and the Formation of Sri Vaishnava Traditions

Ramanuja, traditionally dated 1017–1137, though some modern scholars propose a shorter life in the eleventh and twelfth centuries, became the central figure of the Sri Vaishnava community centered at Srirangam on the Kaveri. He inherited the devotional poetry of the Alvars and the intellectual leadership of teachers such as Yamunacharya, and he joined them with Sanskrit Vedanta. His principal works, including the Sri Bhashya, a commentary on the Brahma Sutras, and his commentary on the Gita, argue for Vishishtadvaita, or qualified non-dualism. In this view the individual soul and the material world are real and distinct, yet they form the body of a single supreme reality, Vishnu-Narayana. Against Shankara's interpretation, which emphasized the ultimate identity of the self with an attributeless Brahman, Ramanuja argued that liberation consists in loving union with a personal God.

His theology gave Tamil devotional poetry a recognized place alongside Sanskrit scripture. The Sri Vaishnava community came to honor both streams, using the Divya Prabandham in temple worship and Sanskrit Vedanta in learned instruction. Temple management at Srirangam, with its ritual schedule, endowments, and learned teachers, became a model for others. Ramanuja is also credited, in tradition, with organizing temple worship according to Pancharatra ritual rules.

The social dimension was complicated. Hagiographies describe Ramanuja as welcoming low-status disciples and as sharing sacred mantras publicly, and Sri Vaishnavas honor members of non-Brahmin communities among the Alvars. Yet the community itself was led by Brahmin teachers, and later centuries brought a split between the Tenkalai and Vadakalai schools over the role of Sanskrit and Tamil, and of divine grace and human effort. The tradition's biographies, composed centuries after Ramanuja's death, tell of conflict with a Chola king and flight to Hoysala territory. These accounts cannot be confirmed from contemporary evidence, and they should be treated as part of the community's own self-understanding.

The tradition attributes to Ramanuja nine Sanskrit works, including the Sri Bhashya, the Gita Bhashya, the Vedarthasamgraha, and three prose hymns of surrender (gadyas). His predecessor Yamunacharya had already defended the devotional reading of Vedanta against rival schools, and Ramanuja developed the argument that God is both the material and the efficient cause of the world, with souls and matter as God's body. He insisted on the reality of the individual soul against what he regarded as the illusionism of other Vedanta teachers, and he defined liberation as eternal service in God's presence.

Surrender (prapatti) to God as an act of grace, open in principle to everyone, became a central teaching in later Sri Vaishnavism, though the degree to which Ramanuja himself taught it is discussed by historians. The later quarrel between the Tenkalai, centered in Tamil and the Alvar hymns, and the Vadakalai, centered in Sanskrit and Vedanta Desika's (1268–1369) scholarship, concerned how far grace works without effort. Both claimed Ramanuja's authority, a reminder that a founder's teaching is interpreted by his heirs.

The institutional record shows Ramanuja's influence in southern Karnataka, where the temple of Cheluvanarayana at Melkote is associated with his residence in the twelfth century, and in the continued organization of Srirangam under learned lineages. The Sri Vaishnava community's wealth and leadership also meant that it became an important regional institution, sometimes aligned with Chola, Hoysala, and Vijayanagara rulers. Chapter 45 examined Vijayanagara's temple patronage, within which Sri Vaishnava institutions held an important place.

Section 48.7

Madhva, Dvaita Philosophy, and Vaishnava Religious Communities

Madhva (traditionally 1238–1317), born in the Udupi region of the Karnataka coast, developed a distinct Vaishnava philosophy known as Dvaita, or dualism. He taught that God, individual souls, and the material world are eternally and really different, and that souls differ from one another in their natures. God, identified with Vishnu, is supreme and independent, and the soul depends wholly on divine grace. Madhva rejected the non-dualism of Shankara and criticized qualified non-dualism as well, writing commentaries on the Gita, the Brahma Sutras, and other texts to argue his position.

Madhva's tradition also created institutions. He is credited with establishing, at Udupi, an eight-monastery system (the ashta mathas) whose heads take turns managing the Krishna temple, and the Udupi temple remains a major center of devotion. Madhva's lineage includes learned philosophers, among them Jayatirtha in the fourteenth century and Vyasatirtha, who was associated with the court of Vijayanagara in the sixteenth century (Chapter 45). The Haridasa poets of Karnataka, such as Purandaradasa, composed devotional songs in Kannada and are linked to this tradition, though they were not simply an arm of its scholarly community.

The comparison of Ramanuja and Madhva shows why the word Vaishnava is insufficient. Both revered Vishnu, but they disagreed on the nature of the soul's relation to God, on how scripture should be interpreted, and on the organization of their communities. Their disputes were conducted in the language of Sanskrit philosophy and also through the institutions that preserved their lineages.

Madhva's writings include commentaries on the Gita, the Brahma Sutras, the principal Upanishads, and the Bhagavata Purana, and an independent work, the Anuvyakhyana. He argued for five eternal distinctions: between God and soul, between God and matter, between soul and soul, between soul and matter, and between matter and matter. His classification of souls into those destined for liberation, those who remain in the cycle of rebirth, and those destined for permanent darkness was distinctive and controversial, and it differs sharply from the universalist tendency of other devotional teachers. His followers called their teaching Tattvavada, "the doctrine of reality."

The Madhva tradition produced a notable body of devotional song in Kannada. Haridasa poets such as Sripadaraja and Vyasatirtha in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, and Purandaradasa (traditionally 1484–1564), composed short songs in everyday language, and Purandaradasa is regarded as a key figure in the formation of Karnatak music. Kanakadasa, traditionally from a shepherd community and a minor chieftain, is associated with the Udupi Krishna temple, and the tradition that the image turned to face him is told as a story about the inclusion of a low-status devotee. As with other such stories, it indicates that the community valued such an ideal while also remembering that an obstacle existed.

Madhva's school also shows how doctrinal disputes shaped institutions. Philosophers of the Udupi and Uttaradi lineages debated rivals in Sanskrit treatises, and the Vijayanagara court under Krishnadevaraya honored Vyasatirtha. The theological argument was inseparable from patronage and from the rivalry among Vaishnava and Shaiva communities that Chapter 45 noted at the Vijayanagara court.

Section 48.8

Pandharpur and the Varkari Tradition: Dnyaneshwar and Namdev

Pandharpur, on the Bhima river in southern Maharashtra, is the pilgrimage center of Vithoba (Vitthala), a form associated with Krishna and Vishnu and also sometimes linked to Shiva. The Varkaris, "those who make the pilgrimage," walk in processions to Pandharpur on fixed days, especially in the month of Ashadha, singing devotional songs. The tradition is very likely centuries old; the earliest firm literary evidence associated with it begins around the thirteenth century.

Dnyaneshwar (Jnaneshwar), traditionally dated 1275–1296, is credited with the Jnaneshwari, a Marathi commentary on the Bhagavad Gita, and with devotional songs. Tradition holds that he completed the commentary around 1290 and died young; scholars debate how much of the traditional biography is reliable, and the text itself exists in many manuscript variants. Its importance lies in making the Gita's teaching available in a spoken language, in a literary register that combined learning with lyric beauty. Dnyaneshwar belonged to a family said to have suffered social exclusion, and later tradition tells of him and his siblings facing the condemnation of orthodox Brahmin authority; as with many saintly lives, the story serves the community's memory as much as it records events.

Namdev (c. 1270–1350, with traditional and uncertain dates) is associated with a tailor's family and with the Varkari community. Marathi abhangs attributed to him express intense love for Vithoba. Other poems in Hindi and Punjabi dialects are also attributed to him, and some of those appear in the Adi Granth (§48.41). Scholars debate whether these represent a single historical Namdev who traveled north, a later poet or poets using his name, or a mixture; the Punjabi connection is one of the clearest examples of the difficulty of attribution in this chapter.

The historical origins of Vithoba worship remain debated. Inscriptions from around the twelfth and thirteenth centuries mention the shrine at Pandharpur, though they say little about the formation of the cult. Scholars have proposed connections between Vithoba worship and Kannada-speaking pastoral communities, drawing on regional language, cult practice, and iconography. R. C. Dhere explored these pastoral associations while cautioning that the evidence does not establish the specific theory that Vithoba originated as a deified pastoral hero represented by a hero-stone. The historical development of the cult remains debated; Pandharpur nevertheless became a major regional pilgrimage center by the late medieval period.

Dnyaneshwar's Jnaneshwari, written in the ovi verse form, expounds the Gita for an audience of Marathi speakers and shows great familiarity with Shaiva and Nath imagery as well as Vaishnava devotion. His other major work, the Amritanubhava, is a more philosophical meditation. Traditions about his family, his siblings Nivruttinath, Sopan, and Muktabai, and their experience of exclusion are told in many later forms. The Varkari tradition honors them as a group, and Muktabai is remembered as a woman poet, though the details of the biography cannot be fixed with confidence.

Namdev belongs to a group of poet-saints connected to Pandharpur that includes Gora the potter, Sena the barber, Savata the gardener, and Narahari the goldsmith, each remembered as combining craft work with devotion. Their abhangs use the images of their trades. The collective memory of these poets provides one of the best illustrations of devotional literature's connection to artisan communities, even though the individual biographies are known mainly from later hagiography such as the works of Mahipati in the eighteenth century.

Section 48.9

The Varkari Community: Chokhamela, Janabai, Eknath, and Social Boundaries

The Varkari tradition is notable for the range of social positions represented among its poets. Chokhamela, active in the fourteenth century, was a member of the Mahar community, a group assigned low status and polluting work in the surrounding society. His abhangs, along with those of his wife Soyarabai, son Karmamela, and others of his family, express devotion to Vithoba and also a painful awareness of exclusion from the temple's inner precincts. Their poems reveal that the tradition's inclusion of such voices was real but incomplete: the poets could be honored in memory while being kept apart in ritual space.

Janabai, traditionally a servant in Namdev's household, composed abhangs that combine household labor, such as grinding grain, with devotion, and they give a rare view of a woman's work and religious life. Other Varkari women poets also appear in later tradition. The preservation of their poems demonstrates that the community valued women's voices, though much about their lives is known only through later accounts.

Eknath of Paithan (1533–1599) belonged to a learned Brahmin family and worked to revive the Dnyaneshwari text, to write a Marathi commentary on the Bhagavata Purana, and to compose devotional poetry and satirical verse. His life shows that the tradition was not restricted to lower-status communities and that learned figures contributed to its literary development. Traditional accounts say that he treated Dalit families with respect, and the stories portray tension with orthodox opinion; again, the stories are best read as the tradition's own reflections on its ideals. The later poet Tukaram, active in the seventeenth century, belongs beyond this chapter's limits but shows the tradition's continuing vitality.

The Varkari tradition thus offers a case in which a community of pilgrims organized around shared songs and a shared deity had room for poets from many occupations, while retaining social boundaries that affected how they could participate. The distinction between the poets' compositions and the community's social organization is the key to understanding this tension.

Chokhamela's poems speak with unusual directness about exclusion. They describe the speaker as unable to enter the sanctuary and as unclean by birth, and yet they insist that God's name sanctifies and that Vithoba is moved by devotion rather than status. Tradition associates him with Mangalvedha, where he is said to have died during the construction of a wall, a story that points to the forced labor expected of his community. The poems of his wife Soyarabai and his son Karmamela, together with those of his sister Nirmala and brother-in-law Banka, give the Chokhamela family a rare place as a group of Dalit authors in the medieval record. Their words were preserved by a community that revered them, but the social order that restricted their access to the temple persisted.

The Varkari community's organization was informal for much of its history. Pilgrimage in a procession (wari) to Pandharpur on the eleventh day of the bright half of the months of Ashadha and Kartika, singing, and the practice of wearing a basil-bead necklace and abstaining from certain foods marked a devotee. The processions known as palkhis, carrying the sandals of Dnyaneshwar from Alandi and Tukaram from Dehu, took their present organized form largely in later centuries. The sources therefore show a tradition whose public rituals were elaborated over time rather than established in a single founding moment.

Eknath's contribution lay partly in editing. Tradition credits him with preparing a corrected text of the Jnaneshwari in the sixteenth century and with composing the Eknathi Bhagavata, a Marathi commentary on the eleventh book of the Bhagavata Purana, as well as bharuds, humorous allegorical songs. His writings show a learned Brahmin addressing a wide audience in Marathi, in a period when the language was gaining prestige at the courts of the Deccan sultanates, a context discussed in Chapter 46.

Section 48.10

Ramananda and the Traditions of Northern Rama Devotion

Ramananda, usually placed in the fourteenth or fifteenth century, is regarded by later tradition as the figure who carried devotion to Rama into northern India and who taught disciples of many backgrounds. Hagiographies identify his disciples as including Kabir, Ravidas, Sena the barber, Dhanna the Jat, and Pipa, a king turned renunciant. The Ramanandi community, today a large ascetic and devotional order, traces its origin to him.

Historians must treat these claims with care. Ramananda left little certain writing; a small number of compositions are attributed to him, including a hymn in the Adi Granth that some scholars regard as of uncertain attribution. The accounts connecting him to Kabir and other poets come largely from sectarian biographies and the Bhaktamal, a work composed by Nabhadas around the early seventeenth century, with later commentary by Priyadas, which organized the lives of many devotional poets into a unified lineage. Such works are valuable evidence for how seventeenth-century communities imagined their past, but they cannot show that every claimed relationship existed.

What can be said is that by the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, Rama devotion had become a significant presence in the Hindi-speaking north, with Ayodhya, Banaras, and other centers growing as religious sites, and that a variety of poets drew on devotion to Rama in different ways. Some, like Kabir, used the name Rama for a God beyond form. Others, like Tulsidas (§48.16), worshipped an incarnate Rama. Ramananda is thus better regarded as a symbol of an emerging tradition than as a documented single founder.

The Ramanandi community, whose ascetics are known as Vairagis, organized in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries into groups called akharas and became a major force in Rama devotion, including at Ayodhya and at Galta near Jaipur, where the order established an important center under the patronage of local rulers. The order's claim to descend from Ramananda became a source of legitimacy, and some Ramanandis also claimed an association with the Ramanuja lineage, a connection scholars see as both a theological argument and a way of claiming a respectable pedigree.

The Bhaktamal of Nabhadas, composed around the beginning of the seventeenth century, and Priyadas's commentary of the early eighteenth century, shaped the memory of the entire devotional world by gathering brief portraits of saints from many regions. The compilation was an act of lineage-building: it presented Ramananda's disciples, including artisans and a king, as a harmonious circle. As a historical source it is invaluable for how Rama-devotional communities in the early Mughal period understood their past, but it is not a record of fifteenth-century relationships.

For historians, Ramananda illustrates a recurring problem: a figure around whom a tradition of inclusion was built, about whom little contemporary evidence exists. Rather than rejecting the traditions entirely, scholars treat the later stories of his disciples as evidence for the way communities explained their origins.

Section 48.11

Vallabhacharya, Pushtimarg, and Krishna Devotion

Vallabha (1479–1531), a Telugu-speaking Brahmin who made his religious home in Braj and at Govardhan, founded a Krishna-centered tradition known as Pushtimarg, the "path of nourishment" or divine grace. His philosophy, Shuddhadvaita or pure non-dualism, holds that the world is a real manifestation of Krishna and that liberation comes through Krishna's grace rather than through effort alone. Vallabha wrote commentaries and Sanskrit works, including on the Bhagavata Purana, and he is associated with the worship of Shrinathji, an image at Govardhan later moved to Nathdwara in Rajasthan.

Pushtimarg worship is household- and temple-based. Devotees tend the deity as a beloved child or royal guest, preparing food offerings, dressing and entertaining the image through the day, and singing songs. The tradition emphasizes service, and its devotees are often householders, including merchants, so that wealthy trading families, especially in Gujarat and Rajasthan, became patrons. Vallabha's son Vitthalnath organized the community after Vallabha's death and established a group of eight poets, the Ashtachhap, among them Surdas (§48.15), who sang for the deity.

The community's leaders, descendants of Vallabha, came to be called Goswamis or Maharajas, and the lineage inherited authority and property. This combination of household devotion, hereditary leadership, and wealthy patrons shows how a devotional tradition could become an institution with significant economic power, a theme that returns in the comparison at the end of this chapter.

Vallabha's writings include the Anubhashya on the Brahma Sutras, the Subodhini commentary on the Bhagavata Purana, and a set of sixteen short Sanskrit works. His theology classified souls by the way they attain God: by grace (pushti), by following rules (maryada), and by remaining in worldly life (pravaha), and he taught that Krishna's grace was the highest path. Devotion in this tradition emphasized love and service rather than renunciation, and the community's leaders were married householders, a notable difference from ascetic orders.

Pushtimarg worship is conducted in the haveli, a temple often built in the style of a merchant's house, where the image is treated as a living child-god awakened, bathed, dressed, fed, and put to rest through eight daily viewings (darshans). Music was integral: the Ashtachhap poets, whom Vitthalnath is said to have chosen, including Surdas, Paramanand Das, Krishnadas, Nanddas, and Kumbhandas, composed songs for the services, and the tradition of haveli sangeet continues. Tradition records that the image of Shrinathji was moved from Govardhan to Mewar and installed at Nathdwara in the later seventeenth century, an event Pushtimarg sources link to the political turbulence of the period.

The community's strong connection with Gujarati and Rajasthani merchant families meant that its institutions could draw on mercantile wealth and credit networks. The Vallabha community's central role in trade and finance in western India makes it a key example of how devotional tradition and commercial life intertwined, a theme that connects to the Indian Ocean networks of Chapter 49.

Section 48.12

Chaitanya and the Growth of Gaudiya Vaishnavism

Chaitanya (1486–1533), born in Nabadwip in Bengal, became the central figure of the Gaudiya Vaishnava tradition. After a youth devoted to learning, he took renunciant vows and spent his later years chiefly at Puri in Odisha, in the shadow of the Jagannath temple. Chaitanya's own writings are minimal; he is credited with a few verses. What survives is a body of biographical and theological literature by followers: Vrindavana Dasa's Chaitanya Bhagavata and Krishnadasa Kaviraja's Chaitanya Charitamrita, the latter composed around the early seventeenth century, along with the writings of the Six Goswamis of Vrindavan, including Rupa and Sanatana.

Chaitanya's movement centered on love for Krishna, and especially on Krishna's relationship with Radha, and its public form was sankirtan, communal chanting and singing of Krishna's names, often accompanied by drums and cymbals, in streets and courtyards. The practice brought devotion out of temples and into communal public space. The Goswamis, working in Vrindavan, developed a sophisticated theology of devotional aesthetics and organized Krishna's sacred geography, identifying Braj sites with episodes of Krishna's life.

Gaudiya tradition teaches that devotion is open to all, and several of Chaitanya's close associates came from varied backgrounds, but the community also developed teachers' lineages and rules of its own. Bengal's later Vaishnava communities, including groups sometimes called Sahajiyas and Baul traditions that overlapped with it, show how diverse practice could be. The Bengal Sultanate's religious pluralism (Chapter 47) provided part of the setting, though Chaitanya's relationship with political authority is known chiefly through his biographers.

Chaitanya's emergence has a context in earlier Bengali, Odia, and Maithili devotion. Jayadeva's Gitagovinda, sung at Puri's Jagannath temple, and the lyrics of Vidyapati and Chandidas had already made the story of Radha and Krishna familiar. Chaitanya met teachers in the line of Madhavendra Puri, and Advaita Acharya and Nityananda became his major associates. Nityananda in particular is remembered as the organizer who carried the movement to wide audiences of different castes in Bengal, and his wife Jahnava Devi is recorded as an important leader after his death, one of the notable women in medieval Vaishnava leadership.

The Six Goswamis of Vrindavan, Rupa, Sanatana, Jiva, Gopala Bhatta, Raghunatha Bhatta, and Raghunatha Dasa, gave Chaitanya's devotion its theological and aesthetic structure. Rupa's Bhaktirasamritasindhu and Ujjvalanilamani analyzed devotional emotion in terms borrowed from Sanskrit aesthetics, and Jiva Goswami developed the doctrine of acintya-bhedabheda, the inconceivable simultaneous difference and non-difference of God and the world. The group's work in Vrindavan was assisted by patrons, including Raja Man Singh of Amber, who, as a Mughal noble, sponsored the Govindadeva temple in the late sixteenth century. This is a reminder that Mughal-era political elites could support Vaishnava institutions.

Chaitanya's relationship with the Odia ruler Prataparudra, whose kingdom controlled Puri, is described in the biographies as one of respect, and Odisha became a second center of Gaudiya tradition. The biographies were composed after the leader's death and reflect the community's theological preoccupations, so historians use them with the same care applied to other hagiography.

Section 48.13

Shankaradeva and Assamese Vaishnava Religious Culture

Shankaradeva, traditionally dated 1449–1568 though scholars debate the length of his life, was a poet, playwright, and religious teacher of the Brahmaputra valley. He taught Ekasarana Nama Dharma, a tradition of exclusive devotion to Krishna as the supreme God, centered on chanting the divine name and hearing sacred narrative, and he rejected elaborate ritual sacrifice and image-worship centered on multiple deities. His disciple Madhavadeva carried the tradition forward and expanded its institutions.

The movement produced extraordinary literary and performing arts. Shankaradeva wrote devotional songs (borgeets), dramatic plays (Ankiya Nat) in a mixed Assamese-Brajabuli language, and renderings of the Bhagavata Purana into Assamese. The namghar, a prayer hall for communal recitation, became the center of village religious life, and the satra, a monastic-style institution, served as a center for religious teaching, performance, and landholding. These institutions made devotion a central part of village organization across Assam and are still significant today.

The tradition's relation to political authority was complex. Shankaradeva moved among the Ahom and Koch territories, and tradition says he encountered hostility from orthodox groups and some rulers, while the Koch king Naranarayana is remembered as a patron of the community. Later, the satras acquired land and followers and negotiated with Ahom kings. Chapter 47 discussed regional sultanates and neighbors; Assam's political history belongs largely to other chapters, and this section concentrates on the religious culture.

Shankaradeva's central works include the Kirtan Ghosha, a collection of devotional narratives and songs, and his Assamese versions of Bhagavata material. His religion rested on four elements, the deity (Krishna), the Name, the teacher, and the community of devotees, and he stressed hearing and singing the Name over image worship and elaborate ritual. Tradition holds that he made extensive pilgrimages across India in the late fifteenth century and returned to teach in Assam.

His relations with political power shifted. He taught first under local chiefs and the Ahom kingdom's expanding authority, and later found refuge and patronage in the Koch kingdom under Naranarayana (r. c. 1540–1587), where he spent his later years. After his death the movement divided into several branches (sanghatis), the Purusha, Nika, Brahma, and Kala, with disagreements over leadership, caste, and ritual that showed how a movement with egalitarian ideals could face questions of authority. Madhavadeva's Naam Ghosha remains a central text in namghar worship.

Tradition says that Shankaradeva welcomed disciples from tribal and Muslim backgrounds, and the movement did integrate many communities into a common Assamese religious culture. The dance-drama known as Sattriya, the bhaona theatrical performance, and the borgeets form an artistic legacy that is still living. As elsewhere in this chapter, the record includes both an inclusive ideal and later divisions, and it must be read with the traditions' own sources in view.

Section 48.14

Western Indian Krishna Devotion: Narsinh Mehta and Mirabai

Gujarat and Rajasthan produced their own Krishna traditions. Narsinh Mehta, traditionally placed in the fifteenth century and linked to Junagadh, is honored as the foremost early Gujarati devotional poet. Songs of Krishna's childhood and of the soul's love are credited to him, and a famous song describing the true Vaishnava is associated with his name, though the manuscript evidence is later and the attribution of particular poems is uncertain. Gujarat's Vaishnava tradition developed alongside Jain and Muslim communities in one of the subcontinent's richest merchant regions (Chapter 47).

Mirabai, traditionally a sixteenth-century Rajput princess associated with Mewar and Chittor, is remembered for songs of love for Krishna that defy the expectations of her family and royal society. Her biography is difficult to reconstruct. The earliest manuscripts of poems attributed to her date well after her lifetime, and the poems circulate in Rajasthani, Braj, and Gujarati forms, with large numbers added by later singers. Stories of her persecution by in-laws, attempts to poison her, and wanderings to Vrindavan and Dwarka are part of tradition and probably reflect later retellings. Even the traditional date of her life is debated.

That Mirabai is both difficult to document and widely beloved is the point. Her songs show how a woman could become a symbol of religious freedom, and they show how communities, especially women singing in households and temples, kept her voice alive. Historians reading Mirabai must hold together two things: the uncertainty about her historical life and the enduring importance of the tradition attached to her. Some traditions associate her with Ravidas as teacher, a claim that cannot be demonstrated from early evidence.

Narsinh Mehta is traditionally associated with Junagadh and the fifteenth century, and the poems attributed to him include songs of Krishna's life and a corpus known as the Prabhatiya, morning songs. Scholars point out that the manuscripts containing his poetry date well after his life and that the authorship of individual poems is difficult to establish. His fame in modern Gujarat was reinforced in the twentieth century when Mahatma Gandhi popularized a song associated with his name as a prayer, a later episode that belongs to Chapter 79 rather than to the medieval record.

Mirabai's hymns circulate in Rajasthani, Braj, and Gujarati. Traditional accounts say she was married into the Mewar royal family, widowed, and left the court after hostility from her in-laws, but the earliest literary reference to her in the Bhaktamal says little about biographical detail. The corpus of songs attributed to her expanded greatly over time, and modern critical editions disagree about which poems might belong to the sixteenth century. She is nonetheless a historical presence in the sense that communities, especially women's singing traditions, have preserved her memory, and some communities of lower status regard her as their own.

The significance of both poets lies partly in what the traditions say about society. Narsinh's story involves outcastes and the issue of whether a devotee of any caste can be a true Vaishnava. Mirabai's story addresses a Rajput woman's obligations to a husband's family versus a divine husband, in a society where the honor of lineages depended on women's conduct. The stories reveal anxieties and aspirations of the audiences that repeated them.

Section 48.15

Surdas, Braj Poetry, and the Literary Culture of Krishna Devotion

Surdas, traditionally dated c. 1478–1583, is remembered as a blind poet, a singer in the Pushtimarg tradition, and the author of the Sursagar, an enormous collection of poems on Krishna. Poems describing Krishna's childhood mischief, his love for the cowherd women of Braj, and the longing of separation have made Surdas a central figure of Hindi literature. The language of the poems is Braj Bhasha, a literary dialect of the region around Mathura that became a major medium for poetry in the following centuries, in the courts and the temples alike.

The textual history is complex. The Sursagar, as known today, contains thousands of poems, but the earliest manuscripts include only a fraction, and later centuries added many more under his name. Scholars therefore speak of a Sur tradition: a community of singers and copyists who composed in his style and signature. The connection of Surdas with the Ashtachhap and with Vallabha is established by the tradition's own sources, such as the hagiographical Chaurasi Vaishnavan ki Varta, which was composed later and reflects the community's perspective.

Krishna devotional poetry demonstrates how religious literature interacted with courtly and popular culture. Braj poetry circulated among temple musicians, wandering singers, and, under the Mughal rulers, in courts where Hindi poetry was valued (Chapter 56). Music was crucial: poems were set to ragas and sung in temple services. The study of this literature must therefore consider performance as well as text.

Scholars of the Sur tradition, such as Kenneth Bryant in his study of Surdas's poems on the child Krishna, have argued that the corpus is best understood as a layered body of songs, and that the dated manuscripts from the seventeenth century already show differing selections. The core themes, Krishna's childhood (bal-lila), his love with the gopis, and the separation episode known as the Bhramar Geet, in which the gopis send Krishna's friend Uddhava away with their grief, give the poems a dramatic and emotional range. The latter episode contrasts Uddhava's philosophy of formless knowledge with the gopis' loving attachment to Krishna in a particular form, a debate that echoes the nirguna-saguna distinction discussed in §48.1.

Braj Bhasha became the main language of Hindi poetry in the sixteenth through eighteenth centuries, used in devotional, courtly, and musical contexts. The Mughal court's patronage of Hindi poets and musicians, including figures such as Tansen, helped make Braj a language of prestige, a development Chapter 56 will treat. Devotional poetry thus entered a wider cultural field in which temple singers and court performers shared forms and melodies.

The Chaurasi Vaishnavan ki Varta and related Pushtimarg hagiographies describe Surdas as a blind singer who became a disciple of Vallabha, but they were composed generations after his death and embed him in the community's own narrative. For historians, they are evidence for how the community remembered its poets, with the Sur poems themselves as the more direct, though textually fluid, evidence.

Section 48.16

Tulsidas, the Ramcharitmanas, and North Indian Rama Devotion

Tulsidas, traditionally dated c. 1532–1623 and associated with Banaras and Ayodhya, composed the Ramcharitmanas in the Awadhi dialect of Hindi, which tradition says he began in 1574. The work retells the story of Rama, reworking the Sanskrit Ramayana attributed to Valmiki and other versions, and presents Rama as God incarnate whose name saves the devotee. Tulsidas also wrote other works, including the Vinaya Patrika and Kavitavali, though attribution of some works is debated.

The Ramcharitmanas is above all a text of recitation and performance. It is read aloud, sung, and enacted in the Ramlila dramas, which are staged in towns of northern India, and its couplets entered everyday proverbial speech. Manuscripts and, in the nineteenth century, printed editions spread it widely. Its authority as a devotional text rests on communal practice rather than on any formal canonization.

The poem holds ideals of devotion and of social order. It teaches the power of Rama's name, praises humility and faith, and it also presents a vision of an ideal kingdom, Ramrajya, and of family duty in which hierarchy and gender roles are strongly marked. Passages concerning women and low-status communities have been criticized by modern readers and defended by others, which makes the text an example of how a beloved devotional work can contain both devotional inclusiveness and the social assumptions of its time. Tulsidas's Rama worship also differs sharply from Kabir's nirguna teaching, a contrast that makes clear why the Sant and saguna traditions must be treated separately.

The textual history of the Ramcharitmanas is less contested than that of many devotional poems because of the poem's relative coherence and its clear authorial signature, but even here manuscripts differ, and nineteenth-century printed editions fixed a standard text. Other works attributed to Tulsidas, such as the Vinaya Patrika, the Dohavali, and the Kavitavali, show different moods, including petition and lament. Some works popularly attributed to him, such as the Hanuman Chalisa, rest on tradition rather than secure manuscript attribution.

Tradition says Tulsidas was criticized by Sanskrit scholars of Banaras for writing about Rama in a vernacular and defended his choice, and the story reflects a real tension between Sanskrit learning and the growing prestige of vernacular religious literature. The Ramlila tradition, in which episodes of the story are enacted by local performers, spread the text's influence far beyond readers, and it became associated with specific places such as Ramnagar near Banaras in later periods. The poem was both a work of devotion and a medium of social teaching.

The Rama of Tulsidas is both an incarnation of the supreme God and an ideal king and son, and the poem's hierarchical statements about household and society have been defended and criticized. It is therefore a useful example for students of a text whose devotional power and social assumptions coexist. Chapter 56 will return to Hindu religious developments in the Mughal setting, in which Tulsidas's Awadh was an important region.

Section 48.17

Shaiva Siddhanta, Temple Traditions, and Devotional Institutions

Shiva worship in the medieval south combined devotional poetry, temple ritual, and systematic theology. Shaiva Siddhanta, the "established conclusion" of Shaivism, rests on the Sanskrit Agamas, texts that prescribe temple construction, image installation, and ritual, together with the Tamil hymns of the Nayanars. The Agamic tradition teaches that Shiva, the Lord (pati), is distinct from souls (pashu) and from the bonds (pasha) that bind them, and that Shiva's grace, mediated by ritual initiation, frees the soul. In the thirteenth century, Meykandar's Sivajnanabodham, in Tamil, gave the tradition a compact philosophical statement, and his lineage of teachers developed it further.

Shaiva temple communities organized worship around Agamic priests (Adishaivas), who held hereditary rights in many temples, and around monasteries (mathas) led by teachers who instructed disciples and managed property. Chola kings gave lavishly to Shaiva temples, as Chapter 33 showed, and Shaiva teachers served as royal preceptors. Some mathas held substantial land and enjoyed the status of regional powers. Tamil Shaiva tradition also included the sophisticated Tirumantiram, attributed to Tirumular, and other texts, indicating a wide range of devotional and philosophical expression.

The relationship between learned and devotional strands deserves attention. Agamic ritual prescribed hierarchical roles, while the Nayanars' poems and the narrative of the sixty-three saints celebrated devotees from many walks of life, including a hunter, a potter, and a washerman. The institutional tradition thus held up the saintly ideal while preserving the ritual authority of hereditary specialists, a tension typical of the chapter's larger themes.

Shaiva traditions differed in their sources of authority. The Sanskrit Agamas, traditionally numbered at twenty-eight principal texts, guided temple ritual in the Siddhanta tradition. Commentators in Kashmir, such as Bhatta Ramakantha in the tenth century, and in the south, such as Aghorasiva of Chidambaram in the twelfth century, systematized Agamic theology. The Tamil tradition added the hymns of the Tirumurai, with the Tirumantiram and Meykandar's work forming a bridge between Sanskrit and Tamil learning. Chola tradition remembers Rajaraja I as Tirumurai Kanda Cholan, the king who "found" the sacred hymns, a title that connects royal patronage to the recovery and arrangement of the canon.

The Chidambaram temple, dedicated to Shiva as Nataraja, was staffed by a distinctive hereditary community of priests, the Dikshitars, and its prestige in the Chola period made it a model of temple Shaivism. Tamil Shaiva mathas, among them those at Tiruvavaduturai and Dharmapuram whose surviving organization dates from the sixteenth century and later, trained teachers, managed property, and issued commentaries. They were typically supported by the landed Vellala community, which shows how a devotional and philosophical tradition could be tied to a particular agrarian elite.

The Shaiva world also included ascetic communities, such as the Pashupatas and Kalamukhas of earlier centuries, whose practices were more severe and whose temples and mathas appear in Karnataka and Andhra inscriptions. These communities show that Shaivism was not only a poetic or philosophical tradition but a set of institutions with property, disciples, and political connections.

Section 48.18

Basavanna and the Emergence of Virashaiva–Lingayat Traditions

In twelfth-century Karnataka, a religious community arose that centered on Shiva worship in a new form. Basavanna (Basava), traditionally a Brahmin by birth who held an official position at the court of the Kalachuri king Bijjala at Kalyana, is recognized as the community's most important early figure. Inscriptions and other evidence confirm the period and the political setting, but much of what is known about Basavanna comes from later texts, notably the thirteenth-century Telugu Basavapurana by Palkuriki Somanatha and later Kannada hagiographies. Historians therefore debate how far Basavanna should be considered the "founder" of Virashaivism or a reformer within an existing Shaiva milieu that included earlier teachers.

The distinctive practice of the tradition is the ishtalinga, a small linga that each initiated member wears on the body, in place of worship through temple images and priestly mediation. Wearing the linga signifies a personal and constant relation to Shiva and, in principle, a status of religious equality. The tradition, now widely called Lingayat, rejected many Brahmanical rituals, pollution rules, and birth-based ranking, and it taught that work, whether the labor of a weaver, a cobbler, or a merchant, could be an offering. Tradition speaks of the Anubhava Mantapa, a "hall of experience" where saints discussed religion, though the historical details of such an assembly are uncertain.

The social setting matters. Kalyana was an urban center with merchants, artisans, and administrators, and the vacana poets came from many occupations. Yet the later Lingayat community came to include powerful landowning and commercial groups, and internal distinctions emerged. Whether Lingayat identity is best considered a distinct religion or a Shaiva tradition within Hinduism has itself been a modern political question in Karnataka, which shows how historical interpretation can enter contemporary debate and why historians must distinguish evidence from present-day arguments.

The historical setting of Kalyana helps explain the tradition's character. The city was the capital of the later Chalukyas and then of the Kalachuri Bijjala, and it drew merchants, artisans, administrators, and religious teachers. Basavanna is traditionally said to have served as a treasurer or minister, a position that gave him resources and visibility. Earlier Shaiva teachers, such as Dasimayya, traditionally remembered as a weaver, and Ekantada Ramayya, appear in the tradition as predecessors, which supports the idea that the community grew from a broader Shaiva devotional environment rather than from a single founder.

The later literary accounts tell a dramatic story of conflict. A marriage between the daughter of a Brahmin and the son of a cobbler, arranged by Sharanas, is said to have provoked the king to violent punishment, followed by Bijjala's assassination and the dispersal of the community. The story appears in sources composed a century or more after the events, and historians treat it as a memory of social tension, with its details unproven. What can be said securely is that Kalyana in the twelfth century saw both Shaiva devotional innovation and political instability under the Kalachuris.

Scholars continue to disagree about how to describe the community's relationship to earlier Shaiva practice and to the Vedic tradition. Some Virashaiva writers have emphasized an ancient lineage and the authority of five early teachers, while others, especially Lingayat scholars, have emphasized the twelfth-century reform and its break with Brahmanical norms. These are partly theological and partly social arguments, and they show again how a community's interpretation of its own origins is part of its history.

Section 48.19

The Vacana Poets: Allama Prabhu, Akka Mahadevi, and Social Critique

Vacanas, "sayings," are short, prose-like poems in Kannada, composed by dozens of poets of the twelfth century and later. Each vacana typically ends with a signature address to Shiva under a particular name: Basavanna uses "Lord of the meeting rivers," Allama Prabhu "Lord of caves," Akka Mahadevi "white as jasmine." These endings allow attribution but also invite caution, since later poets could adopt a name. Vacanas were collected centuries later, and the earliest large collections date from the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, so the form of the poems as we have them reflects the community's later editing.

The poems criticize temple building, image worship, caste purity, and priestly authority. A recurring contrast sets the "moving" (jangama) living devotee against the "standing" (sthavara) temple. They also treat work and everyday life as a ground for devotion. Allama Prabhu, a poet-ascetic, is remembered for his paradoxical, riddling verses that question religious knowledge itself. Akka Mahadevi, a woman of the twelfth century associated with Udutadi, is remembered for rejecting an arranged marriage to a king, leaving her home, and wandering in search of Shiva, whom she calls her true husband. Her vacanas express longing, defiance, and mystical union. Other women, such as Nilambike, Muktayakka, and Lakkamma, are also credited with vacanas.

As with other saints, biography and poetry should be kept apart. Akka Mahadevi's journey to Kalyana and her meeting with the saints are known from later accounts. The poems nonetheless offer rare evidence of women's religious self-expression and of a language of radical renunciation within the medieval Deccan.

Vacana collections took shape long after the twelfth century. The Shunyasampadane, compiled in the fifteenth century and later, arranges material in the form of dialogues among the Sharanas and presents Allama Prabhu as a central teacher, and later redactors expanded and reordered it. Such compilations reflect the preoccupations of Lingayat monastic communities at the time, in particular their theology of the stages of spiritual progress. The vacanas of individual poets probably circulated earlier in oral or manuscript form, but historians cannot easily separate original poems from later additions.

Akka Mahadevi's tradition includes a famous story that she abandoned clothing and covered herself with her hair as a sign of her rejection of social convention, and that she traveled to Kalyana and then to the Srisailam region. The story is told in hagiographical sources that postdate her, and scholars treat it as a symbolic account of renunciation. Her vacanas, more securely, speak in the voice of a woman who regards Shiva as her husband, and who criticizes the shame and modesty imposed on women. They are among the earliest substantial writings by a woman in Kannada, which makes their textual transmission especially important.

The vacana form itself deserves notice. It is neither verse in the classical Kannada meters nor ordinary prose, and its rhythmic, speech-like character made it available to poets without formal literary training. That formal choice was itself a social statement, a break with the courtly poetry of earlier Kannada writers such as Pampa, and it helps explain the poets' appeal to later readers as voices of common experience.

Section 48.20

Regional Shaiva Religious Worlds: Kashmir, Karnataka, and the Deccan

Shaiva traditions varied sharply by region. In Kashmir, an influential non-dualist Shaiva philosophy, often called Kashmir Shaivism or Trika, flourished among learned Sanskrit authors such as Abhinavagupta (c. 10th–11th century), who wrote on aesthetics and theology. Beside this learned tradition grew vernacular devotional poetry in Kashmiri. Lal Ded (Lalla), traditionally of the fourteenth century, is remembered for her vakhs, short, direct poems about the experience of the divine within, rejecting ritual display. Her life is known chiefly from later tradition, and her poems exist in a textual history shaped by oral transmission over centuries. Both Hindu and Muslim communities in Kashmir claim her heritage.

Kashmir's Muslim religious history developed alongside. Mir Sayyid Ali Hamadani and his followers brought Kubrawi Sufi teaching to the valley in the fourteenth century, and Nund Rishi (Sheikh Nuruddin), c. 1377–1440, became the figure of the indigenous Rishi tradition, whose ascetic, vegetarian, and nature-linked devotional ethic reveals continuities with earlier Kashmiri religious life. The religious culture of the valley therefore shows how traditions overlapped yet remained distinct. Chapter 47 treated Kashmir's political history.

In Karnataka and the Deccan, Shaiva traditions included Lingayat communities, the Kalamukha and Pashupata ascetic orders of earlier centuries, and temple Shaivism supported by regional dynasties. The Tamil south had Shaiva Siddhanta. Putting these together illustrates that "Shaivism" is a family of traditions with different languages, institutions, and teachings, not a single church.

Kashmir's Shaiva tradition includes philosophers whose writings remain central to the study of Indian thought. Utpaladeva and Abhinavagupta, in the tenth and eleventh centuries, developed the Pratyabhijna and Trika systems, which taught that the individual consciousness can recognize its identity with Shiva. Abhinavagupta's Tantraloka and his works on aesthetics, including a commentary on Bharata's Natyashastra, show a learned culture interested in ritual, philosophy, and the theory of aesthetic experience. Kalhana's twelfth-century Rajatarangini, the first major Sanskrit chronicle, is the principal source for the valley's political history, treated in Chapter 36.

Against this learned background, Lal Ded's vakhs speak in the Kashmiri language. They were transmitted orally for centuries and first compiled in a scholarly edition by George Grierson and Lionel Barnett in the early twentieth century, a reminder that the version students read today has a modern editorial history. Nund Rishi's verses, called shruks, similarly developed from oral tradition. The Rishi order, whose members practiced vegetarianism and meditation in forests and mountains, shows how Islam in Kashmir took local forms that drew on the valley's earlier ascetic practices.

In the Deccan, the shrine of Srisailam, a major Shaiva pilgrimage site in the Telugu region, drew devotees from Karnataka, Andhra, and beyond, and was supported by Vijayanagara rulers, as Chapter 45 noted. Shaiva religious culture here overlapped with Virashaiva networks, and Telugu poets such as Palkuriki Somanatha wrote Shaiva narratives that connected the Telugu and Kannada worlds.

Section 48.21

Shaiva Devotion, Religious Institutions, and the Limits of Social Transformation

The Shaiva traditions studied here differ in their relation to hierarchy. Shaiva Siddhanta incorporated devotional poetry into a ritual order still led by hereditary priests. Lingayat communities rejected many Brahmanical rites and, at least in principle, accepted members from many castes, but they developed their own lineages of monastic leaders (jangamas and mathas) and, over time, became closely associated with particular landowning and trading groups. Kashmir's vernacular poets expressed a deeply interior devotion but did not form a unified institution.

Did these communities change everyday social life? The evidence suggests a mixed picture. The vacana poets denounced pollution and caste distinctions, and inscriptions and later documents show Lingayat monasteries receiving patronage from many social groups, including artisans. Yet subcastes within the community persisted, intermarriage across caste lines was uncommon, and later social reformers continued to challenge inequality among Lingayats themselves. This pattern recurs across the chapter: a tradition can articulate a radical ideal and also build institutions that adapt to existing social structures.

Lingayat institutions grew substantially in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. Monasteries called mathas, led by Jangama teachers, administered property, trained disciples, and mediated disputes, and traditional accounts distinguish five great seats associated with the Panchacharyas as well as many local mathas associated with Virakta ascetics. Lingayat merchants, often called Banajigas, and cultivators supported these institutions. Some rulers of regional kingdoms, such as the Keladi or Ikkeri Nayakas of the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries, were Lingayats, which gave the community political prominence in parts of Karnataka. These developments show how a community that began with an urban critique of ritual and wealth developed its own landed and political foundations.

The Tamil Shaiva case is different in kind. There the dominant institutions were temples and mathas supported by Vellala landholders, and the devotional ideal of the sixty-three Nayanars sat alongside a firm hierarchy of temple service. In Kashmir, learned philosophical Shaivism and the vernacular poetry of Lal Ded were not linked by a single institutional structure. Comparing the three shows that the same deity could be the focus of very different social arrangements, and that a tradition's critique of hierarchy must be assessed against the institutions that preserved it.

Kabir, Ravidas, and the Sant Traditions

Section 48.22

The Sant Traditions and the Language of Formless Devotion

The poets known as Sants, who flourished chiefly in northern India between the fifteenth and seventeenth centuries, are associated with a God described as formless (nirguna), accessible through remembrance of the divine name, and not confined to temple, mosque, or pilgrimage. Many came from artisan, cultivator, and trading communities. Kabir was linked with weaving, Ravidas with leatherwork, Dadu with cotton carding, Sena with barbering, and others with similar occupations. They composed in spoken dialects, often called Sadhukkari, a mixed literary language drawing on Hindi, Punjabi, Rajasthani, and others.

Their poetry has several common features. It uses the speech and images of working life, such as the loom, the spinning wheel, the tanner's tools, and the bazaar. It uses paradox and riddle, "upside-down" language, to unsettle assumptions. It criticizes pretension, whether of Brahmin ritualists, Muslim clerics, ascetics who display their austerities, or rulers. And it places religious truth in inner experience and in the company of fellow seekers, the sadh sangat.

The term Sant is a convenient label, but it also conceals diversity. Some Sants taught renunciation; others taught householder life. Some drew on Vaishnava vocabulary and Rama's name, others on Nath yogic traditions, and others on Sufi expressions of love. The category was refined by modern scholars such as Charlotte Vaudeville, and the Sants themselves did not use a single term for themselves. Treating them as a coherent group can reveal real connections, but historians must not assume a single organization or a shared set of doctrines.

Sant poetry drew on several resources. The Nath yogis, followers of Gorakhnath, taught bodily discipline and the experience of a formless absolute, and their vocabulary of the breath channels and the void entered Sant verse. Vaishnava devotion, including the name of Rama, supplied another vocabulary, and Sufi concepts of love and separation supplied a third. The Sants combined these resources without adopting any one tradition wholesale, and they often composed in a mixed literary language, sometimes called Sant Bhasha or Sadhukkari, that could be understood across much of northern India.

The group of voices later gathered as Bhagats in the Adi Granth gives a sense of the Sant world's breadth. Fifteen poets other than the Sikh Gurus are represented, among them Namdev, Kabir, Ravidas, Sheikh Farid, Ramananda, Sain, Dhanna, Pipa, Sadhna, Trilochan, Beni, Bhikhan, Jaidev, Parmanand, and a Surdas. They span centuries and regions, from twelfth-century Bengal to sixteenth-century Banaras, and not all can be called Sants in the strict sense. The anthology therefore reflects the selecting community's vision of a shared devotional past more than a historical movement.

The social profile of the Sants is notable. Many are identified with craft and service occupations, and the use of working-life images in their poems gave them a distinctive voice. But the identification of poet and occupation often comes from later tradition, and it may have served community purposes, such as presenting a saint as one of the community's own. Historians therefore treat occupational labels as meaningful but not always verifiable.

Section 48.23

Kabir: Historical Setting, Life Traditions, and the Problems of Evidence

Kabir is among the best known and least securely documented figures of the period. Traditional dates of 1398–1518 are widely repeated, but many scholars place him later, in the fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries, perhaps c. 1440–1518. He is associated with Banaras, one of the great religious centers of northern India, and with the community of Muslim weavers, the julaha, who were then adopting Islamic identity while retaining older practices. Hagiographical traditions say he was found as an infant, raised by weaving parents, and associated with Ramananda; his death at Maghar, where Hindus and Muslims are said to have disputed his body, is a famous tale. These narratives mix memory and moral story.

The textual evidence is more concrete but still complex. Three major collections preserve poems attributed to Kabir. The Bijak belongs to the Kabirpanth, the community of his followers, especially in Bihar and Chhattisgarh. The Kabir Granthavali and related compilations come from a Rajasthani Dadupanthi tradition and are among the earliest manuscript witnesses. The Adi Granth of 1604 contains a substantial number of Kabir's verses within the Sikh scripture. These collections overlap partially but differ in language, wording, and arrangement, and each reflects the interests of the compiling community.

Because oral performance shaped Kabir's poetry from the start, there may be no single original text. A poem may exist in many versions, and scholars such as David Lorenzen, Charlotte Vaudeville, and Purushottam Agrawal have worked on the problem of which verses can be linked to the earliest layers of the tradition. For students, the lesson is practical: a statement about "Kabir said" must specify which collection supports it.

The three principal textual streams of Kabir's poetry differ in character. The Bijak, the scripture of the Kabirpanth, is traditionally linked to Kabir's disciple Bhagwan Das and is organized into sections called ramaini, shabda, and sakhi; its own traditional dates are not reliable guides to its composition. Rajasthani collections, including the compilation associated with the Dadupanth and a Panchvani containing poets from several traditions, preserve a body of verse in a Rajasthani-influenced language, and scholars point to the Fatehpur manuscript dated 1582, which preserves poems attributed to Kabir, among the earliest dated witnesses; still earlier Kabir-attributed songs occur in the Goindval pothis, generally dated to the 1570s. The Adi Granth of 1604 preserves Kabir's verses arranged by raga, in the language and orthography of Punjab, and it is the earliest datable large collection that has been continuously preserved.

Scholars such as Linda Hess, Shukdev Singh, Charlotte Vaudeville, David Lorenzen, and Purushottam Agrawal have approached the problem by comparing these streams and by considering the social and religious worlds of the communities that preserved them. They have noted that verses shared among the streams are more likely to belong to an early layer, though even that inference is not certain, since compilers may have borrowed from one another. Kabir may also have been an oral poet whose verses were never fixed in a single authoritative form.

Later traditions offer a pattern of conflicts with authority, including stories that the Delhi sultan Sikandar Lodi punished him, and of his death at Maghar, where a dispute between Hindu and Muslim followers is said to have been resolved when flowers replaced his body. The stories are typical of hagiography, and similar motifs appear in other traditions, including accounts of Guru Nanak's death. Their repetition indicates a shared narrative pattern for saints claimed by several communities. The Kabirpanth later divided into branches, notably the Banaras branch centered on Kabir Chaura and the Dharamdasi branch of Chhattisgarh, whose histories belong to the later life of the tradition.

Historical Debate

How Reliable Are Saintly Biographies?

The question: Can the lives of saints such as Kabir, Ravidas, Mirabai, and Guru Nanak be reconstructed from hagiographies and traditions composed long after their deaths?

Interpretation A — Careful use of traditions: Some historians argue that hagiographies preserve genuine memories of social background, places, and relationships, and that when several independent traditions agree on a detail, it deserves consideration. Hagiography can also show what communities valued, which is itself historical evidence.
Interpretation B — Biographies as community memory: Other scholars stress that such texts are shaped by religious purpose, filled with miracles, and written to establish lineage and authority, so they cannot reliably reconstruct individuals' lives. They are best read as evidence for the later community, with the earliest poetry given priority over later narrative.

Evidence: The two approaches draw on the same sources: collections of poetry with uncertain textual histories, janamsakhis, Bhaktamal, the Chaurasi Vaishnavan ki Varta, Sufi tazkiras, and hagiographies of the Nayanars. They differ on how much a late narrative can inform an earlier life.

Why it matters: The question affects how confidently historians can assign dates, social origins, teachers, and doctrines to individual poets, and therefore how they describe connections among traditions.

Section 48.24

Kabir's Teachings: Religious Authority, Ritual Criticism, and Social Boundaries

Kabir's poems question religious certainty. They ridicule those who claim that God is found only in a temple or a mosque, in a pilgrimage, a fast, a recitation, or a particular dress. They set direct experience of the divine, often expressed as love and as the remembrance of the divine name, above book learning. They use the language of Vaishnava bhakti, of Nath yoga, and of Sufi love without belonging fully to any one of them. The poet speaks as a weaver who knows the cost of thread and cloth, and uses craft images for spiritual truth.

Kabir also criticizes caste pretension. Poems ask how Brahmin birth differs from any other birth, or why one should be called pure and another polluted, when all come from the same source. These poems are central to Kabir's later use as a symbol of social protest, but they are only part of a broader critique of any claim that religious status depends on outward marks. Similarly, he criticizes both Hindu and Muslim religious professionals in terms that later communities have read differently, some as a call for unity, others as part of a distinct teaching.

Modern attempts to classify Kabir as Hindu, Muslim, or as a religion unto himself raise anachronistic questions. His audience was a society of overlapping religious practices, in which weavers' communities and yogic lineages, Sufi hospices and Vaishnava temples all existed. The Kabirpanth later became an organized religious community with its own monasteries and scriptural tradition. The poems should therefore be read first in their historical setting, and the later community should be recognized as a separate stage of the story.

Reading Kabir requires attention to genre and rhetoric. His ulatbamsi, "upside-down sayings," set riddles such as a fish that thirsts in water or a lake without water, to disorient listeners and push them to recognize the limits of ordinary language. His use of the terms Ram, Hari, Allah, and Khuda is not a simple mixture of religions but a strategy of naming the one reality beyond all names. His sharpest satire targets the display of religious identity, such as ritual bathing, ascetic dress, circumcision, and recitation, when it substitutes for inward realization.

His treatment of women is more ambiguous than his treatment of caste. Many poems speak of the soul as a bride awaiting her beloved, using women's experience as a source of religious metaphor, but others warn male seekers against the temptation of women, in a language familiar from ascetic traditions. Any simple claim that Kabir was a champion of social equality in every respect therefore needs qualification. The poems offer a powerful critique of birth-based status, but not a comprehensive program of social reform.

The question of Kabir's guru is a measure of how traditions build lineages. Hagiography names Ramananda, and some poems refer to a guru without naming him, while Sufi lineage claims have also been proposed. The poems emphasize the importance of the true guru (satguru) as an inner or outer guide, a theme that would also become central to Sikh teaching, though in a different theological form.

Section 48.25

Ravidas: Caste, Labor, Equality, and Devotional Community

Ravidas (Raidas), probably active in the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries, is associated with Banaras and with the Chamar community, whose traditional occupation included leatherwork. Little certain biographical detail survives. The earliest firm evidence for his poetry is the roughly forty compositions of his that appear in the Adi Granth of 1604, supplemented by later collections in northern India with additional verses. A later tradition links him to Ramananda as a teacher, and a different tradition ties Mirabai to him; these claims arise in hagiographies and cannot be verified.

His poems speak openly from a position of low social status. They affirm that devotion can transform a person regarded as polluted into one honored by God, and they describe himself as a cobbler. The best-known poem presents Begampura, a city "without sorrow," where there is no tax, no suffering, and no distinction of status, an ideal society envisioned by a poet who knew the injustice of the actual one. Historians read Begampura as a vision of equality rather than a political program, but later Dalit movements have claimed it as an inspiration.

Communities honoring Ravidas developed over the centuries, particularly in Punjab and neighboring regions, and in the twentieth century organized as Ravidassia communities with distinctive institutions. That later development belongs to modern history; the earlier question is how Ravidas's memory was preserved, and who preserved it. The Adi Granth's inclusion of his poetry shows that by 1604 his words had a recognized place in a wider devotional canon, though the social inclusion of his followers within that wider world remained a continuing problem.

The roughly forty hymns of Ravidas in the Adi Granth are the main secure witness to his thought, and they reveal a poet who writes of God as the one who raises the lowly. Some of his verses describe the transformation of his own humble condition through devotion, and others use the imagery of his trade. They suggest an audience in the artisan communities of Banaras, who experienced both the commercial opportunities of a major pilgrimage and textile center and the disabilities of low status. The poem on Begampura is brief but memorable for its vision of a place where no one is taxed, no one is in want, and all live in equality. Scholars such as Gail Omvedt have read it as an early expression of the hope of a casteless society, while others caution that it is a devotional vision of a spiritual realm as much as a political ideal.

The memory of Ravidas has been important to many communities. Hagiographies, including the Bhaktamal, depict him as a pious cobbler honored by Brahmins and kings, and sixteenth-century Rajput traditions link him to Rajasthani royal women as a guru. These stories were likely created by communities, including devotees of Mirabai and the Ramanandi order, who wished to associate their traditions with his prestige. In the twentieth century, communities in Punjab and neighboring regions developed organized devotion to Ravidas, and in 2010 some leaders declared Ravidassia a separate religion with its own scripture, a development Chapter 107 will place in the history of contemporary caste and religion.

For historians, Ravidas poses the problem of recovering voices of low-status persons from sources that were often preserved by others. The Adi Granth's inclusion of his verses shows respect for them, but the compilers selected, and the fuller range of his teaching may not survive. This is a general caution for the study of marginalized figures: their presence in the record is often mediated by communities that were not their own.

Section 48.26

Dadu Dayal and the Expansion of North Indian Sant Networks

Dadu Dayal (1544–1603) was born, according to tradition, in Ahmedabad, to a family connected with cotton carding, and lived much of his life in Rajasthan, where he settled at Sambhar and later Naraina. Like Kabir, he taught devotion to a formless God and criticized sectarian division, and his poems use similar vocabulary. His followers formed the Dadupanth, which became an organized community with monastic and householder branches, including a warrior wing in later centuries. Disciples such as Rajjab and Sundardas compiled and wrote poetry that shaped the tradition's literary identity.

Dadu's life coincided with Akbar's reign (Chapter 52), and a tradition says he was invited to Fatehpur Sikri for religious discussion. Whether and how this occurred is known mainly from the community's own accounts, so it should be treated as a memory of the Mughal context rather than a confirmed event. Dadupanthi scholars preserved an important early collection of Kabir's poetry, which makes the community a significant witness to the Sant tradition itself.

Comparing Dadu with Kabir and Ravidas shows both overlap and distinction. All three emphasize inner devotion and criticize ritualism, but their communities, locations, and later organizations differed. Dadupanth became widely linked with Rajasthani society and local ruling houses, while Ravidas's memory remained strongly connected with working communities. These variations caution against presuming a single Sant movement.

Dadu's life shows the importance of regional context. Tradition associates him with Ahmedabad, where he was reportedly raised, and with Rajasthan, where he found followers among merchants, artisans, and peasants. His community's name for its principal text, the Dadu Vani, covers a collection of verses (sakhis and padas) compiled after his death by disciples such as Santdas and Jagannath. The tradition reports that Dadu came from a family of cotton carders, an occupation associated with Muslims in much of northern India, though Dadupanthi hagiography has also described him as a foundling of Brahmin origin. The ambiguity illustrates the way communities adjusted a founder's identity to meet later needs.

The Dadupanth developed internal divisions between householders, celibate monks, and a militarized branch known as the Nagas, who served Rajput states as soldiers in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. That an order founded on the teaching of a formless God and the rejection of sectarian strife developed a warrior wing shows again that institutions can evolve away from their founders' emphases. The Dadupanth also became a major center of manuscript preservation, copying works of Kabir, Namdev, Ravidas, and others.

Other Sants of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, among them Sundardas, who was Dadu's disciple and a learned poet, and Malukdas of Kara, expanded the networks of Sant devotion. They demonstrate that the movement was not limited to a few famous figures, and that local communities formed around dozens of teachers, each with their own manuscript traditions and lineages.

Section 48.27

Oral Poetry, Written Anthologies, Sectarian Lineages, and the Preservation of Sant Teachings

Sant poetry was composed to be sung, memorized, and performed in gatherings. Its preservation depended on singers, followers, and, later, scribes. Written anthologies, such as the Panchvani of the Dadupanthis, the Bijak of the Kabirpanth, and the Adi Granth, were compiled by communities that selected, arranged, and sometimes revised verses to fit their own understanding. The Adi Granth, notably, included poems attributed to many Sants alongside the compositions of the Sikh Gurus, and its compilers clearly selected verses consistent with the Sikh theological outlook.

Lineages of discipleship, often stated in later texts, connected teachers into chains: Ramananda to Kabir, Kabir to Dharamdas, and so on. Such lineages gave authority to new communities, but they also sometimes imposed order on relationships that were looser or may not have occurred. Historians therefore examine whether early sources support a claimed connection.

An enduring tension runs through this process. Teachers who criticized institutionalized religion left words that inspired followers to build institutions: monasteries, scriptures, rituals, and rules. The Kabirpanth developed ritual practices of its own, and Dadupanth, Ravidassia, and Sikh communities each became distinct. This is not a contradiction unique to South Asia, but it is vividly visible here.

The Adi Granth's treatment of the Bhagats illustrates editorial practice. Guru Arjan included their verses but, in many places, added his own verses, often called shaloks, that comment on or reframe the Bhagat's meaning, a practice that Pashaura Singh and other scholars have interpreted as a way of affirming Sikh theology while honoring the earlier poets. For example, a Bhagat's verse about devotion might be followed by a Guru's verse emphasizing the necessity of the Guru's word. The editorial frame thus shows the compilers' interpretive aims.

Manuscript culture affected the sources in several ways. Scribes in different communities copied verses with variant wording, rearranged them, and sometimes added signature lines. The Panchvani anthologies of the Dadupanthis grouped Sant poets in a way that suited that community's vision. In the nineteenth century, printing presses in Banaras, Lucknow, Bombay, and Lahore standardized texts of the Bijak, the Ramcharitmanas, and other works, and colonial scholars such as Grierson, who wrote on the vernacular literature of Hindustan, shaped the way these texts were classified. Students should remember that most texts they read today have passed through several editorial hands.

The transmission process shows a persistent tension: poems created as criticism of fixed religious forms were gathered into collections, copied in monasteries, and recited as scripture. That does not mean the poems lost their force. Many readers and listeners continue to hear in them a challenge to religious complacency, even as the communities that preserve them observe their own rules and rituals.

Section 48.28

Sufism in South Asia: Teachings, Orders, and Historical Sources

Sufism, in Arabic tasawwuf, designates the Muslim traditions of inner purification, ethical discipline, and love of God. By the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, when Sufi teachers began to settle in significant numbers in South Asia, Sufism had become organized into orders (silsilas, literally "chains"), each tracing its teaching authority through a line of masters back to the Prophet Muhammad. A master, called a pir or shaykh, accepted disciples (murids), guided their spiritual training, and appointed a successor (khalifa). The community typically met at a khanqah, a lodge that offered shelter, meals, instruction, and devotional practice. After a master's death, his tomb became a dargah, a shrine that drew visitors for generations.

Sufis were not outside Islam. They studied the Quran and the law, and most accepted Islamic legal norms while emphasizing the inward dimension of religion. They also disagreed with one another, and with other Muslim scholars (ulama), over questions such as the use of music in devotion (sama), relations with rulers, the acceptance of wealth, and the significance of mystical experience. These disagreements are crucial for understanding the diversity of Sufi communities and for avoiding the picture of Sufism as a single, tolerant, mediating force.

The sources are mainly Persian. Malfuzat, records of a master's conversations compiled by a disciple, preserve teachings and social detail. Tazkiras, collective biographies of saints, such as the sixteenth- and seventeenth-century compilations of Abd al-Haqq Muhaddis Dehlawi, collect miracle narratives and genealogies. Letters (maktubat), poetry, and shrine documents add to the record. Historians such as Carl Ernst, Bruce Lawrence, Richard Eaton, and Simon Digby have shown how these sources can be read critically: the texts often present idealized masters, and they were frequently written by disciples with a stake in an order's reputation. Chapter 44 introduced Sufi orders as part of Sultanate society; this chapter examines them as religious communities.

Sufism arrived in South Asia well before the Delhi Sultanate. Ali Hujwiri, who died in Lahore in the later eleventh century in the Ghaznavid period, wrote the Persian Kashf al-Mahjub, one of the earliest Persian treatises on Sufism, and his shrine, known popularly as Data Ganj Bakhsh, remains one of Lahore's major sites. Sufis in Sindh and Multan, and merchant communities on the western coast (Chapter 38), also predated the Sultanate. The thirteenth-century expansion of Sufi lodges was thus part of a longer history.

Sufi practice involved both law and discipline. Most Sufis in the Sultanate followed the Hanafi legal school in daily life, and teachers stressed obligatory prayer, fasting, and moral conduct as the foundation of spiritual progress. Additional practices included remembrance (dhikr), recited aloud in some orders and silently in others, retreats, and vigils. Initiation (bay'a) bound a disciple to a master, who might give a patched cloak (khirqa) as a sign of authority. Theological debate also divided Sufis, such as the later controversy between those who followed Ibn Arabi's doctrine of the unity of being (wahdat al-wujud) and those, like Ahmad Sirhindi, who preferred the unity of witnessing (wahdat al-shuhud).

Sufi culture also included vernacular literature. Hindavi romances, such as Mulla Daud's Chandayan (1379) and Malik Muhammad Jayasi's Padmavat (1540), drew on local stories to express Sufi ideas of love and the search for union, and the genre is called premakhyan. The existence of such works shows that Sufi communities took part in the vernacular literary world, even while Persian remained the main language of learned and administrative life.

Historical Vocabulary
Silsila, Pir, Khanqah, Dargah, and Sama

A silsila is the chain of teachers linking a Sufi to earlier masters and, symbolically, to the Prophet. A pir (or shaykh) is a spiritual guide, and a murid is a disciple who pledges allegiance to him. A khanqah is a lodge for teaching, devotion, and hospitality, while a dargah is the shrine around a saint's tomb. Sama is a devotional audition, often involving poetry and music, that some orders, especially the Chishtis, practiced and others criticized.

These institutions were part of Islamic religious life, not a separate religion. The same terms apply across regions, but practices varied by order, region, and time.

Section 48.29

The Early Chishti Tradition: Muinuddin Chishti, Qutbuddin Bakhtiyar Kaki, and Baba Farid

The Chishti order takes its name from Chisht, a town in present-day Afghanistan. Its South Asian history is traditionally traced to Muinuddin Chishti, who settled at Ajmer and died, according to tradition, around 1236. Very little contemporary evidence survives about him, and most accounts come from later centuries. Ajmer, a Chauhan center that came under Ghurid control (Chapter 39), later became one of the most visited shrines in South Asia, patronized by sultans and, from the sixteenth century, Mughal emperors (Chapter 52). The shrine's importance is therefore much better documented than the saint's life.

Qutbuddin Bakhtiyar Kaki (d. 1235), Muinuddin's disciple, lived in Delhi during the reign of Iltutmish (Chapter 40), and his tomb at Mehrauli became a Delhi shrine. His successor in teaching, Baba Farid (Farid al-Din Ganj-i Shakar, d. 1265), settled at Ajodhan, today Pakpattan in Pakistan's Punjab, where he established a famous lodge that welcomed the poor. His lodge gained a reputation for shared food and generosity and for a rule of life that kept distance from the Sultanate court. Early sources, notably the Fawa'id al-Fu'ad, quote Nizamuddin recalling Farid's teaching, and later tradition added many miracles.

Verses attributed to "Sheikh Farid" in Punjabi appear in the Adi Granth (§48.41). Scholars debate whether these were composed by Baba Farid himself, by a later descendant at the Pakpattan shrine often called Farid II, or by several poets in the tradition. The Chishti connection to Punjabi devotional poetry is nevertheless real, because the shrine at Pakpattan became a center of Punjabi religious culture in which the Sikh Gurus later participated, and Guru Nanak is traditionally said to have visited it.

The earliest detailed source for Muinuddin Chishti is the Chishti hagiography of the later fourteenth century, and later works such as the Akhbar al-Akhyar elaborate his story. The shrine at Ajmer received patronage from the Khalji sultans of Malwa in the fifteenth century and from Mughal emperors, notably Akbar, whose pilgrimages to Ajmer are recorded in Mughal chronicles beginning in the 1560s (Chapter 52). The saint's reputation grew with the shrine's political importance, as rulers linked themselves to his spiritual authority.

Tradition says that Qutbuddin Bakhtiyar Kaki died after hearing a verse during a sama gathering, an anecdote used in later debates as an illustration of the intensity of Chishti practice. His successor Baba Farid developed a lodge at Ajodhan that emphasized poverty and service, and his disciples included Nizamuddin and Ali Ahmad Sabir, whose line later formed the Sabiri branch of the Chishti order. Farid's reputation for charity was linked to a nickname meaning "treasure of sugar" (Ganj-i Shakar), which tradition explains with a miracle story; the story is best read as an expression of the lodge's association with generosity.

The early Chishti attitude toward rulers emphasized distance. Teachers refused state grants and avoided appearing at court, and the community preferred to live on gifts that were immediately redistributed. This ethic, which the community's own sources emphasize, may be partly idealized, since rulers and nobles visited the lodges, and gifts and land grants eventually came to support descendants. The tension between ideal and practice belongs to the history of every institution studied in this chapter.

Section 48.30

Nizamuddin Auliya, Delhi's Chishti Communities, and the Culture of Sufi Instruction

Nizamuddin Auliya (1238–1325) was Baba Farid's most famous disciple and headed the Chishti community of Delhi for decades, through the reigns of several Sultanate rulers, including the Khaljis and early Tughlaqs (Chapters 41 and 42). His lodge at Ghiyaspur, on the edge of Delhi, attracted people from all ranks, including nobles, soldiers, merchants, craftspeople, and the poor. The lodge distributed food daily, kept no endowed property, and maintained a studied independence from the court, though sultans sought his blessing and relations were sometimes tense.

His teaching survives in the Fawa'id al-Fu'ad ("Morals of the Heart"), recorded by the poet Amir Hasan Sijzi from 1307 to 1322. The work records conversations on topics such as renunciation, love of God, service to others, the etiquette of discipleship, and the dangers of pride, along with stories of earlier saints. It is a rich source because it is arranged by date and reports the setting of the conversations, though like any malfuzat it is shaped by the compiler's selection and by the later community's reverence.

Chishti practice included sama, devotional listening to poetry set to music, which Nizamuddin defended against criticism from some jurists. The poet and musician Amir Khusrau (1253–1325), a courtier of several sultans and a close disciple, is traditionally credited with innovations in Persian and Hindavi poetry and with musical forms; later tradition attributes many specific songs and instruments to him that cannot be verified, so his legacy should be separated from the historical man. Nizamuddin's successors, notably Nasiruddin Chiragh-i Dehli (d. 1356), maintained the order, though the Tughlaq period brought strains and migrations of Chishti disciples to the Deccan, Gujarat, and elsewhere.

Nizamuddin's lodge was a social space of unusual breadth. The malfuzat describe visitors of many kinds, including jurists, soldiers, poets, and the poor, and the lodge's open hospitality made it a point of contact across Delhi's social ranks. His relations with the Khalji and Tughlaq courts were complicated: stories say that he refused to meet Sultan Alauddin Khalji and that he came into conflict with Ghiyasuddin Tughlaq, but the sources are devotional and their details cannot be taken at face value. What is clear is that the shaykh's moral authority was recognized by rulers and that the community survived changing political fortunes.

The Chishtis of Delhi were also a network. Nizamuddin sent deputies (khalifas) to distant regions, and the Chishti presence in the Deccan, Gujarat, Bengal, and Malwa grew from these appointments. Burhanuddin Gharib went to the Deccan in the 1330s, and his tomb at Khuldabad became an important shrine. After Nizamuddin, Nasiruddin Chiragh-i Dehli's conversations were recorded in the Khayr al-Majalis, and later Chishti hagiography, such as the Siyar al-Auliya by Mir Khurd, assembled the lives of the order's saints.

Amir Khusrau's relationship with Nizamuddin is among the most celebrated in the order's memory. Khusrau was a prolific Persian poet and a courtier to several sultans; his Hindavi verses are widely attributed to him, but their early manuscript evidence is thin, and many popular songs and musical inventions credited to him cannot be verified. The historical Khusrau is best known through his Persian writings, which include poems on contemporary events and royal patrons and offer valuable evidence for Sultanate culture.

Section 48.31

Suhrawardi Communities, Bahauddin Zakariya, and Relations with Political Authority

The Suhrawardi order, traced to the Baghdad teacher Shihab al-Din Abu Hafs Umar al-Suhrawardi, took root in Multan, Uch, and Sindh. Bahauddin Zakariya (d. c. 1262), based in Multan, is its key early figure. Unlike the Chishti lodges, the Suhrawardi community generally accepted wealth, property, and ties with political authority, and Bahauddin Zakariya maintained a large lodge, supported by endowments, which engaged in trade and agriculture. His grandson Rukn-i Alam (d. c. 1335) is remembered for a notable tomb in Multan, a fine example of early Indo-Islamic architecture (Chapter 44).

Differences between Chishti and Suhrawardi attitudes to wealth are sometimes stated as a sharp contrast, with the former austere and the latter worldly. The sources show a more varied picture: Suhrawardi leaders used wealth to maintain hospitality and to protect their community in a region exposed to Mongol raids and shifting political authority, while Chishti lodges also relied on gifts. Still, the difference in public position remains important, as it shaped the way each order appeared to rulers and to the public.

The Suhrawardi presence in Uch and Sindh persisted through the fourteenth century. Sayyid Jalaluddin Bukhari, known as Makhdum Jahaniyan Jahangasht (d. 1384), based in Uch, was a prominent teacher who traveled widely and had a close connection with the Tughlaq court. Suhrawardi networks thus linked the Punjab, Sindh, and the Gangetic plain, and later spread to Gujarat and Bengal.

The Suhrawardi order's intellectual foundation lay in Shihab al-Din al-Suhrawardi's Awarif al-Ma'arif, a widely used manual of Sufi conduct. Bahauddin Zakariya's training in Baghdad and his establishment of a lodge at Multan gave the community direct links to the wider Islamic world, and Multan's position as a trade and administrative center on the route between Khurasan and the Indus plains made the lodge an important institution. The grants and offerings it received supported an extensive household, and the order's willingness to engage in landholding and trade set it apart from the Chishtis, though both orders relied on community support.

Rukn-i Alam's tomb in Multan, built in the early fourteenth century and notable for its brick construction and glazed tile decoration, is an enduring sign of the order's importance and of Sultanate architectural patronage, which Chapter 44 discussed. The Suhrawardi presence also extended to Bengal, where the thirteenth-century teacher Jalaluddin Tabrizi, associated with Pandua, is remembered by later tradition as a Suhrawardi saint, and to Uch, where the Bukhari sayyids built a network of shrines. Their influence in Sindh and the Punjab continued under the Mughals.

Differences in attitude toward wealth and politics were real but not absolute. Suhrawardis accepted rulers' gifts and sometimes advised them, and Chishtis took gifts as well, though they emphasized distribution. Both worked within a society where saints' shrines attracted donations, and both had to maintain legitimacy before a public that expected generosity as well as piety.

Section 48.32

Sufi Communities in Bengal, Bihar, Kashmir, and the Deccan

Sufi orders extended across the subcontinent. In Bengal, tradition associates the saint Shah Jalal with the conquest and conversion of Sylhet in the fourteenth century, but the surviving accounts are largely later and mix legend with history. Richard Eaton's studies of the Bengal frontier show that Sufi shrines and lodges helped to anchor Islam in a region of expanding agriculture and local religious practice, where saints such as Pir Badr and later folk figures such as Satya Pir attracted devotees of several communities. Pandua and Gaur were Bengal Sultanate centers (Chapter 47) in which Chishti and other Sufis had important lodges, such as that associated with Akhi Siraj and Nur Qutb Alam.

In Bihar, Sharafuddin Yahya Maneri (d. 1381) led the Firdausi order, a Kubrawi branch that had settled in Delhi before taking root in Bihar, from Maner and Bihar Sharif. His letters, the Maktubat-i Sadi, offer extensive guidance on spiritual discipline and on living faithfully amid the pressures of social life, and are among the finest of early Sufi writings in South Asia. In Kashmir, as noted in §48.20, the Kubrawi teaching of Mir Sayyid Ali Hamadani and the local Rishi order shaped the valley's religious culture.

In the Deccan, the Chishti presence expanded with Sultanate conquest and later the Bahmani kingdom (Chapter 46). Gesu Daraz (Sayyid Muhammad Husayni, d. 1422), a Delhi Chishti who moved to Gulbarga, wrote extensively on mystical subjects, and his shrine at Gulbarga became a major pilgrimage site. Sufis were involved in the politics of the Bahmani court, sometimes as advisers and sometimes in tension with rulers, but the focus here is their religious institutions, whose roots lay in earlier Delhi traditions. Regional Sufi shrines thus reveal both the shared structure of the orders and distinct local histories.

The Firdausi order, a branch of the Kubrawi tradition that settled in Delhi and later in Bihar, produced Sharafuddin Maneri, whose letters to disciples cover topics from daily ethics to the highest mystical states. They are valuable for the history of Sufi thought and for glimpses of everyday religious guidance, and the collection Maktubat-i Sadi circulated widely in the region. Bihar's Sufi shrines, like those of Maner and Bihar Sharif, remain active centers.

In Bengal, the shrine culture of Pandua and Gaur, the saints associated with eastern Bengal, and the folk traditions around figures such as Pir Badr and Satya Pir illustrate how Islam became rooted in a land of rivers, forests, and shifting agriculture. Richard Eaton argued that Sufi shrines and the clearing of land for cultivation were closely linked, so that holy men became patrons of settlement and cultural brokers. Other scholars have cautioned that the evidence for such a role varies by region and that Sufi hagiographies idealize their subjects. The debate belongs to a larger question about how conversion to Islam happened, which Chapter 44 raised and which this chapter does not treat as the work of a single agent.

In Gujarat, the shrine of Shaykh Ahmad Khattu at Sarkhej near Ahmedabad, associated with the Sultanate of Gujarat in the fifteenth century (Chapter 47), shows the connection between Sufi saints and regional courts, and in the Deccan the shrine of Gesu Daraz at Gulbarga, with its annual urs, continues to draw visitors of many communities. Gesu Daraz wrote on Sufi doctrine in Arabic and Persian and may have composed Deccani Hindavi verse, though attributions are uncertain. In Kashmir, the arrival of Mir Sayyid Ali Hamadani and his followers in the fourteenth century is associated with significant changes in the valley's religious culture and with works on ethics and statecraft such as the Zakhirat al-Muluk.

Section 48.33

Qadiri, Shattari, and Naqshbandi Traditions in the Sixteenth and Early Seventeenth Centuries

New orders spread in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. The Qadiri order, tracing itself to Abd al-Qadir Jilani of Baghdad, was established in South Asia by teachers such as Muhammad Ghawth of Uch (d. 1517), and became widespread in the Punjab, Sindh, and the Deccan. Its disciples came from varied backgrounds, and it later had a major following among Muslim communities, including some in the Mughal court.

The Shattari order, associated with Shah Abdullah Shattari (d. 1485) and with Muhammad Ghawth Gwaliori (d. 1562 or 1563), whose Persian work Jawahir-i Khamsa dealt with esoteric practices, took a more experimental approach to devotion. Shattari teachers engaged with yogic breathing practices and Sanskrit and vernacular material, and the order established centers at Mandu, Gwalior, and elsewhere. Its openness drew both interest and criticism from stricter scholars.

The Naqshbandi order, arriving from Central Asia, took root with Khwaja Baqi Billah (d. 1603) in Delhi. His disciple Ahmad Sirhindi (1564–1624), called the Mujaddid-i Alf-i Thani ("renewer of the second millennium"), wrote letters criticizing practices he considered un-Islamic, including some court customs and some Sufi devotional forms, and stressed adherence to the law. Jahangir imprisoned him for a time at Gwalior (c. 1619–1620), and he was later released. Sirhindi's influence became large after his death, and scholars debate whether his thought should be seen as a response to Akbar's religious experiments (Chapter 52) or mainly as a reform movement internal to Sufism. This section only establishes the transition; Chapter 53 turns to Jahangir's court in more detail.

These developments demonstrate that Sufism was not a harmonizing tradition by default. Its practitioners disagreed over law, music, relations with non-Muslims, and the use of Indic spiritual practices, and these debates mattered in the Mughal period.

The Qadiri order's growth in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries was linked to the migrations of sayyid families and to the order's emphasis on law-abiding piety. In Lahore, Mian Mir (d. 1635), a Qadiri saint, attracted a following that included the Mughal prince Dara Shikoh, and A later Sikh tradition credits Mian Mir with laying the foundation of the Harimandir at Amritsar. This account is not corroborated by the earliest surviving Sikh or Sufi sources and should be treated as a later historical memory, not an established event. The tradition itself is evidence for how subsequent communities portrayed interreligious respect in the Punjab.

The Shattari order, represented by Muhammad Ghawth Gwaliori, absorbed some yogic and Indic practices, and Persian translations and adaptations of Sanskrit and Hindavi works circulated within its circles. These interactions provoked criticism from stricter Muslim scholars. The Naqshbandi order, in contrast, emphasized the Sunni law, silent remembrance, and the avoidance of music, and Sirhindi's letters, collected as the Maktubat-i Imam-i Rabbani, argued for a renewed emphasis on law and criticized Shia belief and practice as well as certain Hindu and court customs. His influence expanded in later centuries, especially through his successors in the Mughal and Afghan worlds.

The Mughal court's engagement with Sufi teachers was extensive. Abd al-Haqq Muhaddis Dehlawi, a Qadiri scholar and hadith specialist in Delhi, wrote the Akhbar al-Akhyar on Indian saints, and his work shows an effort to align Sufi memory with legal learning. The differing orientations of these figures demonstrate that Sufism was no unified response to Mughal rule but a set of competing approaches within the Muslim community.

Section 48.34

Dargahs, Pilgrimage, Devotional Music, Charity, and Sufi Social Life

Dargahs became places of social and religious life. Visitors came to seek the saint's blessing (baraka), to pray, to fulfill vows, to hear qawwali and other devotional music, and to receive food from the communal kitchen (langar). The annual commemoration of the saint's death, the urs, drew large gatherings. People from many communities, including Hindus, Muslims, and others, visited shrines, though with varying understandings of what they were doing. A dargah's management typically rested with descendants of the saint or successors in the order (sajjada-nashin), who held endowed land and received gifts from rulers and the public. This made shrines economic and political institutions as well as religious ones.

Access was shaped by gender and status. Women visited shrines in large numbers, especially to seek help with family concerns, yet some shrines restricted women's access to inner spaces, and debates over such restrictions, and over music and visitation practices, recur in the sources. Some jurists criticized shrine rituals such as prostration at tombs, and reformist Sufis such as Ahmad Sirhindi shared some of those objections. Such criticism shows that the legitimacy of shrine practice was contested within the Muslim community.

Shrines also carried social memory. They preserved genealogies, documents, and the property rights of descendants, and they appear in rulers' records of land grants. Dargahs thus connect religious history to economic history, linking the Sultanate and Mughal land systems to devotional life, a theme Chapter 56 will take up for the Mughal period.

Shrines were embedded in the economy. Rulers granted revenue-free lands (suyurghal or madad-i ma'ash) to shrines and their descendants, and surviving farmans and shrine records document these grants. Descendants managed endowments, hosted pilgrims, and sometimes acted as local mediators. The shrines of Ajmer, Pakpattan, Delhi, Gulbarga, and many smaller sites each had their own families of custodians and sometimes their own disputes over succession and revenue, which makes their records useful for studying local society.

Debates about conversion cast a long shadow over the study of Sufi shrines. Older narratives suggested that Sufis converted large numbers of people through preaching or miracles, while others emphasized the sword. Contemporary scholarship, including Richard Eaton's studies of Bijapur and Bengal, shows a more complex picture of gradual religious change, patronage, migration, and the adaptation of local practices. In this view, shrines were places where diverse people interacted, and the variety of visitors does not by itself show that they all shared one religion.

Music at shrines, especially qawwali, is closely associated with Chishti practice and with the legacy attributed to Amir Khusrau, though the earliest form of the genre is difficult to date. The role of women, food, and festivals at the shrine, and the arguments about visiting tombs among Muslim scholars, demonstrate that the dargah was always a contested as well as a beloved institution.

Section 48.35

The Punjab before Guru Nanak: Religious Communities and Social Conditions

The Punjab of the late fifteenth century, the "land of five rivers," was an agrarian and mercantile region on the northwestern edge of the Delhi Sultanate's world. Its political life was unsettled. The Lodi dynasty (Chapter 43) held much of the region through governors, local chieftains and landholding clans exercised substantial power, and the Punjab remained exposed to invasion from Central Asia, a fact that would become vivid in the early sixteenth century. Villages were organized around cultivators, among them Jat communities, and around artisans, traders, and service castes, while towns such as Lahore, Multan, and Sultanpur Lodi were centers of administration and commerce.

The religious landscape was plural. Muslim communities followed the law and often visited Sufi shrines, above all Baba Farid's at Pakpattan and the Suhrawardi centers of Multan and Uch (§§48.29–48.31). Hindu communities worshipped at temples, followed Vaishnava and Shaiva traditions, and consulted Brahmin priests, while Nath yogis, whose ascetic centers dotted the region, held considerable religious prestige. Sant poets, including those whose verses reached Punjab from Banaras and Maharashtra, circulated among artisan and trading groups. Punjabi, with growing literary use in Sufi verse, provided a regional language of devotion.

Guru Nanak's teaching emerged within this environment. It would be inaccurate to say it was simply a product of it, since the earliest Sikh sources present Nanak's experience of the divine as the foundation of his message. But historians study the surrounding conditions to see which vocabularies, institutions, and audiences were available to him.

Political change shaped the world Guru Nanak knew. The Lodi sultans, whose dynasty had Afghan roots, ruled through governors at Lahore and Sirhind, and Daulat Khan Lodi's governorship at Lahore overlapped with Nanak's years in Sultanpur. Timur's raid of 1398 was within living memory, and Babur's incursions from 1519, culminating in the invasion that ended in Panipat in 1526 (Chapter 50), made violence a concrete experience for Punjab's villagers and townspeople. The Babarvani hymns discussed in §48.37 must be read against this setting.

Religious life included Sufi poets who wrote in Punjabi. Baba Farid's verses, whatever their exact authorship, represent an early Punjabi devotional register, and Shah Hussain of Lahore (1538–1599) composed kafis, short poems of love and longing associated with the Qadiri and Malamati currents. The Nath yogis maintained important centers, including Tilla Jogian in the Salt Range, and janamsakhi traditions describe Nanak's dialogue with Naths in the text known as the Siddh Gosht. The Sikh message thus took shape in an environment where yogic, Vaishnava, Sufi, and Sant voices were all available and sometimes in competition.

The social composition of the Punjab also mattered. Khatris, who were often merchants, administrators, and accountants, and Jats, many of them cultivators, were prominent in the villages and towns where the early Sikh community grew. The Sikh Gurus themselves came from Khatri families, while Jat cultivators later became numerous in the community. These facts are important for understanding both the Panth's economic base and, in later chapters, its political development.

Section 48.36

Guru Nanak: Life, Historical Evidence, and the Janamsakhi Traditions

Guru Nanak (1469–1539) was born at Talwandi, now Nankana Sahib in Pakistan, to a family of the Khatri community, and his father is remembered as a village accountant. Tradition says that he worked at Sultanpur Lodi for the Lodi governor Daulat Khan, married, and had two sons, and that around 1499 he had a profound religious experience, after which he began teaching. Many accounts then describe long journeys, called udasis, to places across and beyond South Asia, reportedly including centers of Hindu and Muslim pilgrimage. He settled eventually at Kartarpur on the Ravi, where he lived as a farmer and teacher until his death.

Evidence for his life comes from two kinds of source. His own compositions, preserved in the Adi Granth, give his teaching and a few historical hints, such as references to the invasion of Babur in the early 1520s. The janamsakhis, "birth narratives," are the earliest biographies, but they were composed generations after his death, the oldest surviving ones dating from the seventeenth century, and they include miracles and stories that serve devotional and teaching purposes. Different families of janamsakhis, such as those associated with Bala, Miharban, and the Puratan tradition, vary in detail. The varan of Bhai Gurdas, written in the early seventeenth century, offers an earlier outline of Nanak's life from within the community.

Historians such as W. H. McLeod emphasized the gap between the janamsakhis and verifiable history and urged caution, while other scholars, including some within the Sikh tradition, argue that the narratives preserve significant memory and cannot be dismissed. A balanced approach distinguishes what the compositions themselves establish, what can be reasonably inferred, and what remains in the realm of tradition. The dates 1469 and 1539 are widely accepted, and the broad pattern of Nanak's life as a householder-teacher is generally credited; the details of the journeys are less secure.

The details of Nanak's early life illustrate the interplay of tradition and evidence. Janamsakhis describe his sister Nanaki and her husband Jai Ram, who helped him find employment at Sultanpur, and a local chieftain Rai Bular who recognized his piety at Talwandi. They describe an episode in which he disappeared into the Bein river for three days and returned with a divine commission and the declaration that there was neither Hindu nor Musalman. Historians treat such episodes as expressions of the community's understanding of Nanak's calling rather than as reports that can be verified. Nanak's own compositions speak of divine command and of being called to sing God's praise, but without such narrative detail.

The journeys described in the janamsakhis, to the east, south, north, and west, include visits to Banaras, Assam, Sri Lanka, Kashmir, Tibet, Mecca, and Baghdad, with stories that match the interests of the traditions' audiences. Scholars such as Hew McLeod argued that these accounts were not reliable history, while others, including many Sikh scholars, argue that some travel is credible, since Nanak's hymns show familiarity with the practices of yogis and pilgrims. A cautious view accepts that Nanak traveled significantly in the Punjab and perhaps beyond, but regards the specific itineraries as uncertain.

The janamsakhi families differ in character. The Puratan tradition is relatively narrative and plain, the Miharban tradition associated with Prithi Chand's line includes extended commentary and reflects that line's claims, and the Bala tradition, long popular, has been doubted by scholars, who question even whether the companion Bala was historical. Bhai Gurdas's Varan, composed in the early seventeenth century, remains valuable as the earliest sustained account of the Gurus by a Sikh writer. Each source reveals as much about its time of composition as about Nanak's own.

Section 48.37

Guru Nanak's Teachings: The Divine, Nam, Hukam, and Ethical Life

Guru Nanak's compositions, such as the Japji, express a theology centered on one God, called by many names, who is the creator, beyond birth and death, and who is known through divine grace. The opening statement attributed to him, the Mool Mantar, affirms God's oneness and attributes. Central to his teaching is Nam, the divine Name, understood not as a magical formula but as the manifestation of God's presence, to be remembered, sung, and lived. Hukam, the divine order or command, governs the cosmos and human life, and humility before it is the beginning of wisdom. Liberation is achieved while living, through devotion and divine grace, rather than through ritual, asceticism, or pilgrimage alone.

Nanak's ethical teaching is also distinctive. He urged honest labor (kirat karni), sharing with others (vand chhakna), and remembrance of the Name (nam japna), and he rejected withdrawal from household life. He criticized empty ritual, caste-based claims to superiority, the pretensions of rulers and religious professionals, and the exploitation of the poor. In his poems, women are praised in terms that were unusual in their context, although the later community was not free from patriarchal practice.

It is tempting to say that Nanak taught "that all religions are the same." The sources are more specific. His teaching affirms a single God, and his hymns criticize the practices of both Hindu and Muslim religious specialists, but they also set out a distinct path. Scholars such as Pashaura Singh, Kristina Myrvold, and others have urged reading Nanak within his own theological framework, in which the Guru's word (shabad) serves as the means of divine revelation. Historians should not reduce him to a simple message of tolerance or to a mixture of two existing religions.

The structure of the Japji helps convey the arrangement of Nanak's thought. After the Mool Mantar, a series of stanzas reflects on the inexpressibility of God, the power of the Name, the vastness of creation, and the stages of spiritual progress, known as the realms of duty, knowledge, effort, grace, and truth. The poem ends with a verse about the human being's place in the divine order. In the compositions known as Asa di Var and Siddh Gosht, Nanak addresses ritual hypocrisy, the status of women, and the claims of yogic ascetics, and the latter is cast as a dialogue with Naths who questioned his householder way of life.

Two doctrines distinguish Nanak's theology from many other devotional traditions. First, God does not take human birth, so the tradition does not accept the avatar doctrine central to Vaishnava devotion to Rama and Krishna. Second, Nanak identifies the source of human bondage as haumai, self-centeredness or ego, and the means of release as submission to the divine order through the Guru's word. The Guru here is understood as the voice of God, and in the Sikh tradition the Guru's authority passed to the Guru's successors and eventually to the scripture.

These theological claims distinguish the early Sikh message from the Sants who also spoke of a formless God and from Sufis who also spoke of love. Nanak's insistence on household life, on productive labor, and on organizing a community around the Name made it possible for the tradition to develop collective institutions that many Sants did not pursue. At the same time, the tradition's boundaries with its neighbors were porous in the sixteenth century, and the Panth's later insistence on a sharply defined identity was a historical development.

Historical Vocabulary
Nam, Hukam, Sangat, Langar, and Guru

Nam is the divine Name, the presence of God made accessible through remembrance. Hukam is the divine order. Sangat is the congregation of devotees gathered around the Guru's word, and langar is the community kitchen in which people of all backgrounds share a meal. Guru, in Sikh usage, refers to the divine teaching mediated by the founder and his successors, and later to the scripture; it should not be confused with the common English use of "guru" as any teacher.

These terms operate in a distinct theological framework and are best explained in Sikh terms rather than through approximate Hindu or Muslim equivalents, even though some vocabulary is shared with surrounding traditions.

Section 48.38

Kartarpur, Sangat, Kirtan, Langar, and the First Sikh Community

At Kartarpur, "the city of the Creator," on the right bank of the Ravi, Guru Nanak is said to have settled with his family in the 1520s, cultivating land and gathering followers. Tradition and later sources describe a daily routine of morning and evening hymn singing (kirtan), shared work, and a common meal. Gathering as a sangat to sing the Guru's words and to eat together in the langar became the characteristic practices of the early community. The meal, shared by people regardless of caste or religion in principle, was a visible challenge to norms of pollution and commensality, though how fully it operated at the beginning is difficult to determine.

Nanak's companion Mardana, a Muslim musician of the mirasi community, is remembered as accompanying him on the rabab, a plucked lute, and the importance of music in worship is remembered as part of the tradition's foundation. The community was made of householders, including cultivators, traders, and artisans, many of them Khatris and Jats, rather than ascetics. This composition shaped the tradition's social orientation and later strength.

In 1539, before his death, Nanak designated a disciple, Lehna, as his successor, giving him the name Angad ("a part of my own body"). The act is generally regarded as establishing the principle of succession, so that the Guru's authority continued beyond the founder. The sources for this transmission come from the community's own traditions, but the general fact of succession is accepted. Nanak's elder son Sri Chand, who founded the ascetic Udasi order, was not chosen, a reminder that the early community included different paths. Later formalized practices should not be read back into the Kartarpur period.

Kartarpur's community included people who farmed the land and those who came to hear Nanak, and Nanak himself is remembered as having worked as a farmer. The janamsakhis present a daily rhythm of worship at dawn and evening, and traditional accounts mention that the house of worship was called a dharamsal, a place of righteousness, a term that later gave way to gurdwara. The practice of eating together, which later became the langar, appears in Sikh tradition as both a spiritual and social practice, and early evidence for the community kitchen in this period comes mainly from later sources.

Mardana's role requires comment. The tradition says that he was Muslim, played the rabab, a plucked lute, and accompanied Nanak on journeys, singing hymns. The presence of a Muslim musician in the founding story signals the community's interest in affirming an inclusive origin, and it also reflects the importance of music, particularly the performance of hymns in raga, in Nanak's teaching. Nanak's compositions are indeed assigned to ragas, and the Adi Granth preserves this structure.

The motif of the dispute over Nanak's body after death, in which Hindu and Muslim followers each wished to claim him and were said to have found only flowers beneath the cloth, parallels the story told of Kabir. Scholars regard such parallels as a standard hagiographical device for presenting a saint as belonging to all communities, and they differ on whether the Sikh story borrowed from the Kabir tradition or arose independently. The existence of the parallel is another reason to treat death narratives with caution.

Section 48.39

Guru Angad and Guru Amar Das: Succession, Script, Community, and Organization

Guru Angad (1504–1552), Guru from 1539 to 1552, moved the community to Khadur, near the Beas. He is traditionally credited with standardizing and promoting the Gurmukhi script, "from the mouth of the Guru," a script derived from earlier northern Indian writing systems, in which the community's compositions were written. The growing use of Gurmukhi gave the community a recognizable written medium for recording and transmitting bani alongside the other scripts of the region; it supported continuity without making Sikh literary culture wholly independent of neighboring languages and writing practices. Historians note that earlier forms of the script probably existed, and the tradition's credit to Angad reflects the role he played in establishing it. He also encouraged communal kitchen and physical training traditions, according to later accounts.

Guru Amar Das (1479–1574), Guru from 1552 to 1574, founded the town of Goindwal on the Beas, where he built a step-well (baoli) that became a pilgrimage site. Tradition credits him with organizing the Sikh community through a system of preaching districts (manjis) and with insisting that all visitors eat in the langar before meeting him, a measure that dramatized equality in commensality. He is also said to have discouraged the veiling of women, sati, and other practices, though how much of this can be documented is uncertain. A later tradition says the Mughal emperor Akbar visited Goindwal and ate in the langar (Chapter 52), but the historical record is limited.

Taken together, the second and third Gurus show a community acquiring shared script, physical centers, and administrative devices. Questions about their authority also arose. Disputes with the Guru's own family, such as the claims of the Udasis and, later, of other relatives, show that succession was not always smooth, and that the identity of the Panth was still being negotiated.

Evidence for the second and third Gurus is thinner than for Nanak, but it includes their own compositions, preserved in the Adi Granth: sixty-three shaloks of Angad and a larger body of hymns by Amar Das, including the Anand, a hymn used in later Sikh marriage rites and other ceremonial occasions. The Goindwal pothis, early manuscript collections of devotional poems associated with Amar Das's family, which include verses of the Gurus and the Bhagats, are an important sign that written collections existed before 1604. Pashaura Singh and other scholars have studied them as part of the Adi Granth's prehistory. They show that compiling and arranging bani was already an established practice by the 1570s.

Succession was not automatic. At Khadur, Angad's son Datu is remembered as having disputed the succession, and Amar Das's later accession, though recognized by the community, also had critics. The early Panth had to settle questions of who spoke with the Guru's authority, and it did so by emphasizing the divine light passing from Guru to Guru, a theological idea that allowed the tradition to treat each Guru as continuing the same teaching. This idea would become central to the Sikh understanding of the Guru.

Amar Das's organizational measures, including the manji system of preaching districts and appointed teachers, extended the Guru's reach beyond the immediate community, and tradition suggests that he appointed women as well as men to preside over such seats. The Sikh rites of birth, marriage, and death were also distinguished from Brahmanical practice during this period, according to the tradition, which helped the community define itself. As always, later Sikh historiography has retold these developments in line with later understandings, and historians read the sources with that in mind.

Section 48.40

Guru Ram Das, Ramdaspur, and the Development of Sikh Sacred Centers

Guru Ram Das (1534–1581), Guru from 1574 to 1581, was Amar Das's son-in-law and is remembered for founding a new town, first called Guru da Chak and then Ramdaspur, on land in central Punjab associated with a pool (sarovar). Later tradition says the land was granted or purchased with the help of the Mughal emperor Akbar and local landholders, but the circumstances are not known in detail. The excavation of the pool and the settlement of traders and artisans created a town that would develop into Amritsar, the "pool of nectar."

The founding of Ramdaspur is significant for several reasons. It established a sacred center under the Guru's direct control, independent of earlier Hindu or Muslim pilgrimage sites. It encouraged trade and settlement, bringing a mercantile population into the Sikh community's orbit. And it linked the Guru's household and the community's resources, since donations from followers funded building and made the Guru's center a place of economic as well as religious importance.

Ram Das also wrote devotional compositions that entered the Adi Granth, including the wedding hymns (laavan) later central to Sikh marriage. His appointment of his youngest son Arjan as successor, over the objection of the eldest, Prithi Chand, produced a dispute whose consequences lingered, since followers of Prithi Chand's line, called Minas, rejected Arjan's authority and contested the Guru's lineage. The early Sikh community, then, was still shaped by family rivalry as well as spiritual ideals.

Ram Das's succession introduced a new feature: the Guruship became hereditary within his family, the Sodhis. Earlier Gurus came from different families: Nanak was a Bedi, Angad a Trehan, and Amar Das a Bhalla. From Ram Das onward the line remained in the Sodhi family until the tenth Guru. This change contributed to rivalry among the family, as Prithi Chand's resentment showed, and it affected the tradition's institutional development. Historians view it as part of the way the Panth was tied to the Guru's household.

Ramdaspur was a planned settlement with a market. Tradition says that the Guru invited traders and artisans from many trades to settle, and the town became a commercial as well as a religious center on routes between Lahore and Delhi. The excavation of the sacred pool (amritsar) and the construction of a second pool at Santokhsar made the site a pilgrimage destination. The growth of the town connected the Sikh community to the larger economic life of the Punjab and made the Guru's center a place where resources accumulated.

To support such enterprises, the Guru relied on contributions collected by representatives (masands), who traveled to Sikh communities across the region. The system allowed the Panth to extend beyond the villages near the Guru's seat, but it also gave the representatives considerable power, and later complaints about their conduct became a significant issue in the Panth's history, one that belongs to later chapters. The early record shows a community moving from a small gathering of devotees to a network with funds, offices, and a center.

Section 48.41

Guru Arjan, the Adi Granth of 1604, and the Consolidation of the Early Sikh Panth

Guru Arjan (1563–1606), Guru from 1581, developed Amritsar into the Panth's principal center. He completed the pool and oversaw construction of the Harimandir (Golden Temple, later known as Harmandir Sahib) in its midst, a building notable for its open entrances on four sides, which tradition says symbolized access to all. He founded other towns, such as Tarn Taran, and organized a network of community representatives, the masands, who collected offerings from distant congregations on behalf of the Guru. The community's growing wealth and widening geography reflected both devotion and a prospering base of agricultural and trading supporters.

In 1604, Guru Arjan compiled the Adi Granth, with Bhai Gurdas as principal scribe according to tradition. It gathered the compositions of the first five Gurus and a group of poets called Bhagats, whose verses were selected from different regions and communities and included Kabir, Ravidas, Namdev, Farid, and others. Arranged by musical mode (raga) and by author, it was installed at Harimandir. Including non-Sikh voices was a deliberate editorial decision that signaled the Panth's theological openness to like-minded devotion, while selecting only verses consistent with its own teaching. The process also aimed to establish an authoritative text in a context where a rival line, the Minas, was circulating compositions under Nanak's name.

In 1606, Guru Arjan died in custody in Lahore after being arrested by order of the Mughal emperor Jahangir. Sikh tradition views him as the first great martyr of the community. Jahangir's own memoirs, the Tuzuk-i Jahangiri, describe Arjan as a religious leader with a large following whom he decided to eliminate, and they link him with the rebel prince Khusrau, while Sikh sources emphasize religious persecution and the role of Arjan's rivals. Historians continue to discuss the relative weight of political suspicion, the Guru's growing influence, and hostility from rival religious and local figures. Chapter 53 examines Jahangir's reign, and Chapters 54 and 59 follow the Sikh community's later history, including the Khalsa of 1699 and eighteenth-century political developments. Those later developments should not be projected onto the community of 1606.

The compilation of the Adi Granth drew on earlier materials. Tradition says that Guru Arjan obtained the Goindwal pothis from Mohan, the son of Amar Das, and that he used them together with collections of compositions he gathered from other Sikh communities and from the poets whose verses he decided to include. A manuscript preserved at Kartarpur in the Jalandhar district, known as the Kartarpur bir, is traditionally regarded as the original copy, and scholars continue to study its variants. The compilation was installed at Harimandir with Baba Buddha, a venerable early Sikh, as its first reader, according to the tradition.

The selection of the fifteen Bhagats, the arrangement by raga, and the inclusion of Guru Arjan's own compositions, such as the Sukhmani, make the book a carefully designed whole. The compiler also identified spurious compositions, distinguishing authentic bani from works falsely attributed to the Gurus, a concern linked to the existence of a rival line of Gurus who circulated their own verses. The closing sections of the book include verses by the Guru that confirm the compilation's completion. The result was a text that could serve as the community's common point of reference, even as it was distributed among communities that might never see the Guru.

Guru Arjan's arrest and death occurred in 1606, in the first year or so of Jahangir's reign and soon after the rebellion of Prince Khusrau, who was reported to have passed through Punjab. Jahangir's memoir says that he regarded Arjan as a false saint who had misled many simple people, including some Muslims, and it links him to Khusrau, while Sikh tradition describes torture in Lahore and death in the Ravi. Historians, including Pashaura Singh, consider a mixture of political suspicion, concern about the growth of the Guru's following and resources, and opposition from rivals both within and outside the Panth. The Sikh community's later memory of Arjan as the first martyr shaped its relationship with the Mughal state, a subject for Chapters 54 and 59. The sixth Guru, Hargobind, succeeded in 1606, and his reign belongs to later narrative.

Section 48.42

Women, Households, Labor, and Everyday Devotional Life

Devotional traditions reached into daily life. Women sang at household shrines, vowed fasts, visited temples and dargahs, fed pilgrims, and carried songs from village to village. Cultivators sang while working, weavers composed at the loom, and merchants funded temples, lodges, and pilgrimage centers. Pilgrimage, whether the Varkari walk to Pandharpur, a visit to Ajmer for the urs, or a journey to a Sikh sangat, structured the calendar and created temporary communities across caste and region. Shared food, whether temple prasad, Sufi lodge meals, or Sikh langar, was one of the most tangible forms of devotional community.

Women's participation was substantial, but it was shaped by institutions that were usually led by men. The evidence includes the hymns of Andal, Karaikkal Ammaiyar, Akka Mahadevi, Janabai, Mirabai, and women in the Sufi and Sikh traditions, as well as women who gave gifts recorded in inscriptions. Yet women's access to authority varied by tradition. Some, like the Virashaiva vacana poets, honored women as religious voices, while temple priesthoods, Sufi succession, and the leadership of the Panth remained overwhelmingly male. Sources written by men often described women through the categories of devotion, sacrifice, or scandal, so historians must read them carefully.

Occupations also mattered. The Sants' craft images, the Varkari poets' household and trade settings, the Pushtimarg merchants' patronage, and the Sikh community's agricultural base all show that devotion was tied to livelihoods. Households were both the sites of devotional practice and the units of economic life. Reconstructing everyday religion thus requires attention to the ordinary rather than only to the exceptional saint.

Women appear in the sources both as poets and as patrons. The Chola queen Sembiyan Mahadevi, in the tenth century, is remembered in inscriptions for rebuilding and endowing Shaiva temples, and Vijayanagara and regional inscriptions record gifts by women of many ranks. In the Sufi world, Nizamuddin's mother Bibi Zulekha is remembered for her piety, and women visited lodges and shrines, though their own voices rarely survive. In Sikh tradition, Bibi Nanaki, Nanak's sister, is remembered as an early devotee, and later sources praise Mata Khivi, wife of Guru Angad, for managing the community kitchen, though such accounts come from later recollection. In Gaudiya Vaishnavism, Jahnava Devi's leadership shows that a woman could hold institutional authority.

The record thus shows women's contributions to devotional life in many roles, from poets such as Andal, Akka Mahadevi, Janabai, and Mirabai to patrons, teachers, and cooks. Yet the structures of institutional leadership, from temple priesthoods to Sufi succession and Guruship, were largely male. The evidence for ordinary women's devotional practice, such as songs sung while grinding grain, vows, and fasts, is mostly indirect, often recorded by men or preserved in later folk traditions.

Labor and occupation also shaped devotional participation. Weavers, tailors, potters, barbers, goldsmiths, cobblers, farmers, and merchants appear throughout the traditions, and many poets used their trade's images. Merchants in Gujarat and Rajasthan supported Pushtimarg and Jain temples, Lingayat merchants supported their mathas, Khatri traders and Jat cultivators formed the Sikh sangats, and Sufi lodges relied on both rural and urban donors. The point is that devotion was not separate from the economic activities of everyday life.

Everyday Life and Social History

Pilgrimage, Song, Food, and Work

On the road to Pandharpur, Varkari pilgrims walked in groups carrying banners, singing, and sharing food, and the procession itself became an act of devotion. At a Chishti lodge in Delhi, hungry visitors came for a meal, and the lodge's kitchen was the visible form of its ethic of service. In an Assamese village, the namghar served as meeting hall, prayer space, and court for settling local disputes. These routines reveal what historical sources about great teachers often hide: religion was woven into labor, food, travel, and the calendar. The evidence for such life is indirect. Section 48.43

Devotion, Caste, Equality, and the Limits of Social Reform

Devotional traditions varied in what they said about caste. Basavanna and the vacana poets challenged religious claims grounded in birth and purity and ranked devotion above status; the extent to which their communities dismantled ritual and social hierarchy in practice remains historically debated. Chokhamela and the Varkari poets voiced the pain of exclusion while remaining within a tradition that celebrated Vithoba as a God of all. Kabir ridiculed Brahmin pride and the notion that birth determines worth. Ravidas spoke as a low-status worker and imagined a society without humiliation. Guru Nanak affirmed that all people stand equally before God, and the langar and sangat embodied the principle in practice. Other traditions, such as the Sri Vaishnavas and Tulsidas's Rama devotion, honored devotees of all backgrounds but worked within a social hierarchy they did not challenge as a system.

These positions differ in kind. Some rejected the religious basis of caste; some argued that devotion cleansed any status; some built communities whose practice, such as shared meals, conflicted with caste rules; and some left social structures largely intact. Distinguishing among them avoids the assumption that "Bhakti" meant caste reform. Even where equality was declared, later communities developed their own subgroups, rules of marriage, and sectarian hierarchies. Lingayat, Sikh, Kabirpanthi, and Ravidassia communities all show internal differences in status as well as continuing influence from the surrounding society.

The evidence for how much devotion changed everyday life remains thin. Poems record ideals, hagiographies record stories, and inscriptions record gifts, but few sources describe the experience of an ordinary low-status devotee. Historians therefore speak of critique and aspiration more confidently than of social transformation.

The different positions on caste can be examined through institutional evidence. Guru Nanak's langar and Guru Amar Das's insistence that visitors eat together in a common line (pangat) challenged norms of commensality, but the Gurus themselves married within Khatri sub-groups, and later Sikh communities included caste distinctions among Jats, Khatris, and others. The Virashaiva critique of birth-based status produced a community that, over time, developed subgroups within itself. The Varkari tradition honored Chokhamela but did not eliminate the restrictions he describes, and the Kabirpanth and Ravidassia communities have been significant spaces of dignity for members of artisan and Dalit backgrounds while existing alongside broader society's prejudice.

Modern readers have drawn on these traditions in different ways. B. R. Ambedkar, discussed in Chapter 80, named Kabir among his three teachers, along with the Buddha and Jotirao Phule, while also criticizing the capacity of devotional religion to dismantle caste, and Gail Omvedt's study of the quest for Begampura traced a long tradition of anti-caste thinking in India. Others have emphasized the continuing hierarchies within devotional communities. Historians stress that both views are partly right, since the evidence shows genuine challenge and genuine limits.

The sources invite caution about generalizing. A poem expressing equality tells us about an ideal, not about every community's practice. A story of a saint admitted to a temple tells us that exclusion was an issue. Inscriptions and shrine records show who received gifts and who made them, and they show that status distinctions continued to matter. A rigorous argument about caste in these traditions must use all these kinds of evidence together.

Section 48.44

Shared Religious Languages and Distinct Traditions: Interaction without Erasure

Interaction was real. Devotional poets and Sufi masters sometimes shared vocabulary of love and longing, and the Sufi poets of Awadh and elsewhere wrote romances in Hindavi that used local stories. Musical practices crossed boundaries: ragas structured the Adi Granth, and Sufi and Bhakti gatherings sang to similar melodies. Pilgrims visited shrines belonging to other traditions, and rulers patronized several. The Adi Granth's inclusion of Kabir, Ravidas, Namdev, and Farid, and the Kabirpanth's use of Sufi imagery, show borrowing at the level of text and symbol.

But interaction did not erase differences. Sufis were Muslims whose practices rested on the Quran and the Prophet's example, whatever their openness to others. Vaishnava, Shaiva, Sant, and Sikh communities held distinct beliefs about God, scripture, the role of the Guru, and the meaning of liberation. Rival claims about orthodoxy, rituals, and lineage produced disputes, and later communities, especially in the Mughal period and the colonial era, defined boundaries more sharply. The census and the modern politics of religious identity, discussed in later volumes, further hardened categories that had been more fluid.

For these reasons, this chapter avoids describing a single "Hindu-Muslim synthesis." Such a phrase risks implying that two monolithic religions fused. A better description is that South Asian devotional life took place in a shared social and linguistic landscape in which many communities borrowed, argued, and competed while maintaining their own identities.

Musical and literary interaction offers concrete examples. The raga system structures both the Sikh scripture and Sufi sama gatherings, and the shared literary languages of Braj, Awadhi, Punjabi, and Hindavi allowed poets of different traditions to draw on similar images. The Sufi romances of the Awadh region in Hindavi, and Dadupanthi and Kabirpanthi manuscript copying, show cooperation across religious boundaries. Some figures, such as Ramdev Pir of Rajasthan, venerated by both Hindus and Muslims, and Satya Pir in Bengal, are remembered as saints whose appeal crossed communities, though the details of their histories are contested.

Boundaries were also drawn. Ahmad Sirhindi's letters warned against practices he viewed as compromising Islam, the Sikh Gurus established distinct institutions and scripture, Madhva and Ramanuja traditions debated rival theologies, and Sant communities each produced their own lineages. Scholars have debated how fixed religious identities were in this period. Harjot Oberoi's argument that Sikh identity was relatively fluid until the late nineteenth-century Singh Sabha movement has been influential, while other historians, including many who study the early sources, argue that a distinct Sikh identity was already developing in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. The disagreement is not simply academic, because it bears on how the Panth's origins are understood.

A balanced position recognizes both fluidity and distinctness. In everyday life, people moved among shrines, songs, and holy persons, and this movement did not always imply a loss of identity. At the same time, specific communities developed texts, rituals, and institutions that distinguished them from others. The later hardening of categories through colonial censuses and modern politics, which Chapter 73 examines, should not be projected backward as though these categories had always been rigid, nor should the existence of boundaries be denied.

Cause and Effect

Vernacular Religious Literature and the Expansion of Devotional Communities

Long-term causes

Earlier traditions of devotion in the Gita, Puranas, and temple worship; the growth of regional languages and courts; expanding trade and urban populations; and the institutional networks of Sufi orders.

Short-term causes

Patronage from rulers, merchants, and temples; the emergence of charismatic teachers; the use of song and memorization; and the political upheavals of the Sultanate and early Mughal worlds that moved people and ideas.

Event or transformation

Composition and circulation of devotional poetry in Tamil, Kannada, Marathi, Hindi dialects, Bengali, Assamese, Persian, and Punjabi, creating communities around shared words.

Immediate effects

Wider participation in religious life by artisans, women, and cultivators; new performance genres; manuscripts and anthologies; and shrines, lodges, satras, and temples that preserved the poems.

Long-term consequences

Regional literary canons, enduring communities such as the Sikh Panth, Lingayats, Kabirpanthis, and Pushtimarg, and lasting debates over caste, authority, and religious identity.

Historical complexity

No single cause explains the proliferation of devotional traditions. Each region had its own political and social setting, and the outcomes included both challenges to hierarchy and new institutions with their own hierarchies.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Individual Devotional Teachers to Organized Institutions

What Changed

  • Individual poets and teachers gave way to lineages, monasteries, lodges, satras, and congregations
  • Oral songs were collected in manuscripts and anthologies, some becoming scripture
  • Temples, shrines, and sacred centers such as Srirangam, Ajmer, and Amritsar acquired land, officers, and endowments
  • Teachers' memory was reshaped by hagiography and sectarian history

What Stayed the Same

  • Song, pilgrimage, and shared food remained central to devotional practice
  • Households and working communities remained the base of most traditions
  • Tensions between inclusive ideals and social hierarchy persisted
  • Patronage from rulers and wealthy families continued to matter

Who experienced change most directly? Followers who moved from informal gatherings into organized communities, and the leaders who acquired property and authority. Who experienced greater continuity? Ordinary households whose daily devotional routines changed less than the institutions around them. Why the pattern matters: It shows how radical teachings can persist through institutions that also moderate them.

Section 48.45

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has argued that devotional religion transformed South Asian society not through one uniform movement but through multiple, interacting traditions that developed different theologies, social critiques, literary cultures, and institutions. The Tamil Alvars and Nayanars created the earliest great body of vernacular devotional poetry, and temples and patrons transformed it into ritual and canon. Ramanuja and Madhva gave Vaishnava devotion philosophical systems that disagreed with each other. The Varkari poets of Pandharpur, the Krishna communities of Braj, Bengal, Assam, Gujarat, and Rajasthan, and the Rama devotion of Tulsidas show regional literary cultures with separate histories.

In the Shaiva world, Shaiva Siddhanta, Lingayat vacana poets, and the Kashmiri vernacular tradition offered very different relationships to ritual, hierarchy, and women's religious voices. The Sants, led by Kabir, Ravidas, and Dadu, used the speech of working communities to criticize pretension and imagine a formless God, though their surviving words reached us through the editing of later communities. Sufi orders, particularly the Chishti, Suhrawardi, and later Qadiri, Shattari, and Naqshbandi, built lodges and shrines within Islamic tradition and disagreed among themselves over law, music, wealth, and rulers.

The Sikh tradition, from Guru Nanak at Kartarpur to Guru Arjan's Adi Granth and martyrdom, shows a community that gathered the teachings, script, sacred centers, and succession necessary to become a distinct religious path by 1606, one that included non-Sikh devotional voices without dissolving into them. Across these cases, the sources raise methodological lessons: poems circulated orally and were edited; biographies were written by communities; shrine and lodge records reveal economic and social life; and attributions require caution.

Three themes run through the chapter. First, language mattered: composing in spoken languages opened religious participation but did not abolish learned traditions. Second, caste critique was real but uneven, and institutions often reproduced the hierarchies they criticized. Third, interaction among traditions was extensive but did not erase their distinct identities. Politically, these traditions coexisted with the Delhi Sultanate, regional kingdoms, and the early Mughal state, which sometimes patronized and sometimes suppressed them, as the death of Guru Arjan reminds us.

The central question the chapter leaves for students is this: how could devotion challenge established social authority while also producing enduring institutions with their own boundaries and hierarchies? Chapter 49 turns to the Indian Ocean and the arrival of the Portuguese, a different arena of connection and conflict, while Chapters 50 through 54 follow the Mughal empire, within which these devotional communities continued to develop, and Chapters 54 and 59 return to the Sikh Panth.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has studied the devotional, Sufi, Sant, and early Sikh traditions of South Asia between roughly the sixth and early seventeenth centuries. Its central argument is that devotional religion transformed South Asian society through many interacting traditions rather than through one uniform Bhakti movement, and that each tradition must be understood through its own languages, sources, institutions, and social settings.

The Tamil Alvars and Nayanars, whose hymns were composed in the first millennium CE and collected in the Nalayira Divya Prabandham and the Tevaram, created the earliest large body of vernacular devotional poetry. Temples, patrons, and hereditary specialists turned individual poems into ritual and canon, a process that preserved the poets' memory while also placing it within hierarchical institutions. Women such as Andal and Karaikkal Ammaiyar appear as recognized voices.

Vaishnava devotion developed multiple theological schools and regional literatures. Ramanuja's Vishishtadvaita and Madhva's Dvaita offered rival philosophies. Pandharpur's Varkaris, with poets such as Dnyaneshwar, Namdev, Chokhamela, Janabai, and Eknath, created a Marathi devotional culture that included poets of many social backgrounds. Vallabha's Pushtimarg, Chaitanya's Gaudiya Vaishnavism, Shankaradeva's Ekasarana tradition, the songs of Narsinh Mehta and Mirabai, the Braj poetry of Surdas, and Tulsidas's Ramcharitmanas show how Krishna and Rama devotion took distinct forms in different regions and languages.

Shaiva traditions were similarly varied. Shaiva Siddhanta joined Agamic temple ritual to Tamil hymns, the Virashaiva-Lingayat community and its Kannada vacana poets, including Basavanna, Allama Prabhu, and Akka Mahadevi, criticized ritual hierarchy, and Kashmir produced both a learned Shaiva philosophy and the vernacular poetry of Lal Ded. Their institutional histories show that even communities with radical critiques developed their own boundaries and leadership.

The Sant poets Kabir, Ravidas, and Dadu Dayal, drawing on the speech and imagery of working communities, taught devotion to a formless God and criticized pretension, ritualism, and caste claims. Their lives are poorly documented, and their poems survive in multiple collections, such as the Bijak, the Kabir Granthavali, the Panchvani, and the Adi Granth, which reflect the choices of the communities that compiled them. Ravidas's Begampura gave voice to a vision of a society without humiliation.

Sufi orders, as Muslim communities of discipline and devotion, established lodges and shrines in the Sultanate world. The Chishti order, represented by Muinuddin Chishti, Qutbuddin Bakhtiyar Kaki, Baba Farid, and Nizamuddin Auliya, emphasized hospitality and sama and kept a distance from rulers. The Suhrawardi order accepted wealth and political ties. Firdausi, Qadiri, Shattari, and Naqshbandi networks extended Sufism across Bihar, Bengal, Kashmir, the Deccan, and the Mughal north, with disagreements over law, music, and Indic practices. Dargahs became centers of pilgrimage, charity, property, and social life.

Guru Nanak, born in 1469, taught devotion to one God through the divine Name and the divine order, and urged honest work, sharing, and remembrance. His community at Kartarpur practiced collective singing and shared meals. Guru Angad, Guru Amar Das, Guru Ram Das, and Guru Arjan built the Panth through a script, an organizational structure, sacred centers at Goindwal and Ramdaspur, and, in 1604, the Adi Granth, which included the compositions of non-Sikh poets selected by the Sikh community. Guru Arjan's death in Mughal custody in 1606 marks the chapter's end point for Sikh history.

The chapter also showed how women, artisans, cultivators, and merchants took part in devotional life, while recognizing that institutional leadership remained largely male and often high-status. Comparing teachings on caste showed that traditions differed greatly: some rejected the religious basis of caste, some built communities that practiced equality in worship and shared meals, and others left structures largely untouched.

Interaction among traditions was real, visible in shared musical forms, vocabulary, pilgrimage, and the Adi Granth's inclusion of Bhagat bani. Yet each tradition retained its own theology and institutions, and later political and census-based politics sharpened boundaries. Historians therefore avoid describing a single Hindu-Muslim synthesis.

Methodologically, the chapter emphasized the distinction between a teacher, a body of compositions, and a community, and cautioned against treating hagiography as biography. This approach provides a basis for later chapters on Mughal religious life, on the Sikh community's political history, and on the modern reinterpretation of devotional pasts.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows religion as a social and literary force that shaped how ordinary people spoke, sang, worked, traveled, and imagined community. The poems and institutions studied here gave artisans, women, cultivators, and merchants a religious vocabulary, even as they created new forms of authority. They also left a body of literature that remains central to regional cultures from Tamil Nadu to Punjab.

The chapter also demonstrates the value of resisting a single narrative. A unified Bhakti movement is easier to teach than many traditions, but the evidence shows distinct languages, theologies, and institutions. Recognizing this makes it possible to see real connections, such as shared musical forms and the Adi Granth's inclusion of other poets, without erasing difference.

Later developments grew from these foundations. The Mughal empire, examined in Chapters 50 through 56, provided the setting for Sufi reform, Rama and Krishna devotion, and the Sikh community's relation to the state. The Sikh Panth's later history, treated in Chapters 54 and 59, cannot be understood without the world of Guru Nanak and Guru Arjan, and modern debates about caste, religious identity, and historical memory, taken up in Chapters 107 and 111, often return to these teachers and texts.

In the six-volume sequence, this chapter closes the political and religious story of the Sultanate and regional kingdoms and opens the way to the Indian Ocean, the Portuguese, and the Mughals. It reminds students that the political histories of Chapters 45 through 47 coexisted with a vibrant religious and literary life that also made South Asian history.
