Part XI — The Eighteenth-Century Transformation
61

European Companies and the Struggle for Commercial Power

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

In 1716, in the small French settlement of Pondicherry on the Coromandel Coast, one of the wealthiest and most influential men in town was arrested. Nayiniyappa, a Tamil Hindu merchant who had served the French Company as its chief broker, had spent years arranging cloth purchases, advancing money, negotiating with local officials, and mediating between French administrators and the Tamil society around them. His prosecution, and the later review of his case by Company authorities, reveals something that a simple story of European arrival hides: the French Company could not function without people like him, and for that very reason his power was a problem for those who wished to govern.

Chapter 60 examined the Mughal successor states of Bengal, Awadh, and Hyderabad, regimes that kept Mughal political forms while exercising growing autonomy. Chapter 61 turns to a different kind of actor who was arriving, settling, and bargaining within that same political landscape: the European trading companies. Their story is usually told as a sequence of arrivals, Portuguese first, then Dutch, English, and French, followed by a sequence of battles. This chapter tells it differently. It asks how commercial corporations, created by European monarchies and investors to trade profitably, became armed participants in South Asian politics, and it insists that this transformation cannot be understood without the Indian merchants, brokers, bankers, weavers, and rulers who made the companies' business possible.

The chapter's central argument is that the European companies did not enter an economically passive or politically disorganized region. They entered an Indian Ocean commercial world already ancient, sophisticated, and densely organized, in which Gujarati, Tamil, Telugu, Bengali, Armenian, Arab, Persian, and other merchants financed ships, moved cloth and spices, and settled accounts across thousands of kilometers. The companies joined that world on terms largely set by Indian rulers and Indian producers. They obtained trading permissions through negotiation, paid customs, rented buildings, advanced money to weavers through Indian intermediaries, and depended on local credit. Their eventual political capacities grew gradually, through the pressures of competition with one another, the need to defend their settlements, and the opportunities created by the fragmentation of regional authority described in Chapters 57 through 60.

The chapter also insists on distinctions that later colonial history tends to blur. A charter granted in London, Amsterdam, or Paris conferred privileges on the company's own countrymen, not authority over Indian territory. A trading factory was a warehouse and office, not a possession. A fortified settlement such as Madras or Pondicherry was a small, negotiated enclave dependent on a surrounding economy, not a province. Participation in a dynastic war in the Carnatic was not the same as governing a territory. By 1763, the English and French companies had become military and political actors in southern India, while British intervention in Bengal after 1757 was already altering regional power. Chapter 62 examines that separate process of conquest and the acquisition of revenue rights; neither outcome was predetermined by the companies’ initial commercial charters.

The chapter is organized in five divisions of four sections each. Division I examines the Portuguese commercial inheritance and the Dutch East India Company, the largest European enterprise in Asia in the seventeenth century. Division II traces the English and French companies and the rivalry among all four European powers. Division III, intentionally the chapter's heaviest, reconstructs the Indian merchants, brokers, bankers, and textile producers on whom the companies depended. Division IV examines the legal and physical forms through which companies gained a foothold, from factories and farmans to fortified towns, and explains how armed commerce developed. Division V follows the three Carnatic Wars of 1746 to 1763, when Anglo-French rivalry fused with the succession politics of Hyderabad and the Carnatic.

The evidence for these developments is unusually varied. Company charters, consultations, letter books, and account books survive in the archives of London, The Hague, and Paris, but they record what company officials chose to write and often reflect the preoccupations of shareholders thousands of kilometers away. Indian perspectives survive in Persian chronicles, Tamil and Telugu petitions, merchant correspondence, and above all in the remarkable diary of Ananda Ranga Pillai, a Pondicherry broker who recorded events from 1736 to 1761. Historians must read all of these critically, asking whose interests each document serves and whose voices are missing.

Two historiographical cautions run through the chapter. First, scholars have long debated whether the companies were innovative corporate institutions that transformed Asian trade or dependents who largely adapted to Asian systems, a question taken up in the first Historical Debate below. Second, historians continue to debate whether the movement from commerce to political power was planned, inevitable, or contingent, a question addressed in the second. This chapter will not hand students a final verdict on either, but it will show how the evidence constrains the answers.

By the end of the chapter, students should understand how armed commerce developed, why the Carnatic became the proving ground of company political power, and why the companies' dependence on Indian partners is as important to the story as their naval and military innovations. They should also be ready for Chapter 62, which examines how one of those companies, in a very different political setting, acquired fiscal and territorial authority over Bengal.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the Indian Ocean commercial system that European companies entered and explain the commercial institutions the Portuguese Estado da Índia left to its competitors.
  2. Explain the corporate organization, finance, and Asian strategy of the Dutch East India Company, including the importance of intra-Asian trade.
  3. Trace the formation of the English and French East India companies and the growth of their principal Indian settlements.
  4. Compare Portuguese, Dutch, English, and French commercial strategies, capital sources, and relationships with metropolitan governments.
  5. Analyze the roles of Indian merchants, bankers, brokers, and dubashes, and explain why their agency is essential to understanding company commerce.
  6. Describe how cotton and silk textiles were produced and procured, and distinguish negotiated contracts from coercive procurement.
  7. Explain how companies financed Asian purchases through bullion, credit, and intra-Asian trade, and assess who gained and who bore the costs.
  8. Distinguish among a charter, a farman, a factory, a fortified settlement, and territorial sovereignty.
  9. Examine social, legal, and religious life within European settlements, including the position of Indian residents, women, and dependent labor.
  10. Explain why companies developed naval and military capacities and why such capacities did not automatically produce territorial conquest.
  11. Reconstruct the course and significance of the three Carnatic Wars, including the roles of Indian rulers and allies.
  12. Evaluate competing interpretations of whether the company movement from commerce to politics was intentional, inevitable, or contingent.

Historical Context

The coasts of South Asia had been part of maritime networks for millennia. Earlier chapters traced the Indus Civilization's contacts with Mesopotamia and the Persian Gulf, the Roman trade through the Indian Ocean, Chola maritime expeditions, and the commerce of Muslim merchant communities on the western coast. By the fifteenth century this system connected East Africa, the Red Sea, the Persian Gulf, Gujarat, Malabar, the Coromandel Coast, Bengal, Sri Lanka, Southeast Asia, and China. The monsoon, with its predictable seasonal reversal of wind, governed sailing schedules. Cloth from Gujarat and the Coromandel Coast, pepper and spices from Malabar and the archipelago, horses from Arabia and Iran, and gold and silver circulated through ports such as Surat, Calicut, Masulipatnam, and Hugli. Chapter 49 introduced the arrival of the Portuguese in 1498 and their attempt to impose naval control over this system.

On land, the Mughal Empire, described in Chapters 50 through 56, governed the northern and much of the central subcontinent, and Chapter 55 examined the agrarian and manufacturing economy that supported it. Mughal ports such as Surat were wealthy customs centers, and Mughal officials negotiated with foreign merchants as they did with domestic ones. In the south, the Deccan sultanates, the remnants of Vijayanagara and its successor nayakas, and later the Marathas, Mysore, and the Mughal viceroys controlled the coastal and inland territories where Coromandel textiles were woven. Chapters 57 through 60 showed that after 1707 political authority fragmented without collapsing: regional governors, nawabs, and nizams exercised increasing autonomy while retaining Mughal titles and forms. European companies therefore dealt with a mosaic of authorities, not a single imperial government.

The companies were also products of European developments. Joint-stock finance, chartered monopolies, state-backed naval power, and rivalry among Atlantic and North Sea states shaped each company's resources and priorities. European wars frequently reached Asia through the companies, as the Carnatic Wars would show. Yet European demand was only one force among many. Asian markets bought as well as sold, Asian merchants chose their partners, and Asian rulers decided what privileges to allow.

Students should keep three distinctions in mind throughout this chapter. First, a company was a corporation with shareholders and a charter, not a state, although it exercised delegated powers. Second, commerce and political power overlapped but were not identical; a company could be commercially vital to a region while having little political leverage. Third, the period traced here, roughly 1600 to 1763, saw continuous change, so statements about "the companies" must be tied to specific places and decades.

Portuguese and Dutch Commercial Networks

Maritime institutions, Asian trade, and the Dutch East India Company.

Section 61.1

South Asian Maritime Commerce and the Portuguese Commercial Inheritance

When northern European merchants began sailing to Asia in force around 1600, they did not discover a world waiting to be organized. They encountered a system of maritime commerce whose rhythms were set by the monsoon. From roughly April to September the southwest monsoon carried ships from the Arabian and East African coasts toward India; from November to February the northeast winds carried them back. Ships bound for Southeast Asia and China followed related seasonal patterns across the Bay of Bengal. This predictability made long-distance commerce regular, and it meant that merchants planned voyages, loaded cargo, and arranged credit around a seasonal calendar that no European company could alter.

The major regions of the system specialized. Gujarat, with ports such as Cambay and later Surat, produced cotton cloth in enormous quantities and exported it west to the Red Sea, the Persian Gulf, and East Africa, and east to the archipelago. Malabar produced pepper, ginger, and other spices, which flowed to the Red Sea and, by older routes, toward the Mediterranean. The Coromandel Coast, with ports including Masulipatnam, Pulicat, and Nagapattinam, produced painted and dyed cottons that were prized in Southeast Asia. Bengal produced fine cotton, silk, rice, and sugar and was linked to the Bay of Bengal through riverine ports. Arab, Persian, Gujarati, Tamil, Telugu, Bengali, Armenian, and Jewish merchants operated within this network, and Muslim, Hindu, Jain, and Christian traders alike used shared practices of credit and partnership.

Vasco da Gama's arrival at Calicut in 1498, discussed in Chapter 49, introduced a new kind of participant. The Portuguese Crown aimed to divert the spice trade from the Red Sea and Mediterranean routes to a sea route around Africa, and it sought to do so by force. Afonso de Albuquerque seized Goa from the Sultanate of Bijapur in 1510, and further acquisitions, including Malacca in 1511 and Hormuz in 1515, gave the Portuguese strategic points on the routes. These possessions, together with fortresses, factories, and customs houses, formed the Estado da Índia, a state structure whose administrative center was Goa and whose senior official was a viceroy appointed from Lisbon.

The Portuguese did not and could not monopolize Asian trade. Their naval superiority in the early sixteenth century rested on armed ships with heavy guns, an advantage in open-sea combat, but the Estado lacked the manpower to control every route. Asian ships continued to sail, often by purchasing Portuguese protection. The characteristic instrument of this arrangement was the cartaz, a pass that merchant ships were required to purchase, which exempted them from seizure by Portuguese warships in exchange for payment and agreement to call at Portuguese customs houses. The cartaz system sought to make Portuguese naval coercion a source of customs revenue and leverage, but merchants also evaded or negotiated the requirement. By the mid-sixteenth century the old Red Sea spice trade had revived, and Portuguese hopes of a complete monopoly had faded, although the Estado remained a significant participant.

What the Portuguese left to their successors was therefore not monopoly but a set of institutional models and relationships: fortified coastal enclaves, naval escort, licensed passage, customs revenue, and extensive intermediaries who spoke Portuguese and knew the coast. The Dutch, English, and French would copy, adapt, and challenge these practices. They also inherited a legacy of resentment among some Indian rulers and merchants, who had experienced Portuguese coercion, and a legacy of opportunity, since Portuguese weakness opened coastal sites where newer entrants could negotiate. Chapter 49 treated the arrival and early expansion of the Portuguese; this chapter takes up what happened afterwards.

Historical Vocabulary
Cartaz

The cartaz was a Portuguese maritime permit or safe-conduct pass issued by Portuguese authorities in Asia. A ship carrying one was exempt from capture by Portuguese warships, provided it paid the required fees, followed designated routes, and avoided prohibited goods such as certain spices. A vessel found without one could be seized.

The cartaz matters because it shows how Portuguese power actually worked: not as an absolute blockade but as a system of licensed passage and taxation. A modern reader may mistake it for a proof of Portuguese control of Asian seas. It is better understood as a price Asian merchants paid for security and as a model that later European companies sometimes imitated or resisted. The Dutch, for example, sought to enforce commercial exclusivity in the spice islands, while the English and French more often sought trading permissions from Asian rulers.

Section 61.2

Portuguese Networks, Goa, and the Persistence of Luso-Asian Commerce

By the early seventeenth century Portuguese maritime supremacy was under pressure, but it would be a mistake to describe the Estado da Índia as simply collapsing. The Dutch and English captured or contested important positions. The Dutch took Colombo in 1656 and Nagapattinam in 1658, and in 1663 seized Cochin, long a major Portuguese base on the Malabar Coast. The English and Persians together captured Hormuz in 1622, depriving the Estado of its principal Gulf fortress. The Marathas took the Salsette stronghold of Thane in 1737 and, after a prolonged campaign commanded by Chimaji Appa, captured Bassein in 1739; these losses sharply reduced Portuguese control of the northern Konkan. Yet Goa, Daman, and Diu remained in Portuguese hands, and Goa continued to function as an administrative, religious, and commercial center.

Goa in this period was a substantial city with a cathedral, convents, a viceregal court, a hospital, and a port. It was also the seat of the Goa Inquisition, established in 1560, which investigated converts and others suspected of heterodoxy, an institution that shaped relations with Hindu, Muslim, and Jewish communities in Portuguese territories. The Catholic Church in Asia operated under the Portuguese Crown's right of patronage, the padroado, so that missionary work and Portuguese commercial and political presence were closely interwoven. Jesuits, Franciscans, Dominicans, and other orders maintained colleges and parishes in Goa and across coastal India, and their presence later complicated relations with French Capuchin and Jesuit missionaries.

Much Portuguese commerce was carried out not by the Crown but by private traders. Many were casados, Portuguese men who had married locally and settled, and many more were people of mixed Portuguese and Asian descent, sometimes called topasses or mestiços. These communities operated in ports across the Bay of Bengal, from São Tomé near Madras to Hugli in Bengal and Chittagong and Arakan on the eastern shores. Portuguese settlers at Hugli traded in salt, rice, textiles, and, in some cases, slaves, until the Mughal governor of Bengal, acting under Shah Jahan, captured the settlement in 1632. Portuguese private traders in the Bay of Bengal also operated outside Estado authority, sometimes as mercenaries or pirates serving local rulers, and their ambiguous position illustrates that Portuguese presence was never identical with Crown policy.

The durability of Luso-Asian relationships had another dimension: language and mediation. Portuguese served as a trading lingua franca along parts of the coast well into the eighteenth century. Dutch, English, and French agents commonly hired Portuguese-speaking interpreters and relied on Catholic communities for local knowledge. Families of mixed descent became pilots, soldiers, clerks, and translators for rival companies. Portuguese church architecture, food, music, and personal names persisted in coastal communities long after Portuguese political authority had receded. In Goa itself, local Hindu and Catholic elites, including Konkani-speaking families, remained central to the commercial life of the territory.

For the chapter's argument, the Portuguese case offers two lessons. First, the weakening of Portuguese naval supremacy did not end Portuguese commercial and cultural influence, and a simple narrative of one European power replacing another misses networks that continued beneath formal political change. Second, the Estado's experience showed that a European power with fortresses and guns could extract revenue from maritime trade but could not command it without the cooperation of Asian merchants and rulers. Their northern European successors would face the same constraint on a larger scale.

Section 61.3

The Dutch East India Company: Charter, Capital, and Asian Commercial Strategy

The Dutch East India Company, the Verenigde Oostindische Compagnie or VOC, was chartered by the States General of the Dutch Republic in 1602. It was created by merging several smaller competing companies that had been sending ships to Asia since the 1590s, and it received a monopoly on Dutch trade east of the Cape of Good Hope for an initial period of twenty-one years, renewed repeatedly thereafter. Its organization was new in important ways. The company had six regional chambers, at Amsterdam, Zeeland, Delft, Rotterdam, Hoorn, and Enkhuizen, and was governed by a board of seventeen directors, the Heren XVII. Its initial capital, about 6.4 million guilders, was raised from a broad range of investors, and its shares could be traded on the Amsterdam exchange. Unlike earlier voyage-by-voyage ventures, the VOC had a permanent capital stock, which allowed it to maintain fleets, fortresses, and warehouses over decades.

The charter also delegated sovereign-like powers in Asia. The VOC could make treaties and wars, build fortresses, appoint officials, administer justice, and coin money in the name of the States General, within the limits of the charter. This did not make it a state, but it meant that the company, unlike a purely commercial firm, was authorized to use force and diplomacy. Overseas, it was administered by a governor-general and the Council of the Indies, based from 1619 at Batavia, on the northwest coast of Java, founded by Jan Pieterszoon Coen on the site of the town of Jayakarta. Batavia served as the VOC's Asian headquarters, a fortified entrepôt, and a center for gathering and redistributing goods.

The VOC's first priority was the spice trade. Pepper, cloves, nutmeg, mace, and cinnamon were highly profitable in Europe, and the company sought control of the sources of fine spices in the Moluccas, Banda, and Ceylon, sometimes through violent means, including the conquest of Banda in 1621. Yet a crucial point for South Asian history is that the VOC's operations were not limited to exporting Asian goods to Europe. The company was above all a participant in intra-Asian trade, the exchange of goods among Asian markets. Spices could not be purchased in the archipelago with European goods alone, for there was little demand for them. Instead, the VOC acquired Indian textiles, especially from the Coromandel Coast and Gujarat, and exchanged them for spices and other commodities in Southeast Asia. Copper and silver from Japan, porcelain and silk from China, and cloth from India circulated through VOC shipping networks.

This explains why India mattered so much to the Dutch. Indian cloth was the currency of the spice trade, in effect, and Coromandel and Bengal textile procurement became a central business of the company. The VOC also sold Indian cloth in Europe, and it traded Indian commodities such as saltpetre, indigo, and opium. Dutch merchants hoped to profit not only from European demand but from the price differences among Asian markets. This strategy required reliable access to Indian ports, producers, and credit, which in turn required cultivating relationships with Indian rulers and merchants, not simply fighting them.

The company's size and organization gave it advantages that smaller rivals lacked: continuous capital, a large fleet, a dense network of agents, and sustained access to bullion. But its costs were also large. Maintaining fortresses, garrisons, and a large administrative staff required high revenue, and the company's commitment to monopoly enforcement in the spice islands produced costly wars. By the eighteenth century the VOC's profits were eroding even as its commercial operations remained substantial. Students should therefore avoid the older image of the VOC as a straightforwardly triumphant European institution. It was powerful, but its power depended on the Asian markets it served.

Section 61.4

Dutch Trading Settlements in Coromandel, Bengal, and Malabar

The VOC established its Indian presence along three coasts. On the Coromandel Coast, Dutch merchants appeared at Masulipatnam from 1605, where they dealt with the Qutb Shahi sultanate of Golconda, and in 1613 they built Fort Geldria at Pulicat, north of Madras, with the permission of the local nayaka authority. Pulicat became the VOC's principal Coromandel base for several decades. Further south, the Dutch took Nagapattinam from the Portuguese in 1658 and made it a regional center. These establishments gave the company access to weavers and painters of cotton cloth, who produced goods that the VOC sent to Batavia and onward to markets in Southeast Asia.

In Bengal, the Dutch established factories at Hugli and nearby Chinsurah, with additional establishments at Kasimbazar, Patna, and elsewhere. Bengal supplied silk, cotton textiles, saltpetre, and rice, and Patna was important for saltpetre and opium. The Dutch relied on grants from the Mughal governors of Bengal and paid customs on their trade, and by the early eighteenth century the Bengal factories had become one of the VOC's most profitable operations. Along the Malabar Coast, the Dutch captured Cochin in 1663 and extended influence over several small rulers, from whom they sought pepper under agreements that gave the company exclusive purchase rights, although local producers and rulers did not always comply.

These settlements differed in legal and political character, a distinction that matters throughout this chapter. A trading factory was an establishment where company agents lived and worked under the permission of the local ruler, paying customs and taxes. A fortified settlement added walls and guns for protection, usually with the ruler's consent or acquiescence, but remained within a larger political order. Territorial sovereignty meant authority over land and population, including revenue and justice. In the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, almost all Dutch Indian establishments fell into the first two categories. Cochin and Nagapattinam, conquered from the Portuguese, came closer to small territorial possessions, but even these depended on local grain supplies, local labor, and the tolerance of neighboring rulers.

Relationships with Indian rulers, officials, and merchants were constant and detailed. At Masulipatnam, Pulicat, and Chinsurah, the Dutch negotiated customs rates, secured permission to export specific goods, and sought protection from local officials. They also depended on Indian chief merchants who arranged purchases of cloth, advanced funds to weavers, and managed local credit. Political authorities in turn valued the revenue the Dutch trade produced. Indian rulers could and did limit Dutch privileges, restrict exports, or impose new charges when relations soured.

The VOC's position in India shifted in the first half of the eighteenth century. Its commercial institutions remained substantial: Dutch shipping, warehouses, and agents continued to operate on a scale the English and French companies could not yet match. But competition intensified, and the company's costs rose. In 1741 the kingdom of Travancore under Marthanda Varma defeated Dutch forces at Colachel on the Malabar coast, a reminder that regional Indian states could and did defeat European companies and that the Dutch Malabar position depended on local politics. In Bengal, the English increasingly overshadowed Dutch commerce, and in 1759 a Dutch expedition sent up the Hugli was defeated by English forces, a development that belongs more fully to Chapter 62. The VOC did not vanish from India, but by mid-century its strategic position was weaker than its commercial scale suggested.

Section 61.5

The English East India Company: The Charter of 1600 and the Establishment of Trade

The English East India Company received its first royal charter from Elizabeth I on 31 December 1600, as the Governor and Company of Merchants of London Trading into the East Indies. The charter granted the company a monopoly over English trade in a vast area stretching east of the Cape of Good Hope to the Straits of Magellan, for an initial period of fifteen years, subject to termination on notice. It authorized the company to make bylaws and to be governed by a governor and elected committees. Subsequent, separate royal permissions were important to the export of bullion used to purchase Asian goods. More than two hundred subscribers were named as members. The monopoly was a privilege exercised against other English subjects: it forbade rival English merchants from trading in the same region, but it said nothing that bound Indian rulers or other European powers.

The early English voyages were initially separate ventures, each financed by separate subscriptions, and only later were they replaced by joint stocks. A permanent joint stock was established under Oliver Cromwell in 1657, which gave the company a more continuous capital base. English hopes at the outset were focused on the spice trade of the archipelago. But the Dutch, who had a head start and greater capital, excluded the English from much of the spice trade, notably at Amboyna in 1623, where the Dutch executed English company agents and others. Faced with this setback, the English turned increasingly to India, where textiles could be purchased for sale in Europe and elsewhere in Asia.

Access to Indian markets was not simple. The English Company's early agents lacked contacts, capital, and reputation. William Hawkins reached Surat in August 1608 and arrived at the Mughal emperor Jahangir’s court at Agra in April 1609 and obtained favor for a time, but the Portuguese, who had their own relationships with Mughal officials, opposed him. In 1612, English ships under Captain Thomas Best defeated a Portuguese fleet near Swally, off Surat, an episode that impressed Mughal and Gujarati observers and strengthened the case for dealing with the English. In 1613 the company received permission to establish a factory at Surat, and from 1615 to 1619 Sir Thomas Roe served as King James I's ambassador at Jahangir's court, seeking a commercial treaty. Roe's mission did not secure a formal treaty of the kind he wanted, but it helped establish a framework in which English merchants traded at Surat and elsewhere under imperial and local sanction.

Early English organization on the ground was modest. Company servants, called factors, resided in factories under a chief, bought cloth through brokers, arranged shipments, and sold English goods such as woolens, which found limited demand in India. Like the other companies, the English relied heavily on bullion to purchase Indian goods. They also traded privately, a practice both tolerated and contested within the company. These early decades should be understood as a story of a modest English presence seeking commercial space within Mughal and regional systems, not of imperial ambition.

The distinction between a Crown-granted monopoly and actual political control in India is the key lesson of this period. The 1600 charter conferred legal privileges on one English corporation vis-à-vis other English merchants. Whether the company could trade at a given Indian port depended on the grant of the ruler of that port, on payment of customs, on the company's relations with local merchants, and on the balance of naval force. Later charters, especially those of the seventeenth century, added powers to appoint governors, raise soldiers, and make war and peace in the company's dominions, but those powers were not in the 1600 document and they did not translate into rule over Indian land. A student reading the charter should not read back the later Company state.

The distinction between an English corporation's legal rights and its standing in Asian ports was visible in the letters carried by its first representatives. A factory at Surat depended on a Mughal governor's permissions, customs officers, and assurances that the Company's servants would respect the port's regulations. The Company could organize its own internal business under its charter, but it could not compel a Mughal official to accept the charter as an Indian legal instrument. When permission to trade was disputed, merchants negotiated through interpreters, appealed to influential courtiers, or sought the assistance of their Indian creditors. This practical separation of jurisdictions explains why early commercial history cannot be written as the progressive extension of English law over the subcontinent.

Section 61.6

Surat, Madras, Bombay, and Calcutta: The English Company's Expanding Network

The English Company's first major Indian base was Surat, a prosperous Mughal port on the Tapti river, and for much of the seventeenth century the English Company's western India establishment was its principal Indian presence. Surat's vulnerability was demonstrated in 1664 and 1670, when the Maratha leader Shivaji raided the city, and English merchants had to organize their own defense. The episode made the company more conscious of the need for a safer harbor under its own jurisdiction, a concern that helps explain the importance of Bombay.

On the Coromandel Coast, the English had factories at Masulipatnam from 1611 and elsewhere, but the establishment of Madras was a turning point. In 1639 company agents Francis Day and Andrew Cogan negotiated a coastal trading-site grant through the Damarla Nayaka authorities, particularly the local intermediary Ayyappa Nayaka and his brother Venkatadri Nayaka, near the Portuguese settlement of São Tomé and close to textile-producing districts. The company built Fort St George there in 1640. Around it grew a European "White Town" and an Indian "Black Town," populated by weavers, merchants, and others who settled near company protection. The grant was made by a local authority in the aftermath of Vijayanagara's decline, and the English held Madras by permission and, after 1646, under the shifting authority of the Golconda sultanate and later the Mughals. Fort St George became the headquarters of the company's Coromandel establishments.

Bombay's history was different. The island, one of the seven that became the modern city, had been ceded by the Portuguese to the English Crown in 1661 as part of the dowry of Catherine of Braganza on her marriage to Charles II. The Crown found it costly and, in 1668, transferred it to the East India Company for a nominal annual rent. Under governors such as Gerald Aungier, Bombay developed as a fortified port, attracting Gujarati merchants, Parsis, artisans, and others. Its deep harbor and defensible position made it a naval and commercial center, and in time the Company's western presidency was transferred there from Surat.

The establishment of Calcutta requires care. The Company had maintained a factory at Hugli from 1651 and traded elsewhere in Bengal. In 1686 a conflict with Mughal authorities, sometimes called Child's War after the Company chairman Josiah Child, ended in English defeat and a temporary withdrawal. In 1690, the Company's agent Job Charnock returned and settled at the village of Sutanuti, on the east bank of the Hugli. Charnock was not the "founder" of Calcutta in a simple sense: villages already existed, markets were already established, and the English presence grew over years. In 1696, after a local rebellion led by Sobha Singh, the Company received permission to fortify and built Fort William. In 1698 it purchased zamindari rights over three villages, Sutanuti, Kalikata, and Govindapur, from the existing landholders, with the sanction of Mughal prince Azim-us-Shan. Fort William was named in 1700 and a Bengal presidency formally established. The distinction between earlier settlement, commercial establishment, landholding rights, and fort construction is essential.

By the early eighteenth century the Company had developed three principal Indian administrative centers, Bombay, Madras, and its Bengal settlement around Calcutta, each centered on a fortified port; their formal presidencies evolved over time. These sites became commercial, administrative, and military centers, with councils, warehouses, garrisons, and growing populations. They were not yet capitals of territorial states. Each was an enclave whose prosperity depended on the hinterlands that supplied cloth, grain, and labor. The Company's presence at all three also depended on relationships with the Indian authorities in whose territories they lay: Mughal officials in Surat and Bengal, Golconda and later the nawabs of the Carnatic on the Coromandel Coast.

The Madras site illustrates how a commercial grant emerged from several levels of Indian authority. Francis Day and Andrew Cogan negotiated in 1639 through the local Nayaka leadership, including the Damarla family, before the English established their fortified trading settlement. In later European accounts this could be summarized as the Company's acquisition of Madras, but the process involved permission to establish a trading post, obligations to pay local dues, and continuing relationships with the surrounding political powers. Fort St George grew by attracting weavers and merchants from the interior; its commercial value was not inherent in the strip of coastline. Similar qualifications apply to Bengal, where the Company's local landholding rights around Sutanuti, Kalikata, and Govindapur were narrower than the imperial sovereignty later colonial histories sometimes read back into them.

Section 61.7

The French East India Company and the Development of Pondicherry

France entered the Asian trade later and with a different institutional basis. The Compagnie des Indes Orientales was established in 1664 by Louis XIV's minister Jean-Baptiste Colbert, who intended to rival the Dutch and English. Unlike the VOC, whose capital came largely from private investors, the French company was heavily dependent on the Crown, which provided funds, encouraged investment among court and official circles, and could intervene in company affairs. This dependence gave the company political backing but also exposed it to the shifting priorities of the French state and, in wartime, to the fortunes of French armies and finances.

The company established a factory at Surat in 1668, with the help of François Caron and an Armenian merchant, Marcara, and others followed at Masulipatnam and elsewhere. Its most important Indian establishment, however, was Pondicherry. In 1674 François Martin, a French company agent, obtained a site on the Coromandel Coast from the local governor under the Bijapur sultanate and began building a settlement. In its early decades Pondicherry was small, and it suffered setbacks: the Dutch captured it in 1693 and returned it to France in 1699 under the Treaty of Ryswick, after which it was fortified and grew. The French also established a factory at Chandernagore in Bengal, whose position was secured by a grant from Mughal authorities around 1690 and which became a major French commercial center in Bengal.

The French Company had persistent financial difficulties and was reorganized several times. In 1719 it was merged into the larger Compagnie perpétuelle des Indes, connected with John Law's financial schemes, and later it was restructured again. Under capable governors such as Pierre Benoît Dumas, who governed Pondicherry from 1735 to 1741, the settlement expanded its trade and extended French influence, including by acquiring Karikal in 1739 and Mahé on the Malabar coast in 1725. French trade in textiles, and in the shipping of Indian cloth for sale in Africa and the Caribbean, grew substantially in the early eighteenth century.

Pondicherry's development depended on its surrounding South Asian society. Tamil merchants, weavers, and brokers settled in and around the town, textile production in nearby villages supplied cloth for export, and local rulers and officials granted permissions and collected dues. The French administration encouraged settlement and relied on Indian intermediaries to manage trade and local order. The case of Nayiniyappa, discussed in Section 61.10, shows how deeply French administration was entangled with Tamil commercial power. Pondicherry should be seen as a place created by negotiation between French administrators and the South Asian society that supplied its goods, its labor, and much of its capital.

French finances affected what officials in Pondicherry could promise their Indian partners. A governor might approve purchases or offer a military subsidy on the expectation of remittances from France or profits from future voyages, only to find that European creditors, rival administrators, or a delayed ship had restricted his cash. That uncertainty could shift risk to Tamil chief merchants who had advanced money to weavers or guaranteed deliveries. It also made the French government and the Company's metropolitan investors important actors in local politics: they judged the expenses of fortification and diplomacy by calculations made thousands of miles away. The development of Pondicherry consequently combined French corporate ambition with Indian commercial capital and the constraints of maritime communication.

The settlement's urban society cannot be described solely through French institutions. Tamil-speaking households, religious establishments, market associations, and creditors continued to govern many of the relationships through which everyday business was transacted. Brokers often translated not just words but legal expectations and obligations: a promise made before a French council had to be made intelligible to village suppliers and Indian revenue officials. The resulting disputes over interpretation and authority were not peripheral cultural misunderstandings. They went to the center of the Company's capacity to obtain cloth, collect debts, and retain labor, as the Nayiniyappa prosecution later demonstrated.

Section 61.8

European Commercial Rivalry, Corporate Finance, and the Competition for Privileges

The four European powers pursued different commercial strategies shaped by their institutions and resources. The Portuguese Estado was a Crown enterprise, funded and governed by the state, with a strong emphasis on fortresses, naval control, and customs. The Dutch VOC was a private joint-stock corporation with permanent capital and a vast intra-Asian network. The English Company, financed by London merchants and governed by a court of directors, had lower overhead than the VOC, drew on a rapidly growing domestic market for Indian cloth, and was initially less committed to monopoly enforcement through conquest. The French Company relied on state support and in the eighteenth century combined commercial ambitions with the interests of a Crown engaged in European and colonial wars.

Finance was central. Every company needed capital to buy cloth, pay for ships and crews, maintain forts, and wait many months or years for returns, since a voyage from Europe to India and back took a year or more. The VOC's permanent capital and stock market gave it unmatched staying power. The English Company, after 1657, had a joint stock, and in 1708 it was reorganized after the merger of rival English companies as the United Company of Merchants of England Trading to the East Indies. Its dealings with Parliament and the Crown, including loans to the state in exchange for the renewal of privileges, bound it to metropolitan politics. The French Company's reliance on royal funds left it more exposed when French state finances faltered.

Competition took many forms: rival offers for customs rates, rival claims for exclusive contracts for particular goods such as pepper or saltpetre, competition for access to ports, and competition for protection from local rulers. Competition also took violent form. Dutch and English fleets clashed in the seventeenth century, and the Anglo-Dutch wars had echoes in Asia. In time, the Dutch and English in India often cooperated in practice at Indian courts against Portuguese or French rivals, only to compete elsewhere. Rivalry could produce diplomatic cooperation in one location and violent confrontation in another because the companies' interests varied by place: a company with a settlement under threat from an Indian ruler might seek help from a European rival, while two companies purchasing from the same weavers had reason to bid against each other.

For Indian rulers, European competition offered opportunities. A ruler could grant privileges to one company to raise revenue, play companies against each other to lower demands, or seek military or naval help against enemies. Merchants and brokers likewise moved business among companies. This competitive environment kept the balance of power between European companies and Indian authorities generally favorable to the latter during the seventeenth century and much of the eighteenth, though that balance would shift.

By the 1740s the conditions were emerging under which commercial rivalry could become entangled with Indian regional politics. The Mughal viceroyalty of the Deccan had become the largely autonomous Hyderabad state under Nizam-ul-Mulk Asaf Jah, and the Nawab of the Carnatic at Arcot, nominally subordinate to Hyderabad, governed the Coromandel coast districts where English and French settlements lay. Succession disputes, tribute demands, and military rivalries created openings for outside intervention. At the same time, the outbreak of European wars brought Anglo-French hostility to Asia. These two developments, one European and one Indian, would converge in the Carnatic.

An important comparison concerns the location of financial risk. VOC directors could spread some losses among the company's investors, while a chief merchant who guaranteed an order might put his family's property, credit, and standing in danger if producers failed to deliver. French and English factors also sometimes traded privately alongside the corporation they served, creating conflicts between official purchases and personal transactions. Local rulers could exploit rivalry by renegotiating duties or favoring a company that offered naval assistance, but those choices carried dangers when the preferred company failed to defend a port or fulfill its payments. Competition therefore did not create an equal bargaining market: it placed actors with different information, obligations, and means of coercion into repeated, contingent negotiations.

Section 61.9

Indian Merchants, Bankers, and the Commercial Networks of South Asia

No European company could have bought a bale of cloth, shipped a cargo of pepper, or cashed a bill of exchange without Indian partners. The commercial systems the companies entered were composed of overlapping merchant communities, each with its own capital, kin networks, and regional specialties. In Gujarat, Hindu Bania and Jain merchants and Muslim Bohra and Khoja traders operated at Surat, Cambay, Ahmedabad, and Broach. Surat was home to extraordinary fortunes: the Gujarati Muslim merchant Abdul Ghafur and the Hindu merchant Virji Vora, both active in the seventeenth century, were reputed to command shipping fleets and capital that rivaled those of European companies. On the Coromandel Coast, Telugu Komati and Beri Chetti merchants and Tamil Chettiar and Marakkayar Muslim traders managed the trade of Pulicat, Madras, Masulipatnam, and Nagapattinam, and individuals such as Kasi Viranna in seventeenth-century Madras acted as chief merchants for the English Company while maintaining independent interests.

In Bengal, Hindu and Muslim merchants, bankers, and landholders organized the production and sale of silk and cotton cloth, saltpetre, and grain. Armenian merchants, whose commercial diaspora extended from Iran to India and Southeast Asia, held a prominent place in Bengal, Madras, and Surat, valued for their connections across Asia and for their linguistic and financial skills. Marwari and other northern bankers, including the great banking houses discussed in Chapter 60, controlled credit networks that supplied nawabs and merchants alike. Arab, Persian, and Armenian shipowners carried goods across the Arabian Sea, and the Hajj pilgrimage from Surat added a religiously inflected dimension to maritime enterprise.

Credit was the lifeblood of this system. Merchants and bankers, known as shroffs or sarrafs, issued and cashed hundis, bills of exchange that allowed money to be moved across hundreds of kilometers without transporting coin. A merchant in Surat could pay a supplier in Burhanpur or Ahmedabad with a hundi drawn on a banker in that place, and the hundi could circulate as a form of credit. Money changers assayed coin, converted among currencies, and managed the many rupees and pagodas in circulation. This credit system enabled merchants to advance funds to weavers, finance cargoes before sale, and spread risk across partners and voyages.

Relationships among manufacturers, wholesalers, brokers, and rulers were intricate. A weaver in a village might receive an advance from a local merchant or broker, who in turn was financed by a larger merchant in a port, who supplied cloth to European and Asian buyers. Rulers and officials expected revenue and presents and sometimes participated in commerce themselves. Merchants maintained connections with inland production centers and with the political authorities who controlled access to roads and ports. The system thus combined commercial, financial, and political elements long before European companies arrived.

Indian merchants exercised genuine agency. They chose partners, compared prices, and moved business from one company to another when terms were unsatisfactory. A merchant who sold cloth to the Dutch one year might sell to the English the next. Some invested in European voyages or lent money to company officials, and others carried their own cargoes on their own ships, competing directly with the companies. They also managed risk, diversifying across routes and commodities. European companies often found themselves negotiating for merchants' goodwill, not commanding their cooperation.

It is important to distinguish this period from what followed. Before European political domination, Indian merchants possessed considerable bargaining power. Later, as the English Company acquired territorial authority in Bengal and elsewhere, the opportunities of many merchants narrowed: company monopsony, privileged trade, and eventually colonial regulation altered the terms under which they operated, though some merchants and bankers found new roles as contractors, financiers, and collaborators. The commercial world of 1700 was not the colonial economy of 1800, and the difference should not be erased.

The careers of individual merchants make the limits of a Europe-centered account clear. At Surat, Virji Vora and Mulla Abdul Ghafur operated enterprises that connected the port to wider Indian Ocean markets. Their businesses were not branches of an English or Dutch corporation, and their networks could encompass agents, family credit, shipping, and dealings with multiple foreign buyers. Ron Harris's comparative study of family firms underscores both the commercial reach of such houses and their vulnerability to disruptions affecting the proprietor, kin network, or political environment. In Madras, Kasi Viranna became an influential chief merchant in the later seventeenth century; his authority extended to local community affairs as well as company procurement. These careers should not be flattened into a single story of Indian merchants supplying European firms. They indicate multiple kinds of commercial power, organized through different institutional forms.

Commercial information was itself an asset. A broker might know which weaving districts had spare capacity, which bankers would honor a bill, which official expected customary payments, and which competing buyer had promised an unrealistically high price. This knowledge could be protected through family ties, accounting practices, and trusted agents, rather than being shared openly with a foreign factor. Such complaints reveal a real problem of supervision, but they are not neutral evidence of fraud by Indian intermediaries.

Section 61.10

Dubashes, Commercial Brokers, and Cross-Cultural Negotiation

Between the European companies and Indian society stood a group of intermediaries without whom the companies could not operate. The most prominent in the south were the dubashes (from Hindustani dobhashi, "person of two languages"), who served as interpreters, brokers, and commercial agents. In practice, a chief dubash or chief merchant did far more than translate. He arranged cloth contracts, advanced company money to weavers and merchants, collected information, represented the company in dealings with local officials, and often held his own commercial and landholding interests. In the north, similar roles were played by brokers known by terms such as banyan and gomastha, and chief merchants held positions at the factories of all three major companies.

Their influence derived from knowledge and capital. They knew the languages, the weavers, the revenue officials, and the caste and kin networks through which goods and credit moved. European officials, usually newcomers on short postings and often ignorant of local conditions, depended on them. A broker might provide security for the company's advances, guarantee that cloth would be delivered at a given quality, and persuade local authorities to grant favorable treatment. Brokers often built large fortunes, patronized temples and mosques, and became heads of castes or community leaders in settlements, giving them a social authority that extended beyond commerce.

The career of Nayiniyappa illustrates both the power and vulnerability of such figures. A Tamil Hindu merchant, he became chief broker of the French Company at Pondicherry in the early eighteenth century, and he was among the town's most influential residents, deeply involved in the textile trade and in local social and religious life. His prominence generated rivalries with French officials, with Catholic missionaries, and with other Indian merchants. In 1716 he was arrested and prosecuted by the Company's authorities at Pondicherry. The case involved accusations that centered on the nature and limits of his authority, and it became entangled with disputes over religion, law, and the proper relationship between French administrators and Tamil elites. Later, Company authorities in France reviewed the case, and he was posthumously exonerated. The story has been reconstructed in detail by Danna Agmon in A Colonial Affair (2017), which uses petitions, legal records, and Company correspondence.

The affair demonstrates that commercial wealth, religious identity, legal authority, and administrative rivalry were intertwined. French officials sought to assert control over a settlement whose economy depended on Tamil intermediaries, and missionaries objected to the public religious role of a powerful Hindu. Nayiniyappa and his supporters, for their part, understood how to use petitions, family networks, and legal arguments to defend their position. The case shows that colonial settlements were arenas of negotiation, where Indian residents could make claims in the language of French law and Company interests.

A later generation produced another important Pondicherry intermediary: Ananda Ranga Pillai (1709–1761), who served as chief dubash to Governor Dupleix from the late 1740s. He kept a diary in Tamil between 1736 and 1761, recording trade, local disputes, diplomatic exchanges, rumors, and the course of the Carnatic Wars from a vantage point few outsiders possessed. The diary was published in English translation in the early twentieth century (edited by J. F. Price and K. Rangachari) and is one of the most valuable sources for the period. It must be used critically: it reflects the author's position as a powerful intermediary with his own interests, and the English translation involves choices that mediate the Tamil original. Together, Nayiniyappa and Ananda Ranga Pillai show that Indian intermediaries were participants in company politics, not mere functionaries.

Agmon's reconstruction allows the Nayiniyappa affair to be dated and understood more precisely. Arrested in February 1716, the broker was convicted by the French colonial court on 6 June of that year on charges of tyranny and sedition. The sentence included public whipping, confiscation, imprisonment, and the banishment of his sons. Nayiniyappa died in custody in August 1717. His supporters contested the proceedings, particularly the handling of testimony and interpretation, and the case was reopened after royal intervention. Pondicherry's council overturned the conviction in 1719, and in 1720 the French authorities formally restored his reputation and ordered redress for his family. Those decisions do not establish every allegation made by either faction; they do reveal the reach of merchants' petitioning networks from Pondicherry to France.

Language was a major point of contention. Testimony was taken and circulated in Tamil, Portuguese, and French, and the choice of interpreter shaped what the judges claimed to know. A commercial broker accustomed to negotiating across languages could be especially vulnerable when the translation of his own words was controlled by opponents. The affair therefore documents more than disagreement between a Hindu merchant and Catholic missionaries. It reveals how corporate authority was produced through records, legal procedure, commercial dependence, and competing claims about who could speak for the town's inhabitants.

Historical Vocabulary
Dubash

The term dubash derives from the South Asian expression do-bhashi or du-bhashi, meaning a person of two languages, and it was used especially in southern India for an interpreter and commercial agent attached to a European company or official. Its meaning widened in practice to include chief broker, financial manager, and political intermediary.

The term matters because translating it as "interpreter" understates the role. A dubash controlled commercial relationships, credit, and information, and could be among the wealthiest and most socially important people in a settlement. Equating a dubash with a modern employee likewise misleads, for dubashes often had independent capital and obligations to their own communities as well as to the company.

Section 61.11

Textile Commerce: Weavers, Dyers, Merchants, and Company Procurement

Cotton and silk textiles were India's most important manufactured exports and the most valuable commodity the companies bought. Different regions specialized. Bengal, especially the area around Dhaka, produced the finest muslins, and Kasimbazar and its hinterland produced raw silk and silk cloth. Gujarat, with Surat, Ahmedabad, and Broach as centers, supplied plain and patterned cottons for Asian, African, and European markets. The Coromandel Coast produced painted and dyed cottons, famous for fast colors achieved through mordants and resist techniques, which the Europeans called chintz and which were prized in Southeast Asia and Europe. Weaving centers in the interior supplied coastal export towns. These industries had long histories, and their skill, specialization, and organization were not creations of European demand.

Production was organized through households and communities. Weavers, usually belonging to specific caste or community groups, worked on looms in village or small-town settings, often in family units. Cotton was grown and spun by others, frequently women working in households. Dyers, washers, bleachers, painters, and printers carried out finishing processes, and specialized communities handled each. Merchants and brokers connected these producers to port markets, often through advances of money or thread. Village and town markets, periodic fairs, and larger centers where merchants gathered facilitated exchange. Rulers and local officials taxed looms and sales and in some cases controlled access to the most valuable raw materials.

Companies arranged procurement by working through brokers and chief merchants. The company would specify quantities, qualities, sizes, patterns, and dates, particularly for cloth destined for different markets, as European, African, and Southeast Asian tastes varied. In Bengal, the system of advances, in which the company, through its Indian agents, gave weavers money in advance of delivery, became widespread, and in Coromandel, merchants contracted on behalf of companies and distributed advances to weavers and dyers. Quality inspection was crucial: companies rejected cloth that failed standards, and disputes over price and quality were common. Companies also had to make decisions about seasonal schedules, since cloth had to be ready for the monsoon shipping season.

Procurement was largely a matter of negotiated contracts in this period, with weavers and merchants able to bargain, switch buyers, or refuse unprofitable terms. Competition among companies and Indian merchants gave producers some leverage, particularly when demand was high. But the system could also become more coercive. Advances created debts that bound weavers, and in later periods, as the English Company acquired political power in Bengal, its agents sometimes used force and official influence to compel weavers to deliver cloth at fixed prices, discussed in later chapters. This distinction between negotiated commercial contracts and coercive procurement is important: it tracks a change in political context and not simply a continuation of commerce.

European demand also provoked reaction in Europe. English weavers and manufacturers, alarmed by competition from Indian printed and painted cottons, lobbied for restrictions, and Parliament passed the Calico Acts of 1700 and 1720, which prohibited the import and, later, the wearing of most printed or dyed cottons from India. These measures show the impact of Indian cloth on European markets and the political significance of the commerce, but they also show that the companies' business depended on European regulation. The sections of this chapter on Indian production should therefore be read as correcting a common error: the companies did not create Indian textile manufacturing; they purchased from an already highly developed system, and its skill and organization determined much of what the companies could do.

A procurement contract passed through several hands before it reached the loom. Company factors specified a bale's dimensions and appearance; chief merchants converted these specifications into orders for regional brokers; the brokers distributed advances to spinning, weaving, dyeing, and finishing households; and inspectors had to determine whether the completed cloth matched samples. Delay or rejection could therefore produce disputes about responsibility at each step. In some districts merchants organized production through personal and family networks that no European official could readily inspect. The company could demand uniformity on paper, but the practical quality of an order depended on local knowledge of dyes, seasons, thread, and the capacities of particular groups of artisans.

Bargaining power also varied over a commercial cycle. During periods of intense competition, highly skilled producers could redirect output to another merchant or ask for better prices. In years of poor harvests, interrupted shipping, or wartime demand, debt and food insecurity could narrow those choices. Women who spun cotton, prepared yarn, washed cloth, or contributed unpaid household labor were essential to this system even though contracts commonly identified male merchants or male heads of weaving households. Production histories built solely from company invoices therefore risk disguising labor and unequal access to earnings. The survival of Indian textile exports says much about artisanal capabilities, but much less by itself about the material welfare of every producing family.

Section 61.12

Bullion, Credit, Intra-Asian Trade, and Commercial Competition

European demand for Indian goods far exceeded Indian demand for European goods. Woolen cloth, metals, and other European products found limited markets in India, and the companies therefore paid for their purchases largely in silver and, to a lesser extent, gold. Silver came to Europe from the Americas and from Japan, and the companies exported it to Asia in large quantities. This bullion flowed into the Indian economy, where it was minted into coin, especially into the Mughal rupee, and circulated through merchant and banking networks. Mughal mints at Surat, Dhaka, and elsewhere recoined foreign silver, and the abundance of silver supported the commercial economy and the revenue system described in Chapter 55.

Companies did not rely only on bullion. They borrowed locally, drew bills on Indian bankers, and used credit extended by brokers and merchants. A factor in Bengal or Coromandel might take a loan from an Indian banker, repayable through bills drawn on Company funds elsewhere, so that the company could buy cloth before European silver arrived. Indian financiers therefore stood at the center of company operations, and their willingness to lend affected the companies' ability to function. In wartime or political crisis, credit could tighten sharply, a fact that would matter during the Carnatic Wars.

Trade between India and Europe formed only part of the picture. Indian ports exchanged goods with each other, with Southeast Asia, the Persian Gulf, the Red Sea, and East Africa. Indian merchants carried much of this trade in their own ships, and companies increasingly took part in it, particularly the Dutch and, later, English private traders. A cargo of Coromandel cloth might go to Batavia or Aceh to be exchanged for pepper or spices, which in turn were shipped to India or Europe. The Dutch used Indian textiles to acquire spices in the archipelago, while English "country traders," private merchants licensed or tolerated by the Company, carried cargoes among Asian ports. This intra-Asian commerce provided profits that helped finance European-directed trade and gave Europeans a stake in the political stability of Asian coasts.

The economic consequences of changing European demand varied. For Indian weavers and dyers, rising demand brought employment and income in some regions, but also dependence on a few large buyers. For merchants and bankers, it created opportunities for profit and risks of default. For rulers, it increased customs revenue and the circulation of money but also exposed their territories to competition for resources and to foreign naval power. For the companies, bullion exports caused concern among critics in Europe who feared loss of precious metal, even though much of it returned in the form of re-exported goods.

It would be misleading to treat bullion inflows or trade surpluses as simple measures of Indian prosperity. A large inflow of silver might increase the supply of money, raise prices, and benefit merchants and officials who received it, while leaving the situation of ordinary producers ambiguous. Likewise, rising exports might benefit some weaving communities while straining others. Historians therefore ask how gains and costs were distributed among different commercial and producing groups, not whether "India" gained or lost. This question leads naturally into the first of the chapter's historical debates.

The movement of precious metal across the Indian Ocean required substantial coordination. Silver arriving at a port might need assay, conversion into locally acceptable coin, transport to an inland weaving district, and delivery through a banker who could settle accounts months before the next European vessel arrived. Exchange rates, discounting, and the reliability of a named banker consequently mattered as much as the nominal quantity of bullion shipped from Europe. A company could possess substantial assets in one presidency yet face a shortage of spendable money in another. The account books of trading establishments are best read as evidence of these linked but uneven credit markets, not as the simple circulation of a single European treasury.

Intra-Asian trade further complicates the familiar idea that European corporations merely carried Indian merchandise to Europe. Coromandel cotton cloth might be exchanged in Southeast Asia for pepper or spices; other proceeds might support a purchase elsewhere before the cargo was shipped west. Local merchants also undertook these circuits independently. A European company that tried to monopolize a commodity therefore confronted rival Asian demand as well as competition from other European flags. Control of a factory or shipping route rarely meant control of production. These overlapping commercial chains made the region prosperous for some financiers, but they also transmitted distant shortages, wars, and changes in demand to producing communities.

Historical Debate

Were European Companies Powerful Commercial Innovators or Dependents on Existing Asian Networks?

The question: Did the chartered companies transform Asian commerce through new forms of corporate organization, capital, and naval power, or did they largely adapt to, and depend on, commercial systems already operating in Asia?

Interpretation A — Corporate innovation: Scholars emphasizing corporate organization point to joint-stock finance, permanent capital, large fleets, continuous record-keeping, and armed capacity. The VOC especially introduced a form of enterprise that could sustain operations over decades and coordinate trade among many Asian markets. On this view, the companies' institutional features gave them advantages that individual Asian merchants lacked.
Interpretation B — Dependence on Asian systems: Historians such as Om Prakash, whose work on the Dutch in India highlights the importance of Indian producers and markets, and Tapan Raychaudhuri, who studied Indian commercial conditions, emphasize that the companies relied on Indian weavers, brokers, bankers, and shipping, and operated within terms set largely by Indian rulers and markets. C. A. Bayly and others have stressed the vitality of Indian merchant and banking networks and the continuing importance of Indian capital, while scholarship on Indian Ocean commerce more broadly stresses the longevity of Asian commercial institutions.

Evidence: Both views draw on company archives, Indian commercial records, travelers' accounts, and studies of textile production and credit. Company records document organization and procurement; Indian evidence, though scarcer, documents the role of merchants and brokers.

Why it matters: If companies were chiefly innovators, the eighteenth-century turn to political power may appear as a natural extension of corporate dynamism. If they were dependents, their eventual political role appears more contingent on political circumstances. The debate is not a contest between European superiority and Asian stagnation: both interpretations recognize that Asian commerce was dynamic. Many scholars combine them, treating corporate institutions as real advantages whose effects were limited by dependence on Asian partners until political conditions changed.

Section 61.13

Trading Factories, Farmans, Customs, and Commercial Privileges

A European factory was not a manufacturing plant. The word derives from factor, a commercial agent, and a factory was the establishment where factors lived, kept accounts, stored goods, and conducted company business. A typical factory included a warehouse, offices, residences for the staff, a counting room, and sometimes a chapel and a garden. It operated under the permission of the local ruler, and its legal status was typically that of a tenant and trader rather than an owner of sovereign territory.

To establish and maintain a factory, a company had to obtain permission. This usually involved negotiations with local officials, payment of presents, and agreement about customs. Rulers granted written authorizations: Mughal emperors and princes issued farmans, formal decrees conferring privileges, while provincial governors issued parwanas and other orders. The Qutb Shahi sultans, the nayakas, and Maratha and other authorities issued their own grants, called by various names. These documents specified where the company could trade, what customs it would pay, what goods it could export, and sometimes provided assurances of protection. They were granted at the ruler's discretion and could be revised, reinterpreted, or withdrawn.

Customs were central. Indian ports charged duties on imports and exports, usually a percentage of the value of goods, and different merchants might pay different rates depending on their status and privileges. Companies sought exemptions or reduced rates, and a company that obtained a favorable rate gained a competitive advantage. In Mughal territories, customs at Surat and in Bengal were important sources of revenue, and officials guarded them carefully. Companies also sought rights to rent or hold land around their settlements for warehouses and housing, and to mint coin, but such rights were limited and negotiated.

The English Company's grants of 1717 from the Mughal emperor Farrukhsiyar are among the most discussed. After negotiations by a Company delegation led by John Surman, with the surgeon William Hamilton credited with medical service to the emperor, Farrukhsiyar issued farmans confirming and extending the Company's privileges. In Bengal, the Company obtained customs exemptions for its official trade in return for an annual payment of 3,000 rupees. Separate ministerial instructions addressed the treatment of Company dastaks, or passes, used for goods in transit; the various grants and instructions must not be conflated. The settlement also included permission, subject to local procedures, to seek leases in villages near Calcutta. These grants were significant, but they must be distinguished from later claims. Provincial authorities in Bengal, particularly the governor Murshid Quli Khan, limited the implementation of some provisions, notably the acquisition of additional villages. Later, Company servants used dastaks to cover private trade, a practice that provoked conflict with Bengal's nawabs and is a subject of Chapter 62. The 1717 grants were official privileges, not a transfer of sovereignty.

The lesson is that company charters granted in Europe did not confer authority over Indian territories. A charter authorized a company within its home jurisdiction. In India, a company's rights existed only as far as local rulers granted them and as far as those rulers could be persuaded or compelled to uphold them. The gap between the paper privileges in London or Paris and the facts of power in Indian courts was wide, and companies spent decades trying to narrow it.

There was a basic mismatch between the duration of a commercial privilege and the shifting authorities who were expected to enforce it. A farman might be issued at the imperial court, interpreted by an agent in Bengal, contested by a customs official at a river station, and presented again to a new provincial governor. Company servants commonly argued that a grant exempted them from particular duties, while local officials insisted that the company's private traders or inland movements exceeded its scope. Such conflicts could remain matters of petitions and accounting for years before military power entered the argument. To understand later confrontation one must first reconstruct what the document actually granted, to whom, and under whose jurisdiction it was enforceable.

Historical Vocabulary
Factory

In seventeenth- and eighteenth-century usage, a factory was a trading establishment where the company's factors, or agents, bought and sold goods, stored cargo, and kept accounts. The term derives from the Latin factor, one who acts or does business for another.

The modern meaning of "factory" as a place of industrial manufacture is misleading here. Factories in this period were closer to branch offices with warehouses. They did not produce cloth; weavers worked in their own homes and workshops. Understanding the term correctly also helps distinguish a factory from a fort and from a territorial possession: a company could have a factory without owning land or governing anyone.

Section 61.14

Fortified Trading Settlements and the Growth of Port Cities

Companies fortified their settlements to protect goods, personnel, and capital from pirates, rival Europeans, and sometimes local rulers. Fort St George at Madras, Fort William at Calcutta, the fortified establishment at Bombay, the French fortifications at Pondicherry, Dutch Fort Geldria at Pulicat, and the Portuguese strongholds of Goa, Daman, and Diu were typical. Walls, bastions, and artillery were often modest by the standards of European fortresses, but they made a settlement more defensible than an open factory and gave the company a base for negotiation.

A fortified settlement was more than a fort. At Madras, the fort precinct included the Company’s offices, warehouses, and a substantial European residential quarter often called "White Town"; the larger "Black Town" expanded beyond the core fort area and housed numerous merchants, artisans, and laborers. Docks or beaches for lighters and boats, customs facilities, markets, and residential quarters grew around the walls. Madras was exposed to surf and had no natural harbor, so goods were carried by boats across the breakers, an arrangement that employed large numbers of boatmen. Bombay had a deep natural harbor and in time developed shipyards. Calcutta, on the Hugli, offered river access to Bengal's interior but was low-lying, unhealthy, and prone to flooding. Pondicherry's regular grid layout reflected French planning ideas, with separate quarters for different communities.

Merchants, artisans, soldiers, religious groups, and migrants settled near company establishments because they offered security, markets, and opportunities. Weavers and painters moved to Madras and Pondicherry in search of orders and protection from the exactions of local officials. Merchants of many communities, including Armenians, Parsis, Gujarati Banias, Tamil Chettiars, and Muslim traders, found advantages in a place with relatively predictable commercial rules. Catholic, Protestant, Hindu, Muslim, Jewish, and Armenian Christian institutions took root. The settlements therefore became cosmopolitan towns whose populations were overwhelmingly Indian, with small European minorities.

The relationship between a fortified enclave and its surroundings was one of interdependence and tension. Settlements depended on local rulers for the land beneath them, on surrounding villages for food and labor, and on regional trade networks for goods. Indian authorities received revenue from the settlements and could threaten them, as Mughal forces did in Bengal in 1686 and as the Marathas and Mysore did in later decades. Jurisdictional boundaries were often unclear, and disputes arose over who could collect taxes, try offenders, or control markets in adjacent villages.

It is essential to see how limited these places were. In the early eighteenth century, Madras, Calcutta, Bombay, and Pondicherry were small enclaves, nothing like the territorial states the English Company would govern in the nineteenth century. Their governing authorities controlled small areas, depended on revenue from trade and from rents within the enclave, and negotiated constantly with larger powers. That these enclaves would later become the bases of colonial rule was not a foregone conclusion at the time of their foundation.

The form of a port city influenced company power. Madras, set on an exposed surf coast, relied on boatmen and laborers to bring cargo between offshore vessels and warehouses. Bombay's sheltered harbor could accommodate more shipping facilities, while vessels entering Bengal's Hugli faced shifting channels, tidal conditions, and the need for local pilots. These differences shaped the time and expense of moving textiles, troops, and provisions. They also explain why a large commercial settlement could be vulnerable in war: its walls protected stores and officials but could not by themselves secure nearby food supplies or guarantee the loyalty of neighboring communities.

Settlement boundaries were rarely cleanly divided into an entirely European town and an entirely Indian one. At Madras the Fort St George precinct contained much of the administrative and European residential quarter; the larger Indian town developed outside and around it, with overlapping occupational, religious, and neighborhood affiliations. The categories 'White Town' and 'Black Town' were imposed administrative descriptions, not faithful maps of social separation. Relationships of tenancy, credit, marriage, labor, and petition crossed the walls. This matters when interpreting company claims of jurisdiction: an officer's ability to govern a marketplace depended as much on Indian headmen and brokers as on a fort's guns.

Section 61.15

Government, Law, Religion, and Society within European Settlements

Each settlement was governed by a council of senior company officials, headed by a governor or president. The council directed trade, managed finances, regulated the settlement, and maintained correspondence with superiors in Europe. Justice was administered by a mixture of company courts applying European law, mayor's courts (in the English case, from 1687 at Madras and after 1726 in the presidency towns), and customary arrangements for Indian communities. In practice, headmen of castes and religious communities often settled disputes among their own members, and the company intervened when order or revenue was at stake.

Legal positions differed. Europeans were subject to company law and to the courts of their nation. Indian residents were subject partly to company authority and partly to community institutions, and the line between them was negotiated. Company officials regulated labor, taxes, and markets within the settlement and sometimes extended their jurisdiction over adjacent villages. Mixed populations, including Eurasian descendants of European fathers and Indian mothers, occupied an ambiguous position, sometimes serving as soldiers or clerks.

Religion was a frequent source of conflict. Catholic missions in French and Portuguese territories sought converts and objected to public Hindu ceremonies and caste practices, while Protestant chaplains served English settlements. Hindu processions, temple festivals, and caste disputes generated friction over public space, taxation, and the authority of caste headmen. Disputes over caste precedence at Madras, including between so-called Right-Hand and Left-Hand caste groups, required the English Company's intervention. The Nayiniyappa affair is a concrete example of how a commercial and religious dispute could become a struggle over authority: the question of who had the right to command a settlement's population was bound up with questions of religion and law.

Settlements were also sites of inequality and coercion. The VOC and others participated in the slave trade. Dutch records document the export of enslaved people from the Coromandel Coast, especially during periods of famine in the mid-seventeenth century, and enslaved and dependent laborers worked in European households and on ships. Company servants employed domestic servants, boatmen, porters, and laborers whose conditions were often poor. Women worked in many roles: as weavers and spinners in the surrounding districts, as domestic servants, as petty traders, and, in European households, as wives or partners of company servants, in relationships of varying freedom. Documentation of women's lives is thin and filtered through European and elite sources.

The history of Company territorial courts, civil administration, and colonial bureaucracy in the later eighteenth century belongs to Chapter 63. The settlements treated here were small, and their governance mainly concerned the control of trade and the management of a mixed urban population. Yet the practices of negotiation, petition, and jurisdictional competition seen here would shape later institutions.

The authority to hear a dispute was itself contested. A commercial disagreement could involve a company's contract, a local custom recognized by an Indian community, and the intervention of a ruler's official outside the walls. Company councils often sought final authority, yet they relied on translators, witnesses, and influential residents to make judgments effective. The legal records therefore describe both aspirations to centralized rule and the compromises that daily administration required. In Pondicherry, the Nayiniyappa proceedings show how criminal jurisdiction and linguistic control could be weaponized within a dispute among competing groups of French and Tamil residents. Such cases should not be mistaken for the routine operation of the later colonial court system.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living in a Company Town

An observer in Madras around 1700 would have seen a place where daily life was divided by trade, caste, and religion. Boatmen carried goods through the surf, porters hauled bales to warehouses, and weavers and painters in Black Town worked at looms and dyeing vats. Brokers and merchants passed between their houses and the factor's counting room. European officials rose early to attend to cargo and correspondence, and in the heat of the day retired to rest, as many diaries describe.

Mortality among Europeans was high, as fevers and dysentery killed many within a few years of arrival, so that churches and burial grounds recorded steady turnover. Indian residents faced other hazards, including famine, cyclone, flood, and the costs of war. Markets sold rice, fish, vegetables, and cloth. Festivals, temple processions, and mosque and church services marked the calendar. Documentation of ordinary lives is incomplete, and most of what is known comes from company records, missionary letters, and a few Indian sources. Students should treat reconstructions with care and remember how many voices are absent.

Section 61.16

Armed Commerce: Naval Force, Sepoys, Fortification, and Political Negotiation

Companies developed military establishments because commerce in the early modern Indian Ocean was unavoidably exposed to violence. Ships faced pirates and rival navies; warehouses held valuable goods; settlements could be attacked by local rulers, rival Europeans, or bands of raiders. Companies armed their ships and maintained garrisons to protect their interests. The Portuguese had pioneered armed trading; the Dutch used it systematically; the English and French followed. The expression armed commerce captures the combination of trading and force that characterized these enterprises.

At sea, companies deployed armed merchant ships, convoys, and, in some cases, warships. English victories over the Portuguese at Swally in 1612 and the capture of Hormuz in 1622 demonstrated the usefulness of naval force. Naval escorts protected cargoes against pirates, and naval power gave companies leverage with coastal rulers by threatening their trade. But maritime force did not automatically translate into control of land. The companies' ships could blockade ports and seize vessels, as the Dutch did at times, yet such pressure worked only when rulers depended on seaborne commerce.

On land, garrisons of European soldiers and, increasingly, Indian soldiers defended settlements. Companies recruited locally. In Indian practice, soldiers called sepoys (from Persian sipahi) had long served rulers, and company forces adopted the term for the Indian infantry they hired and trained. European officers drilled sepoys in the use of firearms and in formation fighting; companies also employed peons and local militias. In the 1740s, the French under Dupleix and then the English began to use drilled sepoy units on a larger scale in the Carnatic. Recruitment depended on the labor market for soldiers, a market in which rulers, zamindars, and mercenary captains all competed, so company armies were always bargaining for men and money.

Financial cost shaped military capacity. Garrisons, artillery, fortifications, and campaigns were expensive, and company shareholders in Europe expected dividends, not wars. Company directors regularly warned officials against military adventures, and local revenue from customs, rents, and tax assignments (such as the right to the revenue of districts granted by allies) became crucial in funding troops. Intervention in local conflicts could be financed by taking assignments of revenue from ally rulers, which gave companies a stake in the fiscal arrangements of their allies. This interplay of money and force foreshadowed what would later become fiscal-military state-building.

Companies initially negotiated protection with Indian rulers, paying for it with customs or presents. Gradually, they gained the capacity to intervene in local disputes by supporting one claimant against another. Yet the central argument of this section is that armed commerce created new political capacities but did not automatically produce territorial conquest. Military intervention required money, supplies, local allies, and favorable circumstances. The English Company's war with Mughal forces in 1686 ended in failure, and Dutch forces were beaten at Colachel in 1741. Armed commerce increased what companies could do, but whether they could translate force into political authority depended on opportunities that arose from the fragmentation of regional politics, the focus of the next division.

European drill and artillery did not operate independently of South Asian military markets. Companies hired locally recruited infantry, commissioned Indian officers, engaged suppliers of cattle and grain, and paid men whose continued service could depend on the regularity of wages. A sepoy corps required weapons and training, but also interpreters, accountants, transport workers, and reliable credit. Provincial rulers employed and adapted similar techniques, so military change must be studied as exchange and competition rather than a one-way transfer of expertise. Even well-trained troops could be defeated when supplies failed, allies deserted, or commanders misread the political situation.

Fortification created another fiscal dilemma. A wall could protect a warehouse, but constructing bastions and maintaining artillery drew resources away from the purchase of cargo. Directors in Europe could regard an enlarged garrison as an expensive diversion from the Company's commercial mission, while officers in India treated it as necessary insurance. A settlement's military budget thus generated demands for loans, extra local collections, and negotiations over nearby villages. The capacity to raise money locally, rather than tactical skill alone, helps explain why a trading company might begin to seek revenue rights beyond the precinct of its factory.

Section 61.17

The First Carnatic War, 1746–1748: European War in an Indian Political Setting

The War of the Austrian Succession, which began in Europe in 1740 and drew in Britain and France by 1744, extended Anglo-French hostility to India. Before 1746, the English and French companies had generally tried to avoid fighting in India, and the French governor Dupleix and the Nawab of the Carnatic, Anwaruddin Khan, had discouraged hostilities on the Coromandel Coast. The Nawab, as the ruler of the territory, regarded the companies as tenants of his domain and expected them to refrain from war. This expectation was not unreasonable, since both settlements depended on his tolerance.

The war reached the Coromandel Coast when a French naval squadron under Mahé de La Bourdonnais, governor of the Mascarene Islands, arrived and besieged Madras. The town fell to the French in September 1746. La Bourdonnais negotiated a ransom under which Madras would be restored to the English, but Dupleix, who disputed La Bourdonnais's authority and wished to retain the town, repudiated the agreement. The quarrel between the two Frenchmen is a reminder that company officials often disagreed with one another and that policy was shaped by personal and institutional rivalries as much as by grand strategy. Dupleix had also promised the Nawab that the French would hand Madras over to him, a promise he did not fulfill, which provoked Anwaruddin Khan.

The Nawab sent an army against the French. In November 1746, near the Adyar river and the St Thomé settlement, a small French force under Captain Paradis, composed of a few hundred Europeans and several hundred Indian soldiers trained by the French, met a much larger Carnatic army and defeated it. The engagement is often cited as proof of European military superiority, but the circumstances deserve analysis. The French had disciplined infantry, effective musket volleys, and a few guns, while the Nawab's forces were larger but poorly coordinated and attacked in difficult conditions at a river crossing. The result demonstrated the value of drilled infantry and sustained fire, yet it did not show that all Indian armies were inferior. Other Indian forces, including Maratha cavalry and the armies of Mysore, would remain formidable opponents for decades.

Elsewhere, the English held Fort St David at Cuddalore, to which many Madras officials, including the young clerk Robert Clive, escaped. French attacks on Fort St David failed, and in 1748 a British fleet under Admiral Boscawen besieged Pondicherry without success. The war thus ended inconclusively on the Coromandel Coast. By the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle in October 1748, Madras was returned to the English, in exchange for the French fortress of Louisbourg in North America, a settlement that treated Indian places as bargaining chips in a European peace.

The consequences were large. The war had shown that European companies could field effective sepoy forces. It had shown the Nawab's limited ability to restrain them. It also encouraged Dupleix to think that French power could be used to influence Indian politics. Within months, the death of the Nizam of Hyderabad would open opportunities for such influence.

The Adyar engagement should also be located in the larger diplomatic sequence. The Nawab of the Carnatic attempted to assert his authority over two European groups that had fought within his jurisdiction. The French ability to resist his intervention challenged his capacity to enforce neutrality, but the outcome did not remove the Nawab or make French commands binding throughout the region. Indian political leaders continued to negotiate with both companies, mobilize their own soldiers, and respond to competing claims. The battle's historical significance lies partly in its demonstration that disciplined infantry and artillery could affect negotiations out of proportion to their numbers, provided political and logistical circumstances allowed them to do so.

Section 61.18

The Second Carnatic War, 1749–1754: Dynastic Succession and Company Intervention

In 1748 Nizam-ul-Mulk Asaf Jah I, the founder of the Hyderabad state, died. His death triggered a succession struggle between his second son, Nasir Jang, who held power, and his grandson Muzaffar Jang, who claimed the succession under a will attributed to the old Nizam. In the Carnatic, a parallel dispute involved Chanda Sahib, a former claimant to the nawabship of Arcot who had been held prisoner by the Marathas since their capture of Trichinopoly in 1741 and who was released around 1748, and Anwaruddin Khan, the incumbent Nawab. These were dynastic and fiscal contests among Indian ruling elites, with their own causes and logic, and company intervention was grafted onto them.

Dupleix saw an opportunity. He allied with Muzaffar Jang and Chanda Sahib, providing French troops and financial support, in the hope of installing rulers friendly to French interests. In August 1749, at Ambur, the allied forces defeated and killed Anwaruddin Khan, and Chanda Sahib became Nawab of the Carnatic. Anwaruddin's son, Muhammad Ali Khan Walajah, escaped to Trichinopoly and sought English support. Meanwhile, Nasir Jang marched against the French and their allies and was killed in December 1750 in a conflict in which some of his own nobles defected. Muzaffar Jang was installed, but he was killed in early 1751, and the French general Charles de Bussy then installed another son of the old Nizam, Salabat Jang, who remained dependent on French military support. For a time, French influence in Hyderabad and the Carnatic seemed commanding.

The English Company, initially cautious, responded by supporting Muhammad Ali. A crucial moment came in 1751, when Robert Clive, a Company officer who had moved from the clerical to the military side, led a small force of Europeans and sepoys to seize Arcot, Chanda Sahib's capital, in an effort to draw off pressure on Trichinopoly. Clive and his garrison endured a siege of about seven weeks, from September to November 1751, and the successful defense raised English prestige. Allies played a part, including Maratha forces that arrived to relieve the siege. The affair is often presented as a feat of individual heroism, but it was a small action within a larger campaign whose outcome depended on supplies, allied cavalry, and political shifts.

The war then centered on Trichinopoly, where the English and French and their respective Indian allies fought a long and costly campaign. In 1752, Chanda Sahib, deserted and trapped, surrendered to the army of Tanjore and Mysore and was killed. The contest continued with changing combinations of allies, including the rulers of Tanjore and Mysore and Maratha leaders, who changed sides according to their own interests. Neither company won a decisive victory. Costs mounted, and the French directors, alarmed at expenses and disappointed in results, recalled Dupleix in 1754. His successor Charles Godeheu negotiated a provisional agreement with the English in January 1755.

The war demonstrated several points. European corporations could become participants in Indian dynastic conflicts, but they remained dependent on regional allies, financial resources, and political negotiation. French influence in Hyderabad did not end with Dupleix's recall, since Bussy remained at the Nizam's court and French forces continued to operate in the Deccan. And the English Company, by backing Muhammad Ali Walajah, acquired a client whose dependence on English support would prove a source of influence and, later, of financial entanglement. Chapter 62 will show that the English Company's later position in Bengal came about by a different route, in a different political setting.

The settlement of these succession struggles was not simply a contest between French and British military technology. A claimant had to establish a convincing dynastic or administrative title, secure troops and revenues, bargain with local elites, and persuade a European company that intervention would pay. Company officers in turn expected reimbursement for soldiers and ammunition, sometimes through assignments of revenue, customs privileges, or promises of land. As campaigns continued, those expectations could outstrip a claimant's ability to pay, leading to fresh dependence and political friction. The resulting obligations help explain how assistance in an Indian succession dispute could become a continuing corporate claim on a regional ruler's treasury.

Section 61.19

The Third Carnatic War, 1756–1763, and the Movement toward Territorial Power

The Seven Years' War, which began in 1756, renewed Anglo-French hostilities worldwide, and in India it brought a new element: substantial forces sent directly from Europe. The French government dispatched an army under Thomas Arthur, Comte de Lally, who arrived in 1758 with royal troops and instructions to expel the English from India. Lally captured Fort St David in 1758 and besieged Madras from December 1758 to February 1759, but a lack of funds, quarrels with French company officials, and the arrival of English naval reinforcements forced him to abandon the siege.

Naval and logistical advantages proved decisive. English squadrons, in clashes with the French fleet under the Comte d'Aché in 1758 and 1759, maintained control of the sea lanes, allowing the English to supply and reinforce Madras while the French struggled to do so. Credit and supply mattered as well. English settlements, backed by Bengal's resources after 1757, could draw on funds, while French finances were strained. In 1759 English forces took Masulipatnam from the French, weakening French position in the Northern Circars and its link with Hyderabad.

The decisive land engagement came at Wandiwash in January 1760, where the English under Sir Eyre Coote defeated Lally's army and captured Bussy, who had been recalled from Hyderabad to join Lally. Coote then besieged Pondicherry, which surrendered in January 1761 after a blockade and starvation. The English demolished Pondicherry's fortifications. The Treaty of Paris of 1763 restored French factories held before 1749 while requiring France to renounce certain subsequent acquisitions; Article XI expressly barred French fortifications and troops in the subah of Bengal. It recognized Muhammad Ali Khan as Nawab of the Carnatic and named Salabat Jang as subah of the Deccan, although Salabat Jang had already been displaced in practice by Nizam Ali Khan. This treaty formula should not be mistaken for a settled political reality across India. French military prospects in India were substantially weakened, though French commerce continued. French trade at Pondicherry, Chandernagore, and other stations persisted, and French adventurers served Indian rulers, including Mysore, in later decades. Lally himself was tried and executed in France in 1766, scapegoated for the defeat.

The larger transformation is more significant than the individual battles. By 1763 the English Company had a substantial military establishment, including European regiments and sepoy battalions, a network of Indian allies, a client Nawab in the Carnatic, and an ability to affect succession and territorial politics in southern India. Its fiscal demands on the Carnatic, financed by assignments of revenue from Muhammad Ali, began to tie the Company to the revenue systems of the region. Its command of the sea and logistics gave it an enduring advantage over French competitors. These were new political capacities, acquired through war and alliance.

It remains necessary to distinguish the outcome of the Carnatic Wars from the separate process by which the Company acquired Bengal's revenue and territorial authority. The Carnatic Wars made the Company a major military and political actor in southern India and eliminated its principal European rival there. Bengal, as Chapter 62 explains, involved a different set of circumstances: a succession to the nawabship, a conflict over trade privileges and fortification, internal conspiracy, and later the grant of the diwani. Linking the two as a single, planned imperial design would flatten the contingencies of each.

Article XI of the Treaty of Paris requires close reading. It directed Britain to restore to France the factories it had held in parts of India at the beginning of 1749, while France renounced its subsequent acquisitions on specified coasts. Its express ban on French fortifications and troops applied to the dominions of the subah of Bengal; it should not be generalized as a uniform prohibition on fortifying every restored French settlement. The same article named Muhammad Ali Khan as lawful nawab of the Carnatic and Salabat Jang as lawful subah of the Deccan. This last recognition was already politically complicated, since Salabat Jang had been displaced by Nizam Ali Khan. A European treaty could name regional rulers, but its clauses did not settle every Indian succession struggle on the ground.

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has traced how European companies moved from participation in established Indian Ocean commerce toward increasingly militarized political activity. The Portuguese Estado da Índia provided models of fortified enclaves, naval licensing, and customs revenue, while showing the limits of any attempt to command Asian trade. The Dutch VOC added permanent capital, a stock market, and a sprawling intra-Asian network, with Indian textiles at its center. The English and French companies, later and differently financed, built settlements at Madras, Bombay, Calcutta, and Pondicherry and developed relationships with Indian rulers, merchants, and producers.

Indian merchants, bankers, brokers, and manufacturers were essential to every stage. They supplied capital and credit, organized cloth procurement, mediated with rulers, and moved business among competing companies. Weavers, dyers, and painters produced the textiles on which the companies' profits rested, with skills and organization developed long before European arrival. Brokers such as Nayiniyappa and Ananda Ranga Pillai were political actors whose power could both support and threaten European administrations. Corporate organization mattered, but it operated within a world in which Indian agency was substantial.

Factories, farmans, and fortified settlements gave the companies footholds, but each rested on negotiated permission. A charter was not sovereignty, a farman was not conquest, and a fortified port was not a province. Armed commerce expanded what companies could do, but military intervention required money, allies, and favorable circumstances. The Carnatic Wars supplied those circumstances: the fragmentation of Hyderabad and Carnatic politics, the outbreak of European war, and the availability of drilled sepoy forces. The wars made the English Company a significant military and political actor in southern India and ended French military prospects there, without yet creating colonial government.

Chapter 62 turns to Bengal, where a different combination of circumstances, including conflict with the nawab, the events at Plassey and Buxar, and the diwani, would give the Company fiscal and territorial authority. Readers should carry forward from this chapter the understanding that this development was neither the necessary result of corporate organization nor simply the accident of battle, but the product of specific conditions in which a commercial corporation with new military capacities found openings in a politically divided region.

The comparative lesson is therefore institutional as well as military. European corporations assembled shipping, accounting, finance, information, and armed force, but none of those capacities supplied cloth or revenue without cooperation from South Asian actors. A factory's commercial significance should not be confused with an independent right to rule, and a successful intervention in a succession dispute should not be confused with long-term administrative control. These distinctions make the transition to Bengal clearer. The conquest examined in Chapter 62 was a particular political and fiscal transformation, not the preordained conclusion of every European trading voyage since 1600.

Anglo-French warfare in the Carnatic; client rulers dependent on company support; larger military establishments; revenue assignments to fund troops.

English military predominance in southern India; a model of intervention in succession politics; foundations for later expansion, examined in Chapters 62 to 64.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains the outcome. Corporate organization, European war, Indian politics, and finance each contributed, and different outcomes were possible at several points.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Trading Post to Armed Participant

What changed?

  • Companies moved from tenants to armed participants in dynastic politics.
  • Settlements grew from small enclaves into substantial port cities.
  • Sepoy forces drilled on European lines became a significant military instrument.

What remained continuous?

  • Dependence on Indian merchants, brokers, bankers, and weavers.
  • Reliance on textiles and bullion as the core of trade.
  • The need for local rulers' permissions and alliances.

Who experienced change most directly? Rulers in the Carnatic and Hyderabad, and residents of contested districts, felt the effects of warfare most directly. Weavers and village producers experienced greater continuity in their daily work, though many were affected by war, taxation, and disruption. Why the pattern matters: It shows that political transformation could occur atop continuing commercial and social structures.

Historical Debate

Was the Company's Movement from Commerce to Political Power Intentional, Inevitable, or Contingent?

The question: Did the companies' political role grow from deliberate ambition, from the logic of corporate and military organization, or from particular circumstances and decisions?

Interpretation A — Intentional or structural: Some historians stress policies of individuals such as Dupleix, who deliberately sought political influence, and argue that the need to protect investments and finance armies pushed companies toward territorial involvement. On this view, the logic of armed commerce tended toward political power.
Interpretation B — Contingent: Others emphasize that company directors repeatedly discouraged expansion, that interventions depended on opportunities created by Indian political fragmentation and European war, and that failures, such as the French defeat and English reverses, show that outcomes were uncertain. On this view, territorial power in Bengal and elsewhere depended on particular circumstances.

Evidence: Company correspondence, directors' instructions, Indian chronicles, diaries such as Ananda Ranga Pillai's, and military and fiscal records are used, with each type reflecting different perspectives.

Why it matters: If expansion was inevitable, later conquest appears as the working out of a structure; if contingent, historians must explain why opportunities arose and how choices mattered. Chapter 62 will examine Bengal with this question in mind.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has explained how European trading companies became participants in South Asian political and military competition. It began with the Indian Ocean commercial world that existed before and alongside the companies, a monsoon-governed system linking Gujarat, Malabar, the Coromandel Coast, Bengal, the Persian Gulf, the Red Sea, Southeast Asia, and East Africa, in which Indian and other Asian merchants played leading roles.

The Portuguese Estado da Índia, founded on Goa and a chain of fortresses, sought to control this trade through naval power, customs houses, and the cartaz licensing system. It never achieved a complete monopoly, but it left institutional models and a durable Luso-Asian world of private traders, mixed communities, missionaries, and interpreters that persisted after Portuguese maritime supremacy faded.

The Dutch East India Company, chartered in 1602, introduced permanent capital, tradable shares, and a sovereign-like delegation of powers. Its Asian headquarters at Batavia directed an intra-Asian trade in which Indian textiles from Coromandel and Bengal were exchanged for spices and other goods, and its Indian settlements at Pulicat, Nagapattinam, Chinsurah, and Cochin operated by permission and negotiation.

The English Company, chartered on 31 December 1600, initially sought the spice trade but turned to Indian textiles after Dutch exclusion. It obtained a factory at Surat in 1613 and built Madras, Bombay, and Calcutta into fortified bases. The French Company, founded by Colbert in 1664, built Pondicherry with the help of Tamil merchants and weavers. Each company's strategy reflected its capital, its relations with its home state, and its dependence on Asian trade.

Division III showed that Indian merchants, bankers, brokers, and manufacturers were essential partners. Gujarati, Tamil, Telugu, Bengali, and Armenian merchants financed trade through hundis and local credit, and dubashes such as Nayiniyappa and Ananda Ranga Pillai mediated between companies and Indian society. Weavers, dyers, and painters produced textiles through systems of advances and merchant contracts that were largely negotiated before the companies' political power grew.

Companies financed purchases largely through imported silver, supplemented by local credit and intra-Asian trade. Historians debate whether companies were corporate innovators or dependents on Asian systems; the evidence supports an interpretation in which corporate institutions provided real advantages that were limited by dependence on Indian partners and political permission.

Factories, farmans, and fortified settlements gave companies footholds. Charters granted in Europe did not confer authority in India, and farmans such as those of 1717 were privileges granted by rulers. Fortified settlements were small, cosmopolitan enclaves whose governance involved negotiation among company officials, Indian elites, missionaries, and mixed populations, and whose social order included inequality, dependent labor, and the export of enslaved people.

Armed commerce developed from the need to protect trade. Naval force, garrisons, and sepoy forces gave companies new capacities, but military intervention required money, allies, and favorable circumstances. The English reverse of 1686 and the Dutch defeat at Colachel in 1741 show the limits of company force.

The Carnatic Wars of 1746 to 1763 fused European war with the dynastic politics of Hyderabad and the Carnatic. They showed the value of drilled sepoys, drew the companies into succession disputes, and ended French military prospects without ending French commerce. The English Company emerged as a major military and political actor in southern India. Yet this outcome was separate from, and did not predetermine, the acquisition of Bengal examined in Chapter 62.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it corrects a familiar story. The companies' rise is often told as the story of European initiative acting on a passive India. The evidence shows instead a system in which Indian merchants, bankers, brokers, weavers, and rulers held the keys to access, credit, and production, and in which European corporations negotiated their way into an existing world.

It also reveals how political change can emerge from circumstances rather than design. Commercial corporations acquired military capacities for defensive and competitive reasons, and a particular conjunction of European war and Indian succession disputes in the 1740s and 1750s gave those capacities political consequence. The same capacities could fail elsewhere, as at Colachel and in the Mughal conflict of 1686.

Several continuities extend beyond the chapter: the importance of Indian intermediaries and financiers, the central place of textiles, the tension between corporate privilege and local authority, and the use of revenue assignments to fund armies. The enclaves of Madras, Bombay, Calcutta, and Pondicherry became bases for later developments, as did the sepoy model.

Within the six-volume sequence, this chapter closes Part XI by connecting the eighteenth-century successor states of Chapters 57 to 60 with the colonial conquest that begins in Part XII. Chapter 62 now turns to Bengal, where the Company's commercial privileges, a disputed succession, and a new fiscal order would produce a transformation of a different kind.
