Part XI — The Eighteenth-Century Transformation

Bengal, Awadh, Hyderabad, and the Successor States

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

Through much of the eighteenth century, silver rupees struck at Murshidabad and other regional mints carried the name of a Mughal emperor and a year of his reign. The emperor in question was often hundreds of miles away, frequently a prisoner of his own ministers, and sometimes a figure who could not pay his own household. The coins nevertheless circulated, were accepted by bankers and cultivators, and announced that the authority behind them still flowed from Delhi. Those small objects hold the central puzzle of this chapter: how could regimes that were in practice governing, taxing, and fighting for themselves go on presenting themselves as servants of an empire that no longer governed them?

Chapter 57 traced the weakening of the Mughal center after 1707. Chapter 58 followed the Maratha confederacy, whose rise rested on a political order built outside Mughal officeholding, and Chapter 59 examined Sikh, Mysorean, Travancorean, Jat, and Rohilla state-building. This chapter turns to a different route to regional power. In Bengal, Awadh, and the Deccan, the new rulers were not outsiders or insurgents who defeated the empire's governors. They were the empire's governors, revenue officers, and military commanders. Their power grew from within Mughal provincial institutions, and it was exercised in Mughal language, through Mughal offices, and under Mughal titles.

The three cases examined here are Bengal under Murshid Quli Khan and his successors, Awadh under Saadat Khan Burhan-ul-Mulk, Safdar Jang, and Shuja-ud-Daula, and the Deccan under Nizam-ul-Mulk Asaf Jah and the dynasty of the Nizams. They are not identical. Bengal was a delta province whose government rested on a close alliance among revenue officials, great zamindars, and a powerful banking house. Awadh was a central Gangetic province where governors had to bargain constantly with entrenched landholding lineages, and where control of provincial resources became a lever for influence in Delhi itself. Hyderabad grew out of the Mughal conquest of the Deccan sultanates and developed in a politically crowded environment dominated by competition with the Marathas. Treating the three together makes it possible to see what they shared and where they diverged.

Their shared inheritance was an administrative system designed to prevent exactly what eventually happened. The Mughal provincial structure divided authority between a governor responsible for military and political order and a diwan responsible for revenue, and it rotated officials through assignments. The system worked as long as the court could appoint, transfer, and remove its officers. When it could no longer do so reliably, officials who had accumulated local alliances, loyal troops, and control of the treasury found that they could stay in place, name their own deputies, and eventually secure the succession of a son, a son-in-law, or a nephew. Autonomy therefore arrived less through proclamations of independence than through repeated acts of confirmation that the court was in no position to refuse.

A second theme concerns money. A provincial regime needed troops, and troops needed regular pay. It needed reliable revenue, and revenue depended on the cooperation of zamindars, local record-keepers, and market-town elites. It also needed credit. The great banking houses of the eighteenth century, above all the Jagat Seths of Bengal, transferred revenue across hundreds of miles by bills of exchange, advanced money against anticipated collections, and stood behind rulers who were short of cash. The history of these successor states is therefore a history of fiscal and financial coalitions as much as of dynasties and battles, and this chapter gives that dimension sustained attention.

A third theme is the human cost and uneven benefit of state-building. A stronger provincial treasury was not the same thing as a better life for the people who paid into it. Revenue demands, military requisitions, warfare, and the extra levies that grew up alongside the formal assessment weighed heavily on cultivators in some regions and periods, while court patronage, construction, and expanded commercial opportunity benefited particular groups of artisans, merchants, soldiers, scholars, and service families. Historians still argue about whether regional state-building strengthened or weakened the economies of eighteenth-century South Asia, and the chapter presents that argument rather than resolving it by assertion.

The evidence for these questions is unusually rich and unusually partisan. Persian chronicles written by officials and their dependents, such as the Muntakhab al-Lubab, the Riyaz-us-Salatin, and the Siyar al-Muta'akhkhirin, describe events from particular vantage points and, in two of the three cases, decades after the events they narrate and under altered political conditions. Imperial and provincial documents, revenue records, coins, the correspondence and consultations of European companies, and the architecture and literature of regional courts supplement them. Students should read each source asking who produced it, for whom, and from what position.

The chapter has deliberate limits. It does not retell the weakening of Delhi, which belongs to Chapter 57, and it does not narrate the East India Company's conquest of Bengal, which belongs to Chapter 62, or the Carnatic Wars and European commercial rivalry, which belong to Chapters 61 and 64. Its task is to explain how Mughal provincial government became regional government without ceasing to look Mughal, and what that transformation meant for the political, economic, social, and cultural life of three of the largest regions of South Asia. It closes by preparing the transition to Chapter 61, which examines the European companies as participants in a commercial and political world that these regional states had helped to shape.

Historical Context

By 1700 the Mughal Empire governed one of the largest territorial systems in the early modern world. Aurangzeb's conquest of Bijapur (1686) and Golconda (1687) extended direct Mughal administration across much of the Deccan, and the empire's provinces, or subas, stretched from Kabul to Bengal and from Kashmir to the Carnatic. Chapter 54 examined the strains of that expansion, and Chapter 55 reconstructed the agrarian and commercial economy that supported it. This chapter begins at a moment when the machinery of imperial government was still intact on paper and still capable of making and unmaking officials, but when its capacity to coordinate distant provinces was beginning to weaken.

Chapter 57 explained why. The succession wars after Aurangzeb's death in 1707, factional struggles at court, pressure on the assignment system that paid the nobility, the invasions of Nadir Shah in 1739 and Ahmad Shah Durrani after 1748, and the pressure of Maratha power all reduced the court's ability to command. This chapter takes that background as given. Its question is not why Delhi weakened but what happened in the provinces when governors, diwans, and commanders had to make political choices in a more uncertain environment. Their choices differed, and the differences reflect geography, resources, and local society as much as individual ambition.

Three features of the Mughal inheritance matter especially. First, provincial authority was divided. The governor commanded troops and kept order, while the diwan, appointed from the imperial center, supervised revenue and accounts, so that each office could in principle check the other. Second, the system relied heavily on hereditary local intermediaries. Zamindars, who held rights to collect revenue and exercised local authority, and qanungos, who kept the records of local assessment, held knowledge and influence that no governor could replace. Third, the empire's finances were already deeply entangled with merchants and bankers, who remitted revenue between provinces and the court and extended credit to officials. Each of these features would become a resource for provincial rulers.

The chapter's regional settings also differ in ways that readers should keep in view. Bengal, a deltaic province crossed by the Ganges, Brahmaputra, and their distributaries, combined rice agriculture, silk and cotton textile production, river transport, and links to overseas markets. Awadh, in the central Gangetic plain, was a grain-producing region dense with warrior-landholding lineages and with towns that connected Delhi to the east. The Deccan, much larger and more varied, included upland and river plains, former sultanate territories whose elites had Telugu, Marathi, Kannada, and Persianate affiliations, and a long frontier with Maratha power. These contrasts explain much of what follows, and the two Geography Studies in this chapter ask students to examine them on the map.

Section 60.1

The Mughal Provincial System: Subadars, Diwans, and Imperial Authority

To understand the successor states, one must first understand the machinery they inherited. Under the Mughals the empire was divided into provinces called subas, each headed by a subadar, often called the nazim in the context of Bengal and later usage. The subadar commanded the provincial military forces, maintained order, suppressed rebellion, and represented the emperor's political authority. He was usually a high-ranking mansabdar, a member of the imperial nobility holding a numerical rank that determined his salary and his obligation to maintain troops. He was appointed by imperial order and, in principle, could be transferred or dismissed by the same means.

Beside him stood the provincial diwan, who supervised revenue collection, accounts, and expenditure. The diwan was appointed from the imperial center and answered to the emperor's chief revenue minister rather than to the governor. This division was deliberate. A governor with troops but no control over the treasury could not easily pay an army of his own, and a diwan with accounts but no soldiers could not impose his will on the province. Other officers reinforced the checks: the provincial bakhshi, who handled military pay and musters, the waqia-navis or news-writer, whose reports kept the court informed, and the sadr, who oversaw religious and charitable grants. Below the province lay districts, or sarkars, and smaller parganas, where a faujdar exercised military and police authority over a district and a kotwal kept order in towns.

Government on the ground depended on local figures who were not imperial appointees. The qanungo was a hereditary record-keeper at the pargana level, whose registers of local assessments, customary rates, and landholding claims formed the working memory of revenue administration. The chaudhuri and village headmen assisted collection. Above all, zamindars, hereditary holders of rights to collect revenue and of varying degrees of local political authority, ranged from petty village-level claimants to chiefs of extensive territories with their own fortified bases and armed followers. Mughal governance had always been an exercise in bargaining with such figures, and the empire's strength had rested on the court's ability to confirm, reward, or discipline them.

Soldiers and officials were paid largely through the jagir system. A mansabdar received the right to collect the revenue of designated lands, whose estimated yield, the jama, was meant to match his salary. Lands whose revenue went directly to the imperial treasury were called khalisa. Because jagirs were held at imperial pleasure and were regularly reassigned, a jagirdar had limited incentive to invest in a locality, and the gap between the estimated revenue of an assignment and what it actually yielded could be large. As Chapter 57 explained, pressure on this system was one of the engines of political instability. For this chapter, the significance is different: officials who could secure revenue in a stable, directly controlled territory possessed something that jagirdars in a shifting assignment system did not.

Finally, provincial government rested on a third set of partners, the merchants and bankers who made revenue mobile. Revenue collected in rupees in one province had to be remitted to the imperial treasury or to the jagirdars' households elsewhere, and officials needed credit to bridge the interval between expenditure and collection. Bankers, or sarrafs, provided bills of exchange, advances, and money-changing, and in return received access to officials, mints, and protection. The relationship was reciprocal and, as §60.14 shows, became more important as provincial regimes grew more self-reliant.

How, then, could a governor remain an imperial officer while becoming a regional sovereign? The answer lay in several small, cumulative shifts rather than in a single act of defiance. A governor who also held the diwan's office, or whose diwan was his own nominee, removed the check between the two. A governor who appointed his own deputies, faujdars, and revenue officers created a network of dependents loyal to him rather than to the court. A governor who retained surplus revenue and reduced his remittances to Delhi accumulated a military and financial resource that the emperor could not easily seize. And a governor who could recommend or install his successor, and then obtain imperial confirmation, converted a revocable appointment into something dynastic. Imperial confirmation remained valuable to him: it gave his claims the sanction of the sovereign, reassured zamindars and bankers, and made rivals hesitate. The court, for its part, often found it easier to confirm a fait accompli than to resist it.

This pattern distinguishes the successor states from the Marathas, Sikhs, and Mysore (Chapters 58–59). Those powers built authority from outside the Mughal service system or in opposition to it. The governors of Bengal, Awadh, and Hyderabad grew within it, and their institutions bore the marks of that origin even as they diverged from the empire's practice.

Historical Vocabulary
Suba, Nazim, and Diwan: Who Held Which Authority?

A suba was a Mughal province. Its chief military and political officer was the subadar, also called nazim in Bengal, where the governor's office was known as the nizamat. The diwan headed revenue administration and held a separate imperial appointment, the diwani. The distinction between nizamat and diwani is essential to this chapter, because the rise of regional rulers often depended on bringing the two functions under one hand or under hands that cooperated. Students should not confuse these Mughal offices with the later Company usage of the word diwani, examined in Chapter 62, which referred to the right to collect Bengal's revenue and was granted to the Company by the emperor in 1765.

The modern English word governor suggests an official subordinate to a central government in a stable chain of command. The Mughal subadar was formally in such a chain, but his real position depended on soldiers, local alliances, and credit. That is why historians describe the eighteenth-century successor regimes as outgrowths of provincial offices rather than as rebellions against them.

Section 60.2

Bengal as a Mughal Province: Geography, Agriculture, and Commercial Wealth

Bengal, known to the Mughals as the suba Bangalah, was one of the empire's richest provinces. Its core was the great delta formed by the Ganges, the Brahmaputra, and their many distributaries, a landscape of shifting channels, seasonal flooding, and fertile alluvium. The rivers were more than a physical setting. They were the province's highways, carrying grain, cloth, salt, and soldiers, and they shaped settlement, military campaigns, and the location of markets. The delta's silting and channel changes meant that capitals and ports could rise or decline with the course of a river. Hugli, on the western distributary of the Ganges, owed its commercial position to this pattern, and the eastward drift of the major river system over the preceding centuries had already moved the center of gravity of agriculture and trade.

The agricultural base was rice. The delta's monsoon rains and floods supported multiple rice crops in many districts, and the surplus fed not only Bengal's cities and armies but also markets beyond the province, including the Coromandel Coast and overseas destinations. Sugarcane, oilseeds, mulberry for silk, cotton, and betel supported specialized local economies, and saltpetre and opium from Bihar, administered with Bengal, entered the commercial export trade. The province's eastern districts, with their moist, forested lowlands, were still undergoing agrarian expansion in this period, as cultivators and Muslim and Hindu religious figures extended settlement into new land, a process often supported by local revenue-free grants.

Bengal's manufacturing was equally celebrated. Dhaka and the surrounding region produced fine cotton textiles, including the muslins that were among the most valued fabrics of the age. Kasimbazar, near Murshidabad, was the center of a major raw-silk production and trade network, and cotton and silk weavers worked in numerous other towns and villages. Much textile production was organized through village-based weavers who received advances from merchants, brokers, and agents, a system in which Indian merchants and their agents had long played the leading part. Patna, upriver in Bihar, was a commercial hub for saltpetre, opium, and grain, and a gateway between Bengal and the upper Ganges plain.

The province's trading links ran in several directions. Overseas, the Bay of Bengal connected Bengal to Southeast Asia, the Coromandel Coast, and Sri Lanka, while the Arabian Sea routes joined it, through intermediate markets, to the Persian Gulf and Red Sea. By land and river, it was linked to Delhi, Awadh, and the Deccan. Hugli had been the principal port of Mughal Bengal since the seventeenth century, with European companies maintaining factories and trading posts there and at Kasimbazar and Dhaka. These companies, English, Dutch, and French, were significant buyers of Bengal's textiles and silk, but they operated within a commercial landscape that already included powerful Indian, Armenian, and other merchant communities, and the companies' role is best seen as that of participants in a long-established system. Their conflicts with provincial officials over customs and privileges are one thread in the chapter, but they remain in the background here.

These resources explain why Bengal could support a powerful provincial government. A well-governed Bengal produced large and relatively predictable revenue. Its commerce generated customs duties, mint profits, and opportunities for banking. Its dense river network made it difficult for any force to cross without boats, which favored a ruler who could control waterways, and its distance from Delhi, the better part of a month's travel for administrators and months for armies, gave its governors a measure of practical freedom. The province also had a rich pool of literate administrative specialists, notably Bengali Hindu scribal and Brahmin families who staffed revenue offices, and a class of substantial zamindars. All of this would be mobilized in the era of Murshid Quli Khan.

Section 60.3

Murshid Quli Khan and the Consolidation of Bengal, c. 1700–1727

Murshid Quli Khan is the central figure in the early history of autonomous Bengal, and his career illustrates how provincial power could grow out of revenue officeholding. Later Persian histories say he was born into a Hindu family in the Deccan, was purchased as a boy by a Persian merchant, Haji Shafi Isfahani, who raised him in the Islamic faith and in Persian learning, and entered Mughal service as a competent revenue administrator. These accounts were written long after his lifetime and should be treated with caution in detail, but they agree that his reputation rested on administrative ability rather than high birth. He served in the Deccan under Aurangzeb, who appointed him diwan of Bengal around 1700–1701 with the title Kartalab Khan; he received the title Murshid Quli Khan in December 1702. Published studies differ by approximately a year over the initial diwanship.

His appointment immediately created tension with the province's governor, Prince Azim-us-Shan, the grandson of Aurangzeb, whose resources and ambitions he threatened. The administrative response was geographic: the diwan moved his revenue establishment from Dhaka to Makhsusabad, an existing town subsequently renamed Murshidabad, in stages around 1703–1704. The move put the revenue administration at a distance from the governor and near the silk and textile centers of Kasimbazar and the western river network, and it gave the diwan a base of his own. The precise chronology of the transfer and the gradual conversion of the diwani center into the provincial capital should not be compressed. Murshidabad became the seat of the nizamat as well only as Murshid Quli's authority consolidated, and its full character as a capital emerged over the following decades.

After Aurangzeb's death, Murshid Quli Khan retained the confidence of successive emperors by delivering revenue. He was confirmed in Bengal, was joined to Odisha's administration, and in 1713 became deputy governor under Farrukhsiyar, before receiving the full governorship of Bengal in 1717. His interim service as diwan, and his responsibilities in Odisha, had followed several changes of appointment and temporary absences from Bengal after 1707. Chronicles and modern accounts differ in the exact formulation of these appointments, but the essential point is clear. By the end of the 1710s, one man held the leading revenue office and the governor's authority, together with the ability to appoint subordinates. The checks on which the Mughal provincial system depended were gone, and the center accepted this because it received remittances and because it lacked the capacity to impose an alternative.

Murshid Quli's government is best understood through its fiscal measures. He is credited with a major reassessment of Bengal's revenue, the jama kamil tumari of 1722, which reorganized the province into larger revenue divisions, or chaklas, and set a new schedule of assessment. He converted much jagir land into khalisa and, according to the tradition, moved jagirdars' assignments out of Bengal and into Odisha, which reduced the number of competing assignment-holders in the province and increased the revenue under his direct control. Revenue collection was entrusted to zamindars and to revenue contractors, and he enforced payment with a severity that later chronicles remember and probably exaggerate. Some established zamindars were displaced for default while others, including the great zamindars of Rajshahi and Nadia, were confirmed, enlarged, or restructured. He was also strict in preventing officials from enriching themselves at the treasury's expense, though his own reputation for justice coexisted with a reputation for harsh treatment of those who failed to pay.

Hindu revenue officials played a central role. Families of Bengali Kayastha and Brahmin background supplied the secretaries, accountants, and collectors of the system, and the importance of this group, together with the zamindars and bankers who allied with it, is a main theme of Philip B. Calkins's influential study of the formation of a regionally oriented ruling group in Bengal. His argument is that the early nawabs forged a coalition of Muslim administrators, Hindu officials, zamindars, and financiers whose interests were increasingly tied to Bengal rather than to Delhi. Not every historian accepts every element of this account, but it remains a foundational interpretation, and §60.19 returns to it.

The financial dimension matters just as much. Bankers, above all the house of Manikchand and his successors, the later Jagat Seths, became closely associated with the revenue system, remittances to Delhi, and the provincial mint at Murshidabad. Murshid Quli Khan's regime depended on their ability to move large sums, and their prosperity depended on his protection. This association, examined in §60.14, was one of the strongest buttresses of the provincial state. Murshid Quli's relations with the Mughal court were maintained through annual remittances and gifts, and he cultivated imperial recognition by observing the forms of loyalty. When he died on 30 June 1727, Bengal was a province that sent revenue to Delhi and also had its own court, army, treasury, and administrative class, all of which would outlast any particular emperor's wishes.

Section 60.4

Bengal after Murshid Quli Khan: Shuja-ud-Din, Alivardi Khan, and Provincial Stability

Murshid Quli Khan's death exposed the question every autonomous governor faced: how would authority pass? He had no surviving son in the line of succession, and he is reported to have favored his grandson Sarfaraz Khan, the son of his daughter and his son-in-law Shuja-ud-Din Muhammad Khan, who governed Odisha. Shuja-ud-Din moved quickly, and he took possession of the government at Murshidabad, was recognized by the emperor Muhammad Shah, and ruled from 1727 until 1739. The sequence shows both the family's weight in the new order and the continued role of imperial confirmation, which turned a family seizure of power into a regular appointment. His reign brought Bihar under the Bengal governor's authority around 1733, which enlarged the territorial base of the nawabi, and he appointed as his deputy at Patna Mirza Muhammad, later Alivardi Khan.

Shuja-ud-Din's reign was in general a period of consolidation. The dominant political alliance among officials, zamindars, and the banking house of the Seths continued. Historians have traced a growth during these decades in additional levies, abwabs, on top of the formal assessment, a development that raised total demands on the countryside without requiring a formal reassessment. His son Sarfaraz Khan succeeded him in 1739, but his brief rule was undermined by factional tensions among courtiers, officials, and financiers. Alivardi Khan, the governor of Bihar, had built a military and political base there, and a faction at the court including the Jagat Seths reportedly favored a change. He marched on Murshidabad, defeated and killed Sarfaraz Khan at Giria in April 1740, and secured recognition from Delhi, which came with the payment of a substantial sum.

Alivardi Khan's accession shows how provincial legitimacy operated by the 1740s. It rested on military success and the support of zamindars, officials, and financiers, and it was then regularized through imperial confirmation. The emperor, who had suffered the shock of Nadir Shah's invasion the previous year, was in no position to question the outcome. That a successful usurper could present himself as a lawful imperial appointee within months is one of the clearest illustrations of the way Mughal forms outlived Mughal control.

Alivardi's reign, from 1740 to 1756, was dominated by external pressure. Between 1742 and 1751, Maratha forces from the Nagpur Bhonsle state, with the support of other Maratha leaders, repeatedly raided Bengal, Bihar, and Odisha, demanding chauth, a levy of one-quarter of the revenue, as the price of peace. The invasions, discussed from the Maratha side in Chapter 58, caused serious disruption. Cavalry raids devastated parts of western Bengal, affected the cloth and silk producers of the region, and caused refugees to move eastward and toward Calcutta. The Company's construction of the Maratha Ditch around Calcutta during this period is one of the recognized signs of the fear they created. Alivardi's response combined fighting, negotiation, and the mobilization of resources, and he was a capable commander who defeated Maratha forces on several occasions, but he could not secure the western districts and Odisha permanently.

The costs were considerable. Continuous military mobilization required money and manpower, and the nawab's army, which included Afghan and other soldiers as well as the forces of zamindars, was expensive to maintain. In 1748 a mutiny of Afghan commanders in Bihar added to his difficulties. Rural disruption in the areas most exposed to raiding reduced revenue and the output of cloth and silk, and the government's need for funds sharpened the pressure on zamindars and bankers. The peace of 1751 granted the Marathas control over parts of Odisha and an annual payment, and left Alivardi the nominal governor of Odisha. The agreement shows a provincial ruler negotiating territorial and fiscal concessions to a neighboring power, in contrast to the imperial pattern in which the emperor would have been the negotiator.

Alivardi Khan's court also illustrates how family and household became part of the political structure. He had no son, and he arranged the succession through his daughters' families, so that his grandson Siraj ud-Daulah, the son of his youngest daughter Amina Begum, was designated heir. His eldest daughter, Ghasiti Begum, was a wealthy and politically significant figure with her own household and resources, as §60.18 discusses. When Alivardi died in April 1756 in advanced age, Siraj ud-Daulah succeeded him. At that moment Bengal was a regional state in nearly every functional sense, but one whose leader faced rivalries within the family, the court, and the financial elite, and growing friction with the English East India Company over trade privileges and fortifications. The confrontation that followed, including Plassey, the roles of Mir Jafar and Mir Qasim, Buxar, and the Diwani, belongs to Chapter 62 and is deliberately not narrated here.

Division II · Sections 60.5–60.8Awadh and the Rise of the Nawab-Wazirs
Section 60.5

Awadh: Geography, Provincial Society, and Mughal Administrative Inheritance

Awadh occupied the central Gangetic plain between the Ganges and the Ghaghra, a region of fertile alluvial soil, extensive grain cultivation, and numerous rivers. In the Mughal period the suba of Awadh was organized into districts, among them Awadh, Lucknow, Khairabad, Bahraich, and Gorakhpur, and its boundaries shifted over time. Wheat, barley, rice, millets, sugarcane, indigo, and cotton were grown, and the province's surplus made it an important supplier of grain to the capital region and to markets further east. Awadh stood on routes that connected Delhi and Agra with Allahabad, Banaras, and Bengal, and its towns, including Lucknow, Faizabad, Gorakhpur, Khairabad, and a number of weaving and market centers, linked agricultural production to artisan manufacture and long-distance trade.

What most distinguished Awadh from Bengal was its social structure. The province was densely populated with landholding lineages, some of long standing and many with strong martial traditions. Rajput clans, Muslim shaikhzadas and other established families, Bhumihars, and various other groups held zamindari rights that included control of fortified villages and armed retainers. These landholders paid revenue when they judged the government strong enough to compel it and withheld it when they thought they could, and a governor's first task was to establish a working relationship with them. The strength of local zamindars was therefore the central fact of Awadh's provincial politics. Historians such as Muzaffar Alam have emphasized that the stability of the Mughal order in the Gangetic plain depended on the ability of governors to negotiate this relationship, not merely to coerce it.

Mughal administration in Awadh followed the general model examined in §60.1. There were governors, diwans, faujdars, and qanungos, and a large portion of the province's revenue was assigned in jagirs to mansabdars, many of whom did not reside there. The province also held strategic importance. It lay on the route between Delhi and the east, near the great Ganges crossings, and it could supply troops. Its horsemen and infantry were recruited among local martial communities, including Afghans and Rajputs, as well as Hindu ascetic militias. It linked Delhi's political world to Bihar and Bengal, and a governor who controlled it could influence the movement of revenue, goods, and soldiers across northern India.

That meant that Awadh mattered long before nawabi rule emerged. For a governor with ambition, the province offered a large revenue base, a recruiting ground, and a location from which to affect the court. For the court, it offered a stable region to entrust to a trusted noble. The difficulty, as the next sections show, was that the qualities that made a governor effective in managing local landholders also tended to make him less dependent on Delhi. The very practice of accommodation, by which he confirmed some zamindars, subdued others, and rewarded loyal followers, built a local following.

The distribution of power within Awadh also shaped its relationship with its neighbors. The province's eastern districts, around Banaras, Jaunpur, and Ghazipur, lay on the main routes toward Bihar and Bengal and were held by local chiefs and revenue-holders whose loyalty was valuable to anyone who wished to control the corridor. To the north, the forested and marshy country near Gorakhpur and the Himalayan foothills sheltered fortified landholders who were hard to subdue by cavalry. To the west, the Doab and the Rohilla and Bangash Afghan settlements around Bareilly and Farrukhabad formed a competing political zone, so that Awadh's governors faced rivals on more than one front. These local geographies, rather than the formal boundary of the suba on paper, determined whom a governor had to win over and whom he had to fight.

Awadh's towns and markets also mattered to the strength of the province's rulers. Qasbas, small market towns that were often the seats of local Muslim landholding and scholarly families, connected the countryside to the wider economy and supplied soldiers, scribes, and religious scholars. Their families were an important reservoir of administrative and military talent on which governors could draw. A successful governor therefore needed to cultivate not only the great zamindars but also these town-based elites, whose support gave his government its social depth.

Section 60.6

Saadat Khan Burhan-ul-Mulk and the Foundation of Nawabi Authority, 1722–1739

Saadat Khan, born Mir Muhammad Amin, came from a Persian family from Nishapur in Khurasan, and rose in the Mughal nobility during the early eighteenth century, assisted by the factional alignments of the post-Aurangzeb era. In 1722 the emperor Muhammad Shah appointed him governor of Awadh with the title Burhan-ul-Mulk. His mission was to restore order in a province where zamindari resistance and a decline in imperial control had weakened revenue collection. The family was Shia in background, a point of some importance for the later cultural history of Awadh (§60.17), though the political history of the regime cannot be reduced to sectarian identity.

Saadat Khan combined force and negotiation. He campaigned against rebellious zamindars and local power-holders, including powerful landholding families in the region around Lucknow, whom he displaced or subdued, and he fortified his control of key towns. But he did not simply destroy local elites. Where it served him, he confirmed zamindars who accepted his authority, settled their revenue obligations, and incorporated them into his political following, and he recruited local Hindu and Muslim families into administration. Hindu officials from scribal and mercantile backgrounds, many drawn from the Kayastha and Khatri communities, served in revenue and secretarial positions. This reliance on local administrative specialists parallels the Bengal pattern, though the political weight of the zamindars was greater and the process of negotiation more persistent.

His government reorganized revenue arrangements, increasing the regularity of collections and the reliability of assessment where he could, and he built a military force around his personal followers and household troops, often recruited from Persian, Turani, and Indian-born backgrounds, as well as local soldiers. He also expanded his territorial reach by gaining responsibility for neighboring areas such as Banaras and Jaunpur, which he administered through an intermediary revenue arrangement with local landholders. Historians have noted this as an instance of how a governor could entrench a regional authority by combining family, household troops, and agreements with intermediaries.

The result was a political household, a group of kin, servants, soldiers, and officials tied to Saadat Khan personally, that could continue to exercise authority beyond one imperial appointment. His nephew and son-in-law Safdar Jang became his principal lieutenant and heir. The structure was dynastic in effect even though Saadat Khan remained a Mughal noble who held an imperial appointment. He maintained his role at the court, obtained high offices, and participated in imperial politics, which gave him access to the patronage that sustained his following at home.

Saadat Khan's involvement in imperial affairs came to a crisis in 1739. In that year, Nadir Shah, the ruler of Iran, invaded northern India, defeated the Mughal army at Karnal in February, and entered Delhi. Saadat Khan was present at Karnal and was involved in the negotiations that followed, in which he and other nobles are variously described as having tried to limit the damage. Contemporary and later accounts diverge on his actions and on the circumstances of his death, which occurred shortly afterward. Traditions that he took his own life or was poisoned are reported, but the evidence is difficult to evaluate, and this chapter does not rely on them. What is clear is that the catastrophe of Nadir Shah's invasion, which Chapter 57 examines, further reduced Delhi's ability to direct its provinces, and that the succession in Awadh was not disrupted by it. Safdar Jang took over the province, in effect continuing his uncle's regime and receiving imperial recognition. In less than two decades, Awadh had passed from a province entrusted to an official to a territory governed by a family.

Section 60.7

Safdar Jang, the Imperial Wazirate, and Awadh's Regional Power, 1739–1754

Safdar Jang, whose name was Mirza Muqim, succeeded Saadat Khan as governor of Awadh in 1739 and added the governorship of Allahabad. He was the nephew and son-in-law of his predecessor, and his succession shows how the family had become central to the structure of provincial rule. Early in his tenure he maintained the political approach of his uncle, balancing military action with accommodation of local landholders, and enlarging his household and army. He proved more ambitious in imperial affairs. After the death of the emperor Muhammad Shah in 1748, and the accession of Ahmad Shah Bahadur, Safdar Jang was appointed wazir, the empire's chief minister, an office that gave him, in principle, authority over the imperial administration and the management of the court.

The appointment raises one of this chapter's central questions: how did control of a provincial revenue base strengthen an officeholder's position at the imperial court? The answer is that the court was dependent on the revenue and military resources that only a handful of governors could supply. A wazir who could draw on the income of Awadh and the soldiers it supported was more powerful than a court noble reliant on assignments of shifting and unreliable value. His province gave him funds to pay troops and patronage to attract allies, and these in turn strengthened his hand in the factional contests of Delhi. The relationship, however, ran in both directions. The wazirate made Safdar Jang an imperial officer with imperial obligations, and the demands of court politics diverted his attention and resources away from Awadh.

Those demands were intense. The Delhi court of the late 1740s and early 1750s was torn by rivalries among Turani, Irani, and Indian Muslim nobles, and by the pressures of the Durrani invasions and Maratha and Jat activity in the Delhi region. Safdar Jang engaged the Marathas and Jats as allies and fought with regional powers who threatened his position. His most significant regional conflict was with the Bangash Afghans of Farrukhabad, led by Ahmad Khan Bangash, in 1750 and 1751. In the first stage of the conflict his deputy, the Hindu administrator Nawal Rai, was killed in battle at Khudaganj. Safdar Jang obtained the help of Maratha cavalry under Malhar Rao Holkar and the Jat leader Suraj Mal to turn the situation around, a reminder that even Mughal officials relied on regional military networks, including those that would later fight one another at Panipat. The wazir's reliance on these allies, and on the costs of their service, strained his finances.

The tension between Safdar Jang's regional interests and the obligations of the wazirate eventually proved decisive. At Delhi he faced the opposition of court factions, among them the faction around Intizam-ud-Daula and the rising commander Imad-ul-Mulk, who resented the wazir's dominance. In 1753 conflict erupted, with fighting in and around Delhi, and Safdar Jang was dismissed from the wazirate and left the capital for Awadh. He died in October 1754. His career shows the limits as well as the possibilities of the strategy of combining provincial and imperial power. A regional base helped him gain office, but sustaining the wazirate required constant military and financial investment at the center, and the very rivalries that made him necessary also made him vulnerable.

For Awadh itself, the years of Safdar Jang's governance were a time of enlargement and development. He extended the province's influence and strengthened its military establishment, and he showed that the Awadh ruling family could exercise real weight in imperial politics. His residence and building at Faizabad, which grew in importance as a dynastic center, began the process by which that town acquired political and cultural prominence. His tomb in Delhi, built after his death, stands as a monument to his imperial rank, though the Awadh dynasty's own cultural and political center was in the province to which his son Shuja-ud-Daula succeeded.

Shuja-ud-Daula and the Consolidation of Awadh as a Regional State

Shuja-ud-Daula succeeded his father in 1754, at about the age of twenty-two, and ruled until his death in January 1775. His reign was the period in which Awadh became a fully consolidated regional state, with a hereditary ruling family, a stable provincial administration, an army of substantial size, and an increasingly distinctive court. He made Faizabad his principal seat, enlarged its fortifications and palaces, and developed it into a political and commercial center. The city's growth under his rule, and the gardens, mausoleums, and residences associated with it, form the background to the court culture examined in §60.17. It should be remembered that Lucknow's major expansion came later, after the court was moved there in 1775.

His military and fiscal organization combined existing Mughal-style forces with a growing body of regular infantry and artillery. The army relied on cavalry drawn from Afghan, Turani, and Indian groups, on infantry recruited among local peasant-soldiers, and on units trained with the help of European and Indian officers. Paying these troops required high levels of revenue and the cooperation of bankers and revenue contractors. As in Bengal, the growth of a regional army and a regional treasury stood together, and the relationship between the nawab and the zamindars who supplied revenue and armed followers remained a delicate one. Shuja-ud-Daula also continued the practice of employing Hindu officials and of working with ascetic and martial groups, and his court included a range of ethnic and religious communities.

The northern conflicts of the 1750s and early 1760s drew him deeply into imperial and regional politics. He was involved in the struggles at Delhi, where the empire was increasingly dominated by factions and where the emperor Alamgir II was murdered in 1759, and where the fugitive prince, soon the emperor Shah Alam II, sought refuge in Awadh's territories. In the crisis that culminated in the Third Battle of Panipat in January 1761, when Ahmad Shah Durrani and his Indian allies met the Maratha army, Shuja-ud-Daula allied with Durrani, as did the Rohilla leader Najib-ud-Daula. The reasons for his choice, including the Maratha threat to northern India's established elites and the diplomatic persuasion of Durrani's supporters, are discussed in Chapter 58. The Maratha defeat made Shuja-ud-Daula's position in northern India stronger for a time, and he was formally invested as wazir by Shah Alam II on 15 February 1762, a position that, like his father's, gave him an imperial standing that complemented his regional power.

His position changed after the intervention of the East India Company in the region. In 1764, at the Battle of Buxar, Shuja-ud-Daula, with the Bengal nawab Mir Qasim and the emperor Shah Alam II, was defeated by Company forces. In 1765, the Treaty of Allahabad, negotiated with the Company's leadership, left Shuja-ud-Daula in possession of most of Awadh while requiring an indemnity, ceding Allahabad and Kora to the emperor, and establishing a new relationship between the nawab and the Company. These events are the subject of Chapter 62 and are mentioned here only to clarify what was at stake for Awadh. After 1765 the nawab was no longer a regional ruler in an open political field. He was bound by agreements to a rising power whose military reputation was high, even though the Company in those years still treated him as a buffer and ally and had not yet taken over the province. How that relationship developed is a story for later chapters.

The ruling household's importance grew with the dynasty's consolidation. Shuja-ud-Daula's mother, Sadr-un-Nissa Begum (Nawab Begum), and his wife, Bahu Begum, controlled substantial resources, estates, and household establishments, and their roles in the financial and political life of the state are well established in modern scholarship. They continued to hold influence after his death, and §60.18 examines their position in more detail. By 1775, when Shuja-ud-Daula died and his son Asaf-ud-Daula succeeded, Awadh had become a durable hereditary regional state with its own court, army, treasury, and elites, still using Mughal titles and offices, still acknowledging the emperor, and increasingly entangled in a political environment that included the Company. Its history from Awadh's side thus shows both the strength of the successor-state model and the vulnerability of states whose autonomy depended on a balance of forces that outside powers could alter.

Division III · Sections 60.9–60.12Hyderabad, the Nizams, and the Transformation of the Mughal Deccan
Section 60.9

The Mughal Deccan after Aurangzeb: Provincial Institutions and Political Competition

The Mughal conquest of Bijapur in 1686 and Golconda in 1687 placed a very large and varied territory under imperial administration. The Deccan sultanates, long-established states with their own elites, revenue systems, and cultural traditions, were absorbed into a framework of Mughal subas. By the end of Aurangzeb's reign the empire's Deccan provinces included Khandesh, Berar, Aurangabad, Bidar, Hyderabad, and Bijapur, together with the Carnatic territories to the south that were administered through a separate structure centered on Arcot. Aurangabad, named for the emperor, served as the main Mughal administrative and military center, and Aurangzeb himself spent his last decades in the region.

Mughal administration in the Deccan was never a simple copy of northern practice. The territories included former sultanate lands with long-established local holders of authority: Telugu-speaking warrior-lords, Marathi-speaking deshmukhs and deshpandes, who held hereditary revenue and record-keeping rights comparable to the northern zamindars and qanungos, and village headmen and local chiefs. Many nobles of the old Deccan courts, whose families had served the Qutb Shahs and Adil Shahs, were absorbed into the Mughal nobility and brought administrative expertise, local connections, and cultural traditions with them. The tension between this Deccani element and the northern and Persian-origin nobles who came with the Mughal armies was a persistent theme, and it would resurface in Hyderabad's later politics.

After Aurangzeb died in 1707, instability followed. The long wars had exhausted resources and disrupted the countryside, the assignment system was under strain, and the Marathas, whose power Chapter 58 traces, were expanding. A crucial moment came in 1719, when an agreement negotiated by the Sayyid brothers at Delhi gave the Maratha ruler Shahu recognized rights to collect chauth and sardeshmukhi in the Deccan subas, a legal acknowledgment that the Mughal officials of the Deccan resented and resisted in different degrees. The governors of the Deccan thus had to manage Maratha demands in addition to the ordinary tasks of governing. The viceroyalty itself changed hands several times, and competition among Mughal nobles for the office was intense, since the Deccan offered one of the largest concentrations of revenue and military patronage in the empire.

This background is necessary for understanding what Hyderabad was. It was not an invention from nothing, and it was not simply the work of a single great noble. It was the transformation of a Mughal viceregal administration, with its offices, officials, revenue practices, and landholding intermediaries, into a regional state. Many of its institutions were Mughal in origin, modified by the legacy of the old sultanates and shaped by the constant pressure of Maratha military competition.

The Deccan's administrative inheritance had other distinctive features. Hyderabad itself, founded in 1591 by the Qutb Shahs, had been the capital of a prosperous sultanate with a cosmopolitan elite, a flourishing trade in textiles and diamonds, and links to Iran and the Persian Gulf. After 1687 it became the seat of a Mughal subadar rather than of a sovereign court, and some of its elite families moved away or lost position, though many of its institutions and its commercial life persisted. To the south, the Carnatic, with its center at Arcot, was administered as a semi-autonomous Mughal territory under a deputy governor, the nawab of the Carnatic, who was formally subordinate to the viceroy of the Deccan. Each of these arrangements gave a future ruler multiple bases from which to assemble a regime, and none of them was a blank slate.

The Deccan's size also meant that no single officeholder could oversee it closely. Governors depended on subordinate commanders and fortress-holders, who exercised wide discretion and sometimes built local followings of their own. These conditions made the Mughal Deccan more decentralized in practice than its administrative charts suggested, and they help explain why a determined viceroy with a loyal following could turn it into a personal domain more readily than an administrator in a smaller, more closely supervised province.

Section 60.10

Nizam-ul-Mulk Asaf Jah and the Foundations of Hyderabad, 1724–1748

Mir Qamar-ud-Din Khan, known by the title Nizam-ul-Mulk and later Asaf Jah, came from a Central Asian noble family long in Mughal service. He had been a prominent commander under Aurangzeb in the Deccan and served as viceroy of the Deccan for a short period around 1713 to 1715 under Farrukhsiyar before being moved elsewhere. In 1722 he was summoned to Delhi and appointed wazir by Muhammad Shah, with the hope that he would restore order to the court and the finances. He found the position frustrating. The court was divided by factions that defied his authority, and the emperor was reluctant to support the reforms he sought. In late 1723, he left Delhi, apparently with the intention of returning to the Deccan, and the court's reaction included the appointment of Mubariz Khan as governor of the Deccan with orders to oppose him.

In October 1724, Nizam-ul-Mulk defeated and killed Mubariz Khan at the battle of Shakar Kheda in Berar. The battle was a decisive turning point. It made him the effective master of the Deccan, and the emperor, in no position to reverse it, recognized him as viceroy and conferred upon him the title Asaf Jah. Here the pattern examined in Bengal and Awadh appears in its sharpest form. An imperial minister defeated an imperially appointed governor in open battle and was then confirmed in the office by the emperor. The Nizam never formally renounced his allegiance to the Mughal ruler, and he continued to acknowledge the emperor's name in public forms, but he governed the Deccan in his own right.

Asaf Jah's government rested on a political household of retainers, kin, and officers whose loyalty was personal. He appointed his own commanders and civil officials, placed relatives and trusted followers in key governorships and fortresses, and cultivated a mixed elite that included northern Mughal nobles, Deccani Muslim families, Hindu administrators, and military leaders. His revenue came from the Mughal-era territories of the Deccan and the Carnatic, supplemented by tribute from regional chiefs, and he managed the competing claims of landholders and local rulers through a combination of force and negotiation. His political outlook is often described as pragmatic. He avoided prolonged entanglement in Delhi's quarrels after 1724 and focused on securing his hold on the Deccan, though he did not abandon the imperial arena entirely. In 1737 to 1739, he was called north to deal with crises that culminated in Nadir Shah's invasion, and his defeat at Bhopal in 1738 by the Maratha Peshwa Baji Rao showed both the limits of his power and the reach of Maratha arms.

Students should take particular care with the history of his capitals. Asaf Jah's principal center of administration was Aurangabad, the old Mughal viceregal seat, and he also spent time at Burhanpur. Hyderabad, founded in 1591 under the Qutb Shahs and the later seat of the Mughal subadar of Hyderabad, did not yet serve as the dynasty's capital. It was under his successors that Hyderabad became the Nizams' principal city, a transition completed in 1763 (§60.12). To attribute the creation of Hyderabad as a dynastic capital to the first Nizam would be an error of chronology, and the existing pattern, in which the founder's power was exercised from the Mughal Deccan's northern center, is an important feature of the early Asaf Jahi state.

He died at Burhanpur in 1748. Despite the long and eventful reign, the Asaf Jahi state was still unfinished in many respects. Its core territory was not clearly bounded, its authority over many local chiefs was partial and negotiated, and the succession had not been settled in a way that other powers would accept. It was nevertheless a functioning regional government with a recognized ruler, a substantial army, and a way of dealing with the empire and the Marathas that allowed it to survive, and the dynasty he founded would remain a ruling house in Hyderabad until the middle of the twentieth century.

Section 60.11

Hyderabad's Revenue, Military Organization, and Relations with Regional Powers

The institutional foundations of the Asaf Jahi state were Mughal in form and Deccani in practice. Revenue was assessed and collected through the provincial and district hierarchy, with the Nizam's diwan and his subordinates overseeing accounts, and local collection depended on hereditary intermediaries: the deshmukhs and deshpandes, village headmen, and, in the Telugu-speaking regions, local warrior-chiefs sometimes called samasthanams in later usage, who controlled forts and territories and paid tribute. Many of these holders had inherited authority from the pre-Mughal sultanates and had been confirmed by the Mughals in turn. The Nizam, like his predecessors, confirmed them in exchange for payment and service, and he dealt with them as partners and subordinates rather than as officials.

Territorial assignments continued the Mughal practice of granting revenue to commanders in return for military service, though the system adapted to circumstances. The Nizam placed trusted officers in charge of major forts and districts and allowed them considerable discretion, and a number of these officers' families became hereditary power-holders in their own right. Revenue farming, in which collection rights were sold or contracted to individuals who advanced money to the government, grew in importance in the eighteenth century, as the state's need for cash increased. Bankers, merchants, and moneylenders in Aurangabad, Hyderabad, and the Deccan's other trading towns advanced funds against revenue and handled remittances, and they became integral to the state's finances in ways comparable to Bengal's, though the Deccan lacked a single dominant house on the Jagat Seth model.

The military organization of Hyderabad combined several elements. Its core was the cavalry and infantry of the Nizam's household and his principal commanders, supplemented by contingents under local chiefs and by mercenaries recruited from Arab, Afghan, Rohilla, and other groups. The state's reliance on such soldiers reflected the wider labor market for military service in eighteenth-century South Asia, in which skilled soldiers moved between employers. The Nizam's forces were significant, but they were often stretched across a huge territory with long frontiers.

The relationship with the Marathas was the dominant strategic fact. The Peshwa-led network, examined in Chapter 58, claimed chauth and sardeshmukhi from the Deccan, and its armies could raid or besiege across the region. Asaf Jah negotiated, fought, and sometimes yielded. After his defeat by Baji Rao at Palkhed in 1728, he accepted terms confirming Maratha claims in certain areas, and the Bhopal defeat of 1738 again showed that the Peshwa's forces could outmaneuver the Nizam in the field. Yet he and his successors were never extinguished. They maintained a substantial territory because the Marathas themselves were divided among competing leaders, because Hyderabad's resources were large, and because the Nizam could sometimes play Maratha leaders against one another or secure external allies. The relationship was therefore one of recurrent war and negotiated coexistence.

The central problem of the Nizam's government is the gap between claim and capacity. His formal authority extended over a wide area, from Khandesh and Berar to the Carnatic and the Northern Circars coast, but he could not control every district directly. Distant territories, such as parts of the Carnatic under the nawabs of Arcot, who were nominally his subordinates, enjoyed autonomy of their own. Local chiefs paid tribute when pressed and withheld it when they could. This pattern was characteristic of the period. The governing challenge was military-fiscal consolidation in a politically competitive environment: the Nizam had to raise enough revenue to maintain forces capable of deterring neighbors, while keeping the allegiance of the intermediaries who collected that revenue. Success depended on bargaining as much as on command, and Hyderabad survived by keeping that bargaining flexible.

Section 60.12

Hyderabad after Asaf Jah I: Dynastic Succession, French Influence, and Regional Autonomy

Asaf Jah's death in 1748 opened a succession dispute that lasted more than a decade and exposed the dependence of the Nizam's authority on the loyalty of commanders and the support of external allies. His second son, Nasir Jang, who had been serving as his deputy and held the support of much of the army, assumed the throne. He was challenged by Muzaffar Jang, the son of Asaf Jah's daughter, who claimed a right on the basis of a designation by his grandfather, and by other claimants and ambitious commanders. The contest could not be settled by a simple rule of primogeniture, because the Asaf Jahi dynasty was too new for such a rule to have acquired force, and the Mughal emperor's recognition, though sought, did not by itself decide the matter.

The dispute became entangled with the politics of the Carnatic, where rival claimants to the nawabi of Arcot sought backing from different Deccan factions. The French East India Company's leader in southern India, Joseph-François Dupleix, supported Muzaffar Jang and the Carnatic claimant Chanda Sahib, while the English company backed their rivals. In December 1750, Nasir Jang was killed by a faction among his own commanders who had been won over to Muzaffar Jang. Muzaffar Jang succeeded him, but was himself killed in early 1751 during a campaign. The French commander Charles de Bussy then installed Salabat Jang, another son of Asaf Jah, and for several years remained at Hyderabad with a French-trained force, effectively acting as a patron and protector of the new Nizam. In 1753 the French obtained revenue assignments in the Northern Circars along the east coast to pay for this force.

The French connection, important as it was, should not be mistaken for the whole of Hyderabad's politics. Bussy's force gave Salabat Jang a degree of security and made the Nizam a client of an external company, but the Nizam's court included powerful commanders and officials who had their own interests, and the relationship was never simple. As the fortunes of the French declined in the later 1750s, English forces under Colonel Francis Forde stormed Masulipatam in April 1759, and Salabat Jang agreed in May to cede surrounding coastal territory and exclude French military support; the implementation and durability of the settlement require separate consideration. These developments are part of the larger history of the Carnatic Wars and European commercial and military competition, treated in Chapters 61 and 64. For Hyderabad, the key point is that the succession crisis made the dynasty a participant in networks that stretched well beyond the Deccan, and that the dynasty's survival depended on its capacity to negotiate among them.

At the same time, Maratha pressure intensified. In February 1760, a Maratha army under Sadashiv Rao Bhau defeated the Nizam's forces in the Udgir campaign, including fighting near Tandulja, and forced the cession of substantial revenue territories and fortresses. The precise revenue values reported for the treaty differ among accounts, but the scale of the concession was politically significant. The loss was serious, but it came shortly before the Maratha defeat at Panipat in 1761, which changed the balance again. Within Hyderabad, Nizam Ali Khan, another son of Asaf Jah, took advantage of his brother's weakness. He displaced Salabat Jang in 1761 to 1762, and ruled as Nizam from 1762 until 1803. In the early 1760s, conventionally dated to 1763, he shifted his principal seat from Aurangabad to Hyderabad; the transition in political and urban functions took place over time rather than through one instantaneous relocation. This transfer marks the point at which Hyderabad became the Nizams' principal and permanent seat, completing a process that had not been finished in Asaf Jah I's lifetime.

By the 1760s, then, the Nizams had survived a contested succession, French military patronage, Maratha defeats, and the beginning of English intervention on the east coast. They had retained their authority over a large part of the Deccan and had preserved the dynasty's claim to Mughal viceregal legitimacy. They had done so, however, in a political landscape where Marathas and European companies were both significant players, and where agreements with those powers increasingly shaped the dynasty's room for maneuver. The Company's later interventions, including its treaties with the Nizam in the 1760s and the subsidiary arrangements of the end of the century, belong to Chapters 61 and 64.

Cause and Effect

Why Provincial Governors Became Regional Rulers

Long-term causes

A provincial system that depended on local intermediaries, hereditary record-keepers, and banking networks; the erosion of the assignment system; and the court's declining ability to coordinate and enforce transfers.

Short-term causes

Succession crises after 1707; the shocks of Nadir Shah's invasion (1739) and the Durrani invasions; Maratha pressure; and the personal capacity of governors such as Murshid Quli Khan, Saadat Khan, and Asaf Jah to combine revenue control, loyal troops, and local alliances.

Event or transformation

Governorships were converted into hereditary or de facto dynastic regimes, confirmed by imperial farmans, at Bengal (1717 onward), Awadh (1722 onward), and the Deccan (1724 onward).

Immediate effects

Reduced and irregular remittances to Delhi; the growth of regional courts, armies, and treasuries; and the dependence of rulers on zamindars, financiers, and military commanders.

Long-term consequences

Regional states that could resist or negotiate with external powers, but whose fiscal and military structures, as well as their relationships with European companies and the Marathas, shaped the conditions in which the Company later expanded.

Historical complexity

No single cause explains the pattern. Local conditions, individual choices, and the interplay of several kinds of power produced different outcomes in each region, and imperial weakness alone does not explain why these particular governors succeeded.

Section 60.13

Revenue Administration, Zamindars, and the Reorganization of Provincial Finance

Land revenue was the foundation of all three regimes, and a comparison of their fiscal arrangements shows how different regional circumstances produced different solutions to a common problem. Each government needed reliable income to pay troops and officials, and each depended on local intermediaries to collect it. They differed in how they balanced central control against the bargaining power of those intermediaries.

In Bengal, Murshid Quli Khan's reforms aimed at a more regular and centralized system. The reassessment of 1722 and the reorganization into larger revenue divisions gave the provincial treasury a clearer picture of what each area was expected to yield. The conversion of jagir land to khalisa increased the portion of revenue under the nawab's direct control. Collection rested on zamindars, whose number was reduced as some large holdings were consolidated and others were broken up or displaced for default, and on revenue contractors who undertook to deliver specified sums. The government relied heavily on a corps of Hindu accountants and secretaries who maintained the records and supervised collection. The Bengal system thus combined a relatively centralized fiscal apparatus with strong dependence on a limited number of great zamindars, such as those of Rajshahi, Nadia, and Burdwan, who were made responsible for large territories.

In Awadh, the relationship between the government and landholders was more openly negotiated. Saadat Khan and his successors asserted revenue claims over powerful and often armed local lineages, and the result was a constant oscillation between coercion and accommodation. Zamindars who paid and supported the nawab kept their holdings, titles, and local authority, while rebellious ones were attacked. Revenue farming and the use of Hindu administrators, as in Bengal, were important tools, but the province's government could not impose uniform arrangements and had to tolerate variation among districts. The Awadh nawabs also continued to create revenue assignments for their military followers, so that the line between salary and landed interest remained blurred.

In Hyderabad, the fiscal system combined Mughal assessment with the Deccan's own practices of hereditary local officers and tribute-paying chiefs. The Nizam's government depended on the deshmukhs, deshpandes, and local lords who collected revenue and supplied troops, and on revenue farmers and bankers who advanced funds. The territorial extent of the state, and the competing Maratha claims to portions of the Deccan's revenue, made collection less certain and more contested than in Bengal. The state sometimes had to share, divide, or concede revenue to rival powers.

Assessment and collection also operated on different calendars. A government might enter an ambitious jama in its registers, but flood, drought, armed resistance, disputed boundaries, or flight from war could lower the hasil that collectors brought in. Provincial officers therefore relied on inherited local registers and negotiations with people who understood the fields, customary dues, and seasonal markets in each district. A revenue farmer promising a fixed amount transferred some collection risk from the treasury to the contractor but could respond by pressing cultivators more heavily. The institutional tension was not simply between central government and local disorder: it lay between the state’s need for predictable cash and the uneven, contested economic life from which that cash was extracted.

Historical Vocabulary
Jama, Hasil, and Ijara: Assessed Revenue, Collected Revenue, and Revenue Farming

The jama was the estimated or assessed revenue of a territory, the figure on which assignments, salaries, and government budgets were calculated. The hasil was the amount actually collected. In many places and periods the two diverged substantially, because of harvest failure, resistance, war, corruption, or errors in the original estimate. An ijara was a revenue-farming contract by which a person, the ijaradar or mustajir, agreed to pay the government a fixed sum in return for the right to collect revenue from a district or estate, keeping any surplus. The system gave governments cash in advance but also encouraged contractors to extract as much as possible.

These distinctions matter because a historian who reads a high jama figure as proof of prosperity, or a rise in the revenue total as proof of improved conditions, may be misled. Assessed revenue was a claim on the countryside, not a measure of what cultivators could comfortably pay, and the abwabs or additional levies could raise real burdens without appearing in the headline figure.

The relationship between governments and zamindars is the key to the whole fiscal story. Provincial regimes wanted more regular and predictable revenue, and they needed money for armies that were more expensive than before. Zamindars wanted to preserve or enlarge their customary rights, to avoid new demands, and to use government backing against local rivals. Each side had resources: the government possessed troops, the right to confirm or dismiss, and access to credit, while the zamindar possessed local knowledge, armed followers, and control of the cultivators. The outcomes were therefore negotiated. A zamindar who failed to pay might be dispossessed in Bengal, bargained with in Awadh, or accommodated in the Deccan, depending on circumstances.

It is important to distinguish the effect on the state from the effect on the cultivator. A stronger regional treasury did not necessarily make conditions better for cultivators. Extra levies, the pressure on zamindars to pay promptly, the demands of revenue farmers, and the costs of war could all increase burdens. In regions where military pressure was constant, such as western Bengal during the Maratha raids or parts of Awadh and the Deccan during campaigns, the formal assessment was less important than the exactions that accompanied it. Since the surviving records come largely from the government's side, historians often know more about what rulers sought than about what peasants experienced.

Section 60.14

Bankers, Credit, Hundis, and the Financing of Regional States

Provincial regimes could not function without finance. Revenue arrived in installments, but expenditure on troops, officials, gifts, and campaigns was continuous and sometimes sudden. Remittances to the court or to distant jagirdars had to cross long distances, and there was no state bank. The gap was filled by private bankers, whose networks of branches, agents, and correspondents spanned the subcontinent and who handled the mobility of money in the eighteenth century.

A banker’s influence could also be observed in routine accounting rather than spectacular political crises. Commercial paper converted a promise payable in one town into funds usable elsewhere; a ruler or merchant needed to know whether a correspondent would honor the bill, which coins would be accepted, what discount would apply, and when settlement could be made. Trust consequently rested on an extended chain of agents and on enforcement through reputation and political connections. The dated Bengal Company consultations of November 1742, discussed in the source window below, preserve specific interest-bearing obligations to the Seth house. Such transactions show that financiers could be creditors of European companies as well as of Indian governors, and that their commercial agency was not confined to one political camp.

Historical Vocabulary
Hundi and Sarraf: The Instruments and Agents of Indian Credit

A hundi was a written order to pay a sum of money, similar in function to a bill of exchange. A darshani hundi was payable on presentation, while a muddati hundi was payable after a stated period and could be traded or discounted. By buying a hundi in one town and presenting it in another, a merchant or official could move money without transporting coin. A sarraf (also rendered shroff) was a banker and money-changer who dealt in coin, assessed the value of different rupees, issued and honored hundis, and advanced loans. The system depended on trust among bankers, on correspondents in many towns, and on the ability of bankers to enforce obligations through reputation, family ties, and the backing of political authorities.

The ease with which a hundi could be transferred across hundreds of miles shows the sophistication of Indian commercial finance. European companies in the period used these instruments and Indian bankers' services, a reminder that they were entering an existing financial world.

Bengal's most prominent banking house was that of the Jagat Seths. The family came from a Marwari Jain merchant background, with roots in Rajasthan. Manikchand established himself at Dhaka and then at Murshidabad, and became closely associated with Murshid Quli Khan's government. His successor, Fateh Chand, received the title Jagat Seth ("banker of the world") from Emperor Muhammad Shah in 1723; this was distinct from the earlier title Nagar Seth granted to Manik Chand. The house acted as bankers to the provincial government, helped remit revenue to Delhi, handled the minting and assaying of coin at Murshidabad, advanced loans to officials and zamindars, and financed trade. Its network of agents extended to Delhi, Patna, Dhaka, Banaras, and other centers. The scale and range of its activity made it a pillar of Bengal's financial system, and historians generally agree that it was a central participant in the provincial ruling coalition, though they continue to debate how far its power compared to that of the nawabs themselves.

The relationship between political authority and financial capital was reciprocal. The nawabs relied on the bankers for liquidity, remittance, and money management, and for the economic stability that credit supported. In return, the bankers relied on the nawabs for protection, for the recognition of their position, for access to mint and revenue business, and for enforcement of agreements. A banker whose loans to a government could not be repaid, or whose political patron fell, faced ruin, and a ruler whose bankers withdrew support faced a cash crisis. Succession disputes, as in Bengal in 1739 and 1740, could turn on the allegiance of the great financiers, and their opinions affected decisions about who should rule.

Similar relationships existed elsewhere, though they were less concentrated. In Awadh, bankers and merchants in Faizabad, Lucknow, Banaras, and other towns advanced funds against revenue and handled the nawabs' remittances, and the Awadh court's growing expenses made it dependent on them. In Hyderabad, as noted, a range of bankers and revenue farmers financed the government without a single dominant house. Historians such as Karen Leonard have argued that the great Indian banking houses were so central to eighteenth-century finance that their decisions helped shape political outcomes, a thesis sometimes called the "great firm" theory. Critics have challenged whether the evidence supports so strong a conclusion, and the question remains debated. What is not in dispute is that regional states were financed through private credit networks.

The mint was a particularly sensitive point of contact between finance and government. Rupees of different mints, ages, and degrees of wear circulated at different values, and bankers who assayed and exchanged coin earned fees by setting the discount, or batta, between them. A banker who controlled or supervised a provincial mint could therefore influence the circulation of money, and the position carried both profit and public responsibility. Revenue paid in one type of coin had to be converted into another when it was remitted, and the conversion itself generated income. For this reason, historians treat access to the mint as one of the concrete foundations of the Seths' position, and similar arrangements in other provinces drew bankers into close relations with governments.

Section 60.15

Merchants, Artisans, Textile Production, and Regional Commercial Networks

Beneath the courts and treasuries lay an economy of production and exchange that connected villages, market towns, manufacturing centers, and distant ports. Regional states drew revenue from it and were affected by its fortunes, and their policies shaped its conditions. Textile production was at its heart. In Bengal, weavers in the Dhaka region produced fine cotton cloth, those near Kasimbazar and Murshidabad worked in silk and cotton, and thousands of village weavers produced cloth for local and export markets. Merchants and brokers, often Hindu, Muslim, Armenian, or Gujarati, advanced money to weavers and collected finished cloth, supplying markets across India and overseas.

Awadh's economy combined grain, sugar, indigo, cotton, and saltpetre production with weaving and other crafts in its towns. Its location made it a corridor for trade between the north and east, and Faizabad and later Lucknow became centers of consumption and manufacture because of the demand generated by the court, the army, and the officials. Banaras, a major commercial and religious center, linked Awadh to Bengal, and merchants and bankers there were important participants in regional trade. In the Deccan, Hyderabad and Aurangabad, as well as the east-coast ports connected to Masulipatam, linked inland production, including cotton cloth, to overseas trade, and the region's long-distance trade included caravan routes across the plateau.

River transport and caravans carried goods. In Bengal and Awadh, boats on the Ganges and its tributaries carried grain, salt, cloth, and other commodities, while pack animals and bullock carts moved goods across the Deccan and between the Deccan and the north. Metalwork, arms, luxury goods, and the products of the court's patronage, including jewelry, fine textiles, and decorated objects, were produced in towns where wealthy patrons concentrated demand. This urban economy was supported by the presence of soldiers and officials who spent their incomes locally.

European companies took part in this economy as buyers, creditors, and shippers, but they were one set of actors among many. The English, Dutch, and French companies purchased large quantities of cloth and silk, usually through Indian merchants and brokers, and brought silver and other goods into the region. Indian merchants, Armenian traders, and others continued to trade on their own account, and Indian shipowners dominated much of the coastal commerce. Historians such as Sushil Chaudhury have emphasized that Bengal's overseas trade in this period involved many participants and that European companies were not yet dominant.

Regional political fragmentation did not automatically mean economic stagnation, and several scholars have argued that commercial activity in some regions continued to grow in the eighteenth century, with new towns and markets, and with merchants and bankers gaining influence. C. A. Bayly in particular drew attention to the vitality of the "bazaar" economy and of towns under successor regimes. Yet it would be a mistake to assume that all merchants and producers benefited. Warfare disrupted trade routes and production, as when Maratha raids affected western Bengal's cloth and silk areas, and rulers' demands for taxes, loans, and tolls could weigh heavily on commerce. Artisans dependent on advances from merchants or on the favor of a court could be vulnerable when conditions changed. The experience of the economy thus varied by region, trade, and social position, and generalizations must be made with care.

The movement of goods had a social organization of its own. Weaving households depended on access to yarn, credit, and purchasers; advances enabled production but also tied artisans to merchants who could enforce delivery terms. Transport workers, boatmen, grain carriers, brokers, and moneychangers linked the production of one district to demand in another. A court’s expenditure on clothing, horses, paper, metal goods, and building materials created valuable markets, yet wartime disruption of routes or sudden requisition could deprive the same producers of income. Commercial resilience must therefore be assessed through particular trades and places rather than inferred from the prosperity of a provincial treasury or a prominent merchant family.

Section 60.16

Provincial Government, Urban Communities, and the Social Costs of State-Building

Stronger regional governments touched the lives of ordinary people in many ways. For cultivators, the most immediate effects were through revenue demands and through the requirements of war. Revenue was usually collected by zamindars or contractors who stood between the cultivator and the state, and the share demanded could vary with the strength of local intermediaries, the pressure on the government, and the harvest. Additional levies, labor service, and the supply of grain and fodder to passing armies were common. In districts that were fought over, such as western Bengal in the 1740s, parts of Awadh during the conflicts of the 1750s, and Deccan districts exposed to Maratha raids, cultivators could lose crops, animals, and homes and might flee. Cultivators sometimes found protection with powerful local lords, and sometimes found relief by migrating to less disturbed areas, so that patterns of settlement shifted.

Artisans and urban workers had different experiences. Weavers depended on merchants and on markets, and a court or army that increased demand for cloth, arms, and luxury goods could create opportunities, as in the growing towns of Murshidabad, Faizabad, and Hyderabad. At the same time, forced requisitions, tolls, and disruption of trade could harm them. Transporters, boatmen, porters, and servants found employment from the movement of goods and armies, and construction projects under the nawabs, including mosques, palaces, gardens, and fortifications, provided work for masons, laborers, and craftsmen. Soldiers, a significant occupational group in each region, found employment in the armies of the successor states, which recruited widely and paid in ways that depended on the state's finances. When pay was late, soldiers' resentments could destabilize governments, as they sometimes did.

Religious communities and service groups were also affected. Scholars, Sufis, and religious specialists benefited from grants of land and cash, whether under Mughal precedent or the new regimes' own patronage. Hindu and Muslim temples, mosques, and shrines received endowments, and the presence of a court could bring pilgrims and visitors. Urban service groups, including scribes, accountants, physicians, and entertainers, formed a significant part of provincial capitals' populations. The relationship between rulers and these groups was often one of mutual advantage, though patronage could be withdrawn.

The three regions differed. In Bengal, the greatest direct disruption came from the Maratha raids and the heavy fiscal pressure that accompanied them, while the prosperity of the commercial economy before and between those episodes benefited many merchants and weavers. In Awadh, the nawabs' constant need for military resources and their dealings with zamindars shaped the rural experience, and the growth of Faizabad and later Lucknow offered new opportunities for urban groups. In Hyderabad, the long frontier with the Marathas and the weak control over distant districts meant that effective government varied widely and that cultivators in different areas experienced very different conditions. Generalizing across the three would conceal these differences.

This section bridges political economy and culture. The same regional governments that raised revenue and armies also supported courts, and the wealth they gathered paid for the cultural world examined in Division V. Its beneficiaries were not the same people as those who bore the fiscal costs, and a fair assessment must hold both in view.

The costs of rule were also distributed unequally within the same locality. A great zamindar who could negotiate an assessment or borrow against future revenue had more options than a cultivating household that had to meet a demand before harvest. Soldiers and their suppliers could gain wages or contracts from an army’s arrival, while other households lost carts, draft animals, fodder, or stored grain to requisition. The fortunes of urban artisans depended on continued court patronage; a change in capital or a military defeat could redirect employment rapidly. Such comparisons clarify why the formation of capable provincial governments cannot be equated automatically with either universal recovery or universal impoverishment.

Historical Debate

Did Regional State-Building Strengthen or Weaken the Political Economy of Eighteenth-Century India?

The question: Did the emergence of regional states such as Bengal, Awadh, and Hyderabad foster economic growth and commercial vitality, or did it contribute to economic strain, agrarian pressure, and instability?

Interpretation A — Regional states as engines of continued or renewed commercial vitality: Scholars such as C. A. Bayly argued that the eighteenth century saw the growth of regional economies, expanding towns and markets, and the rising influence of merchants and bankers, and that successor states often supported commerce. Sushil Chaudhury has argued, for Bengal, that the province remained commercially prosperous until the middle of the century. On this view, the political fragmentation of the empire did not simply produce decline, and regional rulers had incentives to foster trade and to cooperate with financiers. Muzaffar Alam's work on Awadh and the Punjab similarly suggests that governors stabilized regions by reaching accommodations with local landholders.
Interpretation B — State-building as a source of fiscal pressure and uneven outcomes: Other historians emphasize the costs. Military competition required high revenue, and the growth of extra levies, revenue farming, and requisitions could increase the burden on cultivators. Wars, including the Maratha invasions of Bengal, disrupted production and trade. P. J. Marshall has stressed that conditions varied sharply by region and that Bengal combined commercial dynamism with serious political strain in the 1740s and 1750s, and he cautioned against both simple decline and simple prosperity narratives. Historians of agrarian relations have noted that regional state-building was often accompanied by coercion of local intermediaries and peasants.

Evidence: The argument rests on a limited and uneven evidence base: government revenue records that show claims rather than collections, chronicles and European records that comment on trade, price data that are patchy, and the physical growth of towns and buildings. The reliability of statements about overall prosperity or decline is therefore uncertain, and the evidence often supports regional and local conclusions better than general ones.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians understand the long-term background to the Company's rise. If regional economies were vigorous, the Company's later success cannot be explained by pre-existing collapse, whereas if they were under severe strain, the region's vulnerability may look greater. Most scholars today treat the picture as a mosaic, in which commerce and coercion coexisted.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living under a Regional Regime

For most people in Bengal, Awadh, and the Deccan, the daily rhythm of life followed the agricultural year and the market calendar rather than the fortunes of dynasties. A cultivator's household planted and harvested, paid what the local zamindar or contractor demanded, and sold surplus grain in the nearest market town. A weaver's household might work on a loom in a village, receiving advances from a broker and delivering cloth on a schedule. Urban households, whether those of scribes, shopkeepers, boatmen, or soldiers, were more directly exposed to the court and the marketplace.

The sources are mostly the products of the literate and powerful, and they reveal the lives of ordinary people only indirectly, through revenue complaints, descriptions of flight from raids, accounts of town growth, and the physical remains of buildings. Historians therefore have to infer the experience of the many from the records of the few, and they should be cautious about speaking for groups whose own voices are largely absent.

Section 60.17

Regional Court Cultures: Murshidabad, Faizabad, Lucknow, Aurangabad, and Hyderabad

Political autonomy had cultural consequences. As the imperial center lost its ability to attract and support the empire's artists, scholars, and specialists, regional courts became alternative centers of patronage. Poets, scribes, scholars, painters, musicians, architects, and religious specialists moved from Delhi to the provinces, some after the disruptions of 1739 and the 1750s and 1760s, others because provincial courts offered better prospects. Their movement carried Mughal and Persianate traditions into new settings, where they mingled with local languages, tastes, and institutions.

The five centers developed at different times, and the chronology matters. Murshidabad's rise began with Murshid Quli Khan's transfer of the revenue offices in the early eighteenth century. His construction of a major mosque there in the 1720s, and the growth of the nawabs' court under his successors, made it a significant political and commercial city. Persian remained the language of administration and elite literary culture, while Bengali literary life flourished under the patronage of regional zamindars and rulers, notably at Nadia, where the poet Bharatchandra Ray composed his Annadamangal in the 1750s. Murshidabad's later artistic tradition, including a school of painting that drew on Mughal models, developed in the second half of the century.

Awadh's cultural history requires careful sequencing. Faizabad, as developed by Safdar Jang and especially by Shuja-ud-Daula, was the principal seat in the middle of the century, with fortifications, palaces, gardens, and mausolea. Lucknow, an established town, rose to major importance only after Asaf-ud-Daula moved the court there in 1775, and its famous architectural, literary, and musical flowering belongs largely to the following decades. Poets from Delhi, including some of the leading Urdu poets of the age, found patronage in Awadh as conditions in the capital worsened. The Shia identity of the ruling family fostered Muharram observances, mourning assemblies, and related poetic and architectural forms, which became prominent in Awadh's culture, though one should avoid presenting the whole of Awadh society in sectarian terms, since Hindus, Sunni Muslims, and others participated in the court's cultural and administrative life.

In the Deccan, Aurangabad had been a Mughal provincial and viceregal city, and it remained the Asaf Jahi center under the first Nizam. The region had a distinctive literary tradition in the Deccani form of Urdu, and Aurangabad produced poets who contributed to the development of Urdu literature. Hyderabad, as noted, had a longer urban and cultural history under the Qutb Shahs, with its famous Charminar and its legacy of Persian, Telugu, and Dakhani culture. Its role as the Nizams' principal capital dates from 1763, and the court culture that later made it famous, with its patronage of Persian and Urdu letters, architecture, and learning, developed over the following decades and centuries. Conflating the early and later phases of Hyderabad's history would obscure the real course of development.

Across all three regions, the courts patronized Persianate literary culture, which included poetry, histories, and administrative writing, and also vernacular literatures, music, and religious institutions. Sufi shrines, Shia institutions, Hindu temples and ascetic orders, and Jain and other communities were supported by endowments and elite patronage. Patrons were not only rulers. Zamindars, bankers, and merchants, including those of the Jagat Seth house, also built temples and supported religious and charitable causes. The regional cultures that emerged combined Mughal inheritances with local traditions, and they were not copies of Delhi's court.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Imperial to Regional Courts

What Changed

  • Patronage moved from a single imperial court to several regional courts competing for talent.
  • Provincial capitals such as Murshidabad, Faizabad, Lucknow, and Hyderabad acquired new political and cultural prominence at different times.
  • Regional languages and local traditions gained prominence alongside Persian.
  • Financiers, zamindars, and regional officials became more prominent as patrons.

What Stayed the Same

  • Persian remained the principal language of administration and learned culture.
  • Mughal titles, etiquette, and court forms remained the models for regional courts.
  • Religious endowments and elite patronage of Sufi, Shia, Hindu, and other institutions continued.
  • Court culture remained concentrated among elites and urban groups.

Who experienced change most directly? Scholars, poets, and artists who relocated and found new patrons experienced the shift most visibly, as did families whose fortunes were tied to particular courts. Rural communities experienced greater continuity in daily life, though they paid for court life through revenue. Why the pattern matters: It shows that political decentralization could coincide with cultural diffusion and not simply cultural decline.

Section 60.18

Women, Dynastic Households, Religious Patronage, and Regional Political Authority

Political history written around appointed male officeholders omits much of how these regimes operated. In dynastic states, the household was not merely a domestic space. It was an institution that held property, managed resources, maintained retainers, arranged alliances, and shaped succession. Women in these households could hold significant authority, and the sources, though uneven, allow some of it to be reconstructed.

Awadh offers the clearest documentation. Sadr-un-Nissa Begum (Nawab Begum), the daughter of Saadat Khan, wife of Safdar Jang, and mother of Shuja-ud-Daula, and Bahu Begum, Shuja-ud-Daula's chief consort and the mother of Asaf-ud-Daula, held substantial estates and revenue assignments, managed large establishments of servants, retainers, and soldiers, and controlled considerable cash resources. These resources made them financial actors of consequence. They lent money, supported dependents, and gave patronage, and they had political influence in matters of succession and policy. After Shuja-ud-Daula's death in 1775, their wealth and position became a matter of political importance, and the relationship between the Begums and the next nawab and the Company would become a major issue in the following years. The later controversy over their treasure and estates, an episode of the 1780s examined in later chapters, indicates how significant their holdings were. Bahu Begum remained a prominent figure in Faizabad for decades.

In Bengal, the evidence concerns especially the family of Alivardi Khan. Ghasiti Begum, his eldest daughter, maintained a large household and considerable wealth, and Amina Begum, the mother of Siraj ud-Daulah, was a member of the family whose position was tied to the succession. Chronicles and European records also report that women of the family were influential in the factional politics around the succession of 1739 and 1756. Historians must treat such reports carefully. Some come from observers eager to attribute court intrigue to women, and others reflect partisan interests. The evidence is stronger for women's possession of property and household resources than for their detailed political decisions.

For the Asaf Jahi household, the surviving evidence of women's authority in the period before the 1760s is thinner, and it is wiser to say that royal women certainly took part in household management and dynastic arrangements, as in other Mughal-derived courts, than to ascribe specific political roles that the sources do not document. Historians should distinguish between documented authority, such as ownership of estates or recorded patronage, and assumptions based on courtly titles or on the conventions of the period.

Religious patronage was one arena where women's activity is clear. Royal and elite women endowed mosques, shrines, mourning assemblies, charitable foundations, and gardens, and they supported scholars and religious specialists. Such patronage conferred status and merit, expressed piety, and built networks of loyalty. It also left physical traces, such as buildings and endowment deeds, which are among the best evidence available. The study of these households shows that the regional states were sustained by institutions beyond the formal administrative hierarchy, and that the financial and political life of a dynasty could be carried on in spaces that official chronicles seldom described.

These households also functioned as channels of political communication. Marriages joined provincial families to one another and to nobles at Delhi, as the bonds among Saadat Khan, Safdar Jang, and their descendants show, and the daughters and widows of rulers carried property, followers, and claims into other houses. A widow or mother who held substantial resources could support a particular son, sustain a faction among the soldiery, or negotiate with outside powers on the family's behalf. Succession in a dynasty without an accepted rule of inheritance depended heavily on such alliances. Seeing the household as part of the machinery of government helps explain why some successions passed smoothly while others, as in Hyderabad after 1748, produced prolonged conflict.

Section 60.19

Successor States and the Mughal Political Order: Autonomy, Legitimacy, and Transformation

Why did regimes that were effectively independent continue to present themselves as Mughal? The question is the heart of the chapter. In all three regions rulers made use, to different degrees and at different dates, of Mughal titles such as nawab, nazim, and wazir, retained Mughal offices and administrative vocabulary, and sought farmans and robes of honor from the emperor. Coins were struck in the emperor's name, and Friday prayers and court ceremonies acknowledged him. Officials continued to use the Persian language and the forms of Mughal correspondence, and the courts adopted Mughal etiquette.

The reasons were practical as well as symbolic. Imperial recognition gave the new rulers a legal basis for their authority, which mattered to zamindars, bankers, and officials who wanted to be sure that obligations would be honored and that the regime was lawful. It distinguished the nawab from a mere rebel, protected him against rivals who could claim that he was a usurper, and conferred status among the nobility. The Mughal order was still the framework within which political legitimacy was understood in much of South Asia, and no alternative framework had yet replaced it. For the emperor, the arrangement preserved at least the appearance of sovereignty.

It is useful to distinguish four things. Dynastic autonomy means that a ruling family controls succession and cannot easily be removed. Territorial administration means that it governs a region through its own officials, collects revenue, and commands armies. Symbolic sovereignty means that it acknowledges a superior authority in titles, coins, and ceremony. Outright political separation would mean the formal repudiation of that authority and the assertion of an independent title. Bengal, Awadh, and Hyderabad had the first two in substantial measure and retained the third, while avoiding the fourth. In Awadh, a ruler assumed the title of king only in 1819, and the Nizams of Hyderabad did not formally renounce the Mughal emperor's overlordship in the eighteenth century. Conceptually, these regimes occupied a zone that the vocabulary of modern sovereignty does not capture well.

Historical Debate

Were Bengal, Awadh, and Hyderabad Independent States or Autonomous Mughal Provinces?

The question: Should the successor states be described as independent kingdoms or as autonomous provinces of a Mughal order that persisted in modified form?

Interpretation A — Effectively independent states: Focusing on practice, this view stresses that the rulers controlled succession, taxation, armies, and foreign relations. Remittances to Delhi shrank or ceased, the emperor could not remove them, and they made treaties and wars on their own account. On this view, the Mughal titles were a veneer, and describing the states as provinces misses the reality of their sovereignty.
Interpretation B — Autonomous parts of a continuing Mughal order: Focusing on legitimacy and institutions, this view stresses that the rulers continued to derive their offices from imperial grants, to use Mughal administration and law, and to rely on imperial recognition to secure support. The Mughal polity, on this reading, was transformed into a looser system in which provincial rulers acknowledged a shared source of legitimacy, and treating them as fully independent imposes a later concept of sovereignty.

Evidence: Both views draw on the same material: farmans, titles, coinage, remittance records, treaties, and chronicles. They differ in how they weigh legal and symbolic allegiance against fiscal, military, and dynastic realities.

Why it matters: The terminology shapes how the later period is understood, including how the Company's gradual assumption of power is described, since a story of independent states being conquered differs from one of Mughal institutions being taken over. Many historians therefore prefer descriptive terms such as autonomous regimes within a Mughal political order, noting that the modern sovereign nation-state is an imperfect model for the eighteenth century.

Section 60.20

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has examined three regional regimes that grew out of Mughal provincial institutions and became increasingly autonomous without abandoning Mughal forms. Their development shows how a system built on offices and appointments could be converted into one of local power and hereditary succession. In Bengal, Murshid Quli Khan combined the diwani and nizamat, restructured revenue, and built a coalition of officials, zamindars, and financiers that outlasted him. In Awadh, Saadat Khan and Safdar Jang established a household-based regime through negotiation with powerful landholders and used their provincial base to influence the court. In the Deccan, Nizam-ul-Mulk Asaf Jah defeated an imperial appointee, was confirmed by the emperor, and governed a large and contested territory in a competitive environment dominated by the Marathas.

Their trajectories differed, and the differences reflect the regions. Bengal's riverine commerce and banking networks favored a regime built around revenue and finance. Awadh's landholding lineages required continuous bargaining, and its location linked it to Delhi. Hyderabad's military-fiscal politics unfolded in a region with weaker central control and a long frontier. Common to all were the importance of revenue administration, regional coalitions, financiers, landholders, military power, and continuing imperial legitimacy. Where the Marathas, Sikhs, and Mysore, examined in Chapters 58–59, built power from outside or against the Mughal service system, these regimes inherited it, and they kept its offices, language, and etiquette while altering their substance.

The chapter has also shown that regional autonomy was not an unqualified benefit. Stronger provincial treasuries were often built on increased demands, and war and requisition fell heavily on rural communities, while commerce, court patronage, and urban growth benefited other groups. Scholars continue to debate the overall economic effect, and the evidence supports regional rather than sweeping conclusions. The cultural flowering of regional courts, and the visible roles of dynastic women and financiers, reveal institutions and actors that a narrowly political narrative would miss.

These developments set the stage for what follows. The regional states did not collapse in the middle of the eighteenth century, and their survival, with weaknesses and strengths, formed the political environment in which the European companies operated. Chapter 61 examines those companies as participants in a South Asian commercial and political world, and Chapter 62 explains how the East India Company acquired political and fiscal control in Bengal. Students should approach those chapters without assuming that British supremacy was inevitable. The Company's rise depended on specific circumstances, including the fiscal, financial, and military arrangements of the regional states described here.

A comparison with the earlier chapters of Part XI sharpens the point. The Marathas created a confederacy whose claims, such as chauth and sardeshmukhi, were asserted against Mughal officials and whose political center lay in Pune and in its own network of fortresses and commanders. The Sikhs built their power through a militarized community and the misls, and Mysore developed a centralized military and fiscal regime under Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan. The governors of Bengal, Awadh, and Hyderabad differed from each of these. They held their positions through Mughal appointment, they worked through Mughal offices and with Mughal-era intermediaries, and they sought imperial confirmation of what they already held. This origin gave them access to established administrative practice and to credit networks, but it also tied their legitimacy to an empire that could not defend them, and the Company would later exploit precisely that dependence in the settlements discussed in Chapter 62.

Students should keep three distinctions in mind as they move forward. Autonomy is not the same as legal independence, and the regimes in this chapter had the first without formally claiming the second. Assessed revenue is not the same as collected revenue, and provincial wealth is not the same as social prosperity. And changing political authority is not the same as the wholesale destruction of Mughal institutions, many of which survived in altered form under regional rulers and later under the Company. These distinctions guard against the tempting but misleading story in which a decaying empire simply gave way to a foreign power.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how three of the largest regions of eighteenth-century South Asia, Bengal, Awadh, and the Deccan, passed from Mughal provincial government to increasingly autonomous regional rule. Its central argument is that this transformation took place through the institutions of the Mughal state itself. Governors, diwans, and commanders who held imperial offices converted them into durable positions of power, and they did so without repudiating the empire in whose name they governed.

The chapter began with the Mughal provincial system: the subadar or nazim, the diwan, the faujdar, the qanungo, and the zamindar, together with the jagir system and the networks of bankers who moved revenue. It showed that the division of authority between governor and diwan was designed to prevent concentration of power, and that when one person controlled both offices, appointed his own deputies, retained revenue, and secured the succession of a chosen heir, a governorship became something close to a dynasty.

In Bengal, Murshid Quli Khan's rise from revenue official to governor illustrated the process. He moved the revenue establishment to Murshidabad, reorganized assessment, converted jagirs into khalisa, relied on Hindu officials and great zamindars, and worked closely with the Jagat Seth banking house. His successors, Shuja-ud-Din, Sarfaraz Khan, and Alivardi Khan, maintained the regime through succession, force, and imperial confirmation, and Alivardi faced serious pressure from Maratha incursions in the 1740s. The chapter closed the Bengal narrative with Siraj ud-Daulah's accession in 1756 and left the Company's conquest to Chapter 62.

In Awadh, Saadat Khan and Safdar Jang built a regional state by bargaining with powerful zamindars, recruiting loyal followers, and treating the province as a family base. Safdar Jang's wazirate showed how a provincial revenue base could strengthen influence at court, and also how the obligations of imperial office could strain a regional ruler. Shuja-ud-Daula consolidated Awadh as a hereditary state centered on Faizabad, participated in the conflicts of the early 1760s, and then faced a changed environment after Buxar and the Treaty of Allahabad.

In the Deccan, the Mughal conquest of Bijapur and Golconda created provinces whose administration inherited local intermediaries and the traditions of the old sultanates. Nizam-ul-Mulk Asaf Jah defeated Mubariz Khan at Shakar Kheda in 1724, was confirmed by the emperor, and governed the Deccan from Aurangabad. Hyderabad became the dynastic capital only in 1763 under Nizam Ali Khan, after a succession crisis that involved French military support and Maratha pressure. Throughout, the Nizams' claims exceeded their capacity to control every district and they relied on negotiation with local chiefs and rival powers.

The chapter's strongest contribution was its attention to political economy. Revenue assessment differed from collection, and extra levies and revenue farming could raise burdens without changing the headline figures. Bankers, through hundis and credit, financed regional governments, and the Jagat Seths were central to Bengal's ruling coalition. Merchants and artisans operated within long-established commercial networks in which European companies were participants rather than creators. Regional state-building coexisted with commercial vitality in some places and with coercion and disruption in others.

The cultural and social dimensions were equally important. Regional courts at Murshidabad, Faizabad, Lucknow, Aurangabad, and Hyderabad emerged at different times and drew artists, scholars, and religious specialists from the imperial center. Dynastic households, notably those of the Begums of Awadh, operated as financial and political institutions. The chapter stressed that the capitals' chronologies should not be collapsed and that evidence for women's authority must be distinguished from assumptions about courtly titles.

The chapter's principal historiographical conclusion was that the weakening of the imperial center coincided with new regional arrangements, and that historians such as Muzaffar Alam, C. A. Bayly, Philip B. Calkins, and P. J. Marshall have offered complementary rather than simply opposed accounts of them. Whether the regimes are called independent or autonomous depends on whether one stresses practice or legitimacy, and the vocabulary of modern sovereignty fits the period poorly.

The chapter's evidence consists of Persian chronicles written at varying distances from the events, imperial and provincial documents, coins, European company records, and the physical remains of regional courts. Each carries limits of perspective, and the voices of cultivators and artisans are largely absent. These regimes became the political setting in which European companies operated, as Chapters 61 and 62 will show, and their history should not be read backward as a prelude to British conquest.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that the eighteenth century in South Asia was not simply a story of a fading empire and an advancing foreign company. Between those two stories lay powerful regional regimes that governed millions of people, collected revenue, minted coin, patronized culture, and made their own political choices. Understanding them corrects the habit of reading the period backward from 1757 or 1857.

The process these regimes illustrate, the conversion of office into power through local alliances, revenue control, and credit, reveals how states in this region actually operated. Institutions mattered, but so did hereditary intermediaries, bankers, soldiers, and households, and legitimacy was negotiated through forms that outlived the power behind them. The comparison with the Marathas, Sikhs, and Mysore in earlier chapters shows that there was more than one path to regional power.

The chapter also leaves open questions. Historians cannot yet say with confidence how heavily these regimes bore on cultivators, how far banking houses shaped political outcomes, or whether regional prosperity or strain better characterizes the century. These uncertainties are part of the historical record, and they should shape how students read later claims about the causes of the Company's rise.

Chapter 61 now turns to the European companies as participants in the commercial and political world these regimes helped make, and Chapter 62 will explain how, in Bengal, a trading company became a territorial power.
