The Rise of the Marathas

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

On 6 June 1674, in the hill fortress of Raigad in the Sahyadri range of the Western Ghats, Shivaji Bhonsle, the son of a Bijapur military commander, was enthroned with Vedic rites as an independent king. The ceremony, supervised by a learned Brahman from Benares, was attended by English envoys from Bombay and by officers drawn from across the Deccan. Less than a century later, in January 1772, Maratha troops escorted a Mughal emperor back to the palace in Delhi from which his dynasty had ruled since the seventeenth century. Between those two moments lies one of the most consequential political transformations of early modern South Asia, and this chapter is devoted to explaining how it happened.

The story is not a simple one of expansion in a single direction. The polity Shivaji created was small, mountainous, and heavily dependent on forts, and it was challenged almost from the start by the two most powerful states of the Deccan, the Sultanate of Bijapur and the Mughal Empire. After Shivaji's death in 1680 it survived a nearly two-decade war against Aurangzeb that cost the lives of a king, saw a second king flee to the far south, and dispersed authority among commanders operating across a huge area. Out of that war emerged a different kind of political system: a royal court at Satara, an increasingly powerful ministerial office, the Peshwa, and a growing number of military households that raised troops, collected revenue, and bargained over their obligations. The Maratha polity of 1750 was not the kingdom of 1674 on a larger scale. It was a new and evolving structure.

Chapter 57 examined the Mughal Empire after Aurangzeb from the perspective of imperial weakness and provincial reconfiguration. This chapter reverses the vantage point. It reconstructs Maratha history from the inside, asking how rulers, ministers, commanders, village officeholders, cultivators, bankers, and soldiers created and sustained political authority. It asks how legitimacy was constructed through ritual and genealogy, how revenue was claimed, negotiated, and collected, how armies were fed and paid, and how the relationship between a king, a prime minister, and powerful commanders was worked out in practice. The Mughal-Maratha war of Aurangzeb's reign was examined from the Mughal side in Chapter 54; here it is treated only from the Maratha side, focusing on survival, adaptation, and political reorganization.

The chapter also resists three common simplifications. The first is the tendency to speak of a single, unchanging "Maratha Empire." Historians distinguish at least four phases: Shivaji's kingdom, the wartime resistance of 1680–1707, the royal and Peshwa government of the Shahu era, and a later eighteenth-century network in which the Peshwas, the Shinde, Holkar, Gaekwad, and Bhonsle households, and other chiefs shared and contested authority. The second simplification is the portrayal of Maratha history as a Hindu-Muslim contest. Religious symbols mattered, but Deccan politics was shaped by alliances, military service, and rivalries that regularly crossed religious boundaries. The third is the habit of writing political history as though only rulers and generals were historical actors. Maratha power rested on the labor, taxes, and compliance or resistance of cultivators, artisans, merchants, transporters, and local officeholders, and the chapter attends to their experiences wherever the evidence permits.

The evidence for this history is unusually varied, and it is also unevenly distributed across periods. For Shivaji's career, historians draw on Marathi chronicles known as bakhars, such as the Sabhasad Bakhar composed in the 1690s; on Sanskrit court poetry; on Persian histories written from the Mughal and Deccan Sultanate perspectives; and on the correspondence of English, Dutch, French, and Portuguese traders. For the Peshwa era the evidence expands dramatically, because the Peshwa Daftar, the administrative archive at Pune, preserves letters, accounts, and revenue records in enormous quantity, supplemented by Persian news-letters, European observers, and the testimony of participants in great events such as Panipat. Each category of source has its own perspective and its own silences, and the chapter's source features ask students to read them critically rather than simply to extract facts.

Modern memory adds a further layer. Shivaji became a central figure in nineteenth- and twentieth-century Indian nationalism and remains an important symbol in the public life of Maharashtra and beyond; the Maratha defeat at Panipat became, in turn, a byword for national catastrophe. These later uses of the past are themselves historical developments and have shaped how earlier scholarship framed the evidence. This chapter tries to recover seventeenth- and eighteenth-century political actors on their own terms: as participants in a Deccan world of overlapping sultanates, imperial claims, hereditary rights, and negotiated loyalties, not as representatives of a modern nation that did not yet exist.

The chapter's twenty numbered sections are arranged in five divisions. The first examines the western Deccan and Shivaji's creation of a kingdom, his military methods, and his confrontations with Bijapur and the Mughals. The second turns to kingship, government, and the survival of the polity through the war with Aurangzeb. The third traces the post-1707 contest over kingship and the rise of the Peshwa office. The fourth analyzes the fiscal, political, and military systems of the eighteenth-century network. The fifth examines the northward expansion that led to the Third Battle of Panipat in 1761, the nature of the defeat, and the recovery that followed under Madhavrao I. A final section, 58.20, synthesizes these developments and links the Marathas to the other regional powers examined in Chapters 59 through 61. The Anglo-Maratha Wars are reserved for Chapter 64.

Historical Context

The Marathas emerged in the western Deccan, the region of present-day Maharashtra and adjoining areas, a landscape divided by the north-south wall of the Western Ghats (the Sahyadri) into the narrow, high-rainfall coastal Konkan on the west and the drier plateau of the Desh to the east. Chapter 1 introduced the monsoon and the Deccan environment; for Maratha history the crucial fact is the contrast between a steep, forested, fort-studded mountain zone, with limited agricultural land but many defensible positions, and a plateau of black-soil cultivation and trade routes. The region had been ruled in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries by the Bahmani Sultanate (Chapter 46), whose successors, the Nizam Shahi of Ahmadnagar, the Adil Shahi of Bijapur, and the Qutb Shahi of Golconda, dominated the Deccan in the sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries.

Mughal expansion into the Deccan, begun under Akbar (Chapter 51) and pursued under Jahangir and Shah Jahan (Chapter 53), steadily reduced the sultanates. Ahmadnagar was extinguished in 1636, and Bijapur and Golconda were forced into tributary status, before Aurangzeb's final conquests of 1686–87 (Chapter 54). Political authority in this region was shared among sultans and Mughal governors on one hand, and a dense layer of local hereditary officeholders, landholders, and military families on the other. Sultanate courts relied heavily on Marathi-speaking commanders and local revenue officers, and many of the families that later served Shivaji or the Peshwas had earlier served Ahmadnagar, Bijapur, or the Mughals.

The Marathi language and the social category called "Maratha" require care. In the seventeenth century, "Maratha" could designate a regional population, a military service identity, or, more narrowly, particular landholding and warrior families, and its boundaries with the large cultivating Kunbi population were fluid. Brahman families, including the Chitpavan Brahmans of the Konkan who later became prominent as Peshwas, supplied administrators, scribes, and ritual specialists. This chapter uses "Maratha" for the political and military system that grew around the Bhonsle dynasty and its successors, recognizing that its participants came from many communities and that the term's meaning changed over time.

The seventeenth-century Deccan had also been shaped by environmental stress and commercial change. A severe famine struck the Deccan and Gujarat around 1630–32, and recurrent warfare disrupted agriculture. At the same time, ports on the Konkan coast, such as Chaul, Dabhol, and Rajapur, connected the interior to the Arabian Sea trade in textiles, spices, horses, and grain (Chapter 49), and European trading companies, the Portuguese at Goa and Vasai, the English at Bombay and Surat, and the Dutch and French, were active along the coast (Chapter 61). Control of mountain passes and ports thus carried commercial as well as military significance.

Section 58.1

The Deccan before Shivaji: Geography, States, and Regional Society

Maratha power did not emerge in an empty political space. By the late sixteenth century the western Deccan was a zone of competing sultanates, with Ahmadnagar dominating the northern plateau, Bijapur controlling the south and much of the Konkan hinterland, and Golconda to the east. Their rivalry made the region a field of continual military recruitment, and their courts depended on Marathi-speaking commanders, scribes, and revenue officers as well as on Persian-speaking migrants, Abyssinian military men, and Afaqi and Deccani factions (Chapter 46). A career in sultanate service was the normal route to wealth and status for ambitious families of the western Deccan, and the sultanates, in turn, relied on those families to control the countryside.

The landscape shaped what that control meant. East of the Ghats, plateau villages grew millets, pulses, and cotton on black and red soils, with rainfall far less abundant than on the coast. In the mountainous belt along the Ghats, steep valleys, forests, and rice terraces supported smaller populations, among them Kolis, Mavals, and other hill communities, but they offered numerous naturally defensible ridges, and by the early seventeenth century dozens of forts commanded passes and valleys. Along the Konkan, harbors connected the region to Arabian Sea trade, and the Siddis of Janjira, of African descent and at various times allied with Bijapur and the Mughals, held a naval stronghold on the coast. A ruler who controlled both passes and ports could tax trade and deny it to rivals.

Local society was organized around hereditary rights. Each village had a headman, the patil, who was responsible for land revenue and local order, and an accountant, the kulkarni, who kept records; groups of villages were supervised by hereditary district officers, the deshmukhs and deshpandes, whose offices (watans) carried rights to a share of revenue and a customary standing. Villages also depended on hereditary specialists and servants (the balutedars), and cultivating families with long-standing rights in the land, called mirasdars, were distinguished from later tenants, called uparis. These rights were defended with great tenacity, and they mattered politically: a ruler who could secure the cooperation of deshmukhs could collect revenue and raise troops, whereas one who could not found his demands constantly frustrated.

Military service was likewise organized through hereditary or semi-hereditary arrangements. Sultanate and Mughal commanders received jagirs, assignments of revenue from specified territories in return for maintaining troops, and the holders of jagirs in turn relied on local deshmukhs and on retinues of cavalrymen and infantry drawn from village society. Shahji Bhonsle (c. 1594–1664), the father of Shivaji, illustrates the pattern. A Maratha commander of a family with some local standing, he served the Ahmadnagar regent Malik Ambar, whose well-regarded revenue settlement and use of light cavalry influenced later Deccan practice. After Ahmadnagar fell in 1636, Shahji entered Bijapur service and was posted largely to the south, in the Karnatak, while retaining the Pune region as a jagir, which his agents administered in his absence.

The significance of this background is that Shivaji's achievements were built on materials already at hand: forts, hereditary officeholders, a militarized rural society, and the political competition among sultanates and the Mughals. What he did with those materials was original in scale and consequence, but he did not create a society or a vocabulary of political authority from nothing. Understanding the pre-existing structure also explains why many of his later conflicts, for example with other Maratha families who served Bijapur, were struggles among Marathas rather than simple contests between Marathas and outsiders.

Historical Vocabulary
Watan, Deshmukh, and Patil

A watan was a hereditary right to an office, with its associated privileges and income, held by a family from generation to generation. The patil (village headman) and kulkarni (village accountant) held village watans; the deshmukh and deshpande held watans at the level of the district or pargana. Watans could be shared among family branches, sold, mortgaged, or contested at law, and rulers regularly confirmed or granted them.

The term matters because it explains why political authority in the Deccan always involved negotiation with local families. A watan could not be revoked lightly; it was a kind of property and honor. Modern English words such as "landlord" or "official" capture only part of the meaning, since watandars exercised customary authority that existed before and alongside any particular ruler.

Section 58.2

Shivaji's Early Life, Regional Alliances, and the Creation of a Kingdom

Shivaji was born at the fort of Shivneri near Junnar in February 1630, the dating supported by the near-contemporary Jedhe chronology and favored by much modern scholarship; 1627 appears in older and alternative dating traditions. His father, Shahji, spent most of his time in Bijapur's southern campaigns, and Shivaji grew up largely in the Pune region under the care of his mother, Jijabai, of the Jadhav family of Sindkhed, and the jagir manager Dadoji Konddev. Shahji himself married again and established a second household in the Karnatak at Bangalore, where his son Vyankoji (Ekoji) later became the founder of a Maratha line at Thanjavur. The picture of Shivaji as an isolated rebel against his father's masters is therefore a simplification: his early authority rested on his father's jagir and on the cooperation of his father's officers.

Shivaji's earliest independent acts, from about 1646, were the acquisition of hill forts in the Pune and Satara region, including Torna, Rajgad, and others, in some cases by seizing them from Bijapur-appointed commanders, and in others by gaining the loyalty of local officers. Later chroniclers framed these acts as a deliberate move toward independence, but contemporary sources suggest a more incremental process of building a power base and exploiting Bijapur's distractions. According to the traditional narrative, Bijapur arrested Shahji in 1648 to pressure his son, and Shivaji secured his release by negotiating through the Mughals; the details are difficult to verify, but they indicate that Shivaji's moves were entangled in the diplomacy of the three great powers.

His methods of building a following combined coercion, patronage, and negotiation. He recruited the hill-dwelling Mavals as infantry, gave them pay and standing, and assembled a group of capable officers, many from local families, among them Tanaji Malusare, Netaji Palkar, and Baji Prabhu Deshpande. He absorbed or removed rivals; the elimination of the Mores of Javli in 1656 is a well-known example, and gave him control of a fortified territory in the Mahabaleshwar hills, controlling access between the Konkan and the plateau. He also began to levy contributions from territories beyond his own control, and in the late 1650s he moved into the Konkan, capturing Kalyan and nearby ports and starting a navy. Competing elite households, such as the Mores, Ghorpades, and Savants of Wadi, often served Bijapur and opposed him, which means that his rise was also a contest among Maratha families.

Shivaji acquired legitimacy in several overlapping ways. He was the son of a known commander and could invoke inherited rights over the Pune jagir. He cultivated support among Brahman administrators and religious institutions, issued grants to temples, and presented himself as a protector of cultivators against the exactions of sultanate agents. Seals issued in his name used Sanskrit formulas that proclaimed the growth of his dominion, and the language of "swarajya" (own rule) appears in documents of this period, though as Section 58.5 explains, its meaning developed over time. Nothing in the evidence suggests that Shivaji began with a worked-out plan for an independent kingdom, but there is strong evidence that he grasped, early, that control of forts and revenue created political leverage.

Students should be cautious about the biographical tradition. Many of the best-known stories about Shivaji's youth, including his religious education by Jijabai and his early vow to establish a kingdom, appear in sources composed decades or centuries later, when his reputation had become a source of dynastic and then national pride. The earliest substantial biographies, such as the Sabhasad Bakhar and Sanskrit works like Paramananda's Shivabharat, were produced by court writers with a stake in presenting Shivaji's career as a coherent and divinely sanctioned enterprise.

Primary Source Window

The Sabhasad Bakhar and Shivaji's Historical Reputation

Source type: Marathi prose chronicle (bakhar) · Author: Krishnaji Anant Sabhasad · Date: composed c. 1694 according to Prachi Deshpande, though some accounts give 1697 for its completion; produced under Rajaram's patronage at Jinji · Language: Marathi

Context: Krishnaji Anant Sabhasad was an official in the Maratha court who had served under Shivaji. He wrote his biography of the founder at the command of Rajaram, who was then fighting Aurangzeb from the far south, at a time when the legitimacy of the dynasty depended on its connection to Shivaji's achievements.

Editorial paraphrase (not a collated translation): The text presents Shivaji's career as a sequence of well-planned achievements: the acquisition of forts, the defeat of Afzal Khan, the escape from Agra, the coronation, and the organization of government, with the king portrayed as a just, divinely favored ruler concerned for the welfare of his subjects, and with detailed descriptions of his military and administrative arrangements.

Analysis: The Sabhasad Bakhar is valuable because its author was close in time to Shivaji and had access to people who had known him, and its descriptions of administrative arrangements and military organization are among the fullest available. But it is also a work of royal legitimation, written to strengthen the dynasty in a period of crisis, and it idealizes its subject, compresses chronology, and presents debatable episodes as settled. Historians therefore use it together with contemporary Persian chronicles, English and Dutch factory records, and surviving letters and grants. It does not represent the viewpoint of Shivaji's opponents, of cultivators subject to Maratha demands, or of the many Maratha families who served Bijapur.

Section 58.3

Fortresses, Cavalry, Intelligence, and Maratha Military Strategy

The Maratha military system of Shivaji's era was built around the interaction between fortified hill positions and mobile forces. In the Sahyadri, steep basalt plateaus rising above forested slopes allowed small garrisons to hold strongpoints that commanded passes and valleys. Forts such as Raigad, Rajgad, Torna, Purandar, Pratapgad, and Panhala were not merely refuges but administrative centers, treasuries, and granaries, and the control of forts was often the most reliable measure of political authority. Shivaji's government paid close attention to the garrisoning of forts, dividing authority among officers of different ranks and castes so that no single commander could surrender or hold a fort against the king's interests; later chronicles describe a system of three officers, but how consistently it was applied is difficult to verify.

Forts alone could not win wars. Shivaji's forces included cavalry, divided into the bargirs, who were equipped and paid by the state, and the silahdars, who brought their own horses and equipment and were paid more, and a large infantry drawn from the hills and the Konkan. Cavalry gave the Marathas the ability to raid and to cut off supply lines across the plateau, especially in the dry season, when rivers were fordable and fodder was available. The monsoon months, when rivers swelled and the passes became difficult, favored the defenders and often concentrated troops in forts and cantonments. Maratha commanders used this seasonality deliberately.

Intelligence was central. Shivaji maintained a corps of spies and runners, including members of hill and village communities, who reported on enemy movements and on the wealth of towns subject to attack. Information about routes, passes, and the layout of enemy camps was essential for ambush and for the surprise assaults on which his reputation rested. Control of passes also allowed the Marathas to levy payments on merchants and to shield villages that paid protection money. The distinctive feature of Maratha strategy was not any single tactic but this combination of terrain, intelligence, speed, and the willingness to avoid pitched battle when the odds were unfavorable.

It would be a mistake, however, to describe Maratha warfare as simply guerrilla warfare. Shivaji's forces fought sieges, defended against sieges, and in several instances fought set-piece engagements. They also developed a navy. From the 1650s Shivaji sponsored vessels at Konkan harbors and strengthened coastal defenses. Sindhudurg, on an island near Malvan, was developed in the 1660s; other strongholds, including Suvarnadurg, were fortified or improved, while major works at Kolaba belong to the very end of Shivaji's life around 1680 and continued under his successors. Naval power gave him a defense against the Siddis and the Portuguese, a means to protect coastal trade and tax it, and a way to project force along the coast, though his navy was never powerful enough to win a decisive sea battle against European or Siddi fleets. Over the century the character of Maratha warfare changed substantially: infantry and artillery became more important in the eighteenth century, and large cavalry armies replaced the small, rapid forces of Shivaji's early campaigns, a development traced in Section 58.15.

Logistics set limits on what armies could do. Armies required grain, fodder, and draft animals, supplied by merchants, grain-carrying communities such as the Banjaras, and local requisition, and the protection of supply lines determined whether forces could remain in the field. The Maratha reliance on rapid movement and local resources allowed lightly provisioned forces to operate where heavier Mughal armies, with their elaborate baggage and artillery trains, struggled. The reverse was also true: Mughal armies could besiege and capture forts that Maratha field forces could not relieve, a dynamic of central importance in the war of 1681–1707.

Section 58.4

Shivaji's Expansion and Confrontations with Bijapur and the Mughals

Between 1657 and 1674 Shivaji moved from a regional chieftain to a ruler whose power neither Bijapur nor the Mughals could ignore. His relations with each were shifting and often cooperative as well as hostile. In 1657, while Aurangzeb was the Mughal viceroy of the Deccan, Shivaji first offered service to him and then raided Mughal territory near Junnar and Ahmadnagar; when Aurangzeb left for the north in the war of succession, Shivaji turned his attention to Bijapur and the Konkan. This pattern, of probing for advantage, negotiating from strength, and switching alliances when conditions changed, was typical of the behavior of Deccan commanders and not unique to him.

Bijapur, which had been weakened by the death of Muhammad Adil Shah in 1656 and by a contested succession, sent a large force under Afzal Khan, an experienced commander, to subdue Shivaji. The two met on 10 November 1659 below the fort of Pratapgad for a parley, with the details recorded differently in Maratha and Bijapuri accounts. Marathi tradition holds that Afzal Khan attempted to seize Shivaji, who killed him with a concealed weapon; Persian and other accounts differ on who initiated the violence. What is clear is that Afzal Khan was killed and that Maratha forces then routed the Bijapuri army and followed the victory with the capture of forts and territory, including Panhala. The episode brought Shivaji substantial booty, weapons, and prestige.

Bijapur returned in 1660, besieging Panhala with an army under Siddi Jauhar. Shivaji escaped from the fort in July 1660, and, in a celebrated narrative whose details acquired later embellishments, the commander Baji Prabhu Deshpande held a mountain pass with a small force while Shivaji reached Vishalgad, dying in the effort. Bijapur then reached a settlement with Shahji and Shivaji, recognizing Shivaji's holdings in return for the cessation of raids, a pattern of conflict ending in negotiation. This outcome was typical: neither side could permanently destroy the other, and both preferred a settlement that preserved their position.

The Mughal challenge was more serious. Aurangzeb, now emperor, assigned his maternal uncle Shaista Khan to the Deccan. Shaista Khan captured Pune and the nearby forts and was staying in the town when, in April 1663, Shivaji and a small party entered his residence at night and attacked; Shaista Khan escaped but lost fingers, and several of his household were killed. In January 1664, Shivaji sacked the great port of Surat, a rich center of Mughal commerce and the point of departure for pilgrims to Mecca, collecting plunder from merchants and carrying it back to his territory; English and Dutch merchants defended their factories successfully. The raids were both military and political acts, humiliating the emperor and demonstrating that Mughal rule was vulnerable.

Aurangzeb responded by sending Mirza Raja Jai Singh of Amber, an experienced Rajput commander, and Dilir Khan to besiege Shivaji's forts. By June 1665, Jai Singh had surrounded Purandar, and the Treaty of Purandar required Shivaji to surrender twenty-three of his forts, retain twelve, pay compensation, and serve the Mughals against Bijapur; his young son Sambhaji was given a Mughal rank. In 1666, Shivaji traveled to Agra to meet Aurangzeb in the expectation of recognition. The meeting at court on 12 May went badly, with Shivaji given a lower place than he expected, and he was placed under guard. In August he escaped, according to the traditional account hidden in a basket, and returned by a circuitous route to Maharashtra by the end of the year. A peace followed, in which he received the title of raja, but hostilities resumed in 1670.

Between 1670 and 1674 Shivaji recovered most of the forts lost at Purandar, including Sinhagad, where Tanaji Malusare was killed in the assault, and sacked Surat a second time. He also pushed into the northern Konkan and the Nashik region and levied chauth on Mughal territories. The Mughal-Maratha conflict thus included both sharp military confrontation and repeated negotiation; neither approach alone explains the pattern. Jai Singh's reasoning, Aurangzeb's priorities elsewhere, Bijapur's internal politics, and Shivaji's own calculations all shaped the outcome. By 1674 Shivaji ruled a compact but significant territory stretching along the Western Ghats and the Konkan, held an army and navy, and had demonstrated that he could not be removed. What he lacked was a status that matched his power: he was still, in the eyes of the Deccan sultans and the Mughals, a subordinate commander. The coronation of 1674 was designed to resolve that problem.

The treaty and siege sequence also reveals why early Maratha sovereignty was so difficult to institutionalize. A fort could be surrendered under the terms of a peace agreement, yet its garrison, neighboring deshmukhs, and the surrounding villages might remain attached to a different political patron. Shivaji consequently had to combine military action with renewed negotiation after each diplomatic reversal. His recovery after 1666 was not simply a return to raiding: it required placing trustworthy officers in recovered forts, reestablishing revenue collection, confirming local allies, and demonstrating that protection by his government could last beyond the passage of an army. The geography of the plateau and coast thus allowed rapid reversals, but durable power depended on administrative follow-through as much as battlefield success.

Section 58.5

The Coronation of 1674 and the Construction of Maratha Kingship

On 6 June 1674 (Jyeshtha Shuddha 13 in the Hindu calendar, a date set by astrological calculation), Shivaji was enthroned at Raigad, which he had captured from the Mores of Javli in 1656 and developed into his principal seat. The ceremony was the culmination of preparations that extended over months. A critical problem was Shivaji's social standing. The Bhonsle family was of Maratha origin and its claim to the status of Kshatriya, the warrior varna from which Brahmanical tradition drew kings, was contested by some Brahmans of the region. To secure legitimacy, Shivaji sought the support of Gaga Bhatt (Vishweshwar Bhatt), a learned Brahman scholar of Benares whose family had a reputation as authorities on ritual law. Gaga Bhatt provided a genealogy tracing the Bhonsles to the Sisodia Rajputs of Mewar, and presided over Shivaji's investiture with the sacred thread and a Vedic coronation. The genealogical claim is not accepted as factual by most historians, but it shows how legitimacy was constructed through the recognized idiom of Brahmanical kingship.

The ritual included purification, the sacred thread ceremony, weighing the king against gold and other valuables, gifts to Brahmans, and the formal enthronement with a canopy and throne. Shivaji adopted the title Chhatrapati ("lord of the umbrella"), signifying sovereign rule, along with other titles, and began a new regnal era. Royal seals from this period state his sovereignty in Sanskrit, and the court produced new coinage in his name, including a copper coin, the shivrai, though the extent of its circulation is limited. Jijabai, Shivaji's mother, died soon after the coronation, and some later accounts report a second, Tantric ritual performed by another priest, reflecting concerns about the astrological or ritual adequacy of the first.

The coronation had several political purposes. Internally, it elevated Shivaji above other Maratha chiefs who had long been his peers, many of whom served other powers, and gave his officers a sovereign to whom they could owe loyalty distinct from Bijapur or Mughal service. Externally, it announced a claim to independent authority, giving him a basis for negotiation with the sultanates and the Mughals as an equal, and for treaties with European companies. The English envoys who attended, led by Henry Oxinden of the Bombay council, negotiated a commercial treaty at the same time, indicating that foreign traders took the new kingship seriously.

The concept of swarajya deserves careful treatment. In Shivaji's documents the term referred to the territories and revenue rights under his direct authority, in contrast to mulukhgiri, the territories beyond his rule from which he claimed contributions. Sources of the later seventeenth century also associate it with the idea of rule by the people of the region rather than by foreign sultans. The meaning broadened over time: in the eighteenth century swarajya came to designate the core Maratha territories under royal rule, and the claim to swarajya was included in the 1719 Mughal grants (Section 58.10); in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries nationalist writers read it as a precursor of Indian self-rule. Historians distinguish these meanings. Seventeenth-century swarajya was a dynastic and territorial claim within a world of kingdoms and empires, not a modern nationalist program, and it coexisted with Shivaji's cooperation with Muslim officers and his employment of a large number of Muslims in his army and navy.

The religious symbolism of the coronation was significant but should not be read as evidence of a campaign against Islam as such. Shivaji presented himself as a protector of Brahmans, cows, and temples, in language common to Hindu kingship, and his sources and court poetry sometimes depicted conflicts with Muslim powers in religious terms. At the same time, he honored Muslim shrines, granted land to mosques and to the Sufi dargah of Baba Yakut at Kelshi, and was served by Muslim commanders such as Ibrahim Khan and Daulat Khan, and by Siddi Hilal in the navy. Aurangzeb's own religious policies, examined in Chapter 54, were a contrasting factor. Scholars such as Gijs Kruijtzer have argued that religious identity became more politically salient in the later seventeenth century, particularly in Maharashtra, but that it interacted with other lines of division rather than replacing them.

The coronation did not instantly settle every political disagreement over Shivaji's ancestry, social status, or relation to other regional chiefs. Royal ritual asserted a claim that still had to be enacted through appointments, seals, lawgiving, military success, and the reception of embassies. The Sanskritized titles and genealogical language of the ceremony offered one vocabulary of kingship; Persian correspondence, Marathi accounts, and the practical dealings of deshmukhs offered others. The presence of commercial envoys was important for the same reason: diplomatic recognition was not merely symbolic when ports, passes, and maritime traders depended upon clear understandings of whose orders would be enforced. A historian should therefore distinguish the coronation as a deliberately staged claim to sovereign status from the gradual and contested acceptance of that claim across the Deccan.

Historical Vocabulary
Swarajya and Chhatrapati

Swarajya (literally "own rule") referred in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries to the core territories directly governed by the Maratha king, distinguished from the wider areas in which the Marathas claimed tribute. Chhatrapati ("lord of the umbrella") was the title of sovereign kingship, with the umbrella a standard symbol of royal authority in South Asia. Together the terms express a claim to independent political authority.

Later nationalist usage of "swaraj" for Indian self-rule gave the word new meanings, and the modern association should not be projected back onto seventeenth-century documents.

Section 58.6

Administration, Revenue, Local Government, and Maratha Society

Shivaji's government combined a central council, a cadre of salaried officers, a fort-based military administration, and an effort to define the king's relationship to local hereditary rights. At the center stood a council of eight ministers, the Ashta Pradhan: the Peshwa (chief minister), the Amatya (finance and revenue), the Mantri or Waqia-navis (records and intelligence), the Sachiv or Surnis (correspondence), the Sumant or Dabir (foreign affairs), the Senapati (commander of the army), the Pandit Rao (religious affairs and charity), and the Nyayadhish (justice). The names reflect Sanskrit titles, but the offices drew on earlier Deccan practice, and the titles in some cases also referred to Persian posts. In Shivaji's lifetime the Peshwa was one minister among eight, with a precedence that was not yet decisive; the later growth of the office, traced in Sections 58.10–58.12, was a historical development, not a design of the original system.

Revenue administration was crucial. The land revenue system built on practices associated with Malik Ambar of Ahmadnagar, who had introduced measurement of land and assessments based on the productivity of the soil, and on the experience of Bijapur. Shivaji's officers, among them Annaji Datto, are credited with surveys and assessments that fixed demands in cash or kind and reduced the arbitrary exactions of intermediaries. The aim, as described by later sources, was to collect revenue directly from cultivators through the king's officers, bypassing the hereditary deshmukhs and, in some cases, curtailing their privileges and fortified residences. In practice, the evidence suggests a more mixed result. The Maratha state could not dispense with watandars in many regions and often confirmed their rights in return for service, and later documents show deshmukhs and deshpandes continuing to collect and to bargain.

Beyond the territories of swarajya, Shivaji's forces also demanded payments from neighboring districts, including contributions later described and formalized as chauth; the more extensive combination of chauth and sardeshmukhi rights claimed over Mughal provinces is analyzed in Section 58.13. Such payments could involve promises to spare a territory from attack, but their political meaning went beyond a simple commercial sale of protection. Revenue supported an army that included salaried cavalry and infantry, garrisons for the forts, and a navy. Shivaji is credited with a preference for paying soldiers from the treasury rather than through jagirs, though later documents show that grants of land revenue also became common as the polity expanded. Military assignments of revenue, later called saranjam, became the principal means of paying commanders in the eighteenth century.

Local government rested on villages. Patils and kulkarnis performed their traditional functions, with village councils (panchayats) resolving disputes, and Shivaji's rule did not attempt to restructure village society. The state's engagement with villages was through taxation, labor requisition for forts, and the provision of supplies, along with grants to temples and Brahmans that connected local religious institutions to the king. Forts housed garrisons and storehouses, and surrounding villages were responsible for supplying them. Shivaji's government also sought to limit plunder by its own soldiers within swarajya, and chroniclers praised his orders against harming cultivators, although soldiers' behavior in lands beyond the kingdom was another matter.

Historians have long debated how original these arrangements were. Many features, the offices, the revenue survey, military pay, and fort administration, had Deccan Sultanate precedents, as Stewart Gordon and others have emphasized. At the same time, the combination was new: an independent Maratha-led government with direct links to a wide range of local officers, a conscious effort to rely on salaried officials, and a ritually legitimated kingship. The debate below examines this question.

The Ashta Pradhan deserves particular caution because later accounts can make it resemble a modern collective cabinet. The ministers held portfolios and titles, but the king did not surrender sovereignty to them, and some officeholders also conducted campaigns or supervised territory in the field. The effectiveness of revenue surveys depended on accountants, patils, and deshmukhs who knew individual holdings and often possessed claims older than the new kingdom. A central order could promise restraint toward peasants, but implementation was mediated by local officials and by the cash and grain needs of soldiers. Nor was administrative practice identical in the compact areas of direct rule and in neighboring districts subjected to contributions. The distinction between ideals laid down in royal instructions and actual local practice must remain visible.

Historical Debate

Was Maratha Power a Distinct Political Innovation or a Reorganization of Existing Deccan Institutions?

The question: To what extent did Shivaji and his successors create new political and administrative structures, and to what extent did they redeploy those of the Deccan Sultanates and the Mughals?

Interpretation A — Innovation: Earlier historians in the tradition of M. G. Ranade, G. S. Sardesai, and, in important respects, Jadunath Sarkar emphasized Shivaji's originality, depicting him as the creator of a new kind of state grounded in regional identity, a salaried bureaucracy, and the Ashta Pradhan. Some read this as the beginning of a national revival. The coronation, the revival of Sanskritic titles, and the effort to centralize revenue collection were cited as evidence of a conscious departure from sultanate models.
Interpretation B — Reorganization: More recent scholarship, notably Stewart Gordon, The Marathas 1600–1818 (1993), emphasizes the continuities with Deccan Sultanate practice: the revenue methods of Malik Ambar, jagir-like military assignments, the watan system, the use of forts, and the employment of cavalry. On this view, the Marathas succeeded not by inventing new institutions but by combining existing ones more effectively and by exploiting political opportunities created by sultanate decline. Others, such as Andre Wink, stress the continuing importance of fitna, a politics of disorder and rival claims, as the context in which Maratha authority grew.

Evidence: Both views depend on the same sources: Marathi chronicles and documents describing the Ashta Pradhan and revenue practice, Persian chronicles and revenue manuals describing sultanate practice, and surviving grants and letters. The difficulty is that the later Maratha chronicles may attribute to Shivaji arrangements that evolved afterward.

Why it matters: The debate bears on whether Maratha history is read as a nationalist origin story or as part of the wider political history of the Deccan, and on how historians understand the creative role of political actors operating within inherited structures.

Everyday Life and Social History

Village Life in the Maratha Country

Most inhabitants of the Maratha country were cultivators living in villages, growing millets, pulses, oilseeds, and cotton on the plateau, and rice in the wetter west. Their lives followed the monsoon calendar: sowing at the onset of the rains, harvest in the autumn and winter, and a dry season when armies moved and revenue was collected. A bad monsoon could bring famine, and war compounded the danger by destroying crops, requisitioning draft animals, and disrupting markets. The village community included the patil and kulkarni, the cultivating families, a set of hereditary craftsmen and servants (carpenters, blacksmiths, potters, leatherworkers, watchmen) who received customary shares of the harvest, and groups, often of low status, such as Mahars, who served as boundary-keepers, messengers, and watchmen, and whose labor and knowledge of the terrain were important to armies and to the state. Social hierarchy was marked by caste, with Brahmans, Marathas, Kunbis, and a range of artisan and untouchable groups occupying different positions, and the village's customary arrangements were jealously guarded.

Women's lives are less well documented. Women of cultivating households contributed labor in the fields and the household, and some women of elite families, such as Jijabai, exercised influence over family affairs and, later, politics. Evidence for most women comes indirectly through legal disputes over property, marriage settlements, and grants. Merchants and bankers (sahukars and shroffs) financed cultivation and the state, and moneylending bound many cultivators in debt. Shivaji's and later governments' revenue demands, labor requirements for forts, and the passage of armies formed the backdrop to ordinary life, and the chronicle tradition, centered on kings and battles, records them only incidentally.

Section 58.7

Sambhaji, Rajaram, and the Maratha War against Aurangzeb, 1680–1700

Shivaji died at Raigad on 3 April 1680, at about fifty years of age. His death opened a succession dispute between his elder son Sambhaji, then at Panhala, and the supporters of his younger son Rajaram, the child of Soyrabai, who had the backing of some ministers. Sambhaji prevailed, and was formally crowned in January 1681. He was an educated man who wrote in Sanskrit, but his reign was dominated by war and by tensions with the ministers and with some Maratha officers. In 1681, Aurangzeb's rebellious son Prince Akbar took refuge with Sambhaji, and the emperor, who had come south in 1681 partly in response, spent the rest of his life campaigning in the Deccan.

The Mughal campaigns, narrated in Chapter 54, brought the destruction of Bijapur in 1686 and Golconda in 1687, eliminating the sultanates that had formed a buffer. Aurangzeb then concentrated on the Marathas. In February 1689, a Mughal force under Muqarrab Khan surprised Sambhaji at Sangameshwar in the Konkan and captured him with his adviser Kavi Kalash. They were taken to the Mughal camp and executed in March 1689 after being offered, according to later accounts, the opportunity to convert. Raigad fell in the autumn of 1689, and Sambhaji's wife Yesubai and young son Shahu were captured. Aurangzeb treated Shahu as a hostage with a Mughal rank, raising him in the imperial camp with the intention of using him later, a decision with large consequences.

Rajaram was crowned and escaped from Raigad before its fall, traveling in disguise to the fort of Jinji in the far south, in Tamil country, where he arrived in late 1689. The move was strategic: Jinji, a massive fortified complex in the Carnatic, lay beyond easy reach of the Mughals in Maharashtra, and Shivaji's southern conquests of 1676–78 had established a Maratha presence in the region. From Jinji Rajaram issued commissions and grants, coordinating a resistance that was now geographically scattered. The Mughals besieged Jinji from 1690 under Zulfiqar Khan, and the siege continued for most of eight years, ending in 1698; Rajaram escaped before its fall.

The resistance in these years depended on commanders operating independently. Santaji Ghorpade and Dhanaji Jadhav led cavalry forces that attacked Mughal armies and supply lines in the Deccan, achieving some striking successes, including the defeat of Mughal commanders in the mid-1690s; Santaji was killed in 1696 in a feud with another Maratha chief. Ramchandra Pant Amatya and Parashuram Pant (Pratinidhi) administered affairs in Maharashtra. Rajaram made liberal grants of land revenue and watans to attract and retain commanders, and in 1698 created the office of Pratinidhi (king's representative), whose first holder was Parashuram Trimbak Kulkarni. These practices, born of necessity, lowered the center's control over armed forces but increased their number and mobility.

The war had profound consequences for Maratha political organization. First, authority became decentralized: commanders raised their own troops, collected revenue in the field, and negotiated with Mughal officers, sometimes accepting Mughal ranks and switching sides. Aurangzeb's policy of offering mansabs to Maratha commanders and recognizing their watans drew some away, but it also meant that Maratha families developed relationships with the imperial system that persisted. Second, the practice of levying chauth and sardeshmukhi, originally claimed beyond swarajya, spread across Mughal territory in the Deccan, as commanders collected from villages that preferred to pay rather than suffer raids. Third, the geographic horizon of Maratha activity widened to include Khandesh, Berar, Gujarat, and Malwa, where raids began from about 1699. Fourth, the war devastated the countryside, and the Mughal forces, which had taken a long series of forts, were stretched and ultimately exhausted.

Rajaram returned to Maharashtra in 1698 and died at Sinhagad in March 1700. His death, with the Mughals still in the field, seemed to many the end of the Maratha cause, but his widow Tarabai now took charge, the subject of the next section. For the purposes of the larger argument of this chapter, the central point is that the Maratha polity survived not because it had a strong center but because it had a flexible structure, in which royal commissions, hereditary rights, and military rewards bound a wide range of local commanders to the dynasty without requiring continuous direction from the throne.

The move to Jinji also changed the geography of the conflict. A Mughal force concentrating on Maharashtra could not eliminate the Maratha kingship without conducting difficult operations far to the southeast; conversely, a king at Jinji relied heavily on correspondents and commanders operating at great distance from him. Military delegation was a practical response to the scale of the war, not merely a collapse of royal discipline. The surviving records often preserve the campaigns of celebrated cavalry commanders more clearly than the burdens imposed on villages through requisition, forced transport, or contested taxation. Their success consequently needs to be measured both by the survival of the political system and by the uneven costs that survival imposed on the communities that supported or endured its warfare.

Section 58.8

Tarabai, Wartime Leadership, and Maratha Resistance, 1700–1707

Tarabai, daughter of the Maratha commander Hambirrao Mohite and widow of Rajaram, took command in 1700 as regent for her young son, who was enthroned as Shivaji II. Contemporary Mughal chroniclers, including Khafi Khan, acknowledged her energy and effectiveness. She issued orders to commanders, appointed officers, and directed operations against Mughal forces, working with experienced officers, including Ramchandra Pant Amatya, Dhanaji Jadhav, and Parashuram Pant Pratinidhi. Women had been influential in earlier Maratha politics through family networks, as with Jijabai, but Tarabai's exercise of public command was unusual and in the later political order was both celebrated and resented.

Between 1700 and 1705, Aurangzeb's forces captured a series of major forts, including Satara, Panhala, Vishalgad, Sinhagad, Rajgad, and Torna, in costly sieges that consumed enormous resources. These conquests did not end Maratha resistance. Maratha commanders operated beyond the forts, raiding across the Deccan, and recaptured some forts when Mughal garrisons weakened. From 1699, Maratha detachments entered Malwa, and in 1703 a column led by Nemaji Shinde reached as far as Sironj; in 1705, Khanderao Dabhade defeated a Mughal force at Nandurbar in Khandesh, and Maratha columns subsequently penetrated Gujarat and sacked the region around Baroda. Here lay the beginnings of the later northward expansion examined in Sections 58.11–58.16.

The conduct of the war changed the political character of the polity. Commanders of troops expected recognition and revenue, and the court had little to offer other than commissions and the right to collect chauth and sardeshmukhi from territories they controlled. The result was a system in which royal authority remained active in issuing orders, allocating offices, and legitimating service, even as independent leverage grew among commanders such as Dhanaji Jadhav, Khanderao Dabhade, Nemaji Shinde, and Kanhoji Angre, who from about 1698 built an independent naval power on the Konkan coast. This relationship between throne and military leaders, bargaining over rights and rewards rather than command and obedience, would shape eighteenth-century Maratha politics.

By 1706, Aurangzeb, now in his late eighties, withdrew to Ahmednagar, his forces exhausted and his treasury strained. He died there on 3 March 1707. His death did not end Maratha political competition, partly because the structure of the Maratha war economy, based on commanders' independent claims, outlasted the emperor, and partly because his sons immediately began a war of succession. The Mughal dynastic crisis created opportunities that Maratha leaders were quick to exploit, as the next section shows. The Maratha polity of 1707 differed greatly from Shivaji's: territorially larger, militarily more dispersed, fiscally dependent on claims to revenue beyond its own territory, and politically divided among competing centers.

Tarabai's authority is especially significant because regency was not an empty label during an emergency. Orders and appointments issued in her son's name required the court to manage allegiance, settle disputes, and decide which military leaders could claim revenue in newly raided areas. Her position depended on kinship and royal succession, but it also depended on political judgment and the practical ability to keep a dispersed coalition cooperating. Later accounts sometimes celebrate her as an exceptional heroine or dismiss her authority as derivative of male commanders; neither approach adequately explains why she could remain a formidable rival after 1707. The case reveals the importance of royal household politics, women as institutional actors, and the limits of reconstructing government solely from battlefield narratives.

Section 58.9

Shahu, Tarabai, and the Reorganization of Maratha Kingship after 1707

Shortly after Aurangzeb's death, Prince Azam Shah, who was contesting the throne against his brother Muazzam (the future Bahadur Shah I), released Shahu from Mughal custody in May 1707, in part to create a rival to Tarabai in the Deccan and to gain Maratha neutrality. Shahu had spent his youth in the Mughal camp; he returned a young man in his mid-twenties, with a claim to the throne as Sambhaji's son, and with the advantage of knowing the Mughal court. His mother Yesubai remained in Mughal hands as a guarantee of his conduct for another twelve years.

Tarabai rejected Shahu's claim, denouncing him as an imposter or a Mughal creature, and the Maratha polity split. On 12 October 1707, at the battle of Khed near Pune, Shahu's forces defeated those of Tarabai after the senapati Dhanaji Jadhav and others defected. Shahu was enthroned at Satara in January 1708, though his authority was still far from secure. Tarabai established a rival court at Kolhapur, and in 1714, she was overthrown by a faction supporting Rajaram's other son, Sambhaji II, who ruled at Kolhapur. The rivalry between the Satara and Kolhapur lines continued until the Treaty of Warna in 1731 fixed their respective territories.

Shahu's success was due less to military victory than to negotiation. He needed the allegiance of commanders and hereditary officers who had acquired power during the war and who had a choice of patrons. Many, such as Dhanaji Jadhav, Khanderao Dabhade, and Kanhoji Angre, joined him or remained neutral in exchange for confirmation of their holdings, titles, and revenue rights. Shahu's administration issued hundreds of grants confirming watans and saranjams, and his court at Satara became the arbiter of rights across a wide area. The process shows that Maratha unification was neither automatic nor imposed: it was an extended bargain in which the throne exchanged legitimacy for loyalty.

The practical implications were large. Shahu was an amiable and conciliatory ruler, often described as more inclined to hunting and pleasure than to campaign, and he relied on capable ministers. The Mughal court in this period was in disarray, Maratha power spread across the Deccan, and the Marathas were accepted, if reluctantly, by successive Mughal viceroys as a force that had to be accommodated. From the standpoint of political organization, 1707–1713 accelerated the shift from a kingdom once directed by a warrior-king toward a polity in which Shahu actively brokered and confirmed obligations among powerful, increasingly autonomous subordinates; his kingship should not yet be reduced to ceremony. That structure created the conditions for the rise of a minister who could coordinate the network, a role filled from 1713 by the Peshwa.

Shahu's court was still a place where political decisions were taken, not a passive repository of royal prestige. Recognition of a chief's inheritance, title, or revenue assignment could settle a dispute that local force could not resolve cleanly. It also mattered which claimant to the Maratha throne a commander regarded as legitimate: rivals had to obtain seals, appointments, and a place in a chain of obligation intelligible to other officers. From this angle, Satara's authority was not simply shrinking as Pune's influence grew. Royal decisions helped establish the arrangements within which Peshwa ministers later operated, and the king retained real options in choosing among competing advisors. The distinction between political authority and the location of military and fiscal resources becomes more important as the eighteenth century proceeds.

Section 58.10

Balaji Vishwanath and the Rise of the Peshwa Office

Balaji Vishwanath Bhat (c. 1660–1720), a Chitpavan Brahman from Shrivardhan in the Konkan, came to Shahu's attention as a skilled revenue administrator who had served in various official capacities under Dhanaji Jadhav and his son. He helped win Shahu's early supporters and, in 1713, Shahu appointed him Peshwa, replacing Bahiropant Pingale. Balaji's first achievements were diplomatic: he negotiated an agreement in 1713 with Kanhoji Angre, who controlled much of the Konkan coast and had been loyal to Tarabai, bringing him to Shahu's side with confirmation of a number of coastal forts and territories. Through similar agreements with other commanders, he reduced the threat of the Kolhapur rival and consolidated Shahu's rule.

The rise of a Brahman Peshwa in a state whose king was of Maratha descent was significant. Brahman officials had long served in Deccan government, and Shahu's reliance on Balaji and his family was due to their administrative skill, their loyalty, and their lack of an independent military following that threatened the throne. The Peshwa's role increasingly became that of coordinating the military commanders on the king's behalf, negotiating with the Mughals, raising money, and assigning revenue. Some Maratha military families resented the elevation of a Brahman family, and tensions between Brahman administrators and Maratha commanders were a recurring feature of eighteenth-century politics, though such tensions should not be reduced to caste conflict alone: they also reflected competition for resources and influence between ministers and military chiefs.

The most significant episode of Balaji's tenure was his involvement in Mughal politics. The Sayyid brothers, Husain Ali Khan and Abdullah Khan, who dominated the Mughal court after 1713 (Chapter 57), needed support against rivals. In 1718, Husain Ali Khan, as viceroy of the Deccan, concluded an agreement with Shahu's representatives, and in 1719 Balaji led a Maratha force to Delhi in support of the Sayyids against the emperor Farrukhsiyar, who was deposed. In return, the Mughal court issued farmans recognizing Shahu's rights to chauth and sardeshmukhi in the six Mughal subahs of the Deccan, and confirming his claim to swarajya, the territories earlier controlled by Shivaji. Shahu's mother Yesubai and other family members were released from captivity at this time.

The grants must be analyzed carefully. They conferred formal recognition on claims that the Marathas had already been collecting by force; for the Mughals, they were a means to buy peace, to convert Maratha raiders into imperial agents, and to secure military assistance. The grants carried conditions: Shahu was to supply a contingent of troops for imperial service, to pay an annual sum, and to maintain order in the territories concerned. The Marathas' interpretation was different: they viewed the grants as recognition of sovereign rights. And the actual collection of revenue depended less on imperial recognition than on Maratha military capacity and the agreement of local officers, as the next sections show. The grants gave the Marathas a legal basis to present claims to Mughal officials and local landholders, and they set the pattern for later negotiations over tribute with the Mughal court and its successors.

Balaji Vishwanath died in April 1720, leaving the Peshwa office significantly stronger than he found it. His success rested on his ability to negotiate among competing interests and to link the Satara court with the wider Mughal system, and the fact that his son was appointed to succeed him began the pattern of hereditary Peshwa succession, a significant departure from the notion of the office as a royal appointment.

The negotiations of 1718–1719 show the practical value of operating within more than one political system. Shahu could appeal to the memory of Shivaji's independence while Balaji Vishwanath sought imperial recognition from a Mughal court whose authority had become fractured. To Mughal ministers, granting claims to revenue could turn an expensive enemy into a conditional ally; to Maratha officers, the grant became a credential to present to local officials who had previously resisted their demands. Neither side necessarily understood the agreement as a surrender of all its other claims. That ambiguity explains why receiving documents was only the beginning: payment, military service, protection of roads, and recognition by provincial officials had to be negotiated repeatedly, and the formal imperial order did not create uniform compliance across the Deccan.

Section 58.11

Baji Rao I and the Expansion of Maratha Power, 1720–1740

Baji Rao I succeeded his father as Peshwa in April 1720 at about twenty years of age, after Shahu passed over more senior claimants. The appointment was criticized by some in Shahu's court, but Baji Rao proved a talented soldier and a persuasive adviser. His political objective, expressed in later accounts of his discussions with Shahu, was to extend Maratha influence into the declining Mughal provinces of the north, above all Malwa and Gujarat, and to remove obstacles in the Deccan. His expansion rested on the fiscal rights acquired in 1719 and on the readiness of regional powers to bargain.

The Deccan had to be settled first. The Mughal viceroy of the Deccan, Nizam ul-Mulk Asaf Jah (Chapter 60), who had established a quasi-independent regime at Hyderabad after 1724, resisted Maratha claims to chauth and sardeshmukhi and encouraged Shahu's rivals at Kolhapur. Baji Rao pursued a campaign of maneuver, cutting off the Nizam's supplies and forcing him into a position of weakness at Palkhed, near Nashik, in February 1728. The Nizam's capitulation in the subsequent Treaty of Mungi Shevgaon, concluded in March 1728, recognized Shahu's claims in the Deccan and gave the Marathas a freer hand to the north. The Nizam later re-entered the contest, so that the relationship remained a combination of conflict and negotiation for decades.

Northward, Maratha columns pushed into Malwa from the early 1720s, defeating Mughal governors in a series of engagements; the imperial governor Giridhar Bahadur was defeated and killed at Amjhera in 1728. Malwa's rich agricultural districts and commercial towns, such as Ujjain and Indore, became bases for the Marathas, and the imperial court confirmed Maratha rights over time, in a sanad of 1741 under Muhammad Shah. In Gujarat, Maratha commanders, including the Dabhade and Gaekwad families, collected chauth, and the confrontation at Dabhoi in 1731, in which Baji Rao defeated the Senapati Trimbak Rao Dabhade, who had challenged the Peshwa, strengthened the Peshwa's position over the other Maratha chiefs and gave the Gaekwads their ascendancy in Gujarat.

In Bundelkhand, the Bundela ruler Chhatrasal appealed in 1729 for help against the Mughal commander Muhammad Khan Bangash; Baji Rao's intervention brought a share of territory, and on Chhatrasal's death in 1731 Maratha influence in the region expanded. Baji Rao's most daring move was the advance on Delhi in 1737, when his forces appeared outside the capital, demonstrating the emperor's weakness. In response, the Mughal court mobilized the Nizam, who marched from the Deccan; Baji Rao outmaneuvered him and besieged his army near Bhopal in December 1737. The Nizam was forced to agree, at Doraha in January 1738, to cede Malwa and pay an indemnity, though the promised terms were not entirely fulfilled. The following year Nadir Shah's invasion of India overwhelmed the Mughal army at Karnal and sacked Delhi (Chapter 57), and the Marathas did not engage his main army, despite Baji Rao's discussions of a possible northward response; the ensuing weakening of Delhi enlarged the political opportunities available to regional powers.

Baji Rao also turned to the west coast. In 1737–39, a Maratha army commanded by his brother Chimaji Appa captured the Portuguese fortified town of Vasai (Bassein) and other positions in the northern Konkan after a costly siege, ending an important Portuguese presence in the region. Conflict with the Siddis of Janjira continued.

Historians have asked why Maratha armies were so successful. Mobility mattered: light cavalry could cover long distances, live off the land, and avoid costly sieges, as in the Palkhed campaign. Regional alliances were crucial: Baji Rao made use of disaffected local rulers, Rajput princes, and Bundela chiefs, and avoided fighting except when he had a clear advantage. The system of revenue claims provided incentives for commanders and funded expansion, though at the cost of heavy borrowing. And the Mughal state's weakness offered openings. It would be misleading to attribute his success to cavalry tactics alone. Baji Rao died of a fever in April 1740 at Raverkhedi on the Narmada while on campaign, leaving a political position much expanded beyond the Deccan, but also a fiscal burden and a system of semi-autonomous commanders that his successors would have to manage.

The Peshwa's position in Malwa and Gujarat nevertheless remained different from his position in the western Deccan. Northern collections were sometimes negotiated through existing Mughal revenue officers and hereditary landholding families, leaving Maratha commanders dependent on intermediaries and vulnerable to changing local alliances. The commanders who acquired the responsibility to collect revenue also developed followings of their own, since campaigns required loans, horses, supplies, and reliable associates. Expansion thus created a recurring institutional problem: how could Pune direct military men whose resources and regional knowledge increasingly lay outside Maharashtra? Baji Rao's personal authority and continued success could manage this tension for a time, but the problem survived him and would become more pronounced when distant theaters had to be coordinated simultaneously.

Section 58.12

Balaji Baji Rao and the Growth of the Maratha Political Network, 1740–1761

Balaji Baji Rao, known as Nanasaheb, succeeded his father in 1740 at the age of about nineteen. Over the next two decades he presided over the largest expansion of Maratha influence, and over a significant relocation of power: from Satara, where the king resided, to Pune, where the Peshwa built his administrative capital, adding to the palace complex of Shaniwar Wada begun by Baji Rao. Pune grew into a large commercial and administrative city, home to bankers, officials, and artisans, and the Peshwa's secretariat, the huzur daftar, became the center of the state's records and finance.

Shahu, who had no surviving son, died on 15 December 1749. The succession was disputed. A young man named Ramaraja was brought forward as Shahu's heir, with the support of Tarabai, who claimed he was her grandson and her own son's offspring, a claim some contemporaries doubted. Nanasaheb, supported by many commanders, was determined not to allow Ramaraja to appoint his own ministers. In 1750, the Treaty of Sangola set up an arrangement under which the king remained at Satara with ceremonial prestige while the Peshwa governed in his name; Ramaraja was kept at Satara under guard, and Tarabai came into open conflict with the Peshwa. In practice, the Peshwa became the effective head of the Maratha government, while the Chhatrapati remained a legitimating symbol.

Territorial expansion proceeded in several directions. In the east, Raghuji Bhonsle of Nagpur, with the Peshwa's occasional support and rivalry, launched repeated raids into Bengal and Orissa from 1742, extracting payments from the Nawab Alivardi Khan; in 1751, a settlement ceded Orissa's administration to the Marathas and committed Bengal to an annual payment. The raids, remembered as the bargi invasions, devastated parts of western Bengal and left a durable memory of suffering, even as historians debate the scale of the damage. In the south, the Peshwa's forces intervened in the Karnatak and the Deccan, and in 1760 a force under Sadashivrao Bhau defeated the Nizam's army at Udgir, extracting significant territory. In Malwa, Gujarat, and Rajasthan, Shinde and Holkar commanders established themselves as regional powers. In April 1752, Safdar Jang negotiated a Maratha protection agreement involving proposed payments, offices, and fiscal rights in Punjab and neighboring regions; however, Emperor Ahmad Shah Bahadur declined to ratify the agreement after a separate settlement with Ahmad Shah Durrani, limiting what Maratha commanders could claim from the proposed terms.

This expansion produced a distinctive form of political network. The Peshwa appointed commanders and officials, assigned revenue, and arbitrated disputes, but each of the large commanders retained independent forces, finances, and territory. The Peshwa's government used the leverage it had, appointments, confirmation of saranjams, and joint campaigns, to hold the network together, but growing territory increased the autonomy of distant commanders. The financing of expansion through loans from bankers, and the Peshwa's rising debts, laid the groundwork for the fiscal pressures discussed in Section 58.13. The tensions of expansion, between Pune and the commanders in the field, and between the pursuit of tribute and the capacity to govern, are the subject of Division IV.

The Bengal expeditions underscore the difference between a campaign that secured tribute and an administration that protected a population. A negotiated payment might reduce raids for a period, yet it could not by itself restore households displaced by previous attacks or the trade disrupted by uncertainty. Maratha commanders also had to decide whether revenue would be claimed by the Peshwa's agents, a provincial ruler, or a semi-independent chief such as Raghuji Bhonsle. Those overlapping claims created possibilities for conflict within the Maratha network as well as with local states. At the same time, the rise of Pune as an administrative center required a new concentration of recordkeeping, bankers' credit, and specialized clerical labor. Political expansion therefore involved both new institutions at the center and increasing differentiation of authority beyond it.

Section 58.13

Chauth, Sardeshmukhi, Revenue Collection, and Military Finance

Chauth (from chauthai, a fourth) was a claim to one quarter of the assessed revenue of a territory, and sardeshmukhi was an additional claim to ten percent, based on the hereditary right of the sardeshmukh, the chief of deshmukhs, which Shivaji's family had claimed over the Deccan. Neither was a tax in the ordinary sense of a regular payment to a sovereign who governed the territory. Chauth was often described by contemporaries as a payment for protection from Maratha attack, and, in the 1719 grants, it was reframed as a legitimate revenue assignment attached to imperial service. Sardeshmukhi invoked a claim to superior hereditary jurisdiction, but its legality, collection, and incidence remained contested, especially where other authorities exercised revenue rights.

In practice the system worked through negotiation and coercion. Maratha officers (kamavisdars and mamlatdars) assessed the territory on the basis of existing Mughal or local records, calculated the theoretical share, and demanded payment, sometimes in annual lump sums negotiated with local officials or landholders, sometimes through collection from individual villages. Armed detachments accompanied the demand. Landholders and zamindars might prefer to pay than to risk raiding, and frequently bargained down the amount or paid in installments. Mughal officials and later successor states, such as Hyderabad and Bengal, resisted or paid under pressure; the sum actually received was often well below the nominal claim. Distinguishing the formal claim from realized collections is essential: a claim to twenty-five percent of assessed revenue did not mean that twenty-five percent was received.

The revenue was divided among the Peshwa's treasury, the commanders who collected it, and the holders of assignments. By the mid-eighteenth century, the Marathas had developed a system of mokasa (grants of a share of revenue) and saranjam (military assignments), by which commanders were assigned territories or revenue rights in return for maintaining troops. These assignments could be hereditary in practice, creating powerful households, and the center regularly attempted to audit and reclaim them. Expensive campaigns relied on credit: bankers, including the great banking houses of Pune and northern India, advanced money against future revenue, and the Peshwa's debts rose steadily. Military finance therefore bound the Maratha leadership to a class of financiers whose willingness to lend shaped what campaigns were possible.

The consequences for ordinary people varied. For cultivators in territories paying chauth, the Maratha demand was an additional burden on top of Mughal or local revenue, though in some cases it replaced the risk of raiding. In regions under direct Maratha administration, the demand followed the established customs of the area. Local landholders might be offered recognition of their rights in return for collecting for the Marathas, which could strengthen their position. Merchants and bankers had to pay or provide loans, and the passage of armies disrupted trade. Grain-carriers, camp followers, and the many people who supplied and serviced armies depended on military spending for income. The extractive element is real; but so are the incentives and bargains that made the system durable. Fiscal claims also functioned as assertions of authority: to pay chauth was to acknowledge Maratha power, a point that helps explain why recipients and payers negotiated over terms and formal recognition.

The distinction between a right on paper and an amount actually remitted should guide any attempt to quantify Maratha power. If a district's nominal assessment was high but cultivation had declined, an officer could not necessarily obtain the stated share. Collectors also had to negotiate deductions, military expenses, arrears, and rival assignments. A payment might be advanced by a banker and settled later from a harvest, making fiscal administration simultaneously a system of credit and political coercion. The way sums appeared in records therefore reflected claims, obligations, and bargains rather than a transparent measure of wealth extracted. M. A. Nayeem's research into the working of chauth and sardeshmukhi in Mughal Deccan provinces illustrates why regional documentary evidence is necessary before generalizing from the familiar fractions of one-fourth and one-tenth.

Historical Vocabulary
Chauth and Sardeshmukhi

Chauth was a claim to one quarter of the assessed revenue of a territory, and sardeshmukhi an additional claim of ten percent, grounded in the hereditary rights of the chief deshmukh. Both were claims made on territories outside the Marathas' directly administered core, swarajya, and could be negotiated, paid in lump sums, resisted, or recognized by treaty.

Modern English "tax" is misleading: these were closer to tribute or protection payments, and their political meaning depended on whether the payer accepted them as a legitimate obligation or paid under duress. They should never be assumed to have been collected in full.

Section 58.14

The Maratha Confederacy: Sardars, Military Households, and Regional Authority

By the 1740s the Maratha polity included a number of powerful commanders whose households, largely founded in the service of Baji Rao I, had acquired independent resources and regional bases. Historians often call the resulting system the Maratha confederacy, but the term should be used with caution: it was not a federation established by a constitution, and its components had different origins and changing relationships with the Peshwa.

The Shinde (Scindia) family, founded by Ranoji Shinde, a Maratha of modest origin who had served the Peshwa's household, received territories in Malwa and based itself at Ujjain. Ranoji's successors, Jayappa and Dattaji, and then Mahadji, became prominent in northern politics; Jayappa was killed at Nagaur in 1755, and Dattaji at Barari Ghat near Delhi in 1760. The Holkars, founded by Malhar Rao Holkar (1693–1766), who rose from a modest background in the Peshwa's service, held Indore and parts of Malwa, and their influence extended to Rajasthan and the Doab. The Gaekwads, led by Pilaji and then Damaji, established themselves in Gujarat, with Baroda as a center, through an uneven struggle involving the Dabhade senapati household, the Peshwas, and local authorities. The Bhonsles of Nagpur, descended from Parsoji and Raghuji Bhonsle, ruled Berar and parts of Gondwana and Orissa, with a tradition of independence from the Peshwa and a claim to descent from the royal Bhonsles. Other important families included the Pawars of Dhar and Dewas, the Patwardhans, and the Pratinidhi, Sachiv, and other hereditary ministers of the Satara court.

These households differed in origin and in their relationship with the center. The Shindes, Holkars, and Gaekwads owed their rise to Peshwa patronage but built their own armies, administrations, and finances. The Bhonsles of Nagpur had separate origins and a long record of acting on their own in the east. Their military resources varied, as did their revenue territories and financial capacities. Their degree of autonomy shifted with circumstances: strong Peshwas, like Baji Rao I and Madhavrao I, could assert control, while weak or distracted ones allowed greater independence.

Stewart Gordon has argued that the Maratha system should be understood as a loose political network held together by common interests, shared legitimacy through the Chhatrapati and the Peshwa, and the profitable business of tribute collection, rather than as a centralized empire. The sardars were not simply subordinates, nor were they independent rulers; they were partners and rivals in a shared enterprise. Disputes over territory, over the division of revenue, and over succession within the households were common, and the Peshwa's role as arbiter was crucial. These tensions grew as expansion extended the network's reach and as the commanders who ruled distant provinces developed local interests.

The word 'confederacy' can also conceal the fact that different households acquired regional power in different ways. Some received assignments in the course of Peshwa campaigns; others had older hereditary claims or negotiated their position as the Peshwa's political influence grew. Control of an important town was not always equivalent to control of its entire hinterland, and a commander could collect contributions in one district while leaving its administrative officers in place. As a result, political maps showing continuous Maratha territory at one date risk exaggerating the uniformity of rule. The shared ability to invoke the Chhatrapati and cooperate in campaigns coexisted with disputes over appointments, succession, border contributions, and loyalty. This tension was a structural property of the system, not just the product of individual ambition.

Historical Debate

Was Maratha Expansion a Process of Imperial Integration, Confederate State-Building, or Decentralized Military Extraction?

The question: How should historians characterize the Maratha political network of the eighteenth century and its relationship to the territories it claimed?

Interpretation A — Confederate state-building: Stewart Gordon, The Marathas 1600–1818, emphasizes the institutional capacity of the Maratha system: the Peshwa government, the saranjam system, the use of bankers, and the creation of regional administrations. On this view, the Marathas built a flexible political structure, a "portable" or confederate system, that could claim and govern territory across a vast area by negotiating with local powers.
Interpretation B — Decentralized extraction: Andre Wink, Land and Sovereignty in India, interprets Maratha expansion as an extension of fitna, the politics of disorder and rival claims, in which the Marathas exploited the weakness of established states to collect tribute without creating stable government. Earlier historians such as Jadunath Sarkar were also critical of the character of Maratha rule beyond the Deccan, while nationalist-minded writers presented it as an effort to create an all-India Hindu empire.
Interpretation C — Imperial integration: Some scholars have stressed the Marathas' engagement with Mughal institutions and legitimacy, including the imperial grants, the pursuit of Mughal offices, and the Peshwa's relation to the emperor, and have seen Maratha expansion as an attempt to inherit and reshape the imperial system rather than to overthrow it.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on the Peshwa Daftar, Mughal and Persian documents, European observers, and local records. The variation in practice across regions makes generalization difficult: Gujarat, Malwa, Orissa, and the Deccan were governed very differently.

Why it matters: The debate shapes how historians judge the Marathas' impact on the regions they entered and how they explain both their successes and their later difficulties, including the response of other powers.

Section 58.15

Maratha Military Organization, Diplomacy, and Regional Alliances

The eighteenth-century Maratha army differed substantially from Shivaji's. Cavalry remained the arm of choice for rapid operations, and the major commanders maintained large cavalry forces recruited from the Deccan and Malwa, with pay often supported by the saranjam system and commanders' independent finances. Contingents of different commanders were organized loosely, with the Peshwa or his deputy exercising overall command only in the biggest campaigns. The army traveled with a large retinue of camp followers, merchants, and bankers, making it slow and logistically demanding in extended operations, notably in the north.

Infantry and artillery became more important as the century progressed. The Marathas had used forts and sieges from the start, and in the eighteenth century they maintained artillery and bodies of infantry, including foreign mercenaries. The best-known European-trained units were those led by Ibrahim Khan Gardi, who had served under French officers in the Deccan and brought a disciplined musket-armed infantry and artillery to the Peshwa's service around 1759, and later Mahadji Shinde's forces trained by Europeans such as Benoit de Boigne, whose activities belong mainly to Chapter 64. Yet European-style units did not simply replace older forms: cavalry and traditional forces continued in large numbers, and the Marathas used both according to the task, as the Panipat campaign shows (Section 58.18).

Diplomacy was integral to Maratha operations. In the north, the Marathas negotiated with Rajput rulers, including those of Jaipur, Jodhpur, and Mewar, who could be pressed to pay tribute or enlisted as allies, and Maratha intervention in Rajput succession disputes created resentments as well as alliances. They dealt with the Jat chiefs, especially Suraj Mal of Bharatpur; with the Rohilla Afghans, whose leader Najib ud-Daula became a pivotal and ambiguous figure; with the Nizam of Hyderabad; and with the Mughal court and its wazirs. Alliances were changeable, based on interest, not ideology, and the Marathas often found that those they had forced to pay tribute became enemies at a critical moment.

Intelligence and communication were important, supported by a network of letter-carriers and news-writers, and by the correspondence preserved in the Peshwa Daftar. Negotiated contributions were the typical result of campaigns, and military operations were often a prelude to a bargain. The vulnerability of distant campaigns was obvious: the army depended on local supply and on credit from bankers, a failure of either could be crippling, and political changes in distant courts could turn allies into opponents. These vulnerabilities, apparent in the 1750s as the Marathas operated hundreds of miles from Pune, were important contributors to the disaster at Panipat.

Military diversity likewise affected the financial balance among commanders. A cavalry chief could field mobile forces relatively quickly if he had credit and access to horses, but a large infantry body and artillery train demanded additional specialists, powder, draught animals, trained crews, and dependable supply routes. Improvements in gunnery could provide important advantages in siege and open battle without removing the vulnerability of an army whose pay and food depended on several distant treasuries. Nor should the employment of soldiers trained by European officers be taken to imply the replacement of South Asian military practice by a single foreign model. Commanders experimented, adopted useful techniques, and continued to combine formations according to terrain, resources, and political objectives. Panipat exposed the difficulty of coordinating such a composite force far from its main supply base.

Section 58.16

Northward Expansion, Delhi, Punjab, and the Contest for Imperial Authority

The Marathas' involvement in northern India deepened in the 1750s as the Mughal court, weakened by Nadir Shah's invasion of 1739 and repeated Afghan incursions led by Ahmad Shah Durrani (Chapter 57), sought allies. The defensive agreement negotiated by Mughal wazir Safdar Jang on 12 April 1752 proposed that Maratha commanders defend the empire against Afghan and other threats in exchange for payments, offices, and specified revenue rights. The emperor did not subsequently ratify that arrangement, so its proposed concessions must not be confused with fully operative imperial grants. The wazir's rivals objected, and the arrangement was never completely carried out, but it brought the Marathas into the center of imperial politics. In the following years, Maratha commanders under Holkar and Shinde, frequently allied with the Mughal wazir Imad ul-Mulk, intervened in the succession and administration at Delhi, and in 1754 the Marathas helped depose the emperor Ahmad Shah Bahadur and install Alamgir II.

The key turning point was the Afghan sack of Delhi in 1757. Raghunath Rao (Raghoba), the Peshwa's brother, led a large expedition north in 1757–58, expelled the Rohilla leader Najib ud-Daula from his control of Delhi's affairs, and advanced into Punjab. In April 1758, Maratha forces under Raghunath Rao and Malhar Rao Holkar took Lahore from Ahmad Shah's son Timur Shah, and Maratha detachments reached Attock on the Indus. Adina Beg Khan, a Punjabi administrator who had made himself indispensable, was appointed governor of Punjab under Maratha overlordship, though he died in September 1758. The Maratha claim to Punjab was thus short-lived and thinly rooted, but it appeared to extend Maratha influence from the Deccan to the Indus.

The relationship between Maratha ambition and Mughal legitimacy deserves emphasis. The Marathas did not seek to abolish the Mughal emperor; they sought to control the court and to claim imperial offices and revenues in his name. Maratha leaders held positions such as deputy governor of imperial provinces, and the Peshwa pursued titles and recognition through the Delhi court. This was a strategy of using Mughal legitimacy, as the Mughals' own successors in Bengal, Awadh, and Hyderabad did (Chapter 60), rather than replacing it. A careful distinction is needed among four things: holding imperial offices, collecting tribute, occupying territory, and directly administering it. In the north the Marathas usually did the first two, sometimes the third, and rarely the fourth.

Maratha expansion created strategic problems. It alarmed Ahmad Shah Durrani, who had claims on Punjab and Delhi and who returned to India in 1759. It antagonized Rajput and Jat rulers who resented Maratha demands and interference, and Najib ud-Daula, who lost influence, became a determined enemy. Shuja ud-Daula, the nawab of Awadh, feared Maratha encroachment and was drawn toward the Afghan side. The Marathas' lack of a stable administrative presence in the north, their dependence on credit and local supplies, and the distance from their home base made them vulnerable. By 1759, the Peshwa's government faced a confrontation in which it had overextended its position, the subject of Division V.

Maratha authority in Punjab was especially vulnerable because the territory remained connected to Afghan political and military networks and possessed provincial elites whose interests did not necessarily align with the Peshwa. The participation of local intermediaries such as Adina Beg could ease an expedition's advance but could not instantly build a stable fiscal administration. The distance between a temporary occupation of Lahore or Attock and regular governance was considerable: commanders needed garrisons, reliable revenue flows, accommodation with landed groups, and the ability to resist renewed attacks across the northwest. The northern campaigns must therefore be understood as tests of the Maratha network's capacity for more sustained imperial rule, not simply as proof that any territory through which cavalry passed had become securely governed from Pune.

Section 58.17

The Road to the Third Battle of Panipat, 1758–1760

The Maratha capture of Lahore and Attock in 1758 provoked a response that the Peshwa's government had underestimated. Ahmad Shah Durrani, ruler of the Afghan state centered at Kabul and Kandahar, regarded Punjab, Sindh, and Kashmir as part of his domain, and Timur Shah's expulsion from Lahore was a direct challenge. He returned to India in 1759 with an experienced army, retook Punjab with little difficulty as Maratha garrisons withdrew, and moved on the Doab, where he found a ready ally in Najib ud-Daula and the Rohillas, who had suffered at Maratha hands. In January 1760, at Barari Ghat near Delhi, a Durrani force destroyed a Maratha column commanded by Dattaji Shinde, who was killed. The defeat revealed that the Marathas' northern holdings were insecure and that their field armies could be defeated and isolated.

The Peshwa responded by assembling a much larger force. Sadashivrao Bhau, Nanasaheb's cousin and an able administrator who had recently won the victory at Udgir against the Nizam, took command, accompanied by Vishwasrao, the Peshwa's eldest son, who was nominally the leader. The army included major contingents under Malhar Rao Holkar, Jankoji Shinde, Damaji Gaekwad's representatives, and others, along with Ibrahim Khan Gardi's infantry and artillery, and a very large number of camp followers, including families and pilgrims. It moved north from the Deccan in 1760 and reached Delhi in August, capturing the city and stripping valuable ornaments from the palace to raise funds, a measure that reflected the urgency of its financial position. In October 1760, the Marathas captured the Afghan garrison at Kunjpura, a significant but costly success.

Coalition-building was the decisive issue, and it failed. Bhau and his advisers hoped to attract Rajput rulers, the Jats, and Shuja ud-Daula of Awadh, but earlier Maratha demands and interventions had alienated many of them, and Maratha commanders disagreed over strategy. Suraj Mal of Bharatpur, who had been a significant potential ally, withdrew after a dispute over strategy and the treatment of his territory. Shuja ud-Daula, who had been negotiating with both sides, joined Ahmad Shah in the autumn of 1760, partly through Najib ud-Daula's diplomacy and partly because of his own calculation that a Maratha victory would threaten Awadh. The Durrani coalition thus combined Afghan cavalry, the Rohillas, and the forces of Awadh, supported by the Indo-Muslim political networks of the Doab.

Logistics worsened the situation. From October 1760, the two armies took positions near Panipat, north of Delhi, and each fortified its camp. The Maratha army was entrenched, relying on supplies brought by grain merchants and transporters, while Afghan light cavalry cut communications and raided supply columns. Durrani's forces, camped on better ground with access to the Doab's resources, were able to maintain their supplies; the Marathas' grew critically short. Reinforcements and funds from the Deccan were delayed, and the death of Govind Pant Bundele, a Maratha officer killed in December 1760 while leading an effort to disrupt Afghan supply lines in the Doab, removed an important source of provisions and intelligence. Negotiations were attempted, with Shuja ud-Daula and Kashi Raj, among others, as intermediaries, but did not produce a settlement. By January, the Maratha camp was in severe distress.

It is tempting to explain the outcome by pointing to one decision or one commander's error, whether Bhau's choice to entrench, disagreements with Holkar, or the failure to retreat earlier. A more convincing explanation recognizes layered causes: the overextension of Maratha ambitions into a region where they lacked a stable political base; a diplomatic strategy that alienated potential allies; the logistics of maintaining a very large army, encumbered with non-combatants, far from home; fiscal strain; and the strength and skill of Ahmad Shah's coalition. The Marathas, in other words, had built a political network whose methods, mobile forces, negotiated tribute, and local alliances, worked well in some regions but could not easily meet a determined, well-led opponent in the north.

The documentary record also complicates the idea that the battle was inevitable after Maratha entry into Punjab. Negotiations occurred at several stages, and leading actors differed about which territories or forms of protection mattered most. Suraj Mal's withdrawal, Shuja ud-Daula's eventual alignment with the Afghan coalition, and the responses of Rajput courts were political choices shaped by previous dealings with Maratha commanders, not expressions of an unchanging religious solidarity. Even the decision to maintain a fortified camp reflected an attempt to protect an army containing artillery, officials, transports, and dependents. The difficulty was that once surrounding grain routes were contested, the encampment's protective function could become a trap. Explaining defeat thus requires reconstructing how diplomacy, military movement, and provisions interacted over months.

Section 58.18

The Third Battle of Panipat, 14 January 1761

By early January 1761, supplies in the Maratha camp had run low and, according to several accounts, soldiers and animals were starving. Pressure within the camp mounted for either a negotiated withdrawal or a decisive battle, and the Maratha leadership, after discussions that sources describe differently, decided to leave the entrenchments and fight. On the morning of 14 January 1761, the Maratha army marched out of its camp in battle formation. The date and the identity of the main opposing coalitions are firmly established: a Maratha army under Sadashivrao Bhau and Vishwasrao faced Ahmad Shah Durrani's Afghan forces, together with the Rohillas under Najib ud-Daula and the army of Shuja ud-Daula of Awadh.

The Maratha formation placed the infantry and artillery of Ibrahim Khan Gardi on one wing, the cavalry of Holkar and Shinde and other commanders on another, and the main body under Bhau and Vishwasrao in the center, though the details of the deployment vary in the sources. The Durrani army consisted principally of Afghan cavalry, including heavy cavalry and light horse, camel-borne swivel guns, and Rohilla and Awadh troops. The fighting began in the morning with an artillery exchange and a Maratha attack that, in several accounts, initially made progress, with Gardi's infantry inflicting heavy losses on the Rohilla troops opposite. The Maratha cavalry in the center charged and fought hand-to-hand with Afghan forces.

The turning point came in the early afternoon. Vishwasrao was struck and killed, and his death, which became known through the army, shook morale. Bhau, according to accounts, dismounted and continued to fight, and was lost in the melee; his body was later identified. Fresh Durrani reserves were committed against exhausted Maratha forces, and the Maratha line collapsed. A large part of the Maratha army, and many of the camp followers, were killed in the rout and pursuit, and others were enslaved or died afterwards. Some commanders, including Malhar Rao Holkar and Mahadji Shinde, who was wounded, escaped, and later Maratha tradition criticized Holkar for leaving the field, a charge that is contested and not easily resolved from the evidence.

Accounts of numbers and casualties vary enormously, and historians should treat all figures with caution. Contemporary sources, Maratha, Persian, and European, produced estimates of troops and of the dead that range widely, and it is difficult to separate combatants from the large numbers of non-combatants. Most modern historians accept that the dead numbered in the tens of thousands, and some estimates are far higher, but no precise figure can be established. The loss of experienced leaders, including Bhau and Vishwasrao, along with many commanders and a large portion of the army, was devastating.

The consequences were severe. Maratha leadership was disrupted; the Peshwa Nanasaheb, who had been moving north with reinforcements, received the news and died in June 1761, said by tradition to have been broken by it. The fiscal consequences were large, since the loss of troops, equipment, and treasure left the government in debt. Maratha influence in Punjab and the northern regions was sharply curtailed, and relationships with regional allies were damaged. Yet the defeat must be distinguished from the destruction of Maratha power. Ahmad Shah, whose army was also depleted, and who faced pressure to return to Afghanistan, withdrew from India without establishing permanent control of the north. The Maratha core in Maharashtra, the households of the Shindes, Holkars, Gaekwads, and Bhonsles, and the institutions of the Peshwa government survived, a point developed in the next section.

Accounts of Panipat were written by people in very different positions: participants within the Afghan-led coalition, Maratha correspondents whose reports circulated before the battle, survivors, and later chroniclers seeking to understand the defeat. They cannot automatically be combined into a single unproblematic order of battle. The sequence of charges, the role of the artillery, and the point at which particular commanders withdrew are especially vulnerable to retrospective blame. Kashi Raj's narrative is important partly because it preserves an account from the coalition's side, not because its perspective settles every disagreement. The human losses encompassed soldiers and many accompanying civilians, whose experiences are less visible in operational accounts. A responsible synthesis conveys the scale of the catastrophe without treating contested casualty totals as measured statistics.

Section 58.19

Maratha Recovery, Madhavrao I, and the Political Legacy of Expansion

Nanasaheb's death in June 1761 left the Peshwaship to his seventeen-year-old son Madhavrao I, who was in practice guided at first by his uncle Raghunathrao (Raghoba) as regent. The disaster at Panipat had weakened the government's authority, and factional conflict at Pune soon broke out. Raghunathrao's ambitions and Madhavrao's growing assertion of independence led to open conflict in 1762–63, and the Nizam exploited the factional turmoil of 1762 and invaded Maratha territory; his forces sacked Pune in April–May 1763. Madhavrao's response, a combination of military action and negotiation, defeated the Nizam at Rakshasbhuvan in August 1763 and restored Maratha dominance in the Deccan. The young Peshwa, by temperament cautious, a careful administrator and an able soldier, gradually reasserted central control over the commanders.

Madhavrao also confronted the rising power of Haidar Ali of Mysore (Chapter 59), campaigning in the south in 1764–65 and again later, and with limited but real success, forcing Haidar to pay tribute. He reorganized finances, audited saranjams, and reasserted the Peshwa's claim to oversee the revenue of the Maratha commanders, though he could not eliminate their autonomy. His government was seen by contemporaries as a period of order and recovery, and many later Marathi historians remembered him as the ablest of the Peshwas, though his reign was cut short by tuberculosis. He died on 18 November 1772.

Recovery in the north came through the major commanders. Mahadji Shinde, who had survived Panipat though lame from a wound, rebuilt his forces and political position, using his base in Ujjain and his relationship with the Peshwa. With Tukoji Holkar and other leaders, he led campaigns from 1769–70 against the Jats and Rohillas who had cooperated with the Afghans, regained influence over Rajput rulers, and, after the death of Najib ud-Daula in 1770, re-established a Maratha presence in the Doab. The Mughal emperor Shah Alam II, who had spent years in exile under the protection of Awadh and the English East India Company, accepted Maratha protection in 1771; escorted by Mahadji Shinde's forces, he returned to Delhi in January 1772, restoring the emperor to his capital under Maratha influence.

Recovery after a severe defeat illuminates the nature of Maratha institutions. The Peshwa's government survived because the network of commanders, bankers, and officeholders on which it relied was geographically dispersed and not dependent on a single center or army. Panipat destroyed an army and a generation of leaders, but not the households or the system of revenue claims and assignments. Indeed, the commanders' autonomy, which weakened coordination, also meant that a defeat in one theater did not destroy the whole system. The longer-term consequences were nonetheless significant: the Peshwa's authority over the commanders was not fully restored, the Peshwa's government remained burdened by debt, and the political center of gravity shifted increasingly to commanders such as Mahadji Shinde, whose power in the north would develop independently. Madhavrao's early death and a subsequent crisis at Pune after 1772, which opened the way to the conflicts examined in Chapter 64, would expose the fragility of the system. This section marks the end of the chapter's narrative: the later history of Maratha rule, including the Anglo-Maratha Wars, belongs to Chapter 64.

Madhavrao's recovery program also shows why rebuilding authority after Panipat required more than winning battles. Financial supervision, the confirmation or review of military assignments, and the management of family rivalries at Pune gave the Peshwa a renewed basis for action. The northern campaigns depended on commanders who had survived the defeat and could raise fresh forces from regional bases; success there therefore demonstrated the system's resilience while confirming its dispersed structure. The return of Shah Alam II was politically impressive, but it did not reunite the territories of the old Mughal Empire or place northern India under uniform Maratha administration. By the early 1770s the Marathas had reestablished influence without resolving the long-running question of who ultimately controlled the network's most powerful households.

Section 58.20

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has traced the development of Maratha power across roughly a century and a half, from a regional kingdom in the Sahyadri to the largest indigenous political network of eighteenth-century South Asia. Its central argument is that this was not the steady expansion of one polity, but a series of related transformations. Shivaji built a kingdom from Deccan materials, forts, hereditary officeholders, military service, and sultanate administrative practice, and gave it sovereign status by a ritual coronation in 1674. The war against Aurangzeb transformed it into a decentralized system of commanders bound to a symbolic throne by grants and recognition. Shahu's settlement, negotiated rather than imposed, produced a court at Satara that arbitrated rights, and the Peshwas, beginning with Balaji Vishwanath and powerfully with Baji Rao I and Nanasaheb, turned that court's ministerial office into the practical executive center. Through chauth, sardeshmukhi, saranjams, and credit, the Peshwa government and the sardar households created an expanding network of claims reaching from Bengal to the Indus.

Four relationships structured that history. The first was between central leadership and regional autonomy: every success in extending the network enlarged the independence of commanders, so that the capacity to coordinate was always in tension with the logic of expansion. The second was between military success and fiscal demands. Armies were paid through revenue claims and bankers' credit, which created incentives to expand and made the system vulnerable to both debt and logistical failure. The third was between political legitimacy and practical control. The Maratha leadership drew on Brahmanical kingship, Mughal imperial grants, and claims to hereditary right, but in much of its sphere it collected tribute without governing. The fourth was between expansion and overextension, most visible in the northern campaigns that culminated at Panipat.

The historiographical debates of the chapter bear directly on these relationships. Whether Shivaji's polity was an innovation or a reorganization of Deccan institutions affects how one reads continuity across the whole period, and whether the eighteenth-century network is best called an empire, a confederacy, or a system of decentralized extraction depends on which regions and which forms of evidence one emphasizes. The evidence supports the view that Maratha power was institutionally creative within inherited structures, that its character changed over time, and that it varied by region.

Panipat was a severe defeat, with enormous human cost, but it did not end Maratha political power. The recovery under Madhavrao I and Mahadji Shinde, and the return of Shah Alam II to Delhi in 1772, show that the political network could absorb a loss that would have been fatal to a more centralized state. At the same time, the recovery was incomplete, leaving unresolved problems of finance and coordination that later crises would expose.

The Marathas should be understood alongside the other regional powers of the eighteenth century. Chapter 59 examines the Sikh misls and the Khalsa, Mysore under Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan, Travancore, and other states that, like the Marathas, built new military and fiscal systems amid Mughal decline. Chapter 60 turns to Bengal, Awadh, and Hyderabad, successor states that retained Mughal forms of government while asserting autonomy, and with which the Marathas negotiated and fought. Chapter 61 examines the European trading companies, whose growing involvement in Indian politics would eventually transform the contest among all these regional powers, and Chapter 64 returns to the Marathas in their confrontation with the East India Company.

A useful final test of the chapter's argument is to ask what remained constant amid the change. Hereditary local offices, correspondence networks, fiscal assignments, and the need to negotiate service linked Shivaji's world to the later Peshwas. Yet the scale and balance of power changed markedly: an independent ruler based in western Deccan forts became the starting point for a network operating across much of the subcontinent, in which ministers and regional military households could outrun the authority of the royal court. The paradox is that decentralization made coordination difficult and contributed to disasters such as Panipat, while the same distribution of resources made possible recovery. Later confrontations with the East India Company would test this institutional configuration under different conditions, addressed in Chapter 64.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the development of Maratha power from the regional kingdom of Shivaji to the largest indigenous political network of eighteenth-century South Asia. It has argued that the Marathas did not form a single, unchanging "empire," but a succession of related political systems: Shivaji's kingdom, the decentralized resistance to Aurangzeb, Shahu's Satara court, the Peshwa government, and a wider network in which powerful sardar households shared and contested authority.

The chapter began in the western Deccan before Shivaji, a region of competing sultanates, hereditary officeholders (watandars), fortified hills, and military-service networks. Maratha power grew from those materials. Shahji Bhonsle's career in Ahmadnagar and Bijapur service, and his family's jagir at Pune, provided the base from which Shivaji, from about 1646, began acquiring forts and building a following among Mavals, local officers, and Brahman administrators.

Shivaji's military system combined forts, mobile cavalry, infantry, intelligence, and a small navy. It cannot be reduced to guerrilla warfare, and it changed substantially over time. His confrontations with Bijapur and the Mughals, including the killing of Afzal Khan (1659), the raid on Shaista Khan (1663), the sack of Surat (1664), the Treaty of Purandar (1665), and the Agra visit and escape (1666), combined military action with repeated negotiation.

The coronation of 1674 converted military success into sovereign kingship. Through Vedic ritual, a constructed genealogy, and the title Chhatrapati, Shivaji asserted a status consistent with his power. Swarajya, in this period, meant the territories under direct royal rule, not a modern nationalist program. His government, with the Ashta Pradhan, a revenue system building on Deccan precedents, and fort administration, combined inherited and new elements, a question explored in the first historical debate.

After Shivaji's death in 1680, the Maratha polity survived the war against Aurangzeb through decentralization. Sambhaji's execution (1689), Rajaram's flight to Jinji, and Tarabai's regency (1700–1707) saw authority dispersed among commanders who raised their own troops, collected revenue, and bargained with the throne. The war also extended Maratha operations into Khandesh, Gujarat, and Malwa.

Shahu's release in 1707 led to a negotiated, contested reunification: Khed (1707), his enthronement at Satara, and the rival court at Kolhapur, settled only by the Treaty of Warna (1731). The rise of the Peshwa office under Balaji Vishwanath, and the Mughal grants of 1719 recognizing chauth, sardeshmukhi, and swarajya, created a political and fiscal framework for expansion. The grants gave legal form to claims already pursued, but did not guarantee collection.

Under Baji Rao I (1720–1740) and Balaji Baji Rao (1740–1761), Maratha influence extended into Malwa, Gujarat, Bundelkhand, Orissa and Bengal, and Delhi and Punjab. Success depended on mobility, alliances, revenue claims, and bankers' credit, not cavalry alone. After Shahu's death and the Treaty of Sangola (1750), the Peshwa became the effective head of government, with Pune the administrative center.

The chapter analyzed chauth and sardeshmukhi as claims distinct from direct taxation, and the confederacy of the Shindes, Holkars, Gaekwads, and Bhonsles as an evolving political system with varying autonomy, not a fixed federation. The second historical debate set confederate state-building, imperial integration, and decentralized extraction against one another. The experiences of cultivators, landholders, bankers, and officials differed widely, and the evidence for ordinary people is thin but indispensable.

The northward expansion of the 1750s, with the capture of Lahore and Attock, drew the Marathas into conflict with Ahmad Shah Durrani and his allies. The Third Battle of Panipat (14 January 1761) was a catastrophic defeat, produced by overextension, alienated allies, logistical failure, fiscal strain, and the strength of the opposing coalition. Panipat was a severe blow but not the end of Maratha power: under Madhavrao I and Mahadji Shinde the political network recovered, and Shah Alam II returned to Delhi in January 1772.

The chapter's larger lesson is that the Marathas were participants in the wider transformation of eighteenth-century South Asia, alongside the Sikhs, Mysore, and the Mughal successor states examined in Chapters 59–61. Their experience illustrates how political authority could be built through negotiation, hereditary rights, and revenue claims, and why decentralized systems could be both resilient and fragile.

Chapter Reflection

The history of the Marathas shows how political authority in early modern South Asia could be assembled from many small materials: fortresses, hereditary rights, bankers' loans, ritual, and the willingness of local people to cooperate or resist. It resists both the celebratory narrative in which a regional hero founded a nation and the dismissive one in which Maratha rule was only plunder. The evidence points to a more interesting story of institutional improvisation under pressure.

The chapter also shows that political systems change their character while keeping their names. The Maratha kingdom of 1674, the wartime resistance of 1700, the Satara court of 1720, and the Peshwa-led network of 1750 were connected by memory and personnel but organized quite differently. Recognizing those differences guards against a teleological history in which the later network was inevitable from the beginning.

Within the six-volume sequence, the Marathas connect the Mughal history of Volume III to the colonial history of Volume IV. They were the principal indigenous power that the East India Company would confront in the decades after 1772, and their system of revenue claims, military households, and negotiated sovereignty influenced the political landscape the Company encountered. Chapters 59 through 61 broaden this view by examining the other regional states and the European companies; Chapter 64 returns to the Maratha defeat. Students should carry forward the question of how decentralized systems succeed and fail, and how historians recover the experience of the many people on whom such systems depended.
