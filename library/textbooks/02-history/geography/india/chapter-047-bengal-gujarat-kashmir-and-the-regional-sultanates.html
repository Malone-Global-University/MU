Bengal, Gujarat, Kashmir, and the Regional Sultanates

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

In the 1370s, at Pandua in the Bengal delta, a congregational mosque rose on a scale that few buildings in the subcontinent had matched: a vast rectangular courtyard ringed by arcades, with a prayer hall of great brick vaults. Its builder, Sikandar Shah, ruled from a capital that owed nothing to Delhi, struck his own coins, and corresponded as an equal with distant courts. Within a few decades similar assertions of sovereignty were visible across the north: a new city called Ahmedabad on the Sabarmati, a fortified plateau capital at Mandu, mosques of unusual design at Jaunpur, and a Himalayan court at Srinagar presiding over a valley of rice, saffron, and weavers. None of these kingdoms was a minor fragment of something larger. Each was a functioning state with its own revenue, army, courtiers, languages, and public buildings.

Chapter 42 traced how the Tughlaq experiment in empire overreached, and how Timur's invasion of 1398–99 shattered what remained of central authority; Chapter 43 followed Delhi's partial reconstitution under the Sayyids and Lodis. This chapter looks at the other side of that story: what the decline of Delhi's power made possible. Provincial governors, frontier commanders, and local dynasties built states that outlasted the sultans who had appointed them. Chapters 45 and 46 examined the two great southern systems, Vijayanagara and the Bahmani successor states. Chapter 47 turns to five northern and eastern kingdoms, Bengal, Gujarat, Malwa, Jaunpur, and Kashmir, together with the smaller Faruqi sultanate of Khandesh, and asks why political fragmentation in this period coincided with remarkable economic and cultural vitality.

The five principal cases differ enormously, and the differences are the point. Bengal was a delta kingdom whose wealth came from rice, rivers, textiles, and an agricultural frontier that was still moving eastward. Gujarat was a maritime state whose merchants, shipowners, and bankers linked the Arabian Sea to the Red Sea, the Persian Gulf, East Africa, and Southeast Asia. Malwa was a plateau kingdom of fortresses and strategic passes, ruled from the spectacular hilltop city of Mandu. Jaunpur controlled part of the eastern Gangetic plain and became renowned for learning. Kashmir, enclosed by high mountains, developed a distinctive Sanskrit and Persian literary world and a craft economy known across Asia. Treating them together prevents the mistake of imagining a single "sultanate style" of government.

Historians reconstruct these kingdoms from a particular mixture of evidence. Coins and inscriptions, many of them still standing on mosque walls, record rulers' names, titles, and dates when narrative sources are thin. Persian chronicles such as the sixteenth- and seventeenth-century histories of Gujarat and the regional sections of general histories written at Mughal courts preserve court memory but often reflect the viewpoint of a later conquering empire. In Kashmir a rare Sanskrit chronicle tradition continued through the sultanate period. Travel accounts, including those of Ibn Battuta, Chinese envoys, and Portuguese observers, give outside views. Architecture is evidence too, and in some cases the principal evidence. Each of these source types has limits that this chapter will keep in view.

The chapter also resists two familiar simplifications. One treats regional sultanates as models of harmonious religious blending; the other treats them as theaters of continuous religious conflict. The evidence supports neither. Rulers and nobles were Muslims of varied origins, including Turks, Afghans, Persians, Africans, and local converts, while Hindu and Jain landholders, merchants, scribes, and warriors were integral to government and commerce. Patronage, coercion, accommodation, and rivalry all occurred, differently in different places and reigns, and historians must assess them reign by reign and source by source.

These kingdoms were also not isolated from one another. They fought, intermarried, exchanged envoys, sheltered each other's refugees, and competed for horses, revenue, and prestige. Their stories are woven together by the movement of people and ideas along rivers, mountain routes, and sea lanes. Their eventual incorporation into larger formations, first Afghan and Lodi, then Mughal, occurred at different times and by different means, and the Mughal chapters that follow cannot be understood without them.

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to explain why no single power controlled the subcontinent before the Mughals, how geography and commerce shaped regional states, how rulers legitimized independence, and how ordinary rural, urban, and religious communities experienced these kingdoms. The chapter's thirty-nine sections move through six divisions, from Bengal through Khandesh and then to comparison and synthesis.

Learning Objectives

  2. Trace the political history of Bengal from the Ilyas Shahi dynasty through the Husain Shahis, Afghan rule, and Mughal conquest.
  3. Analyze how Bengal's rivers, floods, and eastward agricultural frontier shaped revenue, settlement, and political authority.
  4. Describe the rise of the Gujarat Sultanate, its relationship with Indian Ocean commerce, and the crisis that ended its independence.
  5. Compare the political institutions, military recruitment, and elite networks of Bengal, Gujarat, Malwa, Jaunpur, and Kashmir.
  6. Assess the evidence for Malwa's and Jaunpur's political and cultural achievements, including architecture, learning, and inscriptions.
  7. Evaluate competing accounts of religious policy, coercion, and cultural patronage in Kashmir, distinguishing contemporary evidence from later tradition.
  8. Interpret coins, inscriptions, chronicles, and travel accounts as historical evidence, identifying whose perspectives they represent.
  9. Explain how commerce, agriculture, and craft production financed regional states, courts, and cities.
  10. Distinguish documented political events from romantic and religious elaboration, as in the traditions surrounding Baz Bahadur and Rupmati, and Raja Ganesha.

Historical Context

Chapters 39 through 42 established the Delhi Sultanate as a military and fiscal system that governed distant provinces through appointed governors and iqta holders. The system worked when the center could pay, supervise, and discipline its agents. Under Muhammad bin Tughlaq (Chapter 42) the demands of distant campaigns, currency experiments, and a shifting capital strained that capacity, and rebellions in Bengal, the Deccan, and elsewhere reduced the sultanate's effective reach. Firuz Shah Tughlaq preserved the core of the state by conciliating nobles and expanding assignments, but he could not recover Bengal. After Timur's sack of Delhi in 1398, governors in Gujarat, Malwa, and the eastern Gangetic plain became in practice independent, and some formalized this by assuming royal titles.

Independence took several forms. In Bengal, local commanders had asserted autonomy by the 1330s, and one of them, Shamsuddin Ilyas Shah, built a durable kingdom. In Gujarat and Malwa, governors appointed by Delhi turned their offices into dynasties. In Jaunpur, a senior court official sent east with extraordinary authority founded the Sharqi house. In Kashmir the situation was different: no Delhi governor was involved, and a series of local and immigrant rulers culminated in the Shah Mir dynasty. In each case the new rulers needed legitimacy, which they drew from the khutba (the Friday sermon naming the sovereign), coinage, investiture claims from the Abbasid caliphal tradition, military success, patronage of religious scholars and saints, and generosity to local elites.

The environmental settings were as different as the political ones. The Bengal delta is built by the Ganges, Brahmaputra, and Meghna, whose channels shift and whose floods both enrich and destroy. Gujarat combines the fertile mainland, the peninsula of Saurashtra, and a long coastline facing the Arabian Sea. Malwa is a plateau commanding routes between the Gangetic plain, Gujarat, Rajasthan, and the Deccan. The eastern Gangetic plain around Jaunpur was agriculturally rich but politically contested between Delhi and Bengal. Kashmir is a mountain-ringed valley watered by the Jhelum, connected to Punjab, Central Asia, and Tibet by high passes.

These states also entered a wider world. Gujarati and Bengali merchants traded across the Indian Ocean, a system discussed further in Chapter 49. Persian-speaking scholars, soldiers, and Sufis moved between Iran, Central Asia, and South Asia. East African Muslims, known in India as Habshis, served as soldiers and sometimes rose to rule. Ming China sent fleets to the Bay of Bengal in the early fifteenth century. Meanwhile, the Lodis in Delhi, Rajput kingdoms such as Mewar, and the great southern powers formed a shifting diplomatic field. Students should read the following sections not as a catalog of parallel dynasties but as an interconnected political system whose parts competed and borrowed from one another.

Section 47.1

Regional Sultanates after Delhi: Geography, Historical Sources, and Political Diversity

The kingdoms studied in this chapter occupied environments so different that they can be understood only by beginning with the land itself. Bengal is the great delta of the Ganges, Brahmaputra, and Meghna, a low, humid, rice-growing country crossed by shifting rivers. Gujarat lies on the northwestern coast, with the Gulf of Cambay and the Kathiawar peninsula giving it long contact with the sea. Malwa is an upland plateau north of the Narmada. Jaunpur sat on the Gomti in the eastern Ganges plain. Kashmir is a mountain valley, reached through passes that are closed by snow for part of each year. Political institutions followed these settings: rulers who depended on rivers and rice behaved differently from rulers who depended on ports, fortress-plateaus, or mountain routes.

The evidence for these kingdoms is uneven. Coins are abundant and reveal rulers' titles, mint towns, metals, and the extent of monetary circulation. Stone and brick inscriptions, usually in Arabic and sometimes in Persian or Sanskrit, record the foundation of mosques, tombs, wells, and gateways, and often give dates and the names of patrons or officials. Persian chronicles survive for Gujarat, Malwa, and Bengal, though many were written after Mughal conquest and may reflect Mughal viewpoints. Kashmir is unusual because Sanskrit chroniclers such as Jonaraja and Srivara continued Kalhana's earlier tradition under Muslim rulers. Foreign observers, including Ibn Battuta, Ming Chinese envoys, and Portuguese writers, add outside testimony. Buildings themselves are evidence for patronage, craft traditions, and the movement of ideas.

Political fragmentation is often equated with decline, an assumption inherited from older histories that treated Delhi as the natural center of any "Indian" state. The regional sultanates challenge that assumption. Smaller states could be more responsive to local conditions, and their rulers competed by building cities, patronizing learning, and encouraging trade. Some, like Gujarat and Bengal, were among the wealthiest states in the Islamic world. Fragmentation did, however, carry costs: wars among neighbors, recurrent succession disputes, and, eventually, vulnerability to larger empires.

The term "sultanate" also needs care. It describes a form of Muslim monarchy, with the ruler's name in the khutba and on coins, but it did not imply a uniformly Muslim population or a uniform administrative model. Bengal, Gujarat, Malwa, and Jaunpur were ruled by Muslim dynasties over majority non-Muslim populations in many districts, with substantial local landholders, merchants, and officials of other religions. The next sections introduce each region in turn, beginning with Bengal.

Historians working on this period distinguish three layers of evidence, and the distinction recurs in every division of the chapter. The first layer is contemporary or near-contemporary material: coins, foundation inscriptions, the Sanskrit chronicles of Kashmir, a few Persian works written at or near the courts they describe, and the reports of travelers such as Ibn Battuta and the envoys of the Ming court. The second layer is retrospective narrative: Persian histories composed in the late sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, often at the Mughal court or under Mughal patronage, such as Nizamuddin Ahmad's Tabaqat-i-Akbari, Firishta's history, and the Gujarati Mirat-i-Sikandari. These works preserve much that would otherwise be lost, but they look back from a moment when the regional kingdoms had already been conquered. The third layer is later tradition and memory, including shrine legends, genealogies, and eighteenth- or nineteenth-century compilations. A careful account uses the first layer to test the second and treats the third as evidence for how communities later remembered the past rather than for what happened.

The category "regional sultanate" itself deserves scrutiny. Older surveys often described these states as "provincial" or "successor" kingdoms, a vocabulary that places Delhi at the center and treats everything else as derivative. The approach taken here is different. It treats each kingdom as a state with its own origins and logic, while still acknowledging that all of them inherited vocabulary, offices, and legitimating practices from the Delhi Sultanate and from the wider Persianate world. The chapter's time frame also overlaps with Chapters 42 to 46: the first regional states appear in the 1330s and 1340s, while the last independent ones disappear in 1601, so students should read these pages alongside the chapters on Delhi's decline, Vijayanagara, and the Deccan.

Section 47.2

The Emergence of an Independent Bengal Sultanate

Bengal had been drawn into the Delhi Sultanate in the early thirteenth century, when Turkish commanders took Lakhnauti (later Gaur). Its distance from Delhi, its river barriers, and its wealth made it difficult to govern from the Gangetic heartland, and governors repeatedly rebelled. Delhi's hold weakened during the 1330s, in the period examined in Chapter 42. By the 1340s three rival centers existed: Lakhnauti in the northwest, Satgaon on the Hooghly in the southwest, and Sonargaon in the east, where Fakhruddin Mubarak Shah ruled and where the traveler Ibn Battuta arrived in about 1346.

Shamsuddin Ilyas Shah emerged from this competition. Persian tradition indicates he began as a soldier or noble in Lakhnauti's service, took power there in the early 1340s, and then extended control over Satgaon and, by about 1352, Sonargaon. This unification is the decisive step: earlier independence had been regional and unstable, whereas Ilyas Shah created a kingdom that combined western and eastern Bengal. Its capital was at Pandua, in the northwest, close to older centers of power yet well positioned toward the river routes that connected it with the delta.

Historians distinguish provincial independence from sovereignty. A governor might ignore Delhi's orders without claiming to be a sultan. Ilyas Shah's coinage and titles asserted full royal authority, and later Tughlaq campaigns against him failed to restore Delhi's rule. Bengal's independence therefore rested not only on distance but on a self-sustaining military and fiscal base, a theme explored in the Historical Debate in this division.

Ilyas Shah's career shows how a regional commander could build a kingdom out of competing power centers. Persian tradition remembers him by the epithet Bhangara, and the sources describe campaigns that reached well beyond the delta, with raids toward Orissa, Tirhut, Nepal, and Varanasi. Such expeditions were as much about plunder, tribute, and the display of capability as about permanent annexation, and the chronicles' claims of reach should be weighed against the thinness of the evidence for lasting administration in those districts. What is better documented is his minting. Silver coins issued in the names of Lakhnauti, Satgaon, and Sonargaon, and later from a mint town at Pandua named Firuzabad, show that a monetary system covering both western and eastern Bengal was taking shape by the 1350s.

The contrast with the 1340s is revealing. Ibn Battuta, traveling from the Maldives to Bengal around 1346, encountered a Sonargaon ruled by Fakhruddin Mubarak Shah, whom he describes as a ruler at odds with the Delhi sultan, and he also reached the northeastern district of Sylhet, where he sought out a revered Sufi, Shaykh Jalaluddin, an episode the later Sylhet tradition identifies with the saint Shah Jalal. His narrative is valuable for what it reveals about how easily travelers and holy men moved across the region's rivers, and for its remarks on low prices. It is not a systematic survey, and details of identities and chronology in his Bengal pages have been debated. Even so, it confirms that on the eve of Ilyas Shah's unification Bengal was not a single polity but a cluster of rival centers connected by water.

Historical Vocabulary

Khutba and Sikka

The khutba is the sermon delivered at Friday congregational prayer, in which the ruler's name is mentioned; the sikka is the right to strike coinage in the ruler's name. Together they were the two classic public signs of sovereignty in Islamic monarchy. When a governor's name replaced the sultan's in the khutba and on coins, contemporaries understood that he had claimed independence. For historians, they provide a way to date independence: coin legends and mosque inscriptions may show a ruler's titles years before chroniclers acknowledged him as a sultan. The terms matter throughout this chapter because every regional dynasty used them to announce sovereignty, and because they remind students that legitimacy was public and ritualized, not merely a matter of military force.

Section 47.3

The Ilyas Shahi Dynasty and the Consolidation of Bengal

Ilyas Shah's kingdom was tested almost immediately. Firuz Shah Tughlaq marched into Bengal in 1353–54, and Ilyas retreated to the fortress of Ekdala, whose marshy surroundings made a siege difficult. After the monsoon approached, Firuz withdrew, and Bengal remained independent. A second Tughlaq campaign in 1359, directed against Ilyas's son Sikandar Shah, ended similarly. Delhi's sources present these expeditions as partial successes, but the outcome, an exchange of envoys and tacit acceptance of Bengal's autonomy, shows the effects of terrain, seasons, and supply.

Sikandar Shah's long reign, from about 1358 to 1390, consolidated the dynasty. Pandua's Adina Mosque, begun in the 1370s, expressed the sultanate's confidence. Its construction used local brick with stone brought from older sites and combined an Iranian-inspired congregational plan with Bengali building techniques. Sikandar's son Ghiyasuddin Azam Shah (r. c. 1390–1411) inherited a prosperous kingdom, exchanged envoys with the Ming court, and became known in later tradition for his cultivation of Persian poetry and for judicial fairness. The tradition that he corresponded with the Persian poet Hafez comes from later sources and should be treated as a legend of cultural prestige, not as a documented event.

Throughout the Ilyas Shahi period the dynasty's legitimacy rested on military capability, religious patronage, and alliances with local elites in a country with few natural centers of fixed control. The Ilyas Shahis cultivated scholars and Sufi shaykhs, including the Chishti shaykhs of Pandua, and issued silver coins of impressive consistency. They also ruled a landscape where rivers made military mobility difficult for outsiders, which reinforced their autonomy but also made internal control depend upon negotiation with local chiefs.

Three features of Ilyas Shahi government deserve emphasis. First, the dynasty obtained external recognition. Firuz Shah's chroniclers describe the Tughlaq campaigns as victories, yet the exchange of envoys and gifts that followed amounted to acceptance of Bengal's separate existence, and by the time of Sikandar Shah the Delhi court treated the eastern ruler as a neighboring sovereign. Second, the dynasty developed relations well beyond the subcontinent. Under Ghiyasuddin Azam Shah, envoys traveled between Bengal and the Ming court, and Chinese sources record that Bengal sent tribute missions in the early fifteenth century. These exchanges were diplomatic and commercial at once, and they placed Bengal inside a wider Indian Ocean and East Asian network that is explored further in Chapter 49. Third, the dynasty relied on a court culture that blended Persian learning with local practice. Sikandar's reign is remembered for the construction of the Adina Mosque, and Ghiyasuddin's for the patronage of scholars and poets.

The dynasty's internal politics were not tranquil. Sikandar Shah died in the early 1390s in a conflict with his son Ghiyasuddin, a succession struggle that tested the kingdom's institutions and showed that the throne remained vulnerable to princely rebellion. Yet the kingdom survived intact, which suggests that by this date noble families, fort commanders, and revenue officers had a stake in the continuity of the state. That continuity is part of what the Historical Debate below asks students to weigh against the effects of distance and terrain.

Historical Debate

Was Bengal's Independence a Consequence of Geographical Distance or Institutional Consolidation?

The question: Did Bengal remain independent of Delhi mainly because of distance, rivers, and monsoon conditions, or because the Ilyas Shahis built institutions that made independence durable?
Interpretation A — Geography: Delhi's armies struggled to cross the Ganges plain's eastern rivers, supply forces in marshy terrain, and campaign through the monsoon. Firuz Shah's retreats from Ekdala and the cooling of Tughlaq ambitions suggest that terrain and logistics constrained any central power.
Interpretation B — Institutional consolidation: Distance alone does not create a state. Ilyas Shah's unification of Lakhnauti, Satgaon, and Sonargaon, the dynasty's silver coinage, diplomatic recognition abroad, mosque-building, and ability to reward nobles and local chiefs show a durable administrative and fiscal system that survived changes of ruler.

Evidence: Both views draw on Tughlaq campaign narratives, coin hoards, inscriptions, and descriptions by outside visitors. Geography explains why conquest was difficult; institutions explain why Bengal remained cohesive and prosperous.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians view regional kingdoms generally: as defaults produced by weak central authority, or as deliberate state-building achievements.

Student task: Identify what additional evidence would help decide between the two explanations, for example, the continuity of coin weights, evidence of revenue officials, or Delhi's success in other eastern campaigns.

Section 47.4

Political Crisis, Raja Ganesha, and the Transformation of Bengal's Court

The early fifteenth century brought a crisis in Bengal. After Ghiyasuddin Azam Shah's death, short-lived successors struggled against powerful nobles. By about 1414–15, Raja Ganesha, a landholder and court magnate whose title appears in sources as Danujamardana Deva, dominated the court. Chronicles written later give conflicting accounts of whether he seized the throne directly, ruled through his son, or acted as a regent. Coins and inscriptions help establish that his son, who took the Muslim name Jalaluddin Muhammad Shah, issued coins in the 1410s and again in the 1420s and early 1430s.

Persian and later Bengali traditions add dramatic details: that Ganesha persecuted Muslim saints, that Sufi leaders appealed to the sultan of Jaunpur, Ibrahim Shah Sharqi, who invaded Bengal, and that Ganesha's son converted to Islam to be installed as ruler. These narratives are valuable but problematic. They were produced in religious communities with their own memories of the crisis, and later authors had reasons to emphasize either Muslim suffering or the legitimacy of Muslim rule. Historians generally accept that there was a political crisis involving Ganesha and Jaunpur's intervention but treat the religious details cautiously, relying on inscriptions and coin evidence for what can be established.

The Ilyas Shahi line returned with Nasiruddin Mahmud Shah I around 1435 and ruled for about a quarter-century, followed by Rukunuddin Barbak Shah (r. 1459–1474), who recruited large numbers of African soldiers known as Habshis for the royal guard. Habshi influence eventually produced a crisis of its own. Between about 1487 and 1494, several Habshi commanders held the throne in rapid succession, with the first, Shahzada Barbak, killing the sultan Fath Shah. Indigenous and immigrant nobles resented the Habshis, and the interregnum ended around 1494, when a Sayyid-descended minister, Sayyid Husain, overthrew the last Habshi ruler.

The monuments of this period offer an independent check on the narratives. At Pandua the Eklakhi mausoleum, a square brick tomb associated with Jalaluddin Muhammad Shah and his family, shows the dynasty investing in royal burial architecture at the very moment later authors say it was in crisis. At Gaur, the Dakhil Darwaza, the ceremonial gateway to the royal citadel, is generally attributed to the reign of Rukunuddin Barbak Shah, and the Firuz Minar, a tall tower associated with the Habshi sultan Saifuddin Firuz Shah, belongs to the late interregnum. These buildings caution against a picture of uninterrupted turmoil. Even in the years when succession was violent, royal patronage and building continued, and the Habshi rulers appear in the architectural record as builders as well as usurpers.

The scale of the Habshi presence is another matter on which sources disagree. Persian chronicles written long after the events say that Barbak Shah recruited many thousands of African soldiers, but the figures cannot be verified, and the later Persian authors had reasons to dramatize the dangers of slave soldiers. A better-supported conclusion is that the Habshi guard was large enough, and close enough to the sultan's person, to give its commanders a decisive voice in succession. Compare the role of Habshi nobles in the Deccan, discussed in Chapter 46, where similar tensions appeared between locally born, Persian-speaking, and African military elites.

The episode is important because it demonstrates the court's openness to talent from across the Indian Ocean world. It also shows the vulnerabilities of military patronage in a state where rulers depended on loyal bodyguards.

Historical Vocabulary

Habshi

Habshi (from the Arabic Habash, meaning Abyssinia or the Horn of Africa) was the term used in South Asia for people of African, especially Ethiopian, origin who arrived as slaves, soldiers, merchants, or sailors. In Bengal, the Deccan, and Gujarat, some Habshis became commanders, administrators, and, in a few cases, rulers. The word does not describe a single ethnic community or a uniform social status. Understanding Habshi history shows that Indian Ocean connections shaped regional courts from the inside, and that slavery in these societies could be a route to military office as well as a condition of coercion. Compare Chapter 46, where Habshi nobles also appear in the Deccan.

Section 47.5

The Husain Shahi Dynasty and the Height of Bengal's Regional Power

Alauddin Husain Shah (r. 1494–1519) restored order after the Habshi interregnum by dismissing the Habshi guard and re-establishing a balanced nobility. He made Gaur his capital, building on its established urban infrastructure, and presided over a reign that later Bengali writers remembered as an age of prosperity. Persian and Bengali sources both praise his governance, although the former emphasize order and the latter his patronage of vernacular poets.

Militarily, Husain Shah expanded eastward and northward. Campaigns against the Kamata kingdom in the Brahmaputra valley brought Kamatapur under his control for a time, though Bengali dominance there proved unstable and was later reversed by Koch power. His armies also campaigned in Orissa and ruled over a territory extending toward Tirhut and Bihar, and he reached an accommodation with the Lodi Sultan Sikandar Lodi that fixed their shared frontier. Diplomacy mattered as much as warfare: Bengal's wealth enabled it to avoid the larger conflicts that consumed neighbors.

His son Nasiruddin Nusrat Shah (r. 1519–1532) continued these policies, giving refuge to Afghan nobles after Babur's victory at Panipat (Chapter 50) while avoiding direct confrontation with the Mughals; Babur's memoirs mention Bengal's ruler by name. Nusrat Shah also patronized architecture; the Bara Sona Masjid at Gaur, completed during his reign, is the largest surviving mosque there. The dynasty's later rulers were less effective, and Sher Shah Suri's seizure of Gaur in 1538 ended Husain Shahi rule.

The Husain Shahi court's government depended on a ruling elite of foreign-origin and Bengal-born Muslims, Hindu officials such as the kayastha families that served as ministers and scribes, and military commanders recruited from various communities. Their system relied on local intermediaries to collect revenue, a pattern explored in the next section.

Two kinds of evidence help show who actually served the Husain Shahi court. Bengali vernacular poems composed in these decades praise the sultan by name; Vijay Gupta's Manasa-mangal, dated to the mid-1490s, speaks of Husain Shah as a ruler under whom the country enjoyed peace. And the later Vaishnava biographical tradition associated with Chaitanya records that two brothers, later known as Rupa and Sanatana Goswami, had served at the sultan's court as senior officials before joining the devotional movement. Their careers, like those of the Hindu ministers and scribes mentioned in Persian histories, suggest that high office was open to non-Muslims who commanded Persian administrative skills and could provide the court with revenue expertise. The sources are late, hagiographical, or panegyrical, and should be used with those limits in mind, but together they make it hard to sustain the notion that the Husain Shahi state was governed by a single religious community.

Husain Shah's eastern expansion was also less secure than the chronicles suggest. Accounts of the advance toward the Brahmaputra valley, which reportedly displaced the ruler of Kamata and installed a Bengali governor, are followed in later sources by reports of reverses against the Ahoms and Koch, and the effective frontier probably fluctuated for decades. Nusrat Shah's relations with the Mughals illustrate the same pragmatism. After Babur's victories in Hindustan, Bengal's ruler sheltered some Afghan nobles but avoided open war, and Babur's own memoirs treat Bengal as a rich and formidable neighbor rather than an immediate target. Chapter 50 follows the Mughal side of these encounters.

Section 47.6

Government, Land Revenue, and Bengal's Riverine Agricultural Economy

Bengal's wealth rested on rice. The delta's alluvial soil, high rainfall, and river floods supported multiple rice crops and also demanded constant adaptation. Rivers changed course, destroying settlements and opening new land. Over the later medieval centuries the Ganges shifted eastward toward the Padma-Meghna system, and the delta's active frontier of cultivation moved with it, drawing cultivators into forested and marshy districts in the east and south.

Revenue administration is only partly documented. The sultans' officials assessed produce and levied taxes, probably through a combination of assignments to military commanders and direct collection by officers appointed to districts. Between the court and the cultivator stood local landholding elites, whom later documents call zamindars and, in the eastern delta, bhuiyans. These chiefs held rights to collect revenue, organized labor for clearing land, and could mobilize armed followers. Sultans negotiated with them rather than replacing them, which preserved stability but limited central control.

The agricultural frontier also carried religious significance. Richard Eaton has argued that in eastern Bengal the extension of cultivation, led by migrants and local leaders, was linked to the spread of Islam, as mosques, shrines, and charismatic holy men became centers of new settlements. This thesis is influential but debated: critics point out that conversion had multiple causes, that evidence is stronger for the Mughal period than for the sultanate era, and that older Buddhist and Brahmanical communities continued in many districts. A sound reading treats agrarian expansion as one important context of religious change, not the sole explanation.

The most vivid evidence for the agrarian frontier in the sultanate period is architectural and hagiographic. In the southwestern delta, around present-day Bagerhat, a fifteenth-century saint and governor remembered as Khan Jahan Ali is associated with mosques, tanks, and roads, including the large multi-domed Shait Gumbad Mosque. The inscriptions and monuments around Bagerhat suggest a leader who organized clearing, building, and religious institutions in an environment where such initiative would have been necessary to turn forest and marsh into settled land. Similar figures appear in local traditions elsewhere in the east and south, though the stories attached to them are often late, and the historical evidence for each should be examined individually. Even with that caution, the pattern supports the view that religious leadership and agrarian expansion could reinforce each other.

Other features of the delta economy are less visible in the sources. Cultivators sowed different rice varieties in different seasons and in different water conditions, from early rice harvested in the rains to deep-water varieties that grew with the flood; the choice depended on local elevation and the behavior of the river. Fishing, boat-building, and salt-making supported households alongside cultivation. Because no fifteenth-century revenue registers survive, historians infer much of the organization of rural society from later Mughal documents such as Abu'l Fazl's Ain-i-Akbari, from inscriptions, and from Bengali literature. This means that the picture offered here, however plausible, rests on analogy more than on direct record, a limitation that applies to almost every region in the chapter.

Ibn Battuta, visiting in the 1340s, marveled at Bengal's low prices and plentiful rice, and Chinese envoys of the early fifteenth century likewise emphasized its abundance. These accounts reflect real productivity, but they also reflect the impression of visitors from poorer or more arid regions. Bengal's rural life included flood, epidemic, and inequality that visitors rarely noticed.

Section 47.7

Bengal's Towns, Ports, Manufacturing, and Commercial Networks

Bengal's major towns had distinct functions. Gaur, on the old course of the Ganges, was a capital whose walls, gates, and mosques can still be traced. Pandua, an earlier capital, preserved sacred and royal associations. Sonargaon, near modern Dhaka, was the commercial center of the eastern delta and a mint town. Satgaon on the Hooghly was a western river port, its importance later declining as silting reduced access. Chittagong, on the southeastern coast, was the kingdom's principal sea port, passing at different times between Bengal, Arakan, and Tripura.

Textiles were Bengal's best-known export. Cotton and fine muslins were woven in many districts, and silk was produced in the northwest. The Chinese envoy Ma Huan, who accompanied Zheng He's expeditions, described Bengal's cloths and its merchants in the early fifteenth century, and Portuguese observers in the early sixteenth century likewise noted the volume of cloth shipped to other regions. Rice, sugar, and other agricultural goods also moved by river and sea. The state benefited through taxes on trade and the minting of silver tankas.

Currency in Bengal combined the silver tanka for large transactions with cowrie shells for small ones, a system sustained by imports of cowries from the Maldives. This arrangement illustrates Bengal's connection with the Indian Ocean: cowries came from islands thousands of kilometers away, and Bengali silver tankas depended on silver supplies from outside the region. Merchants of many origins participated in the trade, including Bengali Hindu traders, Persian and Arab Muslim merchants, and later Portuguese and other Europeans. The relationship with maritime markets is discussed more broadly in Chapter 49.

Inland, rivers served as highways. Boats carried grain, cloth, salt, and timber between towns, and river fairs and markets formed networks beyond the reach of any capital. Religious sites, including shrines and mosques, often anchored such markets.

Chinese observers help fill the gap left by Bengal's own sources. The interpreter Ma Huan, who traveled with the Ming treasure fleets, described Bengal in the early fifteenth century as a country rich in rice and cloth, whose people used cowries for small transactions, and the Ming annals record envoys from Bengal bringing exotic gifts, including, in 1414, a giraffe that had reached Bengal from Africa through Indian Ocean exchange. The episode shows how a regional court could participate in diplomacy and the circulation of rare animals across the Indian Ocean, and it parallels the more routine commercial links through which cowries, silver, textiles, and horses moved. Chapter 49 returns to these maritime networks.

By the early sixteenth century, European observers also described Bengal's ports. The Portuguese distinguished a "great port" at Chittagong from a "small port" at Satgaon, reflecting the decline of the latter as the Hooghly silted. Their accounts, like Ma Huan's, are outside perspectives, and they emphasize trade in cloth, rice, and sugar as seen by foreign buyers. They say less about the artisans, boatmen, and village markets that made production possible. The region's social base is therefore reconstructed from sources that were never written to describe it, which reminds students why the combination of coin evidence, architectural remains, and outside travelers remains so important.

Section 47.8

Bengali Literature, Religious Communities, and Sultanate Architecture

Bengal's sultans presided over a multilingual culture. Persian and Arabic served as languages of administration, law, and learning. Sanskrit remained the language of Hindu scholarship. Bengali, long a spoken vernacular, developed a written literary tradition that benefited from court patronage. Under the late Ilyas Shahis, Maladhar Basu composed the Bengali Sri Krishna Vijaya (c. 1473–1480), receiving the honorific Gunaraja Khan from Ruknuddin Barbak Shah. Under the Husain Shahis, Bengali literary patronage continued, and commanders in eastern districts sponsored a Bengali Mahabharata. Vernacular writing in this period is a significant example of Muslim rulers supporting a regional language that was not their court language.

Religious life was diverse. Sufi shaykhs and their shrines were prominent, especially in Pandua and in the east, and communities of converts and migrants formed around them. Hindu devotional movements, including the Vaishnava revival associated with Chaitanya, who was born in Bengal in 1486, flourished in the same decades as Husain Shah's rule. The relationship of the sultan to these movements is not documented in detail, and the later Vaishnava tradition's accounts of conflict with local Muslim officials show both tensions and the capacity of Hindu communities to organize publicly. Broader devotional history belongs to Chapter 48.

Court patronage for Bengali was not limited to a single genre. The mangala-kavya, narrative poems celebrating regional deities such as Manasa, the snake goddess, circulated in the late fifteenth century, and poets such as Vijay Gupta and Vipradas Pipilai situate their works in the Husain Shahi period. A separate strand of literature, the Bengali Mahabharata sponsored by Paragal Khan and his son Chhuti Khan, was produced in the eastern districts near Chittagong under the sultan's local commanders. The pattern suggests that Muslim officials, regional landholders, and Hindu poets each played a role in making Bengali a written language of narrative, and that court sponsorship was one stimulus among several.

The material record complements the literary one. The Adina Mosque at Pandua, begun in the 1370s, was among the largest mosques ever built in the subcontinent, with a vast courtyard and a long arcaded prayer hall; its scale signals Sikandar Shah's wish to match the great congregational mosques of Delhi and the Islamic world. Smaller brick mosques, tombs, and gateways at Gaur illustrate a range of types, and the Bengal terracotta tradition used molded clay plaques to ornament both Muslim and Hindu religious buildings, which indicates shared craft practice among communities whose religious lives differed.

Sultanate architecture in Bengal developed a regional style. Builders worked in brick rather than stone, used curved cornices derived from bamboo-and-thatch roofs, employed terracotta ornament, and combined multiple small domes. The Adina Mosque at Pandua and the Bara Sona Masjid and Qadam Rasul at Gaur illustrate the range. Some structures reused materials from earlier Hindu and Buddhist sites, a practice that shows both economy and, in some cases, symbolic appropriation. Historians interpret reuse case by case.

Section 47.9

Bengal after the Husain Shahis: Afghan Rule and the Approach of Mughal Expansion

Husain Shahi rule ended in turmoil. Nusrat Shah's successors faced internal rivalry and pressure from Sher Shah Suri, an Afghan commander who had built a power base in Bihar. In 1538 Sher Shah captured Gaur. The Mughal emperor Humayun, arriving shortly afterward, occupied the city briefly, but his position collapsed, and Sher Shah's victory at Chausa in 1539 and Kannauj in 1540 forced him out of India (Chapter 50). Bengal then became a province of the Sur empire, administered by governors, and later achieved renewed autonomy.

After the Suri dynasty collapsed, Afghan rulers reasserted regional independence. The Karrani dynasty, established by Taj Khan Karrani and consolidated by Sulaiman Karrani (r. c. 1565–1572), ruled Bengal and parts of Bihar and Orissa from Tanda. Sulaiman balanced diplomacy with Akbar, acknowledging him nominally while retaining actual autonomy. His son Daud Khan Karrani broke this arrangement and in 1574–76 faced a Mughal invasion. Daud's defeat at the Battle of Rajmahal in July 1576 ended Afghan sovereignty in Bengal.

Mughal conquest, however, was not instant control. Bengal's rivers, Afghan remnants in Orissa and eastern districts, and local landholding leaders delayed full integration for decades. The Bhati chiefs of the eastern delta, notably Isa Khan, resisted Mughal authority until the early seventeenth century. Moreover, an epidemic in the mid-1570s damaged Gaur, and the old capital was gradually abandoned. Chapter 51 discusses Mughal provincial administration; here the key point is that Bengal's regional distinctiveness persisted beyond the end of its sultanate.

The Sur interval shows how Bengal's political position changed without a simple conquest from outside. Sher Shah Suri's capture of Gaur in 1538 and his later victories over Humayun placed Bengal inside a larger Afghan empire, and the Sur administration appointed governors who were supposed to balance local landholding elites against the center. After Sher Shah's death, as the Sur realm weakened, one of his governors in Bengal asserted autonomy, and the province slid toward regional rule once more. The Karrani dynasty, drawing on Afghan soldiers and landholders and ruling from Tanda, was in effect a restoration of independent Bengal on a smaller and more militarized foundation.

Mughal takeover required several campaigns across more than a decade. After the victory at Rajmahal in 1576, imperial officers faced Afghan revolts and the independent chiefs of the eastern delta, among whom Isa Khan of the Bhati region became the best known. Persian sources celebrate Mughal victories; Bengali later traditions remember Isa Khan as a defender of regional autonomy. Both accounts reflect later perspectives. The abandonment of Gaur after an epidemic in the mid-1570s and the movement of the provincial capital to other sites symbolize the end of one urban landscape, but the institutions of landholding and the region's cultural distinctiveness persisted, a theme taken up in Chapter 51.

Section 47.10

Gujarat's Independence and the Foundation of the Muzaffarid Sultanate

Gujarat was incorporated into the Delhi Sultanate in the late thirteenth century, and its governors, who controlled rich ports and fertile districts, were among the empire's most important provincial officials. In 1391 the Tughlaq ruler Muhammad Shah appointed Zafar Khan, a noble of Rajput convert background according to Persian tradition, as governor. Delhi's weakness after Timur's invasion left him effectively independent. A brief earlier assertion of royal status by his son Tatar Khan, reported in the chronicles, ended in failure, and Zafar Khan formally assumed the royal title as Muzaffar Shah in 1407.

The delay is itself instructive. Zafar Khan did not declare independence the moment Delhi faltered; he waited until he had secured the nobility, consolidated control over turbulent districts, and dealt with neighboring rivals, including Malwa. The title of sultan was a claim that required power to sustain. Muzaffar Shah's reign, which ended around 1411, established the basic institutions of the new state: court officers, a royal army, and an alliance of Muslim nobles, Rajput chiefs, and merchants.

Persian chronicles of Gujarat, especially the later Mirat-i-Sikandari (completed in the early seventeenth century), preserve rich detail, but their authors wrote under Mughal rule and tended to present the sultans as precursors of imperial order. Inscriptions, coin finds, and monuments provide an independent check. Gujarat's early sultans also faced the challenge of integrating a region with deep Jain, Hindu, and Muslim merchant communities and a long tradition of maritime exchange.

Two features of Gujarat's route to independence deserve attention. The first is the character of the sources. The principal narrative histories, including the Mirat-i-Sikandari, completed in the early seventeenth century, and the Arabic chronicle of Hajji ad-Dabir, written in the same general period, were composed after the Mughal annexation. They draw on earlier accounts and local memory but also shape the story to fit later political concerns, such as the legitimacy of the Muzaffarid line and the heroism of its founders. Contemporary coins and inscriptions allow historians to test their chronology, and the discrepancies, for example over the date and details of Tatar Khan's brief claim, show how uncertain the early record is.

The second feature is the way Muzaffar Shah's power was built. He quickly came into conflict with neighbors in Malwa and the Rajput chiefdoms. Persian sources report that in 1407 he captured Dhar and brought the Malwa ruler Hoshang Shah to Gujarat as a prisoner, an episode that shows how closely the independence of Gujarat and Malwa was linked. Control of the main land routes toward the Deccan and Rajasthan mattered to both kingdoms, and the competition between them would shape political life for a century. Successful warfare also helped Muzaffar attach nobles and chiefs to his cause with rewards of assignments and honors, a necessary foundation for monarchy in a region without an inherited royal house.

Section 47.11

Ahmad Shah I and the Foundation of Ahmedabad

Ahmad Shah I (r. 1411–1442), Muzaffar Shah's grandson, secured the succession in the midst of rival claims, and traditional accounts say that Muzaffar's death was linked to the succession struggle, though the details are uncertain. His reign set the character of the sultanate. In 1411 he founded Ahmedabad on the east bank of the Sabarmati River, near the older town of Asawal, and moved the capital there from Anhilwada Patan. The city was named for the sultan, and later chronicles attributed its founding to a combination of auspicious signs and a shrewd selection of a river site.

Ahmedabad soon became a major urban center. Ahmad Shah built the Bhadra citadel, laid out a royal square, and constructed the Jami Masjid, completed in 1424, its prayer hall using columns and ornamental elements reflecting Gujarati stone-carving traditions. The city attracted artisans, merchants, scholars, and holy men, and its growth illustrates how a ruler could create a new capital whose population and institutions made the kingdom's identity visible.

The Jami Masjid illustrates how religious and political meaning could be built from existing materials. Its columns and some architectural elements were taken from earlier Hindu and Jain temples, an act that provided finished stone at low cost and, as many historians argue, also displayed the new regime's power over older religious institutions. Yet the building's design, with its carved ornament, elaborate columns, and Gujarati craft detail, depended on local masons and sculptors whose traditions long predated the sultanate. The mosque is therefore evidence of both appropriation and collaboration, and neither can be separated from the other without distorting the picture.

Ahmedabad's growth also reflected sufi and mercantile patronage. The shrine of Shaykh Ahmad Khattu at Sarkhej, near the city, became an important religious center, and later sultans built a complex there that included a tomb, mosque, and tank. Neighborhoods called puras grew around markets, tanks, and religious sites, and merchant families moved into the city to be near the court and the revenue offices. In this way the capital became not only the seat of royal power but also the main node connecting the cotton-growing and weaving districts of the interior with the ports that carried cloth abroad.

Ahmad Shah also campaigned energetically. He subdued chieftains in Idar and elsewhere, fought rivals in Malwa, and extended his authority toward Saurashtra and the Rajput states. His relations with local rulers fit a pattern: defeat, tribute, and reinstatement, rather than systematic annexation. This pragmatic approach reflected the political realities of governing a country in which hereditary chiefs held strong local bases.

Ahmad Shah is also remembered in Persian tradition for severity toward opponents, including the destruction of some temples in the course of campaigns. Historians weigh such accounts against evidence of his use of Hindu and Jain officials and merchants and the continued construction of temples under his successors. As in other regions, royal policy toward religious institutions depended on political circumstances, not on a single consistent doctrine.

Section 47.12

Mahmud Begada: Expansion, Fortresses, and Imperial Ambition

Mahmud Shah I, called Begada, reigned from 1458 to 1511, the longest and most celebrated reign in Gujarat's sultanate history. The sobriquet is usually explained as referring to his capture of two great forts, though other explanations exist. He came to the throne young, quickly asserted himself over court factions, and spent decades extending his kingdom's borders.

His most notable conquests were in Saurashtra and eastern Gujarat. In 1469–70 he took Junagadh, the great hill fortress of the Girnar region, and brought its chiefs into submission; he later secured the coast, including Dwarka, which strengthened control over pilgrimage and shipping routes. In 1484, after a long campaign, he captured the fortress of Pavagadh and the neighboring Champaner, where he built a new city, Muhammadabad, as a second capital. These conquests extended the revenue base and removed centers of resistance on the kingdom's margins.

Begada also pursued diplomacy across the subcontinent, supporting allies in Khandesh and the Deccan and intervening in Malwa. He maintained a large army, drawing on Muslim nobles of Turkish, Afghan, Persian, and African origin, Rajput chiefs, and local levies. His patronage of poets, scholars, and architects was substantial. Persian writers praised his justice and prodigious energy, while some of the more extravagant stories about him, including accounts of his enormous appetite, are best read as folklore.

Late in his reign Begada faced a new challenge: the Portuguese arrival in the Arabian Sea. He sought Egyptian Mamluk assistance, and the combined fleet defeated a Portuguese squadron at Chaul in 1508 before suffering a decisive defeat at Diu in early 1509. These events are summarized here for their effect on Gujarat; Chapter 49 treats Portuguese naval strategy in full.

Persian sources give a vivid account of the siege of Champaner. They describe a campaign extending over many months before the hill fortress of Pavagadh fell in 1484, after which its Rajput ruler was defeated and the surrounding area incorporated. Begada then established a new city at the foot of the hill, named Muhammadabad but usually called Champaner, and gave it palaces, a congregational mosque, and a ring of fortifications; the surviving ruins remain one of the finest archaeological landscapes of the sultanate period. The new capital allowed him to rule the eastern districts, where chiefly power was strong, from a base within reach of the hills.

The sea mattered to him too. Gujarat's merchants and shipowners were being threatened by the Portuguese in the first decade of the sixteenth century, and Begada reacted by seeking allies, among them the Mamluk sultan of Egypt, whose admiral Mir Husain led a fleet to the Indian coast. The governor of Diu, Malik Ayaz, a former slave of foreign origin who became one of the sultanate's most important officials, coordinated the response. The combined fleets defeated a Portuguese squadron off Chaul early in 1508 but were beaten at Diu in February 1509, after which the Portuguese gained a strategic advantage that Gujarat never fully reversed. The pattern shows a state able to mobilize ships and allies but constrained by the technological and organizational edge that Portuguese warships gave their commanders. Chapter 49 returns to the naval history in detail.

Section 47.13

Bahadur Shah, Portuguese Intervention, and the Crisis of Gujarati Independence

Bahadur Shah (r. 1526–1537) was Gujarat's most ambitious late sultan. After a violent struggle among Begada's descendants, he emerged as ruler and quickly expanded the kingdom. He conquered Malwa in 1531 and in 1535 besieged and took the great Rajput fortress of Chittor. These victories made him the most powerful ruler in western India, and Mughal and Gujarati interests began to collide. Babur's son Humayun, newly established in Hindustan, viewed Gujarat's growth with alarm, especially since Bahadur sheltered Mughal opponents.

Humayun invaded Gujarat in 1535. Bahadur's army, weakened by the Chittor campaign and by divided leadership, collapsed, and Champaner and much of the kingdom fell to the Mughals. Humayun's hold proved short-lived, because his brother's revolt and Sher Shah's rise in the east forced him to return, and Bahadur regained his realm. But the crisis had pushed Bahadur to seek an agreement with the Portuguese. In 1534 he had ceded Bassein, and in 1535 he allowed them to build a fortress at Diu in return for military help.

The arrangement soured quickly. In February 1537, during a meeting with the Portuguese governor Nuno da Cunha near Diu, a confrontation broke out and Bahadur Shah was killed, drowned or slain according to different accounts. His death opened a long period of weak sultans, strong nobles, and Portuguese pressure. Gujarat's nobility factionalized, and several leaders invited Mughal intervention. Akbar's armies conquered Gujarat in 1572–73, took Ahmedabad, and besieged Surat, ending the sultanate.

Understanding the collapse requires attention to what followed Bahadur's death. His successors, beginning with Mahmud III, came to the throne young or weak, and real power shifted to leading nobles, among them Itimad Khan, Changiz Khan, and Ulugh Khan Habshi, who competed to control the sultan and the revenues. Persian narratives describe a kingdom in which armies were raised and disbanded by the nobility, provincial governors acted independently, and the court became a prize to be seized. The Portuguese, now entrenched at Diu and Daman, took advantage of the confusion to extract concessions on trade and to harass shipping from rival ports.

Akbar's conquest in 1572 and 1573 was therefore the end of a long political decline, not a sudden blow against a flourishing monarchy. Several Gujarati nobles invited or accepted Mughal intervention; after the fall of Ahmedabad, imperial troops pressed on to Cambay and besieged the port of Surat, which fell early in 1573. The final sultan, Muzaffar Shah III, was taken captive. The episode demonstrates how a kingdom with abundant commercial wealth could still be undone by factionalism. It also set the stage for Gujarat's incorporation as a Mughal province, an event that Chapter 51 takes up from the imperial viewpoint.

The crisis of independence therefore combined several causes: dynastic instability, noble factionalism, Mughal expansion, and the Portuguese presence. No single factor explains the outcome.

Section 47.14

Gujarati Kingship, Administration, Revenue, and Regional Elites

Gujarat's government was a hybrid structure. The sultan stood at the apex, assisted by ministers, a chief judge, military commanders, and officials responsible for revenue, correspondence, and the royal household. The kingdom was divided into administrative units, often called parganas, and strategic towns held garrisons under commanders who answered to the court. Hereditary chiefs, particularly Rajput rulers and chieftains in Saurashtra, retained local authority in return for tribute and military service.

Revenue came primarily from land. The state taxed agricultural produce and collected additional dues from trade, markets, and ports, with customs levies on maritime commerce especially important. Intermediaries called muqaddams, local chiefs, and revenue farmers stood between peasants and the state. The Persian chronicles describe the system in sketchy terms, and much of what is known about practice comes from later Mughal-era accounts. Historians therefore reason cautiously from the evidence of grants, inscriptions, and the narratives of provincial history.

Recruitment was diverse. Gujarat's armies included Muslim nobles of foreign and local descent, many of whom held assignments of revenue; Rajput contingents; infantry and archers; and a fleet manned by seafarers from the coast. African Habshi commanders and Abyssinian guards also served. The relationship with merchants was particularly important. Wealthy shipowners and bankers financed political ventures, lent to nobles, and benefited from state protection of trade. Some merchants held high office: in the later sultanate, Malik Gopi, a Hindu merchant, served as governor of Surat. Merchants were therefore not outsiders to government but part of its funding and administration.

Coinage supplies a good example of how the sultanate's administration worked. Silver coins called mahmudis, associated with Mahmud Begada's long reign, circulated widely, and their consistent weight and design show a state capable of maintaining a monetary standard for decades. The existence of such a currency tells historians that revenue was assessed and collected in cash on a large scale, that ports and markets used it, and that merchants and officials could rely on it across districts. It also indicates a fiscal system in which customs dues from the coast, collected by officials in Diu, Cambay, and other ports, supported the treasury alongside land revenue.

The relationship between the sultan and the provincial chiefs is harder to reconstruct. Persian chronicles mention districts under Muslim assignment holders, others under hereditary chiefs who paid tribute, and fortresses garrisoned directly by royal officers. This patchwork implies that the state did not impose a uniform administration on its territory. Instead it relied on negotiated arrangements that varied with the strength of local rulers, the importance of the district, and the political needs of the moment. The same flexibility helps explain why Gujarat could expand quickly and also why its authority was fragile when the court was divided, as the crisis of the 1530s and 1540s would show.

Comparative Study

Environment, Trade, and State Revenue: Gujarat in Comparative Perspective

Gujarat's revenue structure differed from Bengal's. Bengal's state depended heavily on agrarian expansion and negotiated with landholding chiefs; Gujarat's depended on a combination of land revenue and customs dues from ports. The sultan therefore had a particular interest in the security of sea lanes and the goodwill of merchant communities. Malwa and Jaunpur, inland states, depended more on land revenue and military assignment; Kashmir drew on irrigated agriculture and craft production. Comparison shows that regional state-building was not one model but several, each shaped by its environment.

Section 47.15

Gujarat and the Indian Ocean: Ports, Shipping, and Commercial Networks

Gujarat's ports connected South Asia to a large maritime world. Cambay (Khambhat) at the head of the gulf was the premier port of the fifteenth century, though its shallow, silting waters limited larger vessels. Bharuch (Broach), Surat, Diu, Gogha, and other harbors participated in the trade, and Diu's strategic position on the Kathiawar coast made it the focus of contests over naval control. Gujarati merchants maintained colonies in Aden, Hormuz, Malacca, and East African ports, and Gujarati ships crossed the Arabian Sea to the Red Sea and Persian Gulf.

Exports included cotton textiles, indigo, agate and carnelian beads, grain, and other manufactures, while imports included horses from Arabia and Persia, gold and ivory from East Africa, spices from Southeast Asia and Malabar, porcelain and silk from China, and metals. The trade moved on the seasonal rhythm of the monsoon, which carried ships westward in one season and eastward in another. Shipowners and merchants organized voyages through partnerships, often combining Muslim, Hindu, and Jain capital. Sailors, pilots, carpenters, and port laborers made up a maritime working population about whom the evidence is thin.

The Portuguese observer Tomé Pires, writing in the early sixteenth century, regarded Gujarat's merchants as central to the Indian Ocean economy, and his account is a valuable outside view; it also reflects Portuguese strategic interests. Gujarat's own sources say less about shipping, and historians must use customs records, inscriptions, and foreign accounts to reconstruct it. The fuller maritime story, including Portuguese naval strategy and the later reconfiguration of Indian Ocean commerce, is the subject of Chapter 49.

Shipping at Gujarat's harbors depended on a specialized maritime class. Ships were commanded by captains called nakhudas, many of them wealthy merchants themselves, and carried cargoes belonging to many traders who purchased space in the vessels. Pilots, sailors, carpenters, and caulkers formed a working population organized by community and region. Their labor made the monsoon voyages possible, yet because they left few records, their experience is known mostly from later European descriptions and from the evidence of shipbuilding and port infrastructure. Even the sizes and types of vessels are reconstructed from foreign observers, who describe both large ocean-going ships and smaller coasting craft that carried goods along the western coast.

Two commercial relationships stand out. The first was with the Red Sea and Egypt, where Gujarati cloth and other goods reached the Mamluk markets and, through them, the Mediterranean. The second was with Southeast Asia, above all Malacca, where Pires reports that Gujarati merchants were so numerous that the port assigned a separate harbor officer, a shahbandar, to deal with them. These links meant that Gujarat's prosperity depended on conditions in distant ports and on the security of sea lanes, which explains the alarm with which its rulers and merchants viewed the Portuguese. The gradual silting of Cambay's harbor and the rise of Surat, a theme for later chapters, also altered the geography of Gujarati trade in the sixteenth century.

Section 47.16

Textiles, Merchants, Banking, and Gujarat's Commercial Economy

Cotton was Gujarat's great manufacturing product. Cultivators grew cotton on the black soils of the mainland and Saurashtra, and spinners, weavers, dyers, and printers turned it into cloth. Cambay and Ahmedabad were centers of dyeing and printing, and cloth made in Gujarat reached East Africa, the Red Sea, Southeast Asia, and Europe via intermediaries. Indigo and other dyes, beadwork, leather, and metalwork added to the mix. Production was dispersed in village and town workshops, organized by caste-based and kin-based artisan groups, with merchants providing capital and sometimes advancing credit.

Gujarat's merchant communities were remarkably varied. Hindu Vaishya traders (banias), Jain merchants, Muslim Bohra and Khoja communities, Arab and Persian traders, and, later, Parsis and others took part. Banking was a specialized activity: bills of exchange (hundis), deposit lending, and money changing allowed merchants to move capital across long distances without carrying coin. Credit linked the producers of cloth in inland villages, the brokers of towns, and the shipowners of ports. Because merchant firms were organized through family and community ties, trust rested on reputation and shared institutions.

Textile production followed distinct stages, each tied to a particular group. Cultivators supplied raw cotton; spinners, often women working in households, turned it into yarn; weavers wove it into cloth; and dyers, bleachers, and printers added color and pattern. Coarser cloth served domestic and East African markets, while finer cotton and dyed or printed goods were exported to the Red Sea and Southeast Asia. Agate and carnelian workshops, associated especially with Cambay, produced beads and ornaments for sale across the Arabian Sea, and indigo cultivated in the interior supplied dye for the textile trade. This division of labor shows how a port's prosperity rested on a dense network of rural and urban craft production, not just on merchants.

Credit made such a system function. Merchants advanced funds to weavers and dyers against future deliveries, shipowners raised capital by selling shares of voyages, and bankers moved funds through bills of exchange. Jain and other Hindu merchant families figured prominently in these financial networks, and Muslim traders also participated. The surviving evidence includes inscriptions recording endowments, later commercial documents, and European comments, and it is uneven. It does show that Gujarat's economy relied on a high degree of trust within and across communities, a point that helps explain why the sultans valued merchant goodwill and why disorder in the 1530s and 1540s damaged both state and commerce.

The relationship between rulers and merchants was reciprocal. The sultan's government taxed trade and provided military protection, courts, and public order; merchants lent money, financed building projects, and sometimes held office. This partnership helps explain why Gujarat's sultanate invested heavily in urban infrastructure.

Merchant wealth also supported religious institutions. Jain merchants endowed temples and libraries, Muslim traders supported mosques and shrines, and Hindu communities patronized temples and charity. These patterns of patronage show that commercial prosperity was woven into the sultanate's religious life.

Section 47.17

Gujarat's Architecture, Religious Institutions, Literature, and Urban Society

Gujarat's sultanate produced one of the most distinctive regional architectures in South Asia. Builders drew on a deep tradition of Gujarati stone carving and combined it with the forms of Islamic congregational architecture. Ahmedabad's Jami Masjid (1424) has a vast courtyard and prayer hall with a forest of columns, and the Teen Darwaza gateway, the Bhadra citadel, and the tomb complex at Sarkhej, associated with the Sufi shaykh Ahmad Khattu, show the range of royal and religious patronage. At Champaner, the Jami Masjid and surrounding monuments illustrate Mahmud Begada's second capital. Intricately carved stepwells, such as the one at Adalaj (1499), were commissioned by notable patrons including a Vaghela chief's wife, and they show a shared civic architecture serving all communities.

Religious life was plural. Jain monks and merchants maintained temples and libraries; Hindu temples remained active in many districts; Sufi orders, especially the Suhrawardi and Chishti traditions, built khanqahs and attracted devotees; and Muslim scholars taught in madrasas. Persian was the language of administration, while Sanskrit, Prakrit, and Old Gujarati sustained literary traditions. The devotional poetry associated with Narsinh Mehta, traditionally placed in the fifteenth century though his dates are debated, belongs to this period of vernacular creativity.

Urban society was diverse and stratified. Ahmedabad's neighborhoods (puras) grouped artisans, merchants, and religious communities; guilds regulated some crafts; and large households combined kin, servants, and dependents. Women's lives are known mainly through religious endowments, inscriptions, and chronicle references to royal women. Patronage by women such as Rani Rudabai's commission of Adalaj suggests that elite women controlled resources. Ordinary women's work in textile production, domestic labor, and market trade is documented only indirectly. Slavery existed, and many Habshi and other enslaved persons served in households and armies.

Gujarat's urban culture endured after the sultanate fell. Mughal rule absorbed the commercial networks and architectural traditions, as Chapter 51 notes.

Evidence for religious patronage in Gujarat includes cases that cut against any simple narrative of conflict. Around 1530, the Jain merchant Karma Shah obtained permission from Gujarat's Sultan Bahadur Shah to restore the pilgrimage site of Shatrunjaya, a major Jain center, an episode recorded in Jain inscriptions and later tradition, and it indicates that Jain communities could secure official support for the maintenance of their holy places under the sultanate. At the same time, Persian sources and inscriptions record the building of mosques and tombs on or near older religious sites. Both patterns existed, and the balance varied from reign to reign and place to place.

Gujarati literature developed in this setting. Padmanabha's Kanhadade Prabandha, composed in the mid-fifteenth century in a language that stands between late Apabhramsha and early Gujarati, narrates a Rajput resistance to Delhi and illustrates how regional heroic traditions could be cultivated under a Muslim dynasty. Devotional poetry traditionally ascribed to Narsinh Mehta belongs to the same century, though scholars debate his dates and the authenticity of particular verses. Meanwhile, Sufi families such as the descendants of Shaykh Ahmad Khattu and Shah Alam of Rasulabad established shrines that served communities across religious lines. Women patrons, including queens and local rulers' wives, built wells and religious structures, and their donations show that elite women could control resources and shape public space.

Division III — Malwa: Central Indian Kingship and the Culture of Mandu
Section 47.18

Malwa's Independence and the Establishment of Regional Rule

Malwa, the plateau north of the Narmada, controlled the routes linking Gujarat, Rajasthan, the Gangetic plain, and the Deccan. Delhi had governed it through appointed officers since the early fourteenth century. Dilawar Khan Ghuri, a noble of Afghan or Ghurid descent according to the chronicles, was made governor in the 1390s. After Timur's invasion left Delhi powerless, Dilawar Khan acted independently, and by about 1401 he was ruling in practice as a sovereign, although chronicles differ about when he formally assumed royal titles. His son and successor, Hoshang Shah, is more clearly the first fully acknowledged sultan.

The plateau's position explains much of Malwa's political life. It was a corridor, so it attracted ambitious neighbors, and its fortified hills offered defensible capitals. Its agricultural base, including wheat, cotton, and opium poppy in later periods, supported a respectable revenue, but the kingdom was never as wealthy as Gujarat or Bengal. Its rulers therefore depended on military strength and alliances, and the kingdom spent much of its history in conflict with Gujarat, Mewar, and the Deccan states.

Evidence for early Malwa is mixed: Persian chronicles (several composed in Gujarat or at the Mughal court), inscriptions at Mandu and Dhar, and the buildings themselves. Jain manuscripts and inscriptions from the region document the commercial and religious community that flourished beside the sultanate.

Malwa's earlier history helps explain why the plateau mattered. Before the Delhi conquests, the region had been the heartland of the Paramara dynasty, whose capital at Dhar and fortress at Mandu left a legacy of fortifications, tanks, and temples that the sultanate rulers inherited. The sultans therefore ruled a landscape already marked by royal building and by Jain and Hindu religious institutions, and they governed through a population in which Rajput landholders, merchant communities, and religious establishments were deeply rooted. The kingdom's history is accordingly not just a story of Muslim dynasts on a frontier, but of an older regional society brought under new rulers.

The sources for Malwa are less abundant than those for Gujarat or Bengal. A Persian account associated with Shihab Hakim and devoted to the reign of Mahmud Khalji I is often cited as a near-contemporary work, though its authorship and transmission are discussed by specialists, while later general histories such as Firishta and the Tabaqat-i-Akbari fill in the sequence. Buildings and inscriptions at Dhar, Mandu, and Chanderi provide the firmest dates. As elsewhere, historians reading these works must keep in mind that most were written either at rival courts or under the Mughals, and the picture of Malwa's rulers they present reflects those vantage points.

Section 47.19

Hoshang Shah and the Consolidation of Malwa

Hoshang Shah (r. c. 1406–1435) shifted the capital from Dhar to Mandu, a fortified plateau city on a spur of the Vindhya range, sealed by deep ravines and a perimeter wall that stretches for kilometers. Mandu combined natural defense with ample water, supplied through reservoirs and cisterns. From it Hoshang Shah controlled the surrounding plains and monitored routes toward Gujarat and the Deccan.

His reign began poorly: in 1407–08 he was defeated and held for a time by Muzaffar Shah of Gujarat, and Gujarat's rulers repeatedly interfered in Malwa's affairs. Yet he recovered, strengthened the army, and fortified a string of strongholds. Later chronicles recount a campaign into Odisha (Orissa) around 1421–1422 to obtain war elephants, including a dramatic account of Hoshang travelling disguised as a merchant; the expedition is historically reported, but its dramatic details require caution. What is reasonably clear is that Hoshang developed a royal court with noble ranks, a standing force, and patronage that attracted scholars, poets, and builders.

Administrative arrangements resembled those of other regional sultanates: the ruler appointed governors and fort commanders, granted revenue assignments to nobles, and relied on Rajput and Afghan military support. Hindu Rajput landholders, scribes, and merchants took part in governance. Hoshang's death in 1435 opened a succession dispute that brought a new dynasty to power.

Mandu's political importance lay in how it combined defense, water, and command of routes. The plateau stands high above the Narmada valley, with deep ravines on several sides and a circuit of walls punctuated by gateways, and it offered rulers a refuge as well as a base. Control of the hill, however, also required the ruler to supply and garrison a very large site, which meant dependence on revenues from the surrounding districts and on the loyalty of fort commanders. It is no accident that the great struggles of Malwa's history repeatedly centered on fortresses such as Mandu, Chanderi, and Gagron.

Hoshang's court drew on a wide set of communities. Jain sources record ministers and merchants of Jain background in the service of Malwa's sultans; one of the best known, Mandan, is associated in Jain colophons with Hoshang's court. Their presence shows that religious affiliation did not exclude some skilled Jain officials and merchants from courtly service, and that Jain monks and laity found a place within sultanate society, even as Muslim nobles and scholars constituted the core of the ruling elite. The record is partial, since the surviving colophons and inscriptions mostly reflect the concerns of the communities that produced them, but it is enough to show that Malwa's court was socially more varied than royal chronicles alone suggest.

Section 47.20

The Khalji Dynasty of Malwa: Expansion, Warfare, and Regional Rivalries

After Hoshang's brief successors, the minister Mahmud Khan seized the throne in 1436 and founded the Khalji dynasty of Malwa. Mahmud Khalji I (r. 1436–1469) spent most of his reign at war. He fought Gujarat, Mewar, the Bahmanis, and the Lodis' predecessors in Delhi, and he expanded his kingdom into parts of Rajasthan, Bundelkhand, and Khandesh's borders. His long conflict with the Rana of Mewar, Kumbha, produced competing claims of victory: Mewar built the Vijaya Stambha at Chittor in the 1440s, tradition associating it with a triumph over Malwa, while Mahmud commemorated his own successes with a tower at Mandu. Such parallel claims illustrate why royal chronicles and monuments must be read as propaganda as well as evidence.

His successors, Ghiyath Shah (1469–1500) and Nasir Shah (1500–1510), ruled a kingdom that had reached its limits. Ghiyath Shah's reign is remembered for peace and court luxury, and Persian sources record his large household and personal piety in sometimes extravagant terms. Under Mahmud II (1510–1531), a faction led by the Rajput minister Medini Rai gained influence, provoking noble resentment and Gujarat's intervention. In 1531 Bahadur Shah of Gujarat captured Mandu, marking the end of Malwa's effective independence for a time.

Mahmud Khalji I's wars show the character of Malwa's strategic position. He took the fortress of Chanderi in 1438 and extended influence over parts of Bundelkhand, but his attempts to expand were repeatedly checked: his armies fought with Mewar's Rana Kumbha, with Gujarat, and, in 1440, with the Delhi sultanate, where the Afghan commander Bahlul Lodi helped repel a Malwa advance. In 1456 Mahmud and Gujarat's sultan Qutb-ud-Din Ahmad Shah II agreed at Champaner to a treaty that envisaged joint action against Mewar, an arrangement that illustrates how rivalries could give way to alliances when neighbors shared an enemy. Even so, the partnership did not last, and Malwa and Gujarat remained rivals.

The later Khalji rulers faced the problem of factional politics. In the first decades of the sixteenth century, Mahmud II relied heavily on Medini Rai, whose rise alienated Muslim nobles and provoked a coup attempt. The sultan also fought Rana Sanga of Mewar, who defeated and captured him at Gagron in 1519 and later released him. Each of these episodes weakened the sultan's standing, and by 1531 Bahadur Shah of Gujarat found the kingdom an easy conquest. The Khalji dynasty therefore ended not because of a single defeat but because it could never resolve the tension among noble factions, Rajput allies, and powerful neighbors.

Malwa's limits were partly structural. It lacked Gujarat's port revenues and Bengal's agricultural abundance, it was hemmed in by stronger powers, and its nobility was fractious. The Khalji rulers also recruited Hindu Rajput chiefs, a policy that could strengthen the army but stir resentment among Muslim nobles, as Medini Rai's rise demonstrated.

Section 47.21

Mandu: Palaces, Mosques, Waterworks, and Courtly Culture

Mandu remains one of the most remarkable architectural landscapes of medieval South Asia. Its Jami Masjid, begun under Hoshang Shah and completed under Mahmud Khalji I in 1454, rests on a high platform with a deeply colonnaded courtyard. Hoshang Shah's tomb, of white marble, is often described as an early Indian example of a fully marble Islamic tomb, and an inscription records a visit by architects of Shah Jahan's court in the seventeenth century, evidence of its later prestige. The Jahaz Mahal (Ship Palace), associated with Ghiyath Shah, stretches between two artificial lakes, and the Hindola Mahal, with sloping walls, was probably a royal audience hall.

Water was central. Mandu's builders captured monsoon rain in tanks, baolis, and canals, and an elaborate system supplied palaces, baths, and pleasure pavilions. This waterworks shows both engineering skill and the royal enjoyment of landscape architecture. It also depended on laborers, masons, and craftsmen of many communities whose names are rarely recorded.

Court culture combined Persian poetry and music with regional traditions. Ghiyath Shah and others patronized manuscript production, and Jain scribes copied works in Mandu and its region, including the Kalpasutra manuscript tradition associated with the Mandu school. A prosperous Jain mercantile community lived in the area, and inscriptions record their donations. Mandu thus shows how a smaller sultanate could create a lasting cultural legacy by investing in architecture, water, and patronage.

Mandu's buildings show the dynasties' priorities and tastes. The Jami Masjid, a vast congregational mosque on a raised plinth, and the nearby Ashrafi Mahal, a structure that began as a madrasa and was later expanded as a tomb and commemorative monument, were built on a monumental scale. A tower of victory attributed to Mahmud Khalji I, of which only part survives, can be read alongside the victory monument raised by Mewar's ruler at Chittor; a direct intention to rival it is harder to demonstrate. Hoshang Shah's tomb, completed after his death, uses marble and sandstone and a design of a single large dome, and its later reputation is confirmed by seventeenth-century inscriptions recording visits by architects of the Mughal court. The fact that Mughal builders valued the tomb enough to inscribe their names there shows how regional achievements fed into imperial architecture.

Water engineering was equally important. The plateau lacks rivers, so rain was captured in reservoirs and led through channels to palaces and pavilions. Structures such as the Jahaz Mahal, set between two reservoirs, and the Hindola Mahal, an audience hall with sloping walls, make sense only within this system. The work required skilled masons, surveyors, and many laborers, whose names and conditions are almost entirely unrecorded. Another reminder of the limits of the record is that popular names such as "Ship Palace" or "Swinging Palace" belong to later usage, and attributions of specific rooms to specific sultans remain provisional.

Section 47.22

Baz Bahadur, Rupmati, and the End of Independent Malwa

After Bahadur Shah's death in 1537 and the interlude of Mughal and then Sur rule, Malwa came under the Sur governor Shuja Khan and later his son Baz Bahadur, who asserted independence around 1555. He fought neighbors, but his army was inadequate, and in 1561 Akbar's general Adham Khan and Pir Muhammad Khan defeated him near Sarangpur and took Malwa. Baz Bahadur fled, was later pardoned, and entered Mughal service in 1570, an arc typical of the incorporation of regional rulers into the empire.

Baz Bahadur is also the protagonist of a famous love story with Rupmati, a singer or poet, whom later tradition describes as taking her own life after the Mughal conquest. This story circulated in Persian, Hindi, and later vernacular literature and inspired paintings and poetry, but it appears in sources well after the events. Historians therefore separate the documented political outcome, Baz Bahadur's defeat in 1561–62, from the romantic narrative. Buildings at Mandu and Sarangpur have been associated with them, though modern scholars regard such attributions as popular tradition rather than established fact.

The sequence of Malwa's rulers after 1531 is complicated. Humayun took Mandu in 1535 during his campaign against Bahadur Shah, but he soon withdrew, and local commanders such as Mallu Khan, who took the title Qadir Shah, held power. After Sher Shah's rise, the province came under Sur governors, among them Shuja Khan, and after his death his son Baz Bahadur declared himself ruler. Baz Bahadur was, according to later Persian and Hindi tradition, a patron of music, but the same sources describe a kingdom with a weak army and quarreling nobles. When Akbar's commanders Adham Khan and Pir Muhammad Khan marched into Malwa in 1561, Baz Bahadur's forces were defeated near Sarangpur, and the conquest was followed by harsh treatment of the defeated population, which later Mughal narratives themselves describe with some embarrassment.

Mandu did not vanish after the conquest. The Mughal emperor Jahangir visited it in the early seventeenth century and described its buildings and climate in his memoirs, which shows that the site retained courtly interest. But the political significance of Malwa shifted from an independent plateau kingdom to a Mughal province, and the monuments associated with Baz Bahadur and Rupmati took on their romantic aura only as later poets, painters, and local guides retold the story. The episode is a useful case of how evidence about a place can accumulate layers of memory that must be peeled back carefully.

The episode illustrates a recurring problem for historians: dramatic stories often attach themselves to the last days of dynasties. They preserve cultural memory and artistic imagination but should not be treated as evidence for private lives.

Division IV — Jaunpur: The Sharqi Sultanate and the Eastern Gangetic World
Section 47.23

The Foundation of the Sharqi Sultanate of Jaunpur

Jaunpur, on the Gomti River in the eastern Ganges plain, had been founded or refounded in the fourteenth century and was an important Tughlaq base. In 1394 the Tughlaq Sultan Muhammad Shah IV appointed a court eunuch and senior minister, Khwaja-i-Jahan Malik Sarwar, as governor of the eastern territories with the title Malik al-Sharq ("Lord of the East"). The title gave the dynasty its name, Sharqi. Delhi's authority was weakening, and Sarwar subdued rebellious zamindars and expanded his control over a broad swathe of the Ganges-Yamuna doab, Awadh, Tirhut, and parts of Bihar.

After Timur's invasion Sarwar's position became independent in practice. His adopted son Mubarak Shah took the royal title and issued coins, with the Sharqi state formally dating from about 1399–1402. The kingdom was strategically placed between Delhi and Bengal, and its rulers were drawn into the politics of both. It was rich in agricultural land and was a gateway to the trade routes of the Gangetic plain.

Sharqi chronicle evidence is relatively thin, since much of the narrative comes from Delhi-centered or Mughal-era works. Inscriptions and monuments are therefore especially valuable, as the later sections show.

Jaunpur's early history shows how a provincial center could become a capital. The city was founded in the middle of the fourteenth century on the Gomti, and tradition holds that Firuz Shah Tughlaq developed it during his march to Bengal, naming it in honor of Muhammad bin Tughlaq, whose personal name was Jauna Khan. The site lay on routes connecting Delhi, Awadh, Bihar, and Bengal, and it controlled access to some of the most productive agricultural lands of the Gangetic plain. When a court eunuch of the Tughlaq house, Malik Sarwar, received authority in the east in 1394, he was taking charge of a region that was rich but turbulent, where local landholders had long resisted central control.

Malik Sarwar's appointment illustrates the devices that late Tughlaq rulers used to hold distant territory. A trusted court servant was given a high title and extraordinary authority in the hope that loyalty to the throne would outlast distance. In practice, the strategy failed when the throne itself collapsed. The Sharqis therefore began as agents of Delhi and became independent because the institution that appointed them no longer functioned, a path comparable to that of Gujarat's and Malwa's governors but distinctive in that it ran through the office of a court eunuch rather than a military commander. Persian narratives of these origins were written later, from Delhi or the Mughal court, so the details of Malik Sarwar's rise are better understood through inscriptions and coin evidence than through the surviving chronicles.

Section 47.24

The Sharqi Rulers: Government, Warfare, and Relations with Delhi and Bengal

Ibrahim Shah Sharqi (r. 1402–1440) was the dynasty's greatest ruler. He extended influence over a large part of the Gangetic plain, supported rival claimants in Delhi against Mallu Iqbal Khan, and intervened in Bengal during the crisis around Raja Ganesha, described in section 47.4. His campaigns and diplomacy demonstrate how regional sultans drew on one another's rivalries. He also maintained a reputation for justice and learning that later writers celebrated.

His successors, Mahmud Shah (1440–1457), Muhammad Shah (1457–58), and Husain Shah (from 1458, with authority in Jaunpur ending amid the campaigns of c. 1479–1483), continued competing with Delhi, Bengal, and Orissa. Mahmud Shah campaigned in Orissa and against Delhi. Husain Shah, the last ruler, repeatedly attacked the Delhi Sultanate, and several of his advances brought Sharqi forces to the walls of Delhi, but he could not defeat the Lodis decisively.

Ibrahim Shah's reign shows the ambitions and limits of Jaunpur's power. He conducted campaigns toward Kalpi and Delhi, where rival nobles claimed authority after Timur's invasion, and the sources describe him as a ruler who patronized scholars and kept an orderly court. His intervention in Bengal during the crisis involving Raja Ganesha, in response to an appeal from the Sufi circle at Pandua, shows the interdependence of the eastern kingdoms. Yet the Bengal intervention ended inconclusively, and he withdrew without establishing lasting control, a reminder that even the strongest regional sultans found it difficult to hold distant territory.

Later Sharqi history was dominated by competition with Delhi. Husain Shah Sharqi, who came to the throne in 1458, repeatedly marched toward Delhi, reaching its outskirts on several occasions according to Persian accounts, but he never captured the city. Bahlul Lodi's strategy relied on Afghan military settlers, kinship networks, and treaties, and he was able to exploit divisions within the Sharqi nobility and the loyalty of local zamindars. Historians reading the Persian accounts must remember that they were composed after the Lodi victory, and they emphasize the success of Bahlul's side. The picture that emerges from the fragmentary evidence is of two comparable states, each with a mounted aristocracy and local alliances, whose contest was finally resolved by the combination of Lodi persistence and Sharqi internal weakness.

The Sharqi state was governed through military assignments, appointed governors, and negotiated relations with local zamindars, a pattern similar to that of other regional sultanates. Revenue came from the rich agricultural lands of the region, supplemented by trade along the Ganges and Gomti. Sharqi coinage and inscriptions show that the dynasty claimed full sovereignty, though in some periods it recognized the nominal supremacy of Delhi in diplomatic exchanges. The state's weaknesses included the difficulty of controlling its own nobles and the pressures of its position between two major rivals.

Section 47.25

Jaunpur as a Center of Learning, Religious Institutions, and Architecture

After Timur's invasion, scholars, Sufis, and artisans from Delhi sought refuge in Jaunpur, and the city acquired the reputation of a "Shiraz of India," a label that evokes the Persian city renowned for poetry and scholarship. Jurists wrote works of Islamic law and grammar; court patronage supported madrasas; and Sufi shaykhs attracted disciples. The compilation of legal commentary and the circulation of manuscripts made Jaunpur a notable intellectual center. Vernacular literary traditions in Awadhi and Hindavi also flourished in the surrounding region, though their direct connection to Sharqi patronage requires careful evidence.

Jaunpur's architecture is distinctive. The Atala Mosque, whose foundations were prepared under Firuz Shah Tughlaq in the late 1370s and whose inscription records completion in 1408 under Ibrahim Shah, features a towering central propylon (pylon-shaped gateway) with trabeate and arcuate elements. The Jhanjhri Mosque, the Lal Darwaza Mosque (associated with a queen of Mahmud Shah), and the great Jami Masjid, whose foundation was laid under Ibrahim Shah in 1438 and which was completed in stages under Husain Shah in the 1470s, repeat the grand propylon form. This style blends features of Tughlaq building with local Indian elements, and many structures incorporate reused stone from earlier temples, as in Bengal. Interpretations of the reuse vary, from pragmatic use of available materials to a symbolic display of conquest.

The learned culture of Jaunpur is known in part through the careers of scholars connected with the court. One of the best known, the jurist and grammarian Shihabuddin Daulatabadi, worked in Ibrahim Shah's circle and is credited with works on grammar, Quranic interpretation, and legal matters; later Sharqi-era manuscripts and legal compilations associated with the dynasty's reign testify to an active tradition of Islamic learning. Sufi lineages, including those of the Chishti and other orders, maintained lodges in and around the city, and their relationship with the court combined spiritual authority and political usefulness. Later tradition also associates Husain Shah Sharqi with innovation in Hindustani music, including the raga called Jaunpuri, but the attribution rests on late accounts and should be treated as an example of how cultural reputation could attach to a royal name.

Architecture provides more secure evidence for patronage. The Lal Darwaza Mosque, associated with a queen of Mahmud Shah, indicates that royal women commissioned major buildings, and the Khalis Mukhlis Mosque, named for two nobles, shows that members of the aristocracy sponsored religious architecture as well. The mosques' large propylons, which block the view of the domes behind them, give the buildings a recognizable profile that differs from the Tughlaq architecture of Delhi and from the brick mosques of Bengal. They show a regional style growing out of local materials and craft habits under a ruling house that wished to express independence through building. Inscriptions that give dates, patron names, and Quranic or devotional formulae provide a comparatively precise framework for the architectural sequence.

Monumental survival at Jaunpur is greater than narrative documentation, which is why buildings and inscriptions carry so much weight.

Section 47.26

The Decline of Sharqi Independence and Its Historical Legacy

The Sharqi state faced a determined rival in Bahlul Lodi, who built the Lodi Afghan power in Delhi (Chapter 43). Repeated wars through the 1450s–1470s alternated between truces and campaigns. Husain Shah Sharqi marched on Delhi more than once and failed to take it. After a long conflict, Bahlul gained control of Jaunpur through campaigns beginning in 1479 and culminating in renewed occupation around 1483–1484; the precise endpoint varies among historical reconstructions. His son Barbak later governed the region. Husain Shah attempted to regain the city and survived in Bihar, where a dedicated study dates his death to around 1505.

The Lodi conquest did not erase Jaunpur's institutions. Sikandar Lodi later reorganized the region, and the Lodi administration used Jaunpur's local elites and officials. Resistance persisted among zamindars and Sharqi partisans, and the eastern territories remained politically unsettled until Mughal and Sur consolidation.

Understanding the end of Sharqi independence also means paying attention to the sources. The principal narrative accounts of the Lodi conquest, such as Firishta's history and the Afghan histories compiled in the late sixteenth century, were written long after the events and reflect Afghan and Mughal interests. Their dates and details differ, which is why modern historians do not treat 1479 as a single decisive moment. A better reading is that Bahlul's campaigns weakened Jaunpur through the late 1470s and early 1480s, that the city was taken and retaken, and that Husain Shah's final loss of the capital came after a series of defeats. Efforts to fix a single date risk hiding the gradual nature of the collapse.

Jaunpur's afterlife was long. Under Sikandar Lodi the city became a base for imperial control of the east, and Sikandar's agreement with Husain Shah of Bengal over their shared frontier shows how the former Sharqi region had become a borderland between larger powers. Under Akbar the governor Munim Khan built a major stone bridge across the Gomti, completed in the late 1560s, which still links the city's two banks; it is a reminder that the Mughals continued to invest in Jaunpur's infrastructure. The mosques of the Sharqi period survived as places of worship and as monuments, and the city's reputation as a center of learning persisted in later tradition.

Jaunpur's legacy lies in its architecture, its role as a center of learning, and its example of an independent eastern Gangetic state. Its mosques remain among the most original medieval buildings in northern India. Its brief independence also shows how deeply competition between Delhi and the eastern powers shaped the region before the Mughals.

Division V — Kashmir: Himalayan Kingship, Society, and Cultural Change
Section 47.27

Kashmir's Himalayan Setting and the Sources for Sultanate History

The Kashmir Valley is an oval basin roughly 130 kilometers long, drained by the Jhelum and ringed by the Pir Panjal and Himalayan ranges. Snowmelt supports rice, saffron, orchards, and a dense network of irrigation channels (kuls). Passes carry routes to Punjab, to the Karakoram and Central Asia, to Ladakh and Tibet, and toward Kabul. Winter snow can seal them, giving the valley both protection and seasonal isolation. This geography encouraged a distinctive political life, since invaders had difficulty entering and rulers could not easily project power outward.

Kashmir has an unusually rich historical record. Kalhana's Rajatarangini, composed in the mid-twelfth century in Sanskrit, was continued by Jonaraja (to about 1459) and Srivara (to the 1480s), both writing under the Shah Mir sultans, and later by Prajyabhatta and Suka. Persian histories of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, notably the Baharistan-i-Shahi and the works of Haidar Malik, draw on and reinterpret these accounts. Inscriptions, coins, and shrines complete the picture.

These sources need critical reading. The Sanskrit chroniclers were courtiers who wrote about rulers they served, and the Persian authors wrote when the Mughals had conquered Kashmir and when religious communities had developed their own memories of earlier events. Ruler-centered narratives preserve little about peasants, artisans, or women. When the accounts of religious policy conflict, as they do for several sultans, historians weigh the genre, date, and perspective of each source.

The chronicle tradition requires explanation because it is so unusual. Kalhana's Rajatarangini, finished in the 1140s, was a self-conscious attempt to write critical history in Sanskrit, and later authors, notably Jonaraja, whose narrative reaches about 1459, and his pupil Srivara, who continues into the 1480s, saw themselves as inheritors of that tradition. Because these writers served the Shah Mir court, they wrote about Muslim rulers in a Sanskrit idiom of kingship and with the viewpoint of Kashmiri Brahman scholars. Their works are therefore valuable evidence for how a literate Hindu elite understood and adapted to the new order, and they are not neutral descriptions of events. Persian histories add a different voice, but they date from the early seventeenth century, among them the Baharistan-i-Shahi and the work of Haidar Malik, and they draw on earlier texts while shaping the story to the concerns of their time. Mirza Haidar Dughlat's Tarikh-i-Rashidi, written in the mid-sixteenth century by a participant in Kashmir's politics, is a crucial near-contemporary account for the 1540s.

Geography also shaped the possibilities of rule. Several major routes led into the valley: one through the Pir Panjal passes toward Punjab, another along the Jhelum gorge toward Muzaffarabad, a third eastward over high passes to Ladakh and Tibet. Winter closed some of them and made others dangerous, so seasonal movement of troops, traders, and pilgrims was a basic feature of life. The valley's compactness, with one major river, a dense network of settlements, and a capital in the Srinagar region, made it easier to govern than many larger territories, but its mountain rim made outside intervention difficult, a combination that helps explain both the survival of Kashmir's distinctive culture and the periodic dominance of powerful noble families.

Section 47.28

The End of Earlier Dynasties and the Foundation of Shah Mir Rule

Early fourteenth-century Kashmir was politically fragile. The Hindu ruler Suhadeva faced external raids; in 1320 a force led by Zulju (also rendered Dulucha), a Turkic or Mongol commander, devastated the valley, and the king fled. Rinchana, a prince of Tibetan or Ladakhi origin who had taken refuge in Kashmir, seized the throne. Persian tradition holds that he converted to Islam under the influence of a Sufi named Bulbul Shah and took the name Sadr-ud-Din, becoming the first Muslim ruler of Kashmir; Sanskrit sources preserve a different emphasis, and the story remains partly legendary. His rule was short, ending around 1323.

The political vacuum was filled by several actors. Shah Mir, an immigrant noble from the northwest whose origins in Swat or neighboring regions are described variously, had arrived in the valley in the 1310s and gained position at court. After Rinchana's death, the Hindu prince Udayanadeva held power, and his wife, Kota Rani, who had formerly been Rinchana's queen, exercised influence. When Udayanadeva died in 1338, Kota Rani briefly ruled, but Shah Mir took power in 1339 as Shams-ud-Din and established the dynasty that governed Kashmir, with interruptions, until the 1550s. Kota Rani's fate is told in different ways in the sources: Jonaraja describes her death after Shah Mir's takeover.

Accounts of Rinchana's conversion illustrate the problem of sources. Persian traditions credit the Sufi Bulbul Shah with converting him and describe the conversion as a spiritual event. Sanskrit accounts, as later retold, say that Rinchana sought admission to Hindu society and was rebuffed by the Brahmans of the valley before turning elsewhere. The two stories serve different communities' memories: one stresses the persuasive power of Sufi teaching, the other the rigidity of a Hindu hierarchy. A cautious historian can conclude that Rinchana, a Buddhist or at least non-Muslim outsider by origin, did become a Muslim ruler in the 1320s and that his conversion was entangled with questions of legitimacy in a society that did not easily accommodate an outsider king, but the motives and the circumstances remain uncertain.

The role of Kota Rani, whose story has attracted both scholarly and popular interest, shows that women could be central political actors. She appears in the sources as the wife of successive rulers and, for a short time, as ruler in her own right. Her fate after Shah Mir's takeover is told differently in different accounts, and later tradition has embellished her story with romantic and tragic detail. The reliable core is that the early 1330s and 1340s were a time of intrigue in which immigrant nobles and local elites competed, and that Shah Mir's success depended on building coalitions among them. He founded the first long-lived Muslim dynasty of Kashmir not by dramatic conquest but by outlasting rivals.

This sequence demonstrates how a mountain kingdom could be reshaped by external shocks, immigrant elites, and religious change, and how later chroniclers constructed legitimizing narratives of dynastic succession.

Section 47.29

The Early Shah Mir Sultans, Sikandar, and Political and Religious Transformation

Shah Mir's successors consolidated the dynasty by cultivating local elites, building mosques, and welcoming Sufi preachers and scholars. Sultan Qutb-ud-Din (r. c. 1373–1389) supported the activity of Sayyid Ali Hamadani, a Persian Sufi scholar associated with the Kubrawi order, who visited the valley with followers, and whose son Mir Muhammad Hamadani later became a major figure. Their presence expanded Persian Islamic institutions, including khanqahs, in Srinagar. Sayyid Ali Hamadani's writings on ethics and governance circulated widely, and the shrine of his khanqah, the Khanqah-i-Mu'alla, remains central.

Sikandar (r. c. 1389–1413) is the most controversial early ruler. Persian chronicles remember him as Butshikan ("breaker of idols"), claiming that he destroyed many temples, imposed the jizya, and pressured Hindus, especially Brahmans, to convert or leave, with his minister Suhabhatta, a convert, acting as an instigator. Jonaraja, writing within a few decades, also criticizes the policies of Sikandar's reign, and some temple sites show evidence of damage, though archaeology alone cannot attribute particular episodes of destruction to his reign. Scholars debate scale and motive: some stress ideological zeal, others emphasize the role of fiscal pressure, factional politics, and the later amplification of Sikandar's image by Persian writers who wished to celebrate Islamic piety. Few historians doubt that significant coercion occurred, but exact numbers of conversions and destruction are uncertain. Sikandar also built the Jami Masjid at Srinagar, a large wooden-pillared mosque in Kashmiri style.

Sayyid Ali Hamadani's role is central to the later memory of Kashmir's Islamization, but the details are debated. Tradition places his visits in the 1380s, with a later arrival of his son Mir Muhammad Hamadani in the 1390s, and credits the Hamadani circle with a large number of conversions and with introducing crafts from Iran and Central Asia. Modern scholarship is cautious about such claims. The sources that make them were composed later, often by members of the Sufi lineages that claimed descent from the Hamadanis, and they have an interest in magnifying the founder's achievements. What can be said with more confidence is that the Hamadani visits mark the growing presence of Persian-speaking Sufi networks in the valley, and that their khanqahs became institutions that combined worship, charity, and political influence.

Sikandar's reign belongs within that context. The ruler's minister Suhabhatta, a convert from a Brahman family, appears in Persian narratives as a driving force behind harsh measures toward his former community, and Jonaraja's Sanskrit chronicle mentions a period of suffering for Brahmans in the reign. A Persian tradition describes large numbers of Brahmans leaving the valley. The combination of Sanskrit and Persian testimony makes it likely that serious pressure was applied, even if numbers and motives are uncertain. But historians also point to continuing Hindu participation in the administration, to the king's own mixed court, and to evidence that the policies were not applied uniformly. Chapter 48 considers how Sufi and devotional movements shaped religious life across South Asia.

Historical Vocabulary

Rishi and the Rishi Order

In Kashmiri tradition Rishi refers to an ascetic or holy person, and the Rishi order designates a Muslim mystical tradition associated with Nund Rishi (Sheikh Nuruddin, traditionally c. 1378–1440, though the dates are debated). Rishis combined Sufi ideals with local ascetic practices, including vegetarianism, forest retreat, and veneration of nature, and they preached in Kashmiri rather than Persian or Arabic. The term shows how Kashmir's religious culture blended Islam with earlier Shaiva and Buddhist ascetic traditions. It should not be reduced to either "Hindu" or "Sufi": its significance lies in the interaction of both.

Section 47.30

Zain-ul-Abidin: Kingship, Administration, and Cultural Patronage

Zain-ul-Abidin (r. 1420–1470) is remembered as Kashmir's greatest sultan, honored by the title Budshah ("the great king"). He took power after a dynastic crisis and ruled for half a century. His government reversed many of the policies attributed to Sikandar. Sources report that he invited Hindu Pandits who had left the valley to return, restored or permitted the rebuilding of temples, abolished the jizya and, according to accounts of his reign, prohibited cow slaughter or restricted the consumption of beef in deference to Hindu subjects, and appointed Hindus to high positions. He also built irrigation works, bridges, and the artificial island of Zaina Lanka in the Wular Lake.

Zain-ul-Abidin's rise to power illustrates the dynasty's instability and his own political skill. Sikandar's son Ali Shah ruled from 1413 to 1420 and then, according to tradition, left on pilgrimage, which opened the way for his brother's accession. The new sultan faced rivals and had to reassert control over the nobility, which he did with a combination of patronage and military action. His reign lasted five decades, long enough for institutions to settle, and the contrast with the unstable decades before and after is one reason later memory idealized it. The chronicle of Jonaraja, which ends about 1459, and Srivara's continuation give the most detailed contemporary accounts.

The sultan was also an active builder and organizer of the landscape. He is credited with canals that extended irrigation, bridges across the Jhelum in Srinagar, and the artificial island of Zaina Lanka in the Wular Lake, where he built a palace and pavilions. His campaigns, including expeditions toward Ladakh and the northwest, projected Kashmiri power beyond the valley for a time. Persian sources praise his justice and tell stories of his personal concern for his subjects, but Sanskrit evidence, because it came from a Hindu court scholar, carries special weight for the picture of religious inclusion. His death in 1470 and the struggles among his sons showed how much depended on the ruler's personality, since the institutions he left proved unable to restrain factional rivalry among nobles and princes.

His court was exceptionally cosmopolitan. He supported translation between Sanskrit and Persian, including renderings of the Mahabharata and the Rajatarangini into Persian; encouraged Kashmiri, Persian, and Sanskrit literature; and welcomed Persian, Central Asian, and Indian scholars. Jonaraja and Srivara portray him as a patron of music, poetry, and craft, and Srivara's account is a major source. But these Sanskrit chroniclers were court writers, and later Persian authors burnished his image into that of an ideal ruler. Historians therefore use the sources to reconstruct the ruler's policies without accepting every idealizing detail.

His reputation also reflects the contrast with preceding and succeeding reigns. After his death, his sons fought over the succession, and the kingdom entered a period of weakness.

Section 47.31

Kashmir's Agrarian Economy, Crafts, and Commercial Networks

Kashmir's agriculture combined irrigated rice in the valley floor, wheat and barley in suitable areas, orchards, and the high-value crop saffron, grown on the plateaus near Pampore. Irrigation channels diverted river and spring water, and communities maintained them through collective labor. Land revenue was collected in grain and cash, and state officials supervised assessment, though local landholders and village headmen acted as intermediaries. Documentary detail from the sultanate period is limited; later Mughal sources describe a more elaborate system.

Craft production was prominent. Weavers made woolen cloth and, by the sixteenth century, the fine shawls that later made Kashmir famous. Tradition attributes the introduction of shawl weaving to Zain-ul-Abidin, who is said to have brought weavers from Central Asia. Historians evaluate this foundation legend critically: wool-weaving existed earlier, trade in fine goat-hair (pashm) wool from Ladakh and Tibet is documented, and the industry likely developed over time. Wood carving, papier-mâché, paper-making, stonework, and metalwork also flourished, and Central Asian craftsmen undoubtedly brought techniques.

Water management ties together much of what can be said about Kashmir's economy. Canals and channels, some attributed to Zain-ul-Abidin and others to earlier or later rulers, carried snowmelt to fields and orchards, and the maintenance of these works required both state direction and local cooperation. The saffron crop, cultivated on the dry terraces around Pampore, was labor-intensive and high-value, and it had a long-distance market in India and beyond; its cultivation, like that of rice, shows how the valley's agriculture supported both subsistence and exchange. The evidence for the sultanate period is limited, so historians infer much of the system from the better-documented Mughal era, a method that needs caution since institutions changed over time.

The valley's craft economy also depended on imports. Fine wool from the high plateaus to the east, often called pashm, reached Kashmir through merchants who crossed the mountains from Ladakh, and the weavers of Srinagar and other towns turned it into shawls. Paper-making, bookbinding, wood carving, and the building of wooden mosques also employed specialists, some of them immigrants from Iran and Central Asia. The craft traditions linked the valley to broader Asian markets: shawls went to Punjab, to Delhi, and eventually across the Mughal Empire. Kashmir's economy was therefore both agrarian and artisanal, tied to routes that crossed some of the world's highest mountains, and its prosperity depended on political arrangements that kept those routes open.

Trade connected the valley with Punjab, Kabul, Yarkand, and Tibet. Merchants carried shawls, saffron, fruit, and timber outward and brought horses, salt, wool, and manufactured goods inward. The mountain passes were both barrier and conduit, and rulers profited from tolls. This commerce, although smaller than Gujarat's, made Kashmir part of the wider Himalayan and Central Asian economy.

Section 47.32

Kashmiri Religious and Intellectual Worlds: Sufis, Rishis, Sanskrit, Persian, and Vernacular Traditions

Kashmir's religious life included Shaiva philosophy, Buddhist remnants, Brahmanical traditions, Persian Sufi orders, and the indigenous Rishi movement. Lal Ded (Lalleshvari), a fourteenth-century woman mystic, composed vakhs, short poems in Kashmiri that blend Shaiva yogic and mystic themes and are revered by both Hindus and Muslims. Her exact biography is surrounded by tradition, but her verses are among the earliest major texts in Kashmiri. Nund Rishi, who is said to have been influenced by her, preached an ethical mysticism, and his shrine at Charar-i-Sharif became a major pilgrimage center.

The coexistence of Sanskrit, Persian, and Kashmiri literary cultures was distinctive. Sanskrit remained the language of Hindu scholarship and of the chronicles, but Persian steadily took over administration and, increasingly, learning. Kashmiri developed as a literary language through mystic poetry. Pandit families adapted by learning Persian and entering government service, a development that shaped the valley's social structure.

Sufi institutions, including Kubrawi and later Suhrawardi networks, built khanqahs and attracted followers, while Rishis maintained a more local, ascetic style. Encounters were not always harmonious: Rishi and Sufi leaders sometimes disagreed about practice. Sources offer limited access to the beliefs of ordinary villagers, who probably participated in shrines and festivals in ways that do not fit neat categories.

The poetry of Lal Ded and the verses attributed to Nund Rishi form the foundation of Kashmiri vernacular literature. Lal Ded's vakhs, passed down orally for generations before being written, speak of the body, the self, and the divine in language that draws on Shaiva yogic ideas; Nund Rishi's shrukhs, couplets and short poems, deal with ethical conduct, renunciation, and devotion. The texts have been edited and reinterpreted many times, and scholars caution that what survives is a living tradition rather than a fixed original. For historians, the important point is that these voices represent a religious culture in which Hindu and Muslim idioms intersected, a point later communities have emphasized in different ways.

The intellectual life of the court also deserves mention. Sanskrit scholarship in Kashmir had a distinguished history, including the Shaiva philosophy associated with earlier centuries, and even under the sultans families of Pandits preserved libraries and learned traditions. Persian translations of Sanskrit works made at the sultan's court, and Sanskrit renderings of Persian material, represent an unusual two-way exchange. Yet the long-term trend favored Persian as the language of administration and advancement, and Sanskrit's role shrank. The Kashmiri Pandit adaptation to Persian service, which began in this period, became a durable feature of the valley's social history, with consequences studied in later chapters on the Mughal and later periods.

Section 47.33

The Chak Dynasty and the Mughal Incorporation of Kashmir

After Zain-ul-Abidin, the Shah Mir state weakened. Noble factions, especially the Chaks, Magres, and Rainas, dominated successive sultans. The Chaks, a powerful clan from the northern hills, rose in the sixteenth century. Mirza Haidar Dughlat, a Central Asian relative of Babur, held the valley from 1540 to 1551 and left a valuable account in his Tarikh-i-Rashidi. In 1561 Ghazi Shah Chak established the Chak dynasty, which ruled until 1586. Chak politics were marked by disputes between Sunni and Shia factions and by court intrigue.

Akbar's Mughal forces took the valley in 1586 after a campaign in which the last Chak ruler, Yusuf Shah, was drawn into negotiations and then detained, and his son Yaqub Shah resisted before surrendering. Kashmir was attached to the Mughal province of Kabul at first. Imperial rule brought new administrative arrangements and gardens, and also revenue demands, but the valley's language, shrines, and craft traditions continued.

Mughal incorporation changed Kashmir's political position, linking it to a vast empire and its markets, but it did not extinguish regional culture. Chapters 51 and 56 return to the valley in the Mughal context.

The Chak period also saw sharper sectarian tension. In the late fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, religious figures linked to Iranian intellectual networks, notably Mir Shams al-Din Iraqi, promoted Shi'i traditions in the valley, and the Chaks, whose clan came to dominate the court, were drawn toward it. Sunni nobles resisted, and Persian chronicles describe riots and killings in Srinagar. These conflicts, together with factional rivalry among the clans, weakened the state at the moment the Mughals began to turn their attention to the north. They also remind students that Muslim communities in Kashmir were internally divided, and that Sufi, Rishi, Sunni, and Shia traditions competed as well as coexisted.

The Mughal conquest itself was swift in military terms but not instantaneous in political ones. In 1586 an imperial force advanced through the passes, the last Chak sultan Yusuf Shah was drawn into negotiation and detained, and resistance led by his son Yaqub Shah ended after a few years. The valley became part of the empire, and Akbar visited it in 1589, an event commemorated in later accounts and in garden building. The new rulers brought different administrative practices and demanded revenue, but Kashmir's artisans, shrines, and languages continued, and the Mughals soon recognized the valley's craft and scenic value. Chapter 56 discusses the Mughal gardens and cultural life of Kashmir.

Section 47.34

Khandesh and the Smaller Kingdoms of the Regional Political System

Khandesh lay in the Tapti valley between Gujarat, Malwa, and the Deccan. Its rulers, the Faruqis, claimed descent from the caliph Umar al-Faruq. Malik Raja Faruqi, a Tughlaq-appointed noble, became effectively independent around 1382, a date conventionally used though the process was gradual. His successor Nasir Khan (r. c. 1399–1437) strengthened the state, founded Burhanpur (named for the Sufi Burhan-ud-Din) and fortified Asirgarh, a famous hill fortress controlling the route between north and south.

Khandesh was too small to stand alone, and its rulers survived by diplomacy. They married into neighboring houses, acknowledged the superiority of Gujarat at times, and allied with the Bahmani and Deccan sultans against Malwa or Gujarat when convenient. Its position along the main north-south route gave it commercial importance, and Burhanpur became a major textile and trade center. Its architecture, including mosques and tombs, shows influence from Gujarat and Malwa.

In 1601 Akbar's forces took Asirgarh after a long siege and annexed Khandesh. The case demonstrates that the five principal sultanates stood within a wider system of smaller states whose survival depended on balancing larger neighbors. It also anticipates Chapter 46's discussion of Deccan sultanate relations.

Khandesh's history shows how a small state used its position. The Tapti valley and the pass routes around Asirgarh linked Malwa and Gujarat in the north with the Deccan in the south, so the Faruqi rulers could profit from tolls and trade while finding themselves under pressure from every direction. Nasir Khan's foundation of Burhanpur gave the dynasty a commercial city in a district known for cotton, and the city's textile production and merchant community remained important well into the Mughal period. Persian chronicles report marriage alliances with Gujarat and the Deccan sultanates, and in some periods the Faruqis acknowledged Gujarat's overlordship, an arrangement that maintained their practical independence at the cost of formal deference.

Khandesh also reminds students that the regional political landscape was not limited to the six cases named in the chapter. Along the eastern frontier, rulers in Tripura and Arakan managed their own relations with Bengal, and in Rajasthan, Mewar and other Rajput kingdoms fought and negotiated with Malwa, Gujarat, and Delhi in turn. Including Khandesh in this chapter, as a short case, makes the larger point that the principal sultanates were embedded in a field of many smaller polities whose alliances, rivalries, and dependencies helped shape the larger conflicts. Its absorption by Akbar in 1601, after the fall of Asirgarh, completed the sequence of conquests traced in this chapter.

Section 47.35

Comparing Regional Kingship, Military Power, and Political Institutions

All five principal kingdoms used the formal apparatus of Islamic monarchy: the khutba, coinage, titles, court ritual, and the patronage of religious scholars. They differed in how they achieved independence, as the earlier divisions showed. Bengal's rulers were commanders who fought their way to unification; Gujarat's and Malwa's were governors who waited; Jaunpur's was an appointed deputy; Kashmir's was a succession of local and immigrant figures.

Military recruitment reveals both similarities and differences. Bengal relied on infantry, riverine forces, and, for a time, Habshi guards; Gujarat on cavalry, infantry, and a fleet; Malwa on fortress garrisons and Rajput contingents; Jaunpur on a cavalry-centered Gangetic army; Kashmir on mountain infantry and levies. Horses had to be imported, which tied military strength to trade routes. Nobles in each state held revenue assignments or fort commands, and their loyalty determined stability. Every dynasty experienced crises when nobles, guards, or ministers became too powerful.

Relations with local landholders also varied. In Bengal, zamindars and bhuiyans mediated rural control; in Gujarat, Rajput and Saurashtra chiefs retained local power; in Malwa, Rajput lords joined the court; in Kashmir, clan networks like the Chaks and Magres dominated; and in Jaunpur, zamindars of the doab remained a challenge. In none of them did the sultan govern directly at village level.

Three further comparisons deserve attention. The first concerns legitimacy. All of these rulers adopted the language of Islamic kingship, but they also drew on regional sources of authority: Bengal's sultans on their role as builders and patrons of local culture, Gujarat's on the prosperity they brought to merchant communities, Malwa's on the dramatic setting of Mandu, Jaunpur's on its claim to carry forward Delhi's tradition of learning, and Kashmir's on a blend of Sanskrit and Persian court traditions. The titles they adopted, the buildings they erected, and the ceremonies they performed formed a repertoire that rulers adapted to local audiences.

The second concerns military technology. By the early sixteenth century, firearms and artillery were becoming important. Gujarat employed gunners, among them the soldier known as Rumi Khan, whose artillery was used in Bahadur Shah's Chittor campaign, and Malwa and Bengal also had cannon in their arsenals. Yet the effect of gunpowder was uneven: Rivers, forts, elephants, and cavalry still shaped campaigns, and the Mughals' success owed as much to organization and logistics as to firearms. The third comparison concerns the fiscal-military bargain. Every state paid its soldiers and nobles through revenue assignments or cash, and the ability to collect revenue and reward followers determined survival. Bengal and Gujarat, with large cash revenues from trade and agriculture, could afford large establishments; Malwa and Jaunpur, with less revenue, relied more on Rajput alliances and plunder.

Section 47.36

Regional Societies: Rural Communities, Caste, Labor, Women, and Everyday Life

Most people in these kingdoms were cultivators. In Bengal's delta they planted rice, fished, and cleared land; in Gujarat they grew cotton and grain; in Malwa, wheat and other plateau crops; in the Gangetic plain around Jaunpur, grain and sugarcane; in Kashmir, irrigated rice and orchard crops. Villages were organized through kin, caste, and local headmen. Peasants owed revenue and sometimes labor, and they faced flood, drought, and war. Evidence for their lives is mostly indirect, drawn from later revenue records, religious literature, folk traditions, and archaeology.

Caste and occupational hierarchies shaped social life, but they varied regionally and were not identical to the modern category. Artisans such as weavers, potters, and metalworkers formed communities with their own rules; merchants organized by family and community; Brahmans and other literate groups served as scribes, priests, and advisers; and groups at the bottom of the hierarchy performed labor considered polluting and endured exclusion. Muslim communities likewise included ashraf elites of foreign descent and local converts, along with artisan and peasant groups, so social stratification existed within religious communities as well as between them.

Labor included free peasants, artisans, wage workers, bonded laborers, and enslaved people. Enslaved persons served in households, courts, and armies, with some, such as Habshis, achieving high rank. Women's lives are documented thinly. Royal and noble women appear in chronicles and inscriptions, merchant women through endowments, and ordinary women through occasional references to their work in spinning, weaving, farming, and trade. Prescriptive texts tell what elites thought women should do, not what all women did.

Differences of environment produced different patterns of settlement. In the delta, villages were scattered along rivers and levees, and the management of floods and clearing of land encouraged leadership by local chiefs and religious figures. In Gujarat, larger market towns and ports concentrated craft production and credit. On the Malwa plateau, fortified towns anchored districts of dry agriculture. In the Gangetic plain around Jaunpur, large villages and towns lay along the river routes. In Kashmir, villages followed the canals and orchards of the valley floor. Each pattern affected how much revenue could be collected, how easily armies could move, and how rural communities experienced the demands of the state.

The experience of women and subordinate groups must be reconstructed from fragments. Inscriptions and chronicles name queens such as Kota Rani in Kashmir, Bibi Raji in Jaunpur, and Rudabai in Gujarat, who built mosques, wells, or other works, and they hint at the influence of royal women in court politics. For most women, the evidence is indirect: references to spinning and weaving, to household labor, to marriage alliances among landholding families, or to religious practice at shrines. Enslaved and bonded people, whether domestic servants, soldiers, or laborers, appear in the sources as property or as exceptional rising figures, such as the Habshis who became nobles, which skews the picture. Periodic famine and epidemic, mentioned in chronicles of several regions, also reached the poor hardest, though the sources rarely say how. Recognizing these gaps is part of reading the history responsibly.

Section 47.37

Regional Courts and Cultures: Architecture, Literature, Religion, and Intellectual Exchange

The regional sultanates produced distinctive architectural styles precisely because they were independent. Bengal's brick mosques with curved cornices, Gujarat's carved stone and stepwells, Malwa's marble-and-sandstone palaces and tombs, Jaunpur's propylon mosques, and Kashmir's wooden structures show how common Islamic forms were adapted to local materials and craft traditions. Craftsmen, often Hindu or Jain, executed many of these buildings, bringing local motifs and techniques. Reuse of earlier building materials occurs in several regions and carries different meanings, from convenience to display of victory.

Languages and literatures also diversified. Persian was the common language of courts and learning; Arabic of religious scholarship; Sanskrit continued among Hindu scholars and in Kashmir's chronicles; and vernaculars, including Bengali, Old Gujarati, Awadhi, and Kashmiri, gained literary status, sometimes with court encouragement. Translation between languages occurred, as at Zain-ul-Abidin's court and in Bengal's vernacular epics.

Religious life shows exchange without erasing difference. Sufi shrines attracted devotees of several communities, Jain merchants financed temples and manuscripts under Muslim rulers, Hindu and Muslim poets used overlapping imagery, and yet rulers sometimes demolished temples or pressed communities. Hierarchy persisted: elite patronage did not abolish caste or the inequalities of rural society, and cultural interaction did not guarantee political equality. The next chapter examines the broader devotional movements.

Sufi networks illustrate how religious life crossed political lines. Chishti and Suhrawardi shaykhs maintained lodges in Bengal, Gujarat, and Malwa; the Firdausi branch spread through Bihar and Bengal; the Kubrawis arrived in Kashmir. Shaykhs traveled between regions, corresponded with rulers, and sometimes intervened in politics, as in the appeal from the Pandua shaykhs to Jaunpur during Bengal's crisis. Their shrines attracted visitors from many communities, and shrine endowments became part of local economies. At the same time, Hindu devotional movements such as Vaishnava bhakti in Bengal, and the cult of regional deities celebrated in Bengali poetry, and the Jain communities of Gujarat and Malwa, show that Muslim courts were only one setting for religious creativity.

Cultural exchange also took less visible forms: craftsmen who moved between regions carrying techniques, such as the Central Asian woodworkers and weavers in Kashmir or Gujarati stonecarvers working on mosques; merchants who financed buildings across community lines; and poets who borrowed images from several traditions. But exchange did not erase hierarchy. Elite patrons set the terms, and the benefits of cultural flowering flowed unevenly. Violence against religious institutions occurred in several of these kingdoms, and the historical record preserves evidence of both coercion and accommodation. The balanced conclusion is that these states were complex societies in which culture was shaped by negotiation and power together.

From Delhi's Provinces to Regional Courts

What Changed

  • Provincial governors and commanders became independent sultans with their own coinage, capitals, and courts.
  • New cities, such as Ahmedabad, Mandu's royal complex, Jaunpur's mosques, and Gaur's monuments, gave regional identity visible form.

Section 47.38

Political Multipolarity before the Mughals: Competition, Independence, and Imperial Incorporation

No single power controlled the subcontinent in the fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries. Delhi under the Sayyids and Lodis ruled a core in the north, Vijayanagara and the Bahmani successors dominated the south, and the regional sultanates held the east, west, center, and Himalayas. Distances, rivers, mountains, the cost of maintaining armies, and the strength of local elites made unification expensive. Rival powers also checked one another, so any ambitious ruler faced coalitions.

These states competed and cooperated. Gujarat and Malwa fought; Jaunpur and Delhi fought; Bengal and Jaunpur intervened in each other's crises; marriage alliances and diplomatic embassies connected courts; and refugees moved between them. The pattern is sometimes called multipolarity: several centers of comparable power with shifting alignments.

Incorporation came in different ways and at different times. Jaunpur fell to the Lodis in the 1470s. Malwa was absorbed by Gujarat in 1531, regained, then taken by Mughal forces in 1561–62. Bengal passed to the Sur Afghans in 1538, then the Karranis, and finally the Mughals after 1576. Gujarat fell in 1572–73, Kashmir in 1586, and Khandesh in 1601. Each fall combined external military pressure with internal weakness, such as factionalism, succession crises, or noble defection.

The mechanism of incorporation deserves explicit explanation. First, the states rarely cooperated against outside threats. Bengal and Jaunpur had intervened in each other's crises; Gujarat and Malwa fought repeatedly; Kashmir's rulers were distant from the plains' politics. When the Lodis, Surs, and Mughals advanced, each kingdom confronted them separately, and in several cases rivals or factions invited the invader. Second, the new empires commanded resources that the regional states could not match: large cavalry forces recruited from Central Asia and Hindustan, a revenue system that could pay them, and administrative continuity that allowed campaigns to be sustained over many years. Third, the regional states' own vulnerabilities, from guard revolts to noble factions to disputed successions, provided openings.

Differences in timing also reflected geography. Jaunpur, in the open Gangetic plain between Delhi and Bengal, fell first. Malwa and Gujarat were taken after the Mughals consolidated in the heartland and turned west. Bengal required several campaigns because of its rivers and Afghan military base. Kashmir's mountain setting delayed conquest until a determined expedition in 1586, and Khandesh's remoteness and fortress at Asirgarh kept it independent until 1601. The sequence is therefore explained by both imperial priorities and local conditions, rather than by a single imperial plan. Chapters 50 and 51 explore the Mughal side of the process.

Section 47.39

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has examined six regional kingdoms that arose when Delhi's power faded. Their histories reveal several patterns. First, independence took diverse paths: Bengal's by military unification, Gujarat's and Malwa's by governors who bided their time, Jaunpur's by delegated authority, Kashmir's by succession amid invasion and immigration, and Khandesh's by gradual autonomy. Second, geography and economy shaped each state. Bengal's rivers and rice, Gujarat's ports and textiles, Malwa's fortified plateau, Jaunpur's Gangetic position, and Kashmir's valley and mountain routes influenced revenue, military organization, and political culture.

Third, these were cultural centers. Adina and Bara Sona at Gaur, Ahmedabad's mosques and stepwells, Mandu's palaces, Jaunpur's mosques, and Srinagar's wooden shrines created enduring regional traditions, while vernacular literatures developed alongside Persian and Sanskrit. Fourth, religion was neither a story of uniform harmony nor of unbroken conflict. Episodes of coercion, such as those attributed to Sikandar of Kashmir, coexisted with patronage of Hindu and Jain communities, translation projects, and shared devotional spaces, and the sources require careful criticism.

Fifth, the same vulnerabilities recurred: factionalism among nobles, contested successions, and the pressures of neighbors. These weaknesses, together with the expansion of Afghan and Mughal power, brought the sultanates to an end between the late 1470s/early 1480s and 1601. Their incorporation did not erase their legacies. Mughal provinces inherited their cities, commercial networks, and craftspeople.

The chapter prepares for what follows. Chapter 48 turns from political history to the devotional movements, Sufi communities, and early Sikh tradition that crossed regional boundaries, and Chapter 49 places Gujarat's and Bengal's commerce in the larger Indian Ocean system, where the Portuguese arrival reshaped maritime power. Chapters 50 and 51 then show how the Mughals built an empire on the ruins of the political order described here.

Three methodological lessons follow from the chapter. First, histories of regional states must be built from layered evidence, and the sources closest to events are usually the most valuable, even when they are fragmentary. Second, apparently simple labels, such as "Muslim rule," "Hindu officials," or "decline," conceal diversity: courts included Turks, Afghans, Persians, Africans, local converts, Rajputs, Jains, Brahmans, and merchants, and their relations varied by reign. Third, the regional kingdoms cannot be understood as preludes to Mughal unification. They were states with their own aims and achievements, and some of their institutions, such as Gujarat's commercial finance and Bengal's landholding hierarchies, survived under new rulers.

The chapter also leaves open questions for further study. How did ordinary cultivators and artisans experience the change from Delhi's governors to regional sultans? How did Hindu and Jain communities maintain institutions under different rulers, and what role did coercion play in each region? How did the growth of vernacular literatures relate to court patronage and to popular religious movements? These questions guide the next chapter, which turns to devotional movements and the early Sikh community, and the one after, which places Gujarat and Bengal within the maritime world of the Indian Ocean.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the regional sultanates that emerged as the Delhi Sultanate's authority waned after the mid-fourteenth century. Its central argument is that political fragmentation did not mean cultural or economic decline. Bengal, Gujarat, Malwa, Jaunpur, Kashmir, and Khandesh were functioning states whose rulers built capitals, patronized learning, and managed revenue systems suited to their environments.

Bengal emerged through Shamsuddin Ilyas Shah's unification of Lakhnauti, Satgaon, and Sonargaon by about 1352. The Ilyas Shahi dynasty withstood Tughlaq invasions, built the Adina Mosque, and traded with the wider Indian Ocean world. After a crisis involving Raja Ganesha and the Habshi interregnum, the Husain Shahis restored order and presided over a cultural and political peak. Afghan Sur and Karrani rule followed, and Mughal victory at Rajmahal in 1576 began a prolonged incorporation.

Bengal's economy rested on rice, rivers, and an eastward-moving agricultural frontier, together with textiles and trade through Sonargaon, Satgaon, and Chittagong. Local landholders and chiefs mediated rural control. Bengali vernacular literature and a distinctive brick architecture flourished under court patronage, alongside Sufi shrines and Hindu devotional movements.

Gujarat's governor Zafar Khan became Muzaffar Shah in 1407. Ahmad Shah I founded Ahmedabad in 1411, and Mahmud Begada expanded the kingdom, captured Junagadh and Champaner, and confronted the Portuguese at Chaul and Diu. Bahadur Shah's reign ended with Mughal invasion, Portuguese treachery, and his death in 1537. Akbar annexed Gujarat in 1572–73. The sultanate's wealth came from land, textiles, ports, and a partnership with merchants, bankers, and shipowners.

Malwa, governed from Mandu, passed from the Ghuri dynasty to the Khaljis and endured repeated conflicts with neighbors. Its architecture and waterworks, and the Jain manuscript and mercantile tradition, are its legacy. The story of Baz Bahadur and Rupmati demonstrates how later romance attaches to a documented political end in 1561–62.

Jaunpur's Sharqi sultans controlled the eastern Gangetic plain from the 1390s until Bahlul Lodi's campaigns ended their independence in the years around 1479–1483. The state's distinctive mosques and reputation for scholarship outlasted its independence. Kashmir's Shah Mir dynasty, founded in 1339, included the controversial Sikandar and the celebrated Zain-ul-Abidin, whose patronage of translation, craft, and learning is recorded by Sanskrit and Persian chroniclers. The Chaks followed, and Mughal conquest came in 1586.

The chapter has also stressed historical method. Coins and inscriptions provide firm dates and titles, chronicles preserve court memory but reflect later conquerors' viewpoints, foreign observers describe commerce from outside, and buildings supply evidence where narratives are thin. Religious history requires reading each source for its purpose.

Comparing the kingdoms shows that environment, revenue base, and elite networks shaped each state. All shared recurring weaknesses: noble factionalism, disputed successions, and competition with neighbors. These weaknesses, together with Afghan and Mughal expansion, led to incorporation between the late 1470s/early 1480s and 1601. The regional cultures and commercial networks survived within the larger empire, a theme the following chapters develop.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows that the political map of late medieval South Asia was a mosaic of states with their own histories, not a stage awaiting a unifier. Looking at Bengal's delta, Gujarat's coast, Malwa's plateau, Jaunpur's river plain, and Kashmir's valley together teaches students to see geography, commerce, and culture as active forces in political history.

The chapter also illustrates a lasting lesson about historical evidence. Stones, coins, and monuments speak where chronicles are silent or partisan, while romantic legends and later memories shape how rulers are remembered. Learning to separate documented events from tradition is a skill required throughout this series.

These kingdoms shaped what came after. Mughal rulers inherited their cities, merchants, craft traditions, and ruling elites, and regional languages and architecture they nurtured persisted. The Bengali vernacular, Gujarati commerce, and Kashmiri craft traditions would flourish under imperial rule. Their stories also complicate any account of the subcontinent that treats imperial unity as the only form of historical significance.

Finally, the chapter's place in Volume II reminds us that the age of the sultanates was not simply a prelude to Mughal rule. It was a period of experimentation in kingship, devotion, commerce, and culture, whose diversity Chapter 48 will explore through devotional and Sufi movements, and whose maritime dimensions Chapter 49 will place in the wider Indian Ocean.
