Chapter Opening

A note on method. This is the only chapter in the textbook whose subject has not ended. Every statement about the war's course carries a date, and the chapter's evidence stops on 4 November 2026. Where the sources are partisan, incomplete, or still being gathered, the text says so.

In the hours before dawn on 24 February 2022, residents of Kyiv, Kharkiv, Mariupol, Odesa, and dozens of smaller Ukrainian towns were woken by explosions. In Kharkiv, a city of roughly 1.4 million people located about forty kilometers from the Russian border, many families gathered what they could carry and descended into the metro, whose deep stations would shelter thousands over the following weeks. In Moscow, a recorded address by President Vladimir Putin was broadcast in the early morning, announcing what he called a "special military operation." Within hours, Russian forces were advancing from Russia, from Belarus, and from occupied Crimea, while Russian missiles struck airfields, military headquarters, and, as the days passed, residential districts. What the Russian government described by a euphemism, most of the world, and the great majority of Ukrainians, described as a full-scale invasion.

This chapter studies that invasion and the war that grew from it through 4 November 2026, the evidence cutoff for this edition. It begins with the political and military escalation of 2021 and early 2022, follows the campaigns of the first year, examines the prolonged war of 2023–2026, and then turns to subjects that cannot be told through battles alone: the occupation of Ukrainian territory, the treatment of civilians and prisoners, the transformation of both armed forces, the wartime societies and economies of Russia and Ukraine, and the international diplomacy that surrounded the fighting. At the chapter's cutoff the war continued, negotiations had not produced a settlement, and civilian suffering in Ukraine was, by the reporting of United Nations monitors, continuing at a high level. The chapter therefore cannot conclude, and it does not pretend to.

Why does the war belong in a history of Russia? One reason is scale. It is widely described as the largest interstate war in Europe since the Second World War, and it has reshaped the Russian state: its budget, its armed forces, its media and legal environment, its economy and foreign trade, its demography, and its relations with nearly every other government. A second reason is that the war is inseparable from the long historical questions this textbook has traced since Chapter 1: the relationship between Rus', Muscovy, and the Russian Empire (Chapters 7–12 and 19–21); the meaning of the Pereiaslav Agreement and the Cossack Hetmanate (Chapter 30); the incorporation of Ukrainian lands into the empire (Chapters 34 and 45); the Soviet famine of 1932–1933 (Chapter 56); the Second World War (Chapters 59–60); and the dissolution of the USSR (Chapters 65–66). The war is not explained by those histories, as if they were destiny; but both governments, and many ordinary people, invoke them, and students must understand both the history and its later uses.

The chapter also continues the story told in Chapter 70. The Russian annexation of Crimea in 2014 and the war in the Donbas that began the same year, examined in §§70.14–70.23, were the immediate background. A war already existed in 2021: it had killed roughly fourteen thousand people, by the estimate of the United Nations human rights office, and it had displaced millions. What changed in February 2022 was the scale, the geography, and the objectives. Fighting that had been concentrated along a line of contact in two eastern provinces became a war across the entire country, with strikes reaching Lviv in the west and Odesa in the south, and with the evident aim, in its opening phase, of overturning Ukraine's government.

Those who lived through the war did so in very different circumstances. Soldiers on both sides endured artillery, drones, mines, and trench warfare. Civilians in Ukraine sheltered in basements, evacuated, stayed under occupation, or crossed borders as refugees. Ukrainian officials, volunteers, railway workers, and engineers kept a state functioning under attack. In Russia, conscripts, contract soldiers, recruited prisoners, and families at home experienced the war through military service, bereavement, economic change, and a tightening legal environment; some left the country. Prisoners of war, detainees, and children transferred from occupied areas have their own documented histories. Neighboring states, from Poland and Romania to Moldova, hosted refugees and managed new security risks, and governments from Washington to Beijing, Ankara, New Delhi, Tehran, and Pyongyang made choices that shaped the war's course.

The evidence for this history is unlike that for any earlier chapter. It includes satellite imagery, intercepted communications, drone video, forensic examination of burial sites, the monitoring reports of the United Nations, the International Atomic Energy Agency, and the Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe, court filings and arrest warrants, trade and budget data, public opinion polls, and the official statements of governments that are parties to the war. Each has strengths and limits. Government claims about losses and territorial control are evidence of what a government wished to assert, not neutral measurement; casualty counts often measure different populations; polls conducted under wartime and authoritarian conditions require caution. The chapter's governing rule is to attach each claim to its source and date, and to separate documented events from allegations, interpretations, and propaganda, without inventing a false symmetry where the evidence points strongly in one direction.

The chapter is organized in eleven divisions. The first five trace the road to war, the opening invasion, the southern campaign, the eastern war and attrition, and the prolonged conflict through 2026. The next five examine occupation and international law, military institutions and technology, the two societies, the wartime economies, and international alignment and diplomacy. A final division assesses what historians can and cannot yet say. Place names follow current internationally standard English forms (Kyiv, Kharkiv, Mykolaiv, Dnipro, Sievierodonetsk), with historical or Russian forms noted where they matter. Dates follow the Gregorian calendar. Casualty and damage figures are given only with their source, date, and definition.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the military, diplomatic, and political escalation between 2021 and February 2022 that preceded the full-scale invasion, distinguishing public claims from documented actions.
  2. Evaluate the justifications offered by the Russian government for the invasion against historical evidence, the findings of international bodies, and the arguments of historians.
  3. Trace the principal campaigns of 2022–2026, attaching territorial change to dated and sourced evidence rather than to unverified claims.
  4. Analyze why the Russian operation against Kyiv failed in spring 2022, using military, logistical, political, and social causes.
  5. Distinguish among allegation, documented conduct, investigative finding, arrest warrant, conviction, and final judicial finding in the study of war crimes and accountability.
  6. Interpret civilian casualty, displacement, and damage statistics by identifying who produced them, what they measure, and what they omit.
  7. Compare the institutions, mobilization systems, and wartime economies of Russia and Ukraine and the technological adaptation of both armed forces.
  8. Assess the experiences of different groups—soldiers, civilians under occupation, refugees, families in Russia, prisoners—through documentary evidence.
  9. Contextualize the war within the longer history of Rus', the Russian Empire, the USSR, and the post-Soviet order without treating that history as determining the outcome.
  10. Distinguish battlefield control from internationally recognized sovereignty and annexation claims from legal status.
  11. Apply historical method to an unfinished conflict, identifying what can be established, what is contested, and what cannot yet be known.

Historical Context

The war's immediate background is the post-Soviet settlement and its unraveling. When the Soviet Union dissolved in December 1991 (§§66.8–66.9), Ukraine's independence had just been affirmed by a referendum in which an overwhelming majority of voters, including majorities in every region, supported it. Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus had signed the Belavezha Accords; Ukraine inherited a large share of Soviet military equipment, including nuclear weapons on its territory. In the Budapest Memorandum of December 1994, Ukraine agreed to transfer those weapons to Russia in return for assurances, from Russia, the United States, and the United Kingdom, to respect its independence, sovereignty, and existing borders. In 1997, Russia and Ukraine signed a Treaty of Friendship, Cooperation and Partnership that recognized their common border. These agreements did not resolve disputes over the Black Sea Fleet, the status of Crimea, energy transit, and Ukraine's alignment between Russia and the European Union and NATO, but they established the legal framework within which, in 2014 and 2022, they were violated.

Ukrainian politics after 1991 was competitive and often chaotic. Presidential elections were contested, power changed hands through votes and, twice, through mass protest: the Orange Revolution of 2004 and the Euromaidan of 2013–2014 (§§70.11–70.13). Regional differences in language and political preference existed, but historians and sociologists have cautioned against treating them as a fault line that required, or predicted, partition; surveys before 2014 showed that most citizens in the east and south, including many Russian speakers, supported Ukrainian statehood. Russian government policy toward Ukraine combined economic pressure, energy diplomacy, political patronage, and, from 2014, military force. Ukraine's political leaders pursued closer ties with the EU and NATO, and in 2019 the Ukrainian parliament amended the constitution to state the country's strategic course toward membership in both, although actual membership in NATO was not near in 2021.

The Russian state that launched the invasion had been shaped by two decades of centralization under Putin (Chapters 68–70). The constitutional amendments of 2020 allowed him to remain president potentially until 2036; independent media, civil society organizations, and opposition politicians had been progressively restricted; the arrest of Alexei Navalny in January 2021 and the designation of his organizations as extremist later that year closed the main channel of organized opposition. Foreign policy had hardened after the annexation of Crimea and the subsequent sanctions (§§70.24–70.29). Russia's armed forces had been substantially modernized since the war with Georgia in 2008 (§§69.5–69.10), had gained experience in Syria (§§70.30–70.34), and had been supplied with new precision weapons. Decision-making on the invasion appears to have been confined to a very small circle; the evidence for how the decision was made, discussed in the Historical Debate in §71.4, remains incomplete.

Finally, students should recall the historical arguments that both governments, and many writers, brought to the war. The medieval polity of Rus' (Chapters 7–12) centered on Kyiv, and its heritage is claimed by Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus alike; the seventeenth-century Cossack Hetmanate and its agreement with Muscovy (Chapter 30) have been interpreted as union, protectorate, or alliance; the nineteenth-century Russian Empire restricted the Ukrainian language and publications (Chapter 45); and the Soviet period included a Ukrainian Soviet republic, a Ukrainian national-cultural revival in the 1920s, and the catastrophic famine of 1932–1933 (Chapter 56). The textbook's consistent position is that such histories supply context and rival arguments, not a legal or moral entitlement to the territory or sovereignty of any present state. The war must be studied for what its participants decided and did, not deduced from the medieval past.

Russia's military; its Airborne Troops (VDV), Ground Forces, Aerospace Forces, Navy, and the National Guard (Rosgvardia) all participated in the invasion.
Armed Forces of Ukraine and Territorial Defense Forces
Ukraine's regular military and reserve-based defense branch, reinforced by mass volunteering and general mobilization under martial law.
Donetsk and Luhansk "People's Republics"
Self-proclaimed entities created in 2014 under Russian-backed control; recognized by Russia on 21 February 2022 and not by the great majority of states.
UN Human Rights Monitoring Mission in Ukraine (HRMMU)
Office of the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights in Ukraine; verifies civilian casualties and publishes periodic reports that this chapter treats as principal evidence.
International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA)
UN nuclear agency; stationed experts at Ukrainian nuclear sites, including the Zaporizhzhia plant, from 2022.
International Criminal Court (ICC)
Hague-based tribunal; opened an investigation into the situation in Ukraine in 2022 and issued arrest warrants in 2023 and 2024.
Kyiv, Kharkiv, Mariupol, Kherson
Cities central to the first year's campaigns: the capital that held, the northeastern metropolis that resisted, the port city destroyed by siege, and the regional capital occupied and later recaptured.
Donbas and Zaporizhzhia Nuclear Power Plant
The eastern industrial region of Donetsk and Luhansk provinces and Europe's largest nuclear power station, occupied in March 2022.

The Road to Full-Scale War, 2021–February 2022

Section 71.1

Russia and Ukraine at the End of 2021

At the end of 2021 Russia and Ukraine had been at war for nearly eight years. The conflict that began in the spring of 2014 in Donetsk and Luhansk provinces (§§70.17–70.23) had settled into a static confrontation along a line of contact of roughly four hundred kilometers. Two arrangements negotiated in Minsk in September 2014 and February 2015 had reduced but never ended the fighting. The second agreement, known as Minsk II, committed the parties to a ceasefire, the withdrawal of heavy weapons, local elections in the separatist-held areas, constitutional changes granting those areas special status, and the return of control of the border to Ukraine. Its provisions were sequenced ambiguously, and the two sides read them differently. Moscow insisted that Ukraine first negotiate directly with the Donetsk and Luhansk authorities and enact special status; Kyiv insisted that security guarantees and control of the border must precede elections. The Normandy Format talks among Russia, Ukraine, Germany, and France had produced little since a summit in Paris in December 2019. The United Nations human rights office estimated that by early 2022 the conflict had killed approximately fourteen thousand people, among them more than three thousand civilians, with many more wounded.

The territories on the other side of the line were not simply "separatist" in a local sense. The self-proclaimed Donetsk and Luhansk "people's republics" depended on Russian financing, military support, and administrative integration. From 2019 Russia had issued simplified-procedure Russian passports to residents of these areas, and by 2021 hundreds of thousands held them, which Moscow later cited as a basis for protecting "its citizens." Crimea, annexed by Russia in March 2014, remained internationally recognized as Ukrainian territory; the General Assembly had affirmed that position in 2014 by a large majority. In August 2021 Ukraine launched the Crimea Platform, a diplomatic forum intended to keep the issue alive, while Russia continued to militarize the peninsula and had strengthened its control of the Sea of Azov since the Kerch Strait incident of November 2018, when Russian forces seized three Ukrainian naval vessels.

Ukraine's own politics had evolved. Volodymyr Zelenskyy, a television actor and producer with no previous political office, won the presidency in April 2019 with about three-quarters of the vote in the second round, partly on a promise to end the Donbas war through negotiation. His early initiatives, including prisoner exchanges and the Paris summit, yielded limited results, and by 2021 his government had adopted harder measures: it imposed sanctions on the pro-Russian politician Viktor Medvedchuk and closed three television channels associated with him in February 2021, a step defended as a national-security measure and criticized by some observers as a concentration of executive power. Ukraine's armed forces, reorganized since 2014 with training from the United States, Britain, Canada, and other NATO members, received anti-tank missiles and, in 2021, used Turkish-made armed drones for the first time in the Donbas. Moscow cited each development as evidence of a hostile military build-up on its border.

The two governments therefore entered 2022 with incompatible objectives. The Russian government sought a Ukraine that would remain outside NATO, was militarily limited, and, by Moscow's reading of Minsk II, politically constrained by the inclusion of the Russian-backed Donbas authorities. The Ukrainian government sought to preserve its sovereignty and territorial integrity and to continue integration with the European Union and, in the longer term, NATO, an aspiration written into its constitution in 2019 although neither organization was preparing Ukraine for membership. Public opinion polling in Ukraine before 2022 showed substantial majority support for EU membership and substantial, though more variable, support for NATO membership. Historians emphasize that this was the setting, not the cause, of the invasion: it explains why the relationship was tense, but it does not by itself explain why Russia chose full-scale war at this moment.

One important prewar distinction concerned Ukrainian sovereignty and the interests of people living in the Donbas. International monitors had documented civilian deaths on both sides of the contact line and restrictions imposed by de facto authorities, but those findings did not substantiate Russia's claim that Ukrainian authorities were committing genocide against Russian speakers. The presence of Russian-speaking communities did not mean their political interests were identical. Ukrainian electoral history, social surveys and the continued presence of Russian speakers in the armed forces and government provide evidence against the simplification of linguistic identity into automatic political allegiance. This subject belongs in an opening section because a reader otherwise may confuse an actual unresolved armed conflict with the claims later used to justify a nationwide invasion.

The historical situation also involved geography and institutions more than an abstract confrontation between 'Russia' and 'the West.' Ukraine had land borders with Russia and Belarus, maritime access vulnerable to Russian forces in Crimea, and domestic transport corridors connecting eastern industry with major cities farther west. Moscow controlled different instruments—energy trade, passport issuance, military forces, diplomacy and media messages—while Kyiv possessed a working central government, an elected parliament and more developed armed forces than in 2014. Neither side entered the crisis as it had eight years earlier. The important explanatory question is how leaders interpreted those changed capabilities and whether the information available to them was accurate.

Section 71.2

Russian Military Buildup and Security Demands, 2021–2022

The buildup of Russian forces near Ukraine occurred in two main phases. In March and April 2021, units from several Russian military districts were moved toward Ukraine's borders and Crimea; the concentration was announced as an exercise and, in late April, Defense Minister Sergei Shoigu ordered most of the troops back to base, though equipment remained near Voronezh. A second and larger deployment began in the autumn. Commercial satellite imagery published in October and November 2021 showed tanks, artillery, and logistics units gathering near Yelnya in Smolensk province and in the Bryansk, Kursk, and Voronezh regions, as well as in Crimea. The large Zapad-2021 exercise with Belarus in September had already allowed Russian forces to move and to rehearse operations across a wider area. By January and February 2022 Western governments estimated that well over one hundred thousand Russian troops were deployed around Ukraine, and in mid-February United States officials cited figures of 150,000 or more, including units in Belarus.

The most consequential deployment was to Belarus. In January 2022 Russian forces began arriving for the joint exercise later named "Allied Resolve," held between 10 and 20 February, with Russian aircraft, air-defense systems, Iskander missile launchers, and tens of thousands of troops. The exercise placed Russian forces within roughly a hundred kilometers of Kyiv by road, a distance that mattered once the invasion began. Western intelligence services also reported field hospitals, supplies of blood, and logistics preparations not normally associated with routine training. On 15 February the Russian Ministry of Defense announced that some units were returning to base; American and NATO officials said they saw no corresponding withdrawal and in some places further arrivals. Russian officials repeatedly denied planning an invasion and described Western warnings as hysteria. These denials are historical evidence about what the Russian government said, not evidence about what it intended.

Alongside the military deployments, on 17 December 2021 the Russian Ministry of Foreign Affairs published two draft documents: a treaty between Russia and the United States on security guarantees, and an agreement on measures to ensure the security of Russia and the member states of NATO. The documents required that NATO commit to no further enlargement, specifically including Ukraine; that the alliance withdraw forces and infrastructure deployed to member states that had joined since 1997; that the parties refrain from deploying intermediate-range missiles where they could strike each other's territory; and that NATO members refrain from military activity in Ukraine, Eastern Europe, the South Caucasus, and Central Asia. Moscow demanded written, legally binding guarantees and made it plain that the issue was urgent.

Why were the demands rejected? NATO's governing treaty states that membership is open to European states that meet its criteria, and the allies argued they could not, in a bilateral deal with Russia, deny sovereign states the right to choose their alliances or reverse decisions on states already admitted, such as Poland and the Baltic states. They also noted that the document package was published rather than negotiated quietly and was accompanied by a military build-up. Historians and analysts disagree about what the demands signified. Some read them as a genuine, if maximalist, expression of Russian security concerns about NATO enlargement, which Russian leaders had voiced since the 1990s; others read them as a document composed to be rejected and thereby to supply a justification for force; still others consider that both motives could coexist. What is documented is that Russia coupled sweeping diplomatic demands with large-scale military deployments, and that the demands included provisions, such as the rollback of NATO's presence in central Europe, that no NATO member government was willing to accept.

The December 2021 Russian documents are best interpreted as concrete negotiating texts. Their articles did not simply request that Ukraine be denied membership: they proposed rules affecting NATO troop deployment in states admitted after 1997 and restrictions on military cooperation across several regions. A historian should distinguish the proposals' literal provisions, Russia's accompanying diplomatic statements and later interpretations by analysts. That a provision was unacceptable to NATO does not by itself prove that Moscow never expected negotiation; conversely, declaring a proposal a security concern does not establish that military invasion was a lawful response to its rejection. The sequence of documents and deployments provides the stronger evidence of escalating coercive diplomacy.

The buildup illustrates why intelligence estimates require careful treatment. Satellite imagery could identify vehicles and tent camps but not always the precise number of personnel or their intentions. Government figures often combined troops directly near the border with supporting formations in Belarus and Crimea. Reserve equipment, rail movements, field hospitals and ammunition trains could be more consequential than a single troop total. For students, this creates an important methodological comparison between a count that seems precise and a broader inference about offensive capability. Later events confirmed the deployment was preparation for invasion, but observers in January 2022 had to assess incomplete indicators before the final decision was publicly apparent.

Section 71.3

International Diplomacy, Warnings, and Failed Negotiations

The diplomatic effort to avert war was intense and multilateral. President Joseph Biden and President Putin had met in Geneva in June 2021 and spoke by video link on 7 December 2021, when Biden warned of severe economic consequences and stated that the United States would not send troops to defend Ukraine. In January 2022 American and Russian officials met in Geneva, NATO and Russia met in the NATO-Russia Council for the first time in two years, and the OSCE hosted a meeting in Vienna. On 26 January the United States and NATO delivered written responses to the Russian draft treaties. These rejected the central demands on enlargement and Ukraine but offered discussions on arms control, missile deployments, and transparency about military exercises. The Normandy Format advisers met in Paris on 26 January and in Berlin on 10 February without breakthrough. President Emmanuel Macron visited Moscow on 7 February and Chancellor Olaf Scholz on 15 February, and Biden and Putin spoke on 12 February.

One feature of this period was the unusual public release of intelligence. The United States and the United Kingdom declassified assessments of Russian deployments and plans, including a warning that Russia might stage a pretext, such as a false-flag attack or a fabricated Ukrainian provocation, to justify intervention. On 18 February Biden said he was convinced that Putin had decided to invade. This disclosure strategy was intended to deter Russia by removing the element of surprise and to prepare allies and public opinion. Historians will want to ask how well it worked, and what the intelligence assessments rested on. At the time, governments differed in their confidence. Some European governments and many analysts doubted that Russia would launch a full-scale invasion rather than a limited operation in the Donbas or a campaign of coercion, and Ukrainian leaders at times downplayed the immediacy of the threat, in part to avoid capital flight, panic, and damage to the economy.

At the Munich Security Conference on 19 February, Zelenskyy appealed for security guarantees, criticized allied hesitancy, and said Ukraine would defend itself. The Western governments had prepared packages of sanctions, which they promised to impose if Russia invaded, but they had ruled out direct military intervention. This asymmetry shaped the diplomacy. Russia could regard the sanctions threat as a cost it had weighed, and the absence of a Western military guarantee as proof that the risk was tolerable; the West could not credibly offer concessions on NATO's open-door principle, and Ukraine could not be asked to renounce its alignment under pressure without the sovereignty of a state becoming a bargaining chip, a distinction Western governments repeatedly emphasized.

The record shows that the Russian side did not respond constructively to the proposals for arms-control and transparency talks, repeated that its principal demands had not been met, and continued the deployments. Whether more imaginative diplomacy could have prevented the war is a counterfactual that historians cannot test. The evidence supports narrower conclusions. Diplomacy occurred on several channels; Russia's principal demands were incompatible with the position of the NATO governments and, still more, with the position of Ukraine; and when the invasion came, the diplomatic channels had produced neither an agreement nor a clear Russian signal of limited aims. The Russian archives for these weeks remain closed, so the internal deliberations that would settle whether the decision for war had already been taken are unavailable.

Diplomacy failed in part because the parties were negotiating different kinds of security arrangements. Russia sought commitments that would restrict Ukraine's future alliances and the military presence of NATO in eastern Europe. Western governments proposed discussions on arms control, transparency and missile deployments without conceding a veto over another state's choices. Ukraine asserted that a settlement about its political future could not be validly decided without its participation. This triangular relationship produced several negotiating venues with different membership and mandates. A NATO-Russia Council meeting was not identical to a Normandy-format discussion of the Donbas, and a bilateral Washington-Moscow conversation could not resolve every Ukrainian constitutional question.

The historian should also distinguish diplomatic failure from the absence of any communication. Leaders repeatedly exchanged letters, spoke by telephone and used intermediaries; intelligence warnings were shared publicly as an unusual deterrent against a possible surprise attack. Those warnings were sometimes criticized as alarmist before the invasion, creating a record of how governments judged uncertain evidence. Russian official denials during the buildup became particularly important to later evaluations of credibility. Yet an accurate account must avoid suggesting that every diplomatic participant knew exactly what the Kremlin had decided internally. Documents verify what was requested and rejected, while the private process by which Russia chose war remains incompletely documented.

Section 71.4

Putin's Historical Claims and the Justifications for War

The Russian government's justification for the invasion combined historical, ideological, security, and legal arguments. In July 2021 Putin published a long essay entitled "On the Historical Unity of Russians and Ukrainians," arguing that Russians, Ukrainians, and Belarusians descended from the medieval polity of Rus' and were "one people" and that the separation of Ukraine had been produced by outside forces and by the Bolsheviks' territorial arrangements. In a televised address on 21 February 2022 he went further, stating that modern Ukraine had been created by Soviet, "Bolshevik," Russia, blaming Lenin's nationalities policy, and describing the Ukrainian government as a project of foreign powers that had turned the country into an "anti-Russia." In the address of 24 February, he announced a "special military operation" whose goals, as he described them, were the "demilitarization" and "denazification" of Ukraine and the protection of people who, he asserted, had been subjected to abuse and genocide by the Kyiv government for eight years.

Historians have examined these claims against the evidence. The idea of a shared origin in Rus' is not disputed as a starting point; this textbook has stressed (Chapters 7–12) that Rus' was a medieval polity and cultural world from which Russians, Ukrainians, and Belarusians all draw heritage. What historians reject is the inference that a medieval inheritance makes the modern Ukrainian nation artificial or illegitimate. Ukrainian political and cultural institutions have a long history: the seventeenth-century Cossack Hetmanate (Chapter 30), a Ukrainian national movement in the nineteenth century despite imperial restrictions on the Ukrainian language, the Ukrainian People's Republic of 1917–1920, and a Ukrainian Soviet republic. In December 1991 more than ninety percent of voters in a referendum supported independence, with majorities in every region, including Crimea and the Donbas. National identity, as historians of nationalism generally stress, is produced through institutions, language, schooling, politics, and shared experience; it does not require an unbroken medieval statehood, and it is not delegitimized by the fact that other nations share a medieval ancestor. Putin's account also treated the Soviet territorial arrangements, such as the assignment of the Donbas to the Ukrainian republic in the 1920s and the transfer of Crimea in 1954, as injustices to be reversed, a claim that is a political argument rather than a legal one, since the 1997 treaty and other agreements had recognized the border.

The claims of "denazification" and "genocide" were the most directly testable. Far-right organizations existed in Ukraine, as in most European countries, and some, including the Azov unit created in 2014, had far-right origins before being integrated into the National Guard; but far-right parties performed poorly at the polls, winning about two percent in the 2019 parliamentary election, and Ukraine's president is of Jewish descent. The conflict in the Donbas had caused civilian deaths on both sides of the line of contact, and the United Nations documented abuses by both Ukrainian and Russian-backed forces; however, no body of evidence supported the allegation of genocide against the Russian-speaking population. In an order on provisional measures issued on 16 March 2022 in a case brought by Ukraine, the International Court of Justice stated that it had no evidence substantiating Russia's allegation that Ukraine had committed genocide in the Donetsk and Luhansk provinces, and ordered Russia to suspend military operations, by thirteen votes to two. A provisional order does not decide the merits of a case, but it records the court's assessment of the claim at that stage.

On the legal level, Russia invoked Article 51 of the UN Charter, which recognizes a right of self-defense against armed attack, and the request for assistance from the Donetsk and Luhansk authorities it had recognized the previous days. The General Assembly's emergency special session adopted a resolution on 2 March 2022 that deplored the aggression against Ukraine by 141 votes to 5, with 35 abstentions, and the great majority of international lawyers held that the invasion violated the prohibition on the use of force in Article 2(4) of the Charter, since no armed attack by Ukraine had occurred. The NATO argument, that enlargement threatened Russian security, is an argument about motives and is examined in the Historical Debate below. In sum, students should treat the Russian official statements as primary evidence of the government's declared reasons, compare them with evidence and law, and keep distinct what a state asserts from what independent bodies have found.

Putin's July 2021 essay on Russian-Ukrainian historical relations drew on selective readings of medieval Rus', imperial state formation and the Soviet creation of national republics. Such arguments are important historical evidence about ideology and political justification but cannot be treated as an authoritative description of the past. The populations of medieval Rus' did not share the institutions or national identities of the twenty-first century. Ukrainian independence in 1991 was an act of the modern Ukrainian polity, recognized by Russia in treaties and diplomatic practice. A historical narrative that treats centuries-old cultural connection as proof of contemporary state ownership makes a category error, collapsing distinct periods and political forms into one purported continuous state.

The claims of 'denazification' and genocide require separate evidentiary evaluation. Far-right organizations existed in Ukraine, as they did in other European countries, but their political significance cannot be inferred from Russian propaganda or isolated symbols. The presence of extremist individuals does not establish that an entire elected government was Nazi. The International Court of Justice's provisional order in March 2022 addressed Russia's military operations under the Genocide Convention litigation context; its procedural scope must be stated precisely. Students should compare official claims with independent electoral data, UN human-rights reporting and original treaty obligations, rather than staging a false debate between assertions whose evidentiary support is markedly different.

"Special Military Operation" (spetsial'naia voennaia operatsiia)

Definition: The official Russian term for the invasion of Ukraine, introduced in the presidential address of 24 February 2022.

Historical context: Within Russia the phrase became the standard public designation, and from March 2022 laws punished statements the authorities regarded as discrediting the armed forces, including describing the operation as a "war" or "invasion" (§71.47). Outside Russia, and in the judgment of the UN General Assembly and most scholarship, the event is an invasion and an international armed conflict.

Why it matters: The choice of term shows how language is part of the historical record. This textbook uses "full-scale invasion" and "war" as descriptive terms and puts the Russian official term in quotation marks, attributing it to the Russian government.

Historical Debate

Why Did Russia Launch the Full-Scale Invasion?

The question: Few historians doubt who launched the invasion or that it violated international law. The debate concerns why Russia's leadership chose full-scale war in February 2022 and what it hoped to achieve.

Interpretation A — Security and NATO: Realist international-relations scholars, notably John Mearsheimer, have argued that NATO's eastward enlargement and Western support for Ukraine threatened vital Russian security interests and provoked a defensive response. Supporters point to Russian statements about NATO since the 1990s and to the December 2021 demands.
Interpretation B — Ideology, identity, and imperial revisionism: Many historians, including Serhii Plokhy, stress Putin's own denial of an independent Ukrainian nation, his historical writings, and the stated aim of regime change, and argue that the invasion arose from a view of Ukraine as part of a Russian sphere and from fears about a successful democratic neighbor. Critics of Interpretation A note that Ukraine's NATO membership was not imminent in 2021 and that Russia's own invasion led Finland and Sweden to join the alliance.
Interpretation C — Decision-making and miscalculation: Others focus on the structure of Russian decision-making: a small circle, closed information channels, and confident but flawed assessments of Ukrainian resistance, Western unity, and Russian military capacity, which together made a rapid, low-cost victory appear feasible.

Evidence: Historians use Russian leaders' public statements and writings, diplomatic records, military deployments and operational plans as revealed by the campaign, intelligence disclosures, accounts of insiders, and comparison with earlier Russian interventions in Georgia (2008) and Ukraine (2014).

Points of agreement: The Russian leadership launched the invasion; it expected a quick result, and the initial operation was designed to reach Kyiv in days; and Ukraine did not attack Russia.

Points of disagreement: The relative weight of security concerns, historical ideology, domestic politics, and misperception; whether the aims included conquest of the whole country or coercion; and how far Western policy contributed. These questions are partly unanswerable until Russian records are accessible.

Student task: Select one factual claim from each interpretation and identify what kind of evidence could confirm or weaken it. Explain why the interpretations are not necessarily mutually exclusive.

Section 71.5

Recognition of Separatist Territories and the Final Days Before Invasion

The final week began with a surge of incidents along the line of contact in the Donbas. The OSCE Special Monitoring Mission, whose observers were present on both sides until the war, recorded a sharp increase in ceasefire violations and explosions between 17 and 21 February. On 18 and 19 February the leaders of the Donetsk and Luhansk authorities announced an evacuation of civilians to Russia and alleged imminent Ukrainian attacks. Western governments assessed these steps as preparing a pretext, and Ukraine denied planning an offensive. On 21 February the Russian authorities also said a Ukrainian sabotage group had been killed after crossing into Rostov province and that a shell had landed near a border post, claims that Western and Ukrainian officials rejected as unsubstantiated. Because many such incidents were claimed by the parties and could not be verified independently, the sources must be used with attribution, and historians generally treat the claims as part of the information campaign accompanying the military preparations.

On 21 February Putin convened a televised meeting of the Russian Security Council in which senior officials were invited to state their positions on recognizing the Donetsk and Luhansk "people's republics." That evening, after a lengthy address discussed in §71.4, he signed decrees recognizing both entities as independent states. On 22 February Russia signed treaties of friendship, cooperation, and mutual assistance with their leaders, which the State Duma ratified the same day, and the Federation Council authorized the president to use armed forces abroad. Russian troops were described as "peacekeepers" in the areas recognized. Recognition mattered because it provided a legal-political claim, which Russia later used, that its forces were assisting sovereign states. Almost all other governments did not recognize the two entities. The United States, the European Union, and the United Kingdom announced sanctions on 22–23 February; Germany suspended certification of the Nord Stream 2 gas pipeline; and Biden described the Russian actions as the beginning of an invasion.

In Ukraine, on 23 February the National Security and Defense Council declared a nationwide state of emergency, and the parliament approved it; reservists were called up. Government websites and networks were hit by cyberattacks on 15 and 23 February, and security researchers identified new destructive "wiper" malware, such as HermeticWiper, on Ukrainian systems on 23 February. That night Zelenskyy addressed the Russian public in Russian, said that he had tried to arrange a conversation with Putin without receiving an answer, and appealed to Russians not to believe that Ukraine wished war. On the same day the leaders of the Donetsk and Luhansk authorities sent Putin a formal request for military assistance, a step consistent with the legal structure that Russia had built.

The invasion itself began in the early hours of 24 February. While the UN Security Council, over which Russia held the rotating presidency that month, met in emergency session, Secretary-General António Guterres appealed to Putin to stop; Putin's announcement of the operation was broadcast during those hours. Ukrainian authorities reported explosions in Kyiv, Kharkiv, Odesa, Mariupol and elsewhere. Zelenskyy imposed martial law, severed diplomatic relations with Russia, and ordered general mobilization. The precise date of the decision to invade remains unknown to historians; the available evidence indicates that the military deployments, the recognition decrees, and the diplomatic messaging of the previous weeks were consistent with a plan for war, and that many observers interpreted the 21 February meeting as a staged display of consensus. Whatever the internal timetable, by 24 February the road to war described in this division had ended.

The recognition announcements of February 21 altered the formal position Russia had maintained during earlier Minsk negotiations. Moscow had described itself as a mediator in the Donbas conflict, while Ukraine and many outside observers pointed to Russian support and military involvement. Recognition of the self-proclaimed entities as independent states made it harder to reconcile Russia's position with Ukraine's recognized territorial integrity and the Minsk framework. Russia then signed cooperation agreements and approved deployment arrangements. These steps provided a political vocabulary for the invasion that followed, but they did not establish international recognition of the entities or an independent legal right to attack the rest of Ukraine.

Reports of increased shelling and evacuation announcements in the days before the invasion should be checked against OSCE monitoring and the chronology of public statements. Both sides made accusations, but the presence of a local ceasefire violation cannot be generalized into proof of a nationwide threat warranting conquest. Russian officials also organized or publicized evacuations from separatist-held areas, while Western governments warned that alleged incidents could become pretexts for wider action. The opening episode therefore illustrates how a government can combine recognition documents, emergency language and troop deployment in a closely sequenced political operation. The historical chronology is a safeguard against accepting later official explanations as a full account of prior decision-making.

The Opening Invasion and the Battle for Northern Ukraine, February–April 2022

Section 71.6

February 24, 2022: The Beginning of the Full-Scale Invasion

The invasion advanced along four principal axes. In the north, Russian and Belarusian-based forces crossed from Belarus toward Kyiv through the Chornobyl exclusion zone, which Russian troops seized on 24 February; other formations advanced from Russia's Bryansk and Kursk provinces toward Chernihiv and Sumy. In the northeast, forces moved from the Belgorod region toward Kharkiv, Ukraine's second-largest city. In the east, formations of the Russian army and units of the Donetsk and Luhansk "people's republics" pressed the Ukrainian forces along the Donbas line of contact. In the south, troops from Crimea advanced across the isthmus of Perekop and the Chonhar and Henichesk crossings toward Kherson, Melitopol, and the Dnipro, while another group moved along the Azov coast toward Mariupol. Western governments assessed that between 150,000 and 190,000 Russian and allied personnel took part, organized largely in battalion tactical groups, a formation that combined infantry, armor, and artillery but was thin in infantry strength.

The Russian objectives can be inferred from the plan's shape and from Russian statements. Airborne troops struck at Hostomel airport, within a few dozen kilometers of the capital, on the first morning; mechanized columns were sent toward Kyiv at speed; and the force posture suggested an expectation that Ukrainian resistance would collapse rapidly, the government would fall or flee, and a political settlement favorable to Moscow could be imposed. Reports from the first days that Russian units carried parade uniforms, and the brief publication, apparently by mistake, of a Russian state-news article celebrating a victory on 26–27 February, point in the same direction, though the evidence for Russian planning assumptions remains largely circumstantial. The Russian Ministry of Defense later stated that the operation around Kyiv had been intended to pin down Ukrainian forces and that the main aim was the "liberation" of the Donbas. Ukrainian and many Western analysts regarded this as a retrospective explanation, noting the scale of the northern thrust and the airborne assault. Students should treat the question as unresolved but note that the early operations were not consistent with a limited campaign.

Ukraine's immediate responses were military, political, and civilian. The government declared martial law and general mobilization, restricting men between eighteen and sixty from leaving the country; the Ukrainian armed forces, which had dispersed some units and air defenses beforehand, engaged Russian columns; and, on 25 February, Zelenskyy released a video recording from central Kyiv, with senior officials, to show that the government had not left the capital. American and allied officials offered evacuation, which he declined. In the south, Russian forces made the swiftest gains: by early March they had occupied Kherson, the only provincial capital to fall to Russian troops in the first stage, the Zaporizhzhia nuclear plant, and substantial territory along the Azov coast. Elsewhere, advances were slower than planned and the Ukrainian army held its main cities.

The humanitarian consequences began at once. The United Nations refugee agency reported that more than one million people had left Ukraine by 3 March and more than two million by 8 March, the fastest refugee movement in Europe since the Second World War. Within the first week, Russian and Ukrainian forces and civilians had suffered losses that neither government fully disclosed. Russian officials released little casualty information in the opening weeks; Ukrainian authorities reported some data about civilian harm but not complete military figures. For the first days of the invasion, then, the historian relies on satellite imagery, on the reports of UN monitors who began to verify civilian deaths, on journalists' and open-source analysts' work, and on the statements of the two governments, which each had reasons to exaggerate the other's losses.

The invasion's axes posed different operational challenges. The northern approach from Belarus placed Russian forces relatively close to Kyiv but required movement through forests, wetlands and narrow corridors. The northeastern approaches threatened large urban centers while exposing supply columns to attack over extended roads. The southern offensive moved from previously occupied Crimea, where Russia had concentrated forces and enjoyed prepared bases. These differences explain why Russian troops advanced rapidly in some places while meeting effective defense elsewhere. The initial campaign should not be reconstructed as one coordinated column moving uniformly toward a single objective. It consisted of multiple operational groupings whose schedules, logistics and intelligence were not always successfully synchronized.

Civilian responses unfolded even before national authorities had issued comprehensive instructions. Railway staff helped move people west, local volunteers organized shelter, medical services prepared for casualties and communications networks circulated warnings. Some citizens remained in danger zones because they had elderly relatives, lacked money or could not safely travel. Ukrainian military and political leadership also had to prevent misinformation from triggering panic or diverting people onto unsafe roads. This societal response is a major reason the first weeks cannot be written as a story of armies alone. The resilience of state institutions was produced through concrete decisions by workers and citizens, not simply through a speech from the capital.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From the War of 2014–2021 to the War of 2022

What Remained

  • The central dispute: whether Ukraine would determine its own alignment and territorial status.
  • The role of Russian-backed forces and Russian regular units in the Donbas and Crimea, together with Moscow's denial or minimization of its involvement in 2014.
  • The documentation of civilian harm by United Nations monitors, carried on by the same office.

What Changed

  • Geography: from two provinces to the whole country, including strikes on cities far from the front.
  • Scale: from a conflict of perhaps tens of thousands of combatants to hundreds of thousands, with heavy artillery, air power, and missile bombardment.
  • Objectives and openness: from deniable, hybrid operations to an acknowledged invasion, with the apparent aim of overthrowing the government.
  • International response: from sanctions and limited aid to unprecedented sanctions, large-scale military assistance, and refugee movements.

Why the pattern matters: Treating 2022 as the beginning of a new war ignores the eight years of conflict that preceded it; treating it as merely a continuation understates the discontinuity of scale and aim.

Section 71.7

Air, Missile, Airborne, and Cyber Operations in the Opening Campaign

The opening hours included a large strike by Russian cruise and ballistic missiles, supplemented by aircraft, against Ukrainian air defenses, airfields, command posts, and military infrastructure. Officials in the United States and Ukraine described dozens to over a hundred missiles launched on the first day. Russian planners appear to have intended to suppress Ukrainian air defense and aviation quickly, as in other modern campaigns. They did not succeed. Ukrainian forces had dispersed aircraft and mobile air-defense systems before the invasion, and the Russian strikes, though damaging, did not destroy them. Ukraine's air force continued to fly, and the surviving Soviet-era S-300 and Buk systems, joined within weeks by shoulder-fired missiles supplied by Western governments, imposed losses on Russian aircraft and helicopters. The Russian Aerospace Forces consequently operated cautiously, relied on standoff weapons, and never achieved the air superiority that would have allowed ground columns to advance under cover.

The contrast between the campaigns' expectations and the results is among the best-documented features of the early war. Russia's air operations suffered from a limited capacity to detect and strike mobile targets, inadequate coordination with ground forces, and a shortage of precision weapons suitable for a long campaign; Ukrainian small drones, among them the Turkish-made Bayraktar TB2, were used effectively in the first weeks against Russian columns and air-defense systems, although Russian air defenses soon reduced their success. At the same time, the strikes on cities, power plants, and later the energy network (§71.21) were increasingly central to Russian practice, and international humanitarian monitors began recording attacks that struck civilian objects.

Cyber operations began before the first missiles. Destructive "wiper" malware was discovered on Ukrainian networks in January and again on 23 February, and on the morning of 24 February an attack on the satellite network of the company Viasat disabled modems used by Ukrainian military and civilian customers and, as a side effect, thousands of users elsewhere in Europe, including the remote-control systems of wind turbines in Germany. Western governments attributed the attack to Russia. Ukraine's defenders had, however, prepared for cyber attack with Western assistance, moved government data to cloud servers abroad under emergency legal changes, and diversified communications; in late February Ukrainian officials obtained Starlink satellite terminals from the American company SpaceX, which became widely used by the military and by civil services. Russian forces, by contrast, reportedly relied on insecure radios and on civilian mobile networks that failed, impairing command and exposing officers to interception.

Information was a weapon too, and the opening weeks illustrate the need for source criticism. The story of the "Ghost of Kyiv," a fighter pilot said to have shot down numerous Russian aircraft, circulated widely and was later described by the Ukrainian air force itself as a legend that had boosted morale rather than a single person's record. Russian state media similarly circulated claims, such as that there was no attack on civilians or that evidence had been staged, that independent investigations contradicted. For historians, the lesson is that vivid early stories are not reliable evidence until corroborated. The best evidence for the opening campaign comes from commercial satellite imagery, intercepted and published communications, forensic analysis of wreckage, the monitoring bodies of the United Nations, and the later admissions and records of the participating militaries.

Hostomel's airborne operation showed the risks of using air assault forces without assured control of airspace and timely reinforcement. Helicopters carried assault personnel toward a strategic airport near Kyiv, but Ukrainian defenders contested the landing area and reinforcements faced difficulty establishing durable control. Even if an airfield runway is briefly occupied, the arrival of heavy transport aircraft requires protection from missiles, artillery and ground counterattack. The military function of the airport was therefore different from its symbolic value as a high-profile objective. Later accounts that declare the whole operation an immediate success or failure should specify whether they refer to landing, holding the facility or using it as an air bridge.

Cyber operations also require source discipline. Ukraine experienced intrusions and disruptive attacks against digital systems, yet analysts generally found that cyber activity did not replace conventional firepower or automatically disable essential national command systems. Some disruptions were localized or quickly repaired, while communications technologies helped civilian authorities and military units continue operating. It would be misleading to infer from the existence of cyber operations that all Ukrainian telecommunications failed, or from continued communications that no serious attack occurred. Students should distinguish attempted intrusion, confirmed compromise, service interruption and demonstrated effect on a specific military operation. The evidence for each is different.

Section 71.8

Hostomel, Irpin, Bucha, and the Battle for Kyiv

The first major test of the Russian plan occurred at Hostomel, a cargo airport about twenty-five kilometers northwest of Kyiv and the home of the Antonov aircraft company. On the morning of 24 February Russian airborne forces arrived by helicopter, supported by attack helicopters and fire, to seize the airfield and use it as a base for transport aircraft carrying reinforcements to the capital. Ukrainian National Guard units and other troops counterattacked, fought for the field, and damaged the runway; the Russian aircraft carrying follow-on forces did not land. The airfield was held by Russian troops during the first days, but the intended airbridge never operated at scale. The giant Antonov An-225 Mriya, the world's largest cargo aircraft, was destroyed in its hangar. The episode shows both the ambition of the Russian plan and the vulnerability of an operation that depended on speed.

The ground assault from the north reached the outskirts of Kyiv within days. Columns advanced through Ivankiv and Dymer to the suburbs of Irpin, Bucha, and Hostomel, while a second thrust approached from the northeast. Commercial satellite imagery in the last days of February showed a column stretching dozens of kilometers northwest of the capital, which commentators described as a sixty-four-kilometer convoy. The column's progress stalled. Ukrainian forces destroyed bridges across the Irpin River, flooded part of the river's floodplain, ambushed vehicles with anti-tank missiles and artillery, and fought house-by-house in Irpin for several weeks. Russian columns were confined to roads in forest and marsh terrain made more difficult by the spring thaw, and the forces on the ground encountered fuel, food, and maintenance shortages. Several Russian general officers were reported killed near the front by Ukrainian and, in some cases, Russian sources.

Kyiv itself, a city of nearly three million before the war, was subjected to missile attacks and was shelled at its edges, including a strike on a shopping center in the Podilskyi district on 20 March. Its residents were under curfew, many sheltered in the metro and basements, and large numbers left. At the same time, civilian volunteers and territorial defense units built checkpoints, prepared defenses, and supplied the army. The city's defense depended less on a single decisive battle than on the combination of Ukrainian military capability, the poor logistics and coordination of the attackers, and the lack of an uprising or political collapse of the kind that Russian planners seem to have expected.

By late March Ukrainian forces began local counterattacks. They retook Makariv and, on 28 March, Irpin; Hostomel and Bucha were recovered as Russian units withdrew at the end of the month (§71.12). The Russian Ministry of Defense announced on 25 March that the "main tasks of the first stage" had been fulfilled and the focus would shift to the Donbas. Western and Ukrainian assessments treat the failure to take Kyiv as the central event of the first phase: it ended Russian prospects for a quick victory, preserved the Ukrainian government and capital, and altered the political calculation of Ukraine's international supporters, who had initially hesitated to supply heavy weapons.

Bucha became an especially important case for source analysis because the sequence of occupation and withdrawal created opportunities to compare testimony with dated imagery. Satellite photographs showed bodies or other signs of violence in locations that Russian officials later alleged were staged only after withdrawal. Investigators combined such imagery with witness interviews and physical examination, making the evidence stronger than a single photograph viewed without context. The chapter should not substitute one sensational image for the larger documented pattern, but neither should it pretend that unsupported allegations of fabrication carry the same evidentiary weight. The distinction between a proven civilian killing and the exact legal responsibility of a named defendant must nevertheless remain clear.

Kyiv's defense also depended on mobility and coordination among regular troops, territorial defenders and civilians. Bridges and approaches around Irpin and other suburbs constrained Russian movement; Ukrainian units used ambushes, artillery and intelligence to impede columns and disrupt attempts to encircle the capital. The urban road network created chokepoints where vehicles became vulnerable to anti-tank systems and drones. Russian supply routes were repeatedly attacked and troops encountered difficulties navigating among small settlements. The result was a military failure produced by interacting conditions. A simple formula such as 'Russian troops were badly led' neglects the agency of Ukrainian defenders and the geography they used.

Cause and Effect

Why the Russian Operation Against Kyiv Failed

Long-Term Conditions

Ukrainian military reform and Western training since 2014; the experience of a standing war in the Donbas; a Russian military that had performed well in short, limited operations such as Syria and Crimea.

Intermediate Developments

Russian expectations of rapid political collapse; compressed planning and secrecy that left units uninformed; Western intelligence warnings that allowed Ukraine to disperse its forces; shipments of anti-tank and air-defense missiles.

Short-Term Triggers

Failure to secure Hostomel airport as an air bridge; road-bound columns hit by ambushes; fuel and maintenance breakdowns; Ukrainian destruction of bridges and flooding.

Immediate and Long-Term Consequences

Russian withdrawal from northern Ukraine; survival of the Ukrainian government; a shift in Russian aims toward the east and south; expanded Western military assistance; a prolonged war.

Historical complexity: No single cause is sufficient. Poor logistics alone would not have defeated a determined adversary, and Ukrainian resistance alone might have been overwhelmed by competent planning. Outcomes depended on their interaction, and on contingent events such as the stalled column near Hostomel.

Section 71.9

Chernihiv, Sumy, and the Northern Fronts

The northern campaign included secondary offensives from Russia's territory toward Chernihiv, a city of about 280,000 inhabitants near the border with Belarus and Russia, and toward Sumy, Okhtyrka, Konotop, and other towns of northeastern Ukraine. Russian forces reached Chernihiv within days and, rather than entering the city in force, largely bypassed it and encircled it, cutting the road and rail routes to Kyiv. The city was attacked with artillery and air strikes. A bridge over the Desna River was destroyed, which complicated evacuations, and the city lost electricity, heating, and water for long stretches. Local officials reported that hundreds of civilians had been killed, and humanitarian monitors documented strikes on residential districts, including on 16 March an attack that killed more than a dozen people queuing for bread, an incident investigated by human rights groups and the United Nations.

Around Sumy, Russian troops entered the province from Kursk and Bryansk provinces, occupied several towns such as Trostianets, and fought for Okhtyrka, where Ukrainian authorities reported attacks on residential areas and on military facilities. Ukrainian troops held Sumy itself. On 8 and 9 March Ukraine and Russia agreed on a local evacuation route from Sumy, one of the few humanitarian corridors that functioned in these early weeks, and thousands of civilians, including foreign students, were bused to Poltava. The agreement did not prevent further attacks elsewhere, and later agreements frequently failed (§71.14).

The northern fronts show clearly the difficulty of sustaining a mechanized offensive in hostile territory. Russian supply lines, which ran from Belarus and Russia along a limited number of roads and railways, were exposed to Ukrainian artillery, drones, and sabotage by local civilians and territorial defense units, who also provided information about movements. Russian forces lacked enough infantry to occupy the territory they had overrun and relied on exposed columns; Ukrainian commanders used the terrain's forests and rivers to channel and ambush them. Reports from Ukrainian and independent observers indicated that morale in some Russian units was low, and that supplies, including fuel and food, were running short.

For civilians, the effects were severe. The population of Chernihiv endured weeks of bombardment, food and medicine shortages, and the disruption of heat in cold weather, while residents of occupied villages around Chernihiv, Sumy, and Kyiv later reported killings, looting, detention, and abuse by Russian soldiers (§§71.12 and 71.37). When Russian forces withdrew in early April, they left large areas contaminated by mines and unexploded ordnance, destroyed housing, and damaged bridges and roads, and the work of reconstruction and demining began under the continuing threat of attack. The northern campaign therefore appears in the historical record both as a military failure for Russia and as a period of acute civilian suffering whose full dimensions are still being established by investigators.

Chernihiv and Sumy occupied different positions in Russia's initial military plan. Both lay on approaches from the north or northeast, but local terrain, road networks and Ukrainian defensive organization complicated Russian advances. Troops attempting to bypass cities left defended populations and military units capable of disrupting supply columns in their rear. Continued Ukrainian control of major urban centers prevented Russia from treating them as secure logistical bases. Siege and bombardment also created severe civilian crises, forcing municipal governments to improvise water distribution, medical care and evacuation. The military value of holding a city thus included keeping government and civilian institutions functioning, not merely maintaining a defensive line.

The experience of nearby villages often differed from that of the regional capitals. Some were occupied temporarily, others became staging grounds or suffered repeated artillery fire. After Russian withdrawal residents discovered damaged homes, destroyed bridges and evidence of abuses requiring investigation. Returning to a village did not mean services immediately resumed; transportation, mines, public records and the presence of displaced families all complicated recovery. This local geography is important when comparing the northern campaign with the rapid Russian advance in southern Ukraine. A map colored according to army control provides only one layer of evidence about what populations actually endured.

Section 71.10

The Defense of Kharkiv and Northeastern Ukraine in 2022

Kharkiv was among the first cities attacked. As Ukraine's second-largest city, a major industrial, scientific, and university center, and only a short distance from the Russian frontier, it was a natural objective. On 24 February Russian forces approached from the north, and on 27 February Russian vehicles entered parts of the city; Ukrainian troops and territorial defense units repelled them in street fighting. After this failed attempt to seize the city, Russia turned mainly to bombardment. On 1 March a missile struck the regional administration building on Freedom Square. Russian forces fired artillery and rockets into residential neighborhoods, and human rights investigators documented the use of cluster munitions in populated areas, which are banned by many states though not by Russia or Ukraine, and whose indiscriminate effects make their use in cities difficult to reconcile with the laws of war.

The city's population, estimated at about 1.4 million before the war, responded by sheltering, evacuating, and organizing. Thousands lived for weeks in the deep stations of the Kharkiv metro, which the city's authorities equipped with beds, kitchens, and basic medical care and which also hosted school lessons and community groups. Free evacuation trains from the railway station carried others to western Ukraine and Poland. Estimates of the number who left at some point vary, but a very large proportion of the inhabitants fled temporarily or permanently. Mayor Ihor Terekhov and the regional governor Oleh Synehubov remained and gave regular public briefings. The Ukrainian authorities and international organizations documented large numbers of civilian casualties in the city and region over the following months.

The defense of Kharkiv also had political significance. The city was predominantly Russian-speaking, and Russian officials and planners had presented the population of eastern Ukraine as potentially receptive to Russian forces or at least inclined to passivity. The behavior of Kharkiv's residents contradicted that expectation: Russian-speaking Ukrainians organized volunteer networks and joined defense units, and surveys in the following months indicated that language use was not a reliable predictor of political loyalty. Sociologists studying the war have emphasized that Ukrainian identity is civic as well as linguistic, and that a large share of Russian speakers regard themselves as Ukrainian citizens defending their country.

Militarily, the northeastern front diverted Russian forces from Kyiv and settled into a pattern in which Ukrainian troops held the city and gradually extended their control around it. In May, Ukrainian units counterattacked north and east of Kharkiv, and by mid-May the Ukrainian General Staff reported that its troops had reached the Russian border in places and pushed Russian artillery beyond the range of much of the city. Russia nevertheless retained the ability to fire into Kharkiv from its own territory and occupied large parts of the province to the east and south, including Izium and Kupiansk, until the Ukrainian counteroffensive of September (§71.20). Kharkiv thus remained a front-line city throughout the war.

Kharkiv's proximity to Russia made it a focus of intense shelling and missile attacks, but the city also had a dense civilian population, significant educational institutions and infrastructure that could support organized defense. Ukrainian troops, local authorities and volunteers combined efforts to protect vital routes and sustain services. Russian forces attacked from the north and east but did not obtain lasting control of the city. The use of artillery and rockets in populated neighborhoods caused severe damage and casualties. A historian should distinguish fires directed against identified military objectives from attacks where the indiscriminate effects or absence of a legitimate target are supported by investigation, rather than assuming all impacts have the same legal classification.

Kharkiv's resilience had a social dimension. Residents used metro stations and other underground spaces for shelter; some continued work or volunteered while schools and universities reorganized instruction. Engineers repaired utilities under fire and medical workers maintained emergency services. These efforts helped preserve an urban community that Moscow may have expected to become difficult for Ukraine to govern. The city's Russian-speaking character was frequently invoked in political arguments, but actual resistance demonstrated the inadequacy of equating language use with preference for Russian occupation. The experience of Kharkiv was a powerful example of the gap between a state narrative about 'protecting Russian speakers' and the political choices of many people subjected to Russian attacks.

Everyday Life and Social History

Sheltering Underground: The Kharkiv Metro

The metro was built in the Soviet era and its stations, deep underground and equipped with ventilation, were designed in part as civil-defense shelters. Residents who came in February 2022 slept on platforms and in the trains' parked carriages, brought blankets and pets, and relied on volunteers and city officials for water and meals. Reports from journalists and aid organizations describe makeshift kitchens, medical posts, and play areas for children. Some people lived there for weeks; others returned to apartments by day and went underground at night when shelling intensified.

The experience was not uniform. Elderly and disabled residents faced particular difficulty reaching stations and climbing stairs; families with resources could leave for western Ukraine or abroad, while those without relatives elsewhere, or caring for dependents, were more likely to stay. Men of military age, who were barred from leaving the country, often remained to serve or volunteer, and women, children, and the elderly made up much of the refugee flow. Records of such local experience come largely from journalism, humanitarian reports, and later oral-history projects; they illustrate lived conditions but cannot by themselves supply statistics for the whole city.

Section 71.11

Ukrainian Leadership, Military Resistance, and Popular Mobilization

Volodymyr Zelenskyy's decision to remain in Kyiv and to communicate directly through daily video addresses became one of the defining images of the war. Before the invasion, his popularity had declined and his government had been criticized for corruption, for its handling of the economy, and for the concentration of media and legal authority in the presidency. After 24 February, his approval rose sharply in polling, and his speeches to foreign parliaments helped secure military and financial assistance and sanctions. Historians distinguish the symbolic and diplomatic role of the president from the operation of the state as a whole: ministers, regional governors and mayors, the security services, railway and energy managers, and local officials took thousands of decisions that kept the country running. In March the government suspended the activities of several political parties it described as pro-Russian and consolidated national television programming into a joint "telethon," steps defended as security measures and criticized by some observers as limits on pluralism (§71.44).

The Ukrainian armed forces under Commander-in-Chief Valerii Zaluzhnyi benefited from reforms and training since 2014, from the experience of officers and soldiers who had fought in the Donbas, from the Western intelligence that warned of the attack, and from the delivery before and after the invasion of anti-tank and air-defense missiles such as the Javelin, NLAW, and Stinger. They used these weapons in combination with artillery, drones, and local knowledge. They did not fight alone or without losses: Ukrainian cities and soldiers sustained heavy casualties, and Ukrainian commanders repeatedly appealed for heavier weapons, such as tanks, long-range artillery, and air defense, that Western governments initially provided cautiously, then in growing quantities.

The war also produced a mass mobilization. The Territorial Defense Forces, established as a distinct branch only weeks before the invasion, enrolled tens of thousands of volunteers in the first days, and general mobilization enlarged the armed forces. Civilians built checkpoints, prepared improvised weapons, delivered food and medicine, hosted refugees, and reported Russian movements through a government application and chatbot. Railway workers kept trains running under attack to evacuate civilians, and information technology and engineering firms contributed. An International Legion of foreign volunteers was announced on 27 February; its actual numbers were much smaller than some early claims suggested. The diaspora raised funds and supplies. This civic mobilization was extensive, though it was supported by state organizations and Western assistance and varied by region.

Evidence on public opinion indicates very high support for resistance in the first months. Polling by Ukrainian sociologists, such as the Kyiv International Institute of Sociology, recorded that large majorities across regions, including the east and south and among Russian speakers, rejected concessions of territory and supported continued fighting; these polls cannot capture residents of occupied areas and were conducted in wartime conditions that could influence responses. Not every episode was one of cohesion: Russian forces benefited from the cooperation or failure of some local officials and security personnel in the south, the Ukrainian president dismissed senior security officials in July 2022 over the number of employees suspected of collaboration or treason, and the burden of mobilization fell unevenly. A balanced account notes both the unusual solidarity and these tensions.

The survival of Ukraine's central government had operational and diplomatic consequences. The refusal of senior officials to abandon Kyiv communicated continuity of authority to local governments, soldiers and international partners. Emergency broadcasts, coordination of railway evacuations and rapid appeals for equipment transformed the political response into practical military and administrative measures. Yet the government's position should not obscure the independent decisions of military commanders and local authorities. Territorial defense formations organized checkpoints and logistics, while civilian information networks reported movements and helped identify vulnerable residents. The military value of civilian participation was real but also created difficult obligations to distinguish protected civilians from combatants and to limit their exposure to danger.

The early mobilization showed strengths and limits in Ukrainian society. Volunteers sometimes delivered food, fuel, body armor and medicines faster than formal supply systems, but spontaneous networks differed in capacity and were vulnerable to misinformation or duplication. Public enthusiasm did not eliminate problems of weapons training, command discipline and accountability. A useful historical comparison examines how community organizations that had developed after 2014 supported resistance in 2022 while working alongside a more capable regular army. The state and civil society did not function as substitutes for each other: they relied on each other's resources and legitimacy under emergency conditions.

Historical Vocabulary
Territorial Defense Forces

Definition: A branch of Ukraine's armed forces composed largely of part-time volunteers and reservists organized by region, intended to defend their home areas, guard infrastructure, and support regular forces.

Historical context: Created by law in 2021 and formally established on 1 January 2022, the forces were rapidly expanded after the invasion. Many of their members had little training initially; units were later integrated more closely with the regular army.

Why it matters: The Territorial Defense Forces illustrate how a state can mobilize local manpower and knowledge quickly. They should not be confused with the volunteer battalions of 2014, many of which were integrated into the National Guard, or with unorganized civilian volunteers.

Section 71.12

The Russian Withdrawal From Northern Ukraine and Its Consequences

On 25 March 2022, Russian General Staff officials said that the first stage of the operation had been completed and that the main effort would concentrate on the liberation of the Donbas. Negotiations in Istanbul on 29 March were accompanied by a Russian statement that it would sharply reduce military activity around Kyiv and Chernihiv. Over the next week, Russian and allied units withdrew from the Kyiv region, which Ukrainian authorities declared fully liberated on 2 April, and from Chernihiv and Sumy provinces by 6–8 April. The Russian government described the withdrawal as a gesture of goodwill and a planned redeployment; Ukrainian and Western officials and most analysts regarded it as a forced retreat after the failure to take the capital and the severe logistical and manpower losses. Evidence from satellite imagery and from the pattern of regrouping in Belarus and Russia supports an assessment that the offensive had stalled and that the force was unsustainable, though whether withdrawal was ordered earlier or later is not documented.

As Ukrainian forces and journalists entered the recovered communities, they documented killings of civilians. In Bucha, a town northwest of Kyiv occupied from late February until the end of March, bodies were found on Yablunska Street and in yards, basements, and a mass grave near a church. Ukrainian authorities reported recovering hundreds of bodies in Bucha, and over a thousand in the wider Kyiv region by later counts, many of them civilians killed by gunfire, some with hands bound or with signs of execution. Similar findings were reported in Borodianka, Hostomel, Motyzhyn, and other communities. Russian authorities denied responsibility and described the images as a staged provocation, a claim contradicted by the evidence discussed in the Primary Source Window below.

Several types of investigation proceeded in parallel. The UN Human Rights Monitoring Mission recorded and verified individual cases, including summary executions, in the Kyiv, Chernihiv, and Sumy regions. The OSCE's Moscow Mechanism, invoked by dozens of states, issued a report in April 2022 finding clear patterns of violations of international humanitarian law by Russian forces. The Independent International Commission of Inquiry on Ukraine, established by the UN Human Rights Council, concluded in October 2022 that Russian forces had committed war crimes, including willful killings, torture, and unlawful confinement in the Kyiv, Chernihiv, Kharkiv, and Sumy regions. The International Criminal Court's prosecutor, together with national prosecutors in Ukraine and several European states, opened investigations (§§71.37–71.38). The Commission also documented a smaller number of violations by Ukrainian forces, including the mistreatment of Russian prisoners; the available evidence indicates, however, a large difference in scale and in the pattern of conduct, and an accurate account should not treat the two as equivalent.

The withdrawal had wide consequences. The discovery of the killings affected public opinion and government policy abroad: governments imposed additional sanctions and expelled Russian diplomats, and several accelerated arms deliveries. In Russia, the withdrawal produced no public acknowledgment of defeat, but it changed the war's objectives: the Russian leadership redefined the principal goals as the "liberation" of the Donbas and the south. Militarily, Russia moved forces and equipment to the east, while Ukraine gained time to reorganize and to receive heavier Western weapons. For the people of the liberated areas, the first task was to bury the dead, find the missing, clear mines, and rebuild, tasks that continued long after the front had moved.

The Russian withdrawal from northern Ukraine should be studied as a military decision and a turning point in public evidence. Russian officials described a reduction of activity as part of diplomacy or a shift in strategy, while the battlefield record showed unsuccessful attempts to encircle Kyiv and severe logistical problems. Ukrainian forces pursued retreating units and regained areas that had been under occupation for weeks. The withdrawal freed Russian formations for operations elsewhere but also exposed the material cost of the failed initial offensive. Describing it simply as a planned redeployment hides the mismatch between initial positions, public objectives and actual outcomes.

Liberation did not immediately bring safety. Civilians returning to towns near Kyiv faced mines, damaged bridges, ruined homes, interrupted water and electricity, and the discovery of graves or bodies requiring investigation. Forensic teams had to preserve evidence before moving remains, while survivors sought information about missing family members. The timing of access mattered for verification: investigators could interview witnesses after the occupiers had left, but some physical evidence had already deteriorated. These experiences show how a military withdrawal can initiate a second, painstaking stage of war history involving accountability, reconstruction and the recovery of everyday civic institutions.

Section 71.13

The Southern Offensive: Crimea, Melitopol, and Kherson

The southern offensive was the most successful Russian operation of the opening phase. Forces moving north from Crimea crossed the narrow land links at Perekop, Chonhar, and Henichesk on 24 February, and Russian troops reached the Dnipro River at Nova Kakhovka within a day. They seized the Kakhovka hydroelectric dam and the head of the North Crimean Canal, which Ukraine had blocked in 2014 and which Russian forces reopened, restoring fresh water to Crimea. Melitopol was occupied between 26 February and 1 March, Berdiansk on 27–28 February, and Kherson, a regional capital of about 280,000 people and the main crossing of the lower Dnipro, on 2 March after its mayor negotiated terms with the occupiers to avoid destruction. Enerhodar and the Zaporizhzhia nuclear plant fell on 3–4 March (§71.18). Within about a week the Russian army had established a land corridor from Crimea to the Azov coast and was closing on Mariupol from the east and west.

Why was the south different from the north? Several factors are suggested by the evidence. The terrain is open and favors mechanized advance; Russian forces in Crimea were already deployed and supplied; the Ukrainian defense had fewer forces in the area and, according to later Ukrainian criticism, failed to destroy key bridges quickly; and some local officials and security personnel cooperated with, or did not resist, the occupiers. The Ukrainian government subsequently dismissed and investigated officials for these failures. At the same time, Ukrainian troops stopped the Russian advance at Mykolaiv, a shipbuilding city on the Southern Bug, and at Voznesensk on 2–3 March, which blocked the route toward Odesa and the Romanian and Moldovan borders. The city of Mykolaiv was shelled for months but never captured.

Resistance to the occupation began almost immediately. In Kherson, Melitopol, Berdiansk, and other towns, residents gathered in demonstrations carrying Ukrainian flags in early March; Russian troops dispersed them with warning shots, stun grenades, and in some cases gunfire, and detained activists and officials. Melitopol's mayor, Ivan Fedorov, was abducted by Russian forces on 11 March and released on 16 March in an exchange for captured Russian conscripts, after which Russian-appointed administrators took over the city. The occupation authorities introduced Russian television and, from the late spring, the ruble and Russian passports, and they began to prepare for what they later called referendums (§71.33).

For historians, the southern campaign illustrates how battlefield success can create administrative and political problems for an invader. The occupied south, though predominantly Russian-speaking in places, was not welcoming; the Russian forces seized a land bridge but had to hold hundreds of kilometers of territory with limited manpower and a long, vulnerable supply route across the Dnipro and the isthmus. Ukraine's later counteroffensives in this region (§71.16) exploited exactly these vulnerabilities. The occupation also set the pattern of the administration, detention, and civilian experience examined in Division VI.

The rapid Russian advance from Crimea reflected preparation and favorable initial routes as well as Ukrainian defensive challenges. Forces moved toward Melitopol and Kherson while seeking to secure a land corridor linking Crimea with occupied Donetsk territory. Control of crossings over the Dnipro and roads through southern Ukraine was strategically important because military traffic and supplies depended on them. The speed of advance generated local controversies over preparedness, intelligence and the conduct of officials. Those controversies require specific records; a historian should not infer treason from every rapid withdrawal or assume that all commanders had sufficient personnel to defend every sector.

In occupied Kherson city early civilian demonstrations challenged Russian control despite the presence of armed troops. Residents used public squares and symbols of Ukrainian statehood, while occupation authorities developed systems of surveillance and control. The continued functioning of local utilities and hospitals depended on workers navigating coercive circumstances. Whether an official remained at a post, resigned, fled or cooperated was often a difficult choice with consequences for colleagues and families. The campaign therefore provides a bridge from operational military history to the later section on occupation: capturing territory was only the beginning of an extended struggle over administration and society.

Regional Context

The Southern Provinces: Kherson, Zaporizhzhia, and Crimea

The southern provinces of Kherson and Zaporizhzhia are agricultural and industrial regions along the lower Dnipro and the Sea of Azov, bound economically to Crimea, to the Black Sea ports, and to central Ukraine. Their histories include the Cossack Zaporozhian Host of the early modern period (Chapter 30), incorporation into the Russian Empire's "New Russia" in the late eighteenth century (Chapter 34), and settlement by Ukrainians, Russians, Germans, Greeks, Bulgarians, and other groups. In the Soviet period these areas were industrialized, and Crimea became a Russian republic province before its transfer to the Ukrainian republic in 1954. Languages used in daily life include Ukrainian, Russian, and a mixture; political identification, however, does not follow language in any simple way. The region's importance in 2022 derived from geography: the Dnipro crossings, the nuclear plant and dam, the canal water for Crimea, and the land route between Russia and Crimea.

Section 71.14

The Siege of Mariupol and the Destruction of the City

Mariupol, a port and steel-producing city on the Sea of Azov with a prewar population of about 430,000, lay on the route between Russian-held Donetsk and Crimea and was thus of great strategic value. Russian and Donetsk forces had encircled it by 2 March 2022, and over the following weeks they bombarded it with artillery, rockets, aircraft, and naval fire. The besieged city lost electricity, water, heating, mobile phone service, and food supplies. Residents melted snow, cooked on fires in courtyards, and buried the dead in yards and parks because the cemeteries could not be reached. Attempts to establish humanitarian corridors repeatedly failed: a ceasefire announced on 5 March for evacuation collapsed amid mutual accusations, and convoys of aid were blocked. Ukrainian and Russian officials gave conflicting accounts, but international humanitarian organizations stated that they could not reach the city.

Two incidents became symbols of the siege. On 9 March an airstrike hit a maternity and children's hospital; Ukrainian authorities reported that three people, including a child, died and that many were wounded. Russian officials claimed the building had been vacated and used by Ukrainian forces and that the reports were staged, a claim contradicted by journalists who photographed and interviewed patients and by independent analysis. On 16 March a strike hit the Drama Theater, where hundreds of civilians were sheltering and in front of which the word "children" had been written in large letters visible from the air. Ukrainian authorities estimated hundreds killed; an Associated Press investigation concluded that the toll was probably near six hundred, and Amnesty International, following an investigation, concluded that the building was struck by a Russian airstrike and called it a war crime. Russia denied responsibility and blamed Ukrainian forces.

The number of people who died in Mariupol remains uncertain. Ukrainian officials reported very high civilian losses; independent investigators used burial sites, interviews, photographs and incomplete local records to estimate additional deaths. The UN Human Rights Office reported on 16 June 2022 that it had verified at least 1,348 civilian deaths in the siege, stressing that the actual figure was likely thousands higher. A February 2024 study by Human Rights Watch, Truth Hounds and SITU Research estimated at least 8,034 excess deaths using cemetery-based methods during the first year; it could not classify all those deaths as civilians, combatants, or deaths directly caused by attack. These are different denominators, periods and verification standards, not competing totals to be averaged. Russian-installed authorities supplied other counts and blamed Ukrainian forces, claims requiring their own evidence. Limited access, improvised burials, destroyed records and political interests explain why no comprehensive audited fatality count exists.

The city's fate shows how a siege of a large urban area affects civilians. By the time organized fighting ended, much of the housing stock, the hospitals, schools, and port facilities had been damaged or destroyed, and tens of thousands of residents had left, many through routes controlled by Russian forces and through "filtration" procedures (§71.36), which Ukraine and human rights groups described as coercive and Russia described as security screening. Under the administration subsequently installed, the city was partly rebuilt, and residents who stayed lived under Russian laws, schools, and passports (§71.34). Mariupol is therefore both a military episode, securing the land corridor, and a major humanitarian catastrophe whose human toll and legal characterization continue to be investigated.

The destruction of Mariupol requires special care in counting civilian deaths. OHCHR stated in June 2022 that it had verified at least 1,348 civilian fatalities during the siege while emphasizing that the actual number was thousands higher. Later researchers examining burial records estimated at least 8,034 excess deaths over a longer period, but not all of those deaths could be separated confidently into civilian and combatant categories or assigned to a specific immediate cause. These figures therefore describe different methods and windows, not rival claims that can be averaged. The source register should identify what each count measures and why the absence of complete access prevents a definitive total.

Attacks on the maternity hospital and the drama theater became focal points for investigation. Witness testimony, photographs, satellite images and the known sequence of bombardment enabled investigators to assess Russian official claims that civilian facilities had been used for military purposes. A legitimate military objective, if identified, would not eliminate the requirements of distinction, proportionality and precautions. The physical destruction of the city and the confinement of civilians in shelters made the humanitarian situation especially severe. A historically defensible narrative should include those who could not evacuate and the limits on aid access, rather than treating siege solely as a strategic operation to establish a land corridor.

Historical Vocabulary
Humanitarian Corridor

Definition: A negotiated, temporary route, agreed by the parties to a conflict, enabling civilians to leave a besieged area or aid to enter it.

Historical context: The laws of war require parties to allow civilians to leave and the passage of relief supplies under certain conditions. In Ukraine, corridors agreed in March 2022 worked in a few places, such as Sumy, and failed in others, notably Mariupol, where each side blamed the other and where routes sometimes led only into Russian-controlled territory, which Ukraine refused.

Why it matters: A corridor depends on mutual trust and on the route's destination. Its success or failure is evidence about the parties' conduct, but each side's account must be tested against independent monitoring.

Section 71.15

Azovstal, the End of Organized Resistance, and Prisoners of War

By mid-April 2022 most of Mariupol was in Russian hands, and the remaining Ukrainian defenders, including marines, soldiers, border guards, and members of the Azov Regiment of the National Guard, concentrated in the Azovstal Iron and Steel Works, a vast Soviet-era complex with deep tunnels and bunkers. Hundreds of civilians also sheltered there. Putin declared on 21 April that the city was liberated and ordered that the plant be sealed rather than stormed. Russian forces continued to bombard it. In early May the United Nations and the International Committee of the Red Cross arranged a series of evacuations in which several hundred civilians left the plant; the operation required negotiations among the parties and was repeatedly interrupted.

On 16 May the Ukrainian General Staff ordered the remaining troops to cease defending the plant to preserve their lives, and over the following days more than two thousand fighters surrendered and were taken to Russian-controlled territory. On 20 May the Russian defense minister announced the complete "liberation" of the city and plant. Ukraine described the defense as having tied down Russian forces for weeks, while Russian commentators emphasized the elimination of what they called a stronghold of nationalists. Both framings are interpretive. The military effect of the defense, including how long it delayed Russian redeployment, is debated; its symbolic effect on Ukrainian morale and on the international view of the war is better documented.

The fate of the captured soldiers raised acute issues of law. The Geneva Conventions require that prisoners of war be treated humanely, protected from violence and from public curiosity, and exchanged or repatriated. The Russian Supreme Court in August 2022 designated the Azov Regiment as a terrorist organization, a decision that Ukraine and many legal observers criticized as an attempt to deny its members prisoner-of-war protection and to prosecute them; later trials of captured Ukrainian fighters in Russian-controlled courts were widely criticized. Some Azov commanders were exchanged in September 2022, in an agreement mediated by Turkey that also returned a Russian-aligned Ukrainian politician, Viktor Medvedchuk, to Russia; others remained in captivity.

On 29 July 2022 an explosion killed at least fifty Ukrainian prisoners held at a detention facility in Olenivka, in the Donetsk-controlled area, many of them Azovstal defenders. Russia and Ukraine blamed each other: Russian officials said a Ukrainian rocket strike was responsible; Ukraine said the explosion was caused by Russian forces to conceal abuse or execution. A United Nations fact-finding mission was proposed but was not able to carry out its work, and independent analyses by some news organizations raised doubts about the Russian explanation. No independent investigation gained access to the site, so responsibility has not been established by a body with full access. The episode illustrates the dependence of the historical record on access, and the vulnerability of prisoners in places where independent monitors are barred. Broader treatment of detention appears in §71.36.

The defense of the Azovstal plant acquired intense symbolic significance, but its operational history involved an enormous industrial complex with tunnels, shelters and difficult approaches. Ukrainian soldiers, including personnel from several services and formations, remained in fortified positions after most of Mariupol was under Russian control. Civilians sheltered within or near the complex, and evacuations required international negotiation. The physical environment enabled prolonged resistance while limiting supplies, medical treatment and communication. Public descriptions that attribute the entire defense to a single named unit obscure the broader range of personnel involved and the effects on civilians trapped in and around the works.

When organized resistance ended in May 2022, captured Ukrainian personnel became protected prisoners of war regardless of Russian political descriptions of particular units. Their detention, treatment and eventual exchanges were subject to international humanitarian law. Later incidents involving prisoners from Mariupol, including deaths at a detention facility in occupied Olenivka in July 2022, generated contested claims and investigations whose precise findings should be dated. The outcome of the siege did not settle the status of the prisoners or the circumstances of individual deaths. It began a further history of captivity, tracing and demands by families for information.

Section 71.16

The Kherson Campaign and Ukrainian Recapture, 2022

Kherson was the only provincial capital Russia took after 24 February, and the right (western) bank of the Dnipro became the most exposed Russian position, since it depended for supply on a few bridges and a dam crossing. From June 2022 Ukraine received American M142 HIMARS multiple-launch rocket systems and similar systems from other allies, which could strike with precision at distances beyond Russian artillery's range. Over July and August Ukraine used them to hit ammunition depots, command posts, and above all the bridges: the Antonivskyi road bridge and the rail bridge nearby were repeatedly struck, and the Kakhovka dam crossing was damaged, which reduced the capacity of Russian forces west of the river to be supplied. Russia added pontoon ferries and temporary crossings, which were also attacked.

Ukraine announced a counteroffensive in the south in late August. Early progress was slow and costly against Russian defenses and mines, but in early October Ukrainian forces broke through north of Kherson, along the Inhulets and Dnipro rivers, and advanced toward the river. On 8 October Russia appointed General Sergei Surovikin to command its forces in Ukraine, and the same day an explosion damaged the Kerch Strait Bridge linking Russia and Crimea (§71.29). Russian-installed authorities ordered civilians to leave the right-bank area from late October, and tens of thousands departed. On 9 November Surovikin told Shoigu on television that withdrawal across the river was the correct decision, and Russian troops left in the following days. Ukrainian forces entered Kherson city on 11 November 2022. The Russian Federation continued to describe Kherson as part of Russia in law, an example of the gap between a claim of sovereignty and actual control (§71.33).

The campaign suggests lessons about how modern wars are fought along a river. Interdiction of bridges and supply lines with precision weapons, rather than a frontal attack, made the Russian position untenable at less cost than an assault on fortified lines. Russia's withdrawal was orderly in the sense that it preserved much of its force and equipment across the river, and its forces established a new line along the left bank, reinforced with fortifications; Ukraine did not cross the Dnipro in strength, and the lower river remained the front, with shelling of Kherson city continuing from the east bank for the rest of the war. Ukrainian casualty figures and Russian ones from this campaign are not independently verified.

For the civilians of Kherson, the nine months of occupation left lasting effects. After the city's recovery, Ukrainian prosecutors reported opening hundreds of investigations, including into places where Russian forces or occupation police were alleged to have detained and tortured residents, and the United Nations commission and human rights groups documented cases of enforced disappearance and abuse. Residents also reported the departure of many neighbors, shortages of electricity, water, and heating after the withdrawal, and continued bombardment. Estimates of the city's population vary widely and fell sharply during and after the occupation. The Kherson case therefore combines military, legal, and social history, which the Multiple Perspectives feature below helps to explore.

Ukraine's recapture of Kherson city in November 2022 was the result of a campaign that weakened Russian supply lines across the Dnipro while applying pressure to positions on the western bank. Ukrainian long-range strikes targeted bridges and other crossings, complicating delivery of ammunition and evacuation of equipment. Russia eventually announced withdrawal to the eastern bank, sacrificing a regional capital it had earlier claimed to annex. The withdrawal should be analyzed as a response to operational constraints rather than as proof that Russian forces throughout the south had been defeated. The Dnipro remained a major military obstacle, and fighting continued in areas on both sides.

For Kherson residents, the return of Ukrainian authorities was followed by serious challenges. Mines, damaged infrastructure and the loss of water or electricity complicated recovery, and Russian artillery and drones could still strike the city from across the river. Investigators interviewed residents about occupation-period detention and disappearance, while displaced families considered whether return was safe. This sequence provides a clear example of how territorial recapture does not automatically end civilian exposure to war. The distinction between liberation, restored administration and durable physical security should guide the chapter's language.

Section 71.17

Naval Warfare, Black Sea Access, and Maritime Trade

The Black Sea was a theater of the war from the first day. Russian naval vessels attacked the small, rocky Snake Island on 24 February and its Ukrainian garrison, whose reported radio response to a Russian warship became a popular symbol of defiance, was captured and later exchanged. Russia blockaded Ukrainian ports, which had handled the bulk of the country's grain, oil-seed, and metal exports, and mined the approaches; Ukraine also mined its own coast to deter landings. The blockade threatened Ukrainian revenue and global food supplies, since Ukraine and Russia together accounted for a large share of world wheat, corn, and sunflower oil exports. On 14 April 2022 the cruiser Moskva, flagship of Russia's Black Sea Fleet, sank after an explosion and fire. Ukraine said its Neptune anti-ship missiles struck the ship; Russia said a fire detonated ammunition and the vessel sank in a storm while under tow; American officials said that they believed Ukrainian missiles hit it. The loss was a major blow to Russian prestige and constrained the fleet's operations near the Ukrainian coast. Russia withdrew from Snake Island on 30 June, calling it a gesture of goodwill.

The blockade was partly lifted by the Black Sea Grain Initiative, signed in Istanbul on 22 July 2022 by Ukraine, Russia, Turkey, and the United Nations in parallel agreements. Under the arrangement, commercial ships would transit a corridor to and from three Ukrainian ports, Odesa, Chornomorsk, and Pivdennyi, inspected by a joint coordination center in Istanbul. A separate memorandum between Russia and the UN addressed the export of Russian food and fertilizer, which are not subject to Western sanctions but which Moscow said were hindered by restrictions on finance and shipping. During the initiative's operation, according to UN and Turkish figures, more than a thousand ships carried approximately 33 million tonnes of grain and other foodstuffs. Russia suspended participation briefly in October 2022, resumed it, agreed to extensions in November 2022, March 2023, and May 2023, and then declined to renew the arrangement in July 2023.

After July 2023, Russia declared ships heading for Ukrainian ports to be potential military carriers and struck port facilities in Odesa and on the Danube. Ukraine, meanwhile, adapted: it used naval drones, missiles, and strikes on Russian bases and vessels, including in Sevastopol and Novorossiysk, and by 2024 had forced Russia to relocate much of its fleet from Crimea eastward. Ukrainian officials claimed that a third of the Black Sea Fleet had been destroyed or disabled by early 2024, a claim not independently verified in full. From August and September 2023 Ukraine established a new corridor hugging the western coast near Romanian and Bulgarian waters, which, despite attacks, carried large volumes of exports in later years; Ukrainian officials reported that cumulative corridor exports exceeded 200 million tonnes.

The maritime war continued to evolve into 2026. Reports in July 2026 stated that Russian attacks on ports and merchant ships had caused shipowners to suspend calls and that the corridor saw no traffic for a time, and Ukraine in turn intensified strikes on Russian ports, grain terminals, and vessels in the Black Sea and the Sea of Azov. According to Reuters reporting of 3 November 2026, drawing on trade data, Russian grain exports through Black Sea and Azov ports fell to about 300,000 tonnes in September 2026 from about 4.7 million a year earlier, and Ukraine's port-based grain exports fell to about 810,000 tonnes from 2.1 million, as both countries sought alternative routes. These figures come from press reports of trade data, not from an audited source, and the situation was changing at the cutoff. The Black Sea war illustrates that economic and naval warfare can affect third countries and markets as well as the belligerents.

The sinking of the cruiser Moskva in April 2022 and subsequent Ukrainian attacks highlighted the vulnerability of surface vessels operating within reach of modern missiles and intelligence-supported targeting. The withdrawal of Russian forces from Snake Island in June 2022 reduced one threat to shipping near Odesa, but commercial navigation remained hazardous because of mines, missiles and risks to ports. Ukraine's later deployment of unmanned maritime systems developed this asymmetrical pattern. To assess the campaign, students should distinguish a navy's total order of battle from the practical routes and missions its ships could still perform in particular waters.

Trade data require equal caution. The UN-supervised Black Sea Grain Initiative enabled more than 32 million metric tons of foodstuffs to leave selected Ukrainian ports before its termination in July 2023, but not every shipment went directly to a food-insecure country. Global price effects, food aid and commercial export volumes were related but separate categories. Ukraine's subsequent corridor depended on a shifting combination of coastal security, insurance and port operations. Reports on monthly grain volumes in 2026 should specify the exact commodity, month and source; they do not establish that every difference arose from naval action alone.

Section 71.18

The Zaporizhzhia Nuclear Power Plant, Kakhovka Dam, and Environmental Risks

The Zaporizhzhia Nuclear Power Plant, at Enerhodar on the Dnipro's left bank, is the largest nuclear station in Europe, with six VVER-1000 reactors and a capacity of about six gigawatts before the war. Russian forces attacked the area on 3 March 2022 and took control on the morning of 4 March after a fire broke out in a training building during shelling, causing international alarm. The plant's Ukrainian staff remained at work under Russian control, which the International Atomic Energy Agency and the Ukrainian operator, Energoatom, described as operating under pressure and in violation of safe-operation principles. Russia stated in October 2022 that the plant was federal property and placed it under the Russian atomic-energy corporation Rosatom, a step not recognized by Ukraine, the IAEA, or other governments. Shelling and drone strikes in the area, for which Russia and Ukraine blamed each other, repeatedly damaged power lines and facilities, and the plant lost external power on multiple occasions, forcing reliance on emergency diesel generators.

The IAEA, led by Director General Rafael Grossi, sent a mission to the plant on 1 September 2022 and has since kept experts on site, issuing reports on the "seven pillars" of nuclear safety and security that it said were compromised. By 2024 the reactors had been placed in shutdown states, reducing the immediate risk, although the plant required continued cooling and electric power for safety. The IAEA has not been in a position to assign blame for most attacks on the area. A reasonable historical account therefore distinguishes confirmed facts, such as Russian control, repeated power losses, and documented damage, from disputed attributions and from the possibilities of a radiological accident that did not occur.

The plant's cooling water came from the reservoir behind the Kakhovka dam, further down the Dnipro, which Russian forces had held since 24 February 2022. In the early hours of 6 June 2023 the dam was breached, releasing the reservoir across a wide area of Kherson province. Flooding inundated dozens of settlements on both banks, destroyed homes, forced the evacuation of thousands, and caused deaths whose full number is not known, particularly in the occupied area. Drinking water supplies and irrigation systems failed over wide areas of southern Ukraine and Crimea; the flood spread pollutants from industrial and agricultural sites and destroyed habitats; and the draining reservoir ended a major source of cooling water for the plant, which relied thereafter on other sources. Ukrainian authorities described it as the worst ecological disaster in the country since the Chornobyl catastrophe of 1986. Independent researchers have noted damage extending to the Black Sea.

Responsibility for the breach is analyzed in the Historical Debate below. More broadly, the nuclear plant and the dam illustrate how the war placed critical civilian infrastructure at risk. International humanitarian law gives special protection to dams and nuclear installations because of the potentially catastrophic consequences of their destruction. The Chornobyl exclusion zone was also occupied between February and the end of March 2022; Russian soldiers dug trenches in contaminated ground, according to Ukrainian reports and observers, and radiation monitoring was disrupted. The evidence for health effects on soldiers has been disputed and remains limited. The environmental history of the war, including mines, burned forests, polluted rivers, and the disruption of agriculture, is still being documented.

The Zaporizhzhia Nuclear Power Plant's six reactors and associated cooling systems posed unusual wartime dangers because an operating or shut-down nuclear installation still requires safety functions, reliable electricity and trained staff. The IAEA deployed experts to the plant in 2022 and repeatedly reported problems involving external power lines, the condition of equipment and the difficult situation of personnel working under occupation. A reactor being shut down is not equivalent to a site that can be neglected; spent fuel, cooling circuits and emergency systems still require maintenance. A chronological study should distinguish a loss of external power, a reactor-status change and a confirmed release of radioactive material rather than treating them as interchangeable incidents.

When the Kakhovka Dam was destroyed on June 6, 2023, water levels in its reservoir fell and the plant had to rely on remaining cooling reserves and alternative sources. The IAEA documented the plant's adaptations, while other investigations examined the physical cause of the dam's destruction. Later seismic and structural analyses supported an explosive mechanism; assigning responsibility to an individual person or military command requires additional evidence. The environmental and humanitarian consequences—flooding, water-supply interruption, damage to farmland and displacement—can be documented without assuming that every contested attribution has been conclusively established.

Historical Debate

Who Destroyed the Kakhovka Dam?

The question: Who caused the breach of 6 June 2023, and how can responsibility be established without access to the site? This is a question of attribution on the evidence, not a long-running scholarly controversy, and it should be stated as such.

The Russian government's position: Russian officials, including Putin, said that Ukraine destroyed the dam, describing the act as a means to escalate the conflict and to hinder Russian operations, and denied that Russian forces had done so.
The Ukrainian government's position and independent analyses: Ukraine said that Russian forces, which controlled the structure, destroyed it with explosives. A team of international lawyers working with Ukraine's prosecutors concluded in preliminary findings in June 2023 that it was highly likely Russian forces deliberately destroyed the dam with pre-placed explosives, and described it as potentially a war crime. A New York Times analysis of seismic data, satellite images, and expert opinion also suggested an internal explosion, and a joint investigation by Radio Free Europe/Radio Liberty and Slidstvo.info identified Russian military units that had controlled the dam. Engineers consulted in press reports considered failure from erosion alone unlikely.

Evidence: Seismic records, satellite imagery, the structure's engineering, communications intercepts, and the identity of the controlling force. No court had issued a final judgment and no international body with access to the site had published a definitive finding in the sources consulted for this edition.

Points of agreement: The dam was in Russian-held territory on 6 June 2023; it failed catastrophically; and the consequences were severe for civilians and the environment.

Points of disagreement: The cause of the breach, as asserted by the two governments, and the degree of confidence of independent analyses; the legal characterization depends on findings not yet made by a court.

The Eastern War, Counteroffensives, and Attrition, 2022–2023

Section 71.19

The Donbas Offensive and the Battles of Sievierodonetsk and Lysychansk

After the withdrawal from the north, Russia concentrated its offensive on the Donbas. From April 2022, Russian forces attacked from the north, along the line from Izium toward Sloviansk and Kramatorsk, and from the east and south, with the aim of encircling Ukrainian troops in Luhansk and Donetsk provinces. The campaign relied on massed artillery, an area where Russia held a substantial advantage in guns and ammunition; Ukrainian officials said during the summer that Russian artillery outnumbered theirs by a large ratio. On 8 April a missile struck the Kramatorsk railway station, where thousands of civilians waited to evacuate, killing dozens; Ukraine and Human Rights Watch attributed the attack to Russian forces, and Russia denied responsibility.

The principal battle of the campaign was fought for Sievierodonetsk and its twin city of Lysychansk, on opposite banks of the Siverskyi Donets River. Popasna fell in early May, and Russian forces then entered Sievierodonetsk at the end of the month. Fighting lasted into late June, with street combat and bombardment destroying most of the city; civilians sheltered at the Azot chemical plant. Ukrainian forces withdrew from Sievierodonetsk in late June and from Lysychansk on 3 July, in order, Ukrainian commanders said, to avoid encirclement. Russia announced that it had taken control of the whole of Luhansk province, though small areas remained in Ukrainian hands.

The costs on both sides were high. Ukrainian officials publicly acknowledged losses in the range of dozens to hundreds of soldiers killed or wounded each day during the summer fighting, and Russian casualties were also heavy, although neither side published verified figures. Russia gained ground at the cost of slow progress and exhaustion, and after Lysychansk's capture it paused its offensive for about two weeks, a pause analysts attributed to fatigue and the need to regroup. The campaign indicated that Russian forces, having failed at rapid maneuver, could make slow gains through concentrated firepower, but at a cost that limited further advance.

The balance began to change in July with the arrival of HIMARS and other Western artillery systems. Ukraine used them to strike ammunition depots and command posts behind the front lines, which forced Russian forces to move their stocks farther back and reduced the volume of their fire. By mid-2022 Ukrainian officials estimated that Russia controlled about a fifth of Ukraine's territory, including Crimea and the parts of Donbas occupied since 2014. Sloviansk and Kramatorsk, the principal Ukrainian-held cities of Donetsk province, remained under Ukrainian control and under bombardment, and the front shifted to other sectors.

The battles for Sievierodonetsk and Lysychansk marked Russia's summer 2022 push to occupy remaining Ukrainian-held positions in Luhansk oblast. Artillery dominated the fighting, while rivers and bridges shaped the defenders' ability to resupply or withdraw. Russian forces made important territorial gains, but the costs and tempo limited the idea of a swift breakthrough toward all of Donetsk oblast. Ukrainian commanders faced decisions about whether continued resistance imposed useful attrition or risked encirclement. These decisions should be evaluated using evidence available at the time, not from the assumption that a later withdrawal proves earlier defense had been futile.

For civilians, the fighting destroyed housing and public infrastructure and reduced opportunities to evacuate safely. Some remained in basements or shelters as water and electricity failed, while humanitarian access became increasingly dangerous. The fate of these communities cannot be represented by the dates when a city changed military control. Roads, workplaces, records and family connections were damaged for long periods. The eastern campaign also demonstrated why an army could capture a city and still face an entirely new defensive problem in the next settlement, particularly within the interconnected industrial geography of the Donbas.

Section 71.20

Ukraine's Kharkiv Counteroffensive, September 2022

In the first days of September 2022, while attention focused on a Ukrainian offensive toward Kherson in the south, Ukrainian forces launched a second operation in Kharkiv province. Beginning on 6 September near Balakliia, they attacked a sector held by thinly manned Russian and Rosgvardia units, supported by artillery and reconnaissance, and broke the line within days. Russian forces collapsed or withdrew: Balakliia was retaken on 8 September, and Ukrainian troops reached Kupiansk and Izium, key rail and logistics hubs of the Russian forces, on 10 September. By 12 September Zelenskyy said that Ukraine had recaptured more than 6,000 square kilometers. The Russian Ministry of Defense described the withdrawal as a planned regrouping to strengthen the Donetsk direction. Ukrainian forces continued to advance, and on 1 October they entered Lyman, an important rail hub in Donetsk province.

Why was the offensive so effective? The evidence indicates that Ukraine achieved surprise, in part by the feint in the south; that Russian defenses in the northeast were poorly manned because forces had been moved to Kherson and to the Donbas; that Ukrainian forces used mobility and a well-prepared attack; and that Russian commanders were slow to reinforce. Ukraine captured large quantities of Russian equipment. The operation showed that the front was not static, that Russian forces were vulnerable where their numbers were low, and that Ukraine, supplied with Western weapons and intelligence, could conduct operational maneuver.

The liberated areas yielded evidence of occupation practices. In Izium, Ukrainian authorities discovered a burial site of more than four hundred graves in a forest, and exhumations found bodies that, officials said, included civilians and some with signs of torture. UN investigators and the Independent International Commission of Inquiry subsequently documented cases of unlawful killings and torture in Kharkiv province, and detention sites with evidence of abuse were found in several towns. Residents reported that they had lived without regular supplies, that Russian soldiers had looted, and that people suspected of connections to the Ukrainian army were detained. The accounts come from survivors, investigators, and forensic teams and are corroborated in many cases by physical evidence, although the total number of victims is not established.

The Russian reaction was swift and consequential. Prominent Russian nationalist commentators and bloggers criticized the military leadership; the Kremlin ordered a partial mobilization on 21 September (§71.40), announced referendums in the occupied territories (§71.33), and, in October, began a sustained campaign of missile and drone attacks on Ukraine's energy infrastructure (§71.21). The Kharkiv counteroffensive thus ended the idea that Russia could consolidate quickly its gains in the east, and began a new phase in which both sides expanded their forces, defenses, and strikes. The Timeline Study that follows summarizes the first year.

The September 2022 Ukrainian offensive in Kharkiv oblast demonstrated the value of surprise, concentration and Russian defensive weakness. Ukrainian forces broke through vulnerable positions and advanced toward Balakliia, Kupiansk and Izium, disrupting Russian supply lines and compelling withdrawal from extensive territory. The speed contrasted with the slower fighting elsewhere and showed that positional warfare had not made maneuver impossible everywhere. Yet reports of collapsing enemy formations should not be generalized to the entire Russian army. The operation exploited a particular distribution of forces and local vulnerabilities at a specific moment.

The recovery of occupied settlements brought documentation of detention sites, damaged homes and disrupted services. Ukrainian forces and civilian officials had to clear mines, restore administration and collect testimony while the military situation remained active nearby. Russian mobilization later in September was shaped by multiple political and military pressures, including the losses around Kharkiv and continuing challenges in the south. A historian should avoid claiming a single battlefield reversal mechanically caused every later Kremlin decision. The counteroffensive's enduring lesson lies in the interaction of military surprise, institutional preparedness and political consequences, not simply the dramatic speed with which lines moved on maps.

Section 71.21

The Autumn 2022 Campaign Against Ukraine's Energy Infrastructure

The campaign against Ukraine's electrical system began on a new scale on 10 October 2022, when Russia launched coordinated missiles at Kyiv and numerous other cities two days after an explosion damaged the Kerch Strait Bridge. Earlier strikes had affected energy installations, but the autumn attacks developed into a sustained effort against generation, transmission substations, distribution networks and the equipment that made an integrated grid function. Russian officials characterized the operations as attacks on infrastructure supporting Ukraine's war effort and as retaliation for Ukrainian actions. For millions of civilians, the distinction between an electrical asset and its military use was not abstract: a transformer damaged hundreds of kilometers from a front line could interrupt heating, water pumping, telecommunications, rail services, medical equipment and everyday work during winter. The campaign also exposed the limits of Russia's remaining precision missiles, the availability of Iranian-designed drones and Ukraine's developing air-defense network.

Ukraine's national grid, managed by Ukrenergo, connected nuclear, hydroelectric, thermal and renewable generation to an extensive transmission system inherited from the Soviet period. A strike against a high-voltage substation could prevent electricity produced elsewhere from reaching cities, even if the generating plant itself survived. Specialist transformers were difficult to replace rapidly, and repairs sometimes required carefully planned shutdowns. Engineers improvised around damaged links, separated sections of the grid and appealed for high-voltage equipment from European partners. The country's integration with the continental European electricity network in March 2022, accelerated under emergency conditions, made cross-border assistance and trade possible, although interconnection capacity could not simply replace domestic generation. This infrastructure geography explains why a smaller number of well-selected strikes could disrupt services for very large populations.

As the weather cooled, authorities introduced scheduled and emergency outages. Households charged batteries whenever power returned and organized heating or water deliveries in apartment blocks where pumps stopped. Shops relied on generators; hospitals prioritized operating theaters and intensive care; local governments opened publicly accessible heated spaces, sometimes called Points of Invincibility. A family in one apartment tower might retain heat from centralized supply yet lose electricity, while another might lose running water because pumps depended on the same substation. The distinction between inconvenience and life-threatening exposure depended on building design, disabilities, household income, access to alternative fuel and the duration of repairs. Volunteer networks distributed generators and warm clothing, but their capacity differed sharply among cities and rural districts. Repeated alerts forced workers and schoolchildren to reorganize their days, extending the war's cost beyond the front.

The campaign also became a contest between offensive adaptation and air defense. Russia employed Kh-101 and other cruise missiles, ballistic missiles, and large numbers of one-way attack drones commonly identified as Shahed-family systems, while Ukrainian forces used surface-to-air missiles, aircraft, mobile anti-aircraft teams and progressively more electronic countermeasures. A high reported interception rate did not necessarily mean the grid was safe: one successful strike on a critical transformer could produce disproportionate damage. Conversely, Russian expenditure of expensive missiles could limit future strike options, especially when launches had to be coordinated across distant bases. Both governments released figures about launches and interceptions that could not always be independently confirmed in real time. The more reliable historical account combines official notices with satellite imagery, grid operators' reports, physical damage assessments and verified civilian casualty investigations.

International humanitarian law does not classify every electrical installation as immune from attack, nor does an assertion that a power system serves soldiers automatically make all of it a legitimate target. A particular object must qualify as a military objective under the relevant legal test; attackers must distinguish military from civilian objectives, consider expected incidental harm and apply precautions. When a grid serves hospitals and homes as well as the military, proportionality and foreseeable reverberating effects matter. The International Criminal Court's 2024 warrants concerning strikes on Ukrainian electricity infrastructure cited alleged attacks on civilian objects, excessive incidental harm and crimes against humanity. Warrants represent judicial findings of reasonable grounds for alleged criminal responsibility, not convictions. They are important retrospective evidence about the same 2022–2023 campaign, but the chapter must not silently treat an allegation as a final judicial verdict.

The first winter demonstrated both Ukraine's engineering resilience and its vulnerability. Repairs repeatedly restored electricity, yet each new wave could undo earlier work, and damage accumulated in equipment whose replacement was costly. The economic effects spread to production schedules, refrigeration, transportation and public revenue. In later winters Russia refined its attacks, and the 2025–2026 and autumn 2026 campaigns again targeted energy, making this more than a single retaliatory episode. The relevant historical comparison is between a state's capacity to damage distributed civilian systems and a society's capacity to repair them under continuing threat. In either case, the outcome cannot be judged from a headline about numbers of missiles alone; services actually delivered, harm to civilians and the ability to sustain repairs provide the better measures.

Section 71.22

Fortifications, Artillery, and the Transition Toward Positional Warfare

The failure of a quick Russian victory in early 2022 and Ukraine's counteroffensives that autumn changed the operational problem for both sides. Fast movement depended on breaking through defenses, maintaining supplies, coordinating armor with infantry and exploiting openings before the defender could react. By late 2022 large portions of the front were covered by trenches, firing positions, obstacles and artillery observation networks. Neither army had unlimited trained infantry, ammunition or engineering equipment, and newly mobilized personnel often required time to acquire field skills. The transformation toward positional warfare was not a simple return to 1914: satellites, precision fires, commercial drones, electronic warfare and digital communications changed how exposed troops could be detected and attacked. Nonetheless, the old problem of advancing across an observed, mined and defended field returned with great force.

Terrain helped produce this result. The rolling agricultural land of southern Ukraine provided long fields of fire but limited concealment, especially when trees lost foliage. Roads and river crossings created logistical bottlenecks, while villages, industrial complexes, railway embankments and mine workings offered ready-made defensive positions. In the Donbas, dense towns and factories formed a chain of mutually supporting strongpoints. On the Zaporizhzhia front, Russia had time after 2022 to prepare multiple defensive belts with anti-tank ditches, trenches, concrete obstacles and extensive minefields. The popular term 'Surovikin line' captured only part of a larger system with positions in depth, artillery targets and mobile reserves. A line on a map did not reveal its strength; local preparation, engineers, communications and available troops determined whether it actually impeded an attack.

Artillery remained a principal cause of military losses. Russia initially enjoyed advantages in large stocks of tubes and shells, while Ukraine depended increasingly on Western ammunition supply and the efficiency of counter-battery fire. The arrival of HIMARS and related precision systems in 2022 enabled Ukraine to strike command posts and ammunition depots behind Russian lines, pressing Russian logistics to disperse. Russia in turn adapted depot placement, electronic interference and fire-control techniques. Shell counts were frequently used as evidence of advantage, but the effects of fire varied according to target acquisition, accuracy, barrel wear, maintenance, munition quality and the ability of infantry to exploit damage. Drones shortened the time between spotting a position and firing on it. This combination could make even a modest troop movement costly if concealment failed.

Mines, an especially formidable barrier in 2023, required coordination between reconnaissance, breaching vehicles, infantry, artillery suppression and protection against attack from above. Minefields could be laid and renewed rapidly; unexploded ordnance complicated safe movement for decades after combat. Armored vehicles clearing lanes were visible and vulnerable. Breaching operations demanded specialized equipment often difficult to replace, and an unsuccessful first attempt could reveal the axis of a second. Ukrainian operations in 2023 showed how a defender with observation, mines and reserves could prevent a mechanically superior attacker from producing a decisive breakthrough. Russian attacks against prepared Ukrainian strongpoints faced parallel difficulties, although Russia sometimes accepted high attrition in exchange for small gains. Neither success nor failure can be explained by a single weapon type.

An attritional war places unusual demands on military institutions. Units must rotate soldiers, train replacements, maintain weapons, evacuate wounded, repair vehicles, manage morale and retain enough experienced noncommissioned officers to integrate newcomers. A battalion nominally present at the front may possess far less combat power after months of casualties and fatigue. Commanders may be tempted to hold a threatened position for political symbolism or abandon it early to preserve troops; the merits depend on intelligence and alternatives available at the time. Operational maps alone conceal these decisions. So do casualty announcements, which governments have strong reasons to manipulate. The historian must compare staffing, logistical capacity, repair systems, medical evacuation and the frequency of successful coordinated operations, rather than treating every kilometer gained as decisive.

Positional warfare also shifted risk to civilians who lived near fixed fronts. Villages became launch sites, supply points or evacuation corridors; farmlands were mined, homes abandoned, and access to water interrupted. Russian and Ukrainian authorities issued evacuation notices, but some elderly or disabled residents could not leave easily or refused to abandon property. The line of contact was not stable in the human sense merely because it moved slowly on military maps. Small drones, artillery and aerial bombs could make daily life increasingly dangerous even while territorial change remained slight. The prolonged war thus linked military attrition to depopulation, environmental damage and the gradual erosion of local institutions. Understanding that connection is essential to explaining why civilians experienced a continuously evolving crisis when the front appeared nearly stationary.

Section 71.23

The Battle of Bakhmut and the Campaign of 2022–2023

Bakhmut, a city in Donetsk oblast that had been an important road and railway junction, became the site of one of the war's longest and most destructive battles. Russian attacks increased during the second half of 2022 as Moscow sought to consolidate its hold on the Donbas after losses around Kharkiv and Kherson. Ukrainian forces defended urban districts and nearby heights; Wagner's private military organization became prominent in Russian assaults, alongside regular army formations and artillery. The fighting gradually destroyed residential streets, industrial buildings and public infrastructure. Russian accounts described Bakhmut as a necessary stage toward the larger Ukrainian-held cities of Sloviansk and Kramatorsk. Ukrainian leaders argued that continued defense imposed heavy costs and absorbed forces Russia might otherwise employ elsewhere. These rival objectives changed during the battle and should not be projected unchanged back onto its beginning.

The approach to Bakhmut involved smaller settlements and difficult geography. Fighting near Soledar, captured by Russian forces in January 2023, increased pressure from the north. Roads used to resupply Bakhmut passed through areas exposed to artillery fire, and both sides tried to control heights and routes leading toward Chasiv Yar. Urban defense offered cover but also placed troops in buildings subject to bombardment and collapse. Wagner forces used assault detachments, sometimes drawing on large numbers of recruited prisoners, to probe positions repeatedly, while regular Russian units provided supporting fires and attempted to secure flanks. The description 'human-wave attacks,' common in journalistic accounts, should be examined rather than assumed to describe every operation; smaller assault groups, artillery preparation and rapid replacement of losses better capture the changing tactics documented by military observers.

For the Ukrainian army, holding Bakhmut raised a hard operational question. Defending the city consumed ammunition, experienced infantry and command attention at a time when Ukraine was preparing counteroffensive operations elsewhere. Ukrainian officers and outside analysts debated whether withdrawal before encirclement would preserve combat capability, while others argued that the defenders tied down large Russian formations and inflicted attrition that mattered for later operations. Much of the debate suffers from incomplete casualty figures on both sides. Assertions that one army lost an exact number of soldiers cannot be accepted without a verifiable methodology. A serious assessment needs to consider who incurred losses, how quickly formations could be rebuilt, what strategic objectives were delayed, and whether the battle impaired or strengthened the defender's ability to conduct subsequent campaigns.

Russian forces declared Bakhmut captured in May 2023 after the final Ukrainian withdrawal from the urban center, though Ukrainian units remained active on its flanks. The city had suffered catastrophic physical destruction and massive displacement. Wagner leader Yevgeny Prigozhin publicly accused senior Russian military officials of failing to supply ammunition and of mismanaging the wider campaign, revealing tensions between an armed organization with its own recruitment and publicity machinery and the Ministry of Defense. The battle became a platform for political rivalry as well as a military operation. Weeks after Bakhmut fell, Wagner's June 2023 rebellion demonstrated that disputes between battlefield commanders and central authority could threaten the system supporting the war. The connection is important, but direct causation must be reconstructed rather than assumed from the chronological sequence.

The history of Bakhmut should preserve the distinction between operational and symbolic significance. The city was a transport node, but Russian occupation did not automatically open the way to a swift advance across the fortified urban belt farther west. Conversely, Ukraine's organized withdrawal did not mean that every month of defense lacked military value. Different actors evaluated the battle through local tactical gains, relative losses, public morale and the war's diplomatic setting. Russian media celebrated a costly victory, while Ukrainian commemoration emphasized endurance and sacrifice. These narratives are themselves evidence about each society's wartime needs, but they cannot replace examination of force dispositions and actual consequences. Later scholarship may revise judgments as operational records become available.

The civilian history was larger than the fate of the military garrison. Many residents fled as utilities failed and shelling intensified. Those who remained included older persons, people without transport, and citizens unwilling to abandon homes. Access to bread, medicine, clean water and medical care deteriorated. Rescue personnel operated under fire, while burial records and casualty estimates remained incomplete. Photographs of devastated streets convey scale but not the detailed sequence of individual deaths. Investigators must link images to locations, dates and testimony, and avoid assuming a camera recorded the entire history of a place. Bakhmut became a concise illustration of the prolonged war: tactical objectives continually redefined, enormous expenditure of resources, contested claims about victory, and a civilian landscape transformed beyond easy repair.

Section 71.24

Ukraine's 2023 Counteroffensive and Russian Defensive Systems

Ukraine's summer 2023 counteroffensive was prepared amid considerable expectations. Western governments had delivered armored vehicles, infantry fighting vehicles, air-defense systems and training, and Ukrainian command sought to exploit the earlier success around Kharkiv. The main southern effort was directed toward Russian-held territory in Zaporizhzhia, with fighting around Orikhiv and the approaches toward Tokmak; additional operations occurred along the southern Donetsk front and around Bakhmut. The strategic aim was generally understood as disrupting Russia's land corridor to occupied Crimea, which depended on roads and railways running through the south. Preparations took place while Russia constructed extensive fortifications. Public commentary sometimes described a potential breakthrough as imminent, but the operational problem was far harder than the symbolic map arrows suggested.

The counteroffensive began in early June with attacks that encountered dense minefields, artillery, anti-tank weapons, attack helicopters and well-prepared Russian infantry. Several Ukrainian assault formations suffered losses of armored vehicles while trying to breach defensive belts under observation. The outcome encouraged tactical changes, including greater use of dismounted infantry, smaller movements and deliberate clearing of positions. Such adaptation could reduce some risks while slowing the pace of advance. Russian forces were not passive: they moved reserves, mined new areas, launched counterattacks and used drones for reconnaissance and targeting. A photograph of disabled vehicles could show a real local setback without establishing the fate of an entire campaign. Likewise, reports that a village had been recaptured should be placed in the context of remaining defensive positions and supply routes.

Ukrainian forces achieved some localized gains, including a difficult advance toward Robotyne, but they did not reach the strategically significant transport junction at Tokmak or cut the land corridor to Crimea. Along the southern Donetsk axis, Ukrainian units also made limited gains before their progress slowed. Around Bakhmut, local offensive operations put Russian troops under pressure but did not reverse the broader outcome of the spring battle. Analysts disagree over the precise distribution of responsibility for the limited results: the density of Russian defenses, delays in equipment delivery, shortages of air support and mine-clearing machinery, insufficient experienced personnel, operational decisions, and difficult coordination all feature in assessments. No credible single-factor explanation accounts for the campaign across every sector.

Air superiority is an essential part of this question. Ukraine had no large, modern air force capable of suppressing Russian aircraft and air defenses over the battlefield. Russian helicopters and glide bombs threatened assault groups, while ground-based systems constrained Ukrainian aircraft. Western armored vehicles were designed to operate as elements of coordinated formations that could obtain air support, engineering assistance and sustained logistics. Delivering vehicles alone did not reproduce those conditions. Precision missiles and drones offered alternatives for attacking depots or command posts but could not occupy territory or clear minefields. This distinction between material aid and the complete operational system required to employ it became central to later debates about Western assistance and Ukrainian military planning.

The campaign's failure to achieve its principal strategic objectives did not end the war or make Russian victory inevitable. Ukraine preserved much of its armed force and continued striking logistics and naval targets, while Russia retained extensive positions in the south and increasingly pressed Ukrainian defenders in eastern Ukraine. By autumn both sides had adapted to methods that favored concealment, smaller assaults and continuous drone surveillance. Western governments faced renewed arguments about ammunition, training, financial support and the feasibility of rapid territorial recovery. Russian authorities presented the defense as proof of their forces' resilience, but wartime claims about enemy losses remained particularly unreliable. Historians should reconstruct the campaign from a chronology of verified engagements, geolocated evidence and attributable testimony, not just competing post-battle narratives.

There was also a human geography to the counteroffensive. Soldiers traversed fields and roads already contaminated with mines and explosive remnants from previous fighting, and clearance teams sometimes worked for days to open small safe paths. Civilians living near the front encountered more evacuation orders and renewed danger after months of relative stasis. Agricultural land became hazardous for planting and harvesting, affecting local incomes and future food production. Mines and unexploded ordnance will require extensive clearance independent of the ultimate territorial settlement. The 2023 operations therefore cannot be summarized only by territory gained or lost. Their lasting history includes military adaptation, the exposure of industrial and logistical constraints, and long-term damage to the land that sustained rural communities.

Section 71.25

The Military Balance at the End of 2023

At the close of 2023 the military balance differed sharply from what many governments had expected at the invasion's start. Ukraine had prevented Russia from occupying Kyiv, regained large areas in the northeast and south during 2022, and damaged Russian naval operations in the Black Sea. Russia, however, still occupied substantial parts of southern and eastern Ukraine and had spent 2023 strengthening its positions, increasing production and recruitment, and absorbing the impact of sanctions. Neither side had achieved its original maximal objectives. This was not a frozen conflict: artillery exchanges, small-scale assaults, missile strikes and attacks on infrastructure continued, even as major changes to the front line became less frequent. A historical assessment must distinguish a military stalemate from a diplomatic settlement, and in 2023 there was no settlement.

Manpower was a central constraint. Russia's September 2022 mobilization and subsequent contract recruitment enlarged the pool from which Moscow could replace losses, albeit with widely varying training and equipment. Ukraine relied on repeated mobilization, volunteers and an increasingly stretched set of professional units. Experienced officers, specialists, medics and maintenance crews were not easily replaced merely by registering more personnel. Both governments controlled many casualty figures, and public estimates differed greatly according to whether they counted killed, wounded, missing or permanently incapacitated. The absence of a transparent count does not justify describing losses as insignificant; it requires explicit categories and careful attribution. The ability to rotate exhausted troops and organize training became as consequential as the number of men nominally under arms.

Material resources also changed the balance. Russia had substantial stocks of Soviet-design artillery and vehicles, domestic ammunition factories and access to external supplies, though equipment quality varied and some older systems required extensive refurbishment. Ukraine depended on its own military industry as well as international deliveries of artillery shells, air-defense interceptors, armored vehicles and maintenance components. Western ammunition production was not immediately scaled to the consumption of an extended land war. At several points Ukrainian units faced shortages of particular munitions, while Russia suffered difficulties with drones, precision systems and electronic components and adapted through imports, substitution and redesign. Quantitative comparisons must therefore be dated and specified: an annual production estimate is not the same as usable shells arriving at a front-line battery.

Strategic geography helped explain why neither army could convert small tactical successes into a rapid victory. Ukraine's fortified urban belt in Donetsk oblast, with roads and industrial sites connecting defensive positions, imposed repeated urban operations on attackers. Rivers and broken bridges constrained movement; broad agricultural spaces exposed armor; mined belts delayed mechanized forces. Russian positions in the south also covered transport corridors important to Crimea, while Ukrainian ability to strike bridges, depots and naval assets threatened Russian supply lines. The two armies understood these facts and revised priorities accordingly. Maps showing a continuous colored front are simplifying devices: they conceal contested villages, infiltration, temporary occupation of trenches and zones between stable positions where neither side exercised dependable control.

International politics mattered to the military picture. Ukraine's ability to continue fighting depended in part on decisions by the United States, European governments and other partners to supply finance and weapons. Russia's endurance reflected its own state capacity and relationships that provided ammunition, technology, energy revenues and opportunities to evade or adapt to sanctions. These were not fixed advantages. Legislative disputes, industrial production schedules, shipping routes and currency markets could alter front-line outcomes months later. The question of how long each state could sustain a protracted conflict increasingly displaced predictions of a short decisive campaign. It also made military planning dependent on assumptions about the political will of governments outside the battlefield.

By December 2023 there was sufficient evidence to identify these structural conditions, but not to forecast the war's outcome. A modest Russian advance later in the year did not prove that Ukraine must eventually collapse; the failure of Ukraine's summer counteroffensive did not prove that Ukrainian resistance had run its course. Historical explanation should analyze choices under uncertainty. Commanders faced incomplete reconnaissance, governments lacked full knowledge of opponents' reserves, and outside observers disagreed about how to weigh population size, industrial capacity and social cohesion. The section therefore closes with a measured conclusion: the war had become a sustained competition of military institutions, industrial systems and social endurance, with local campaigns continuing to determine how those resources were spent.

Section 71.26

Avdiivka and Russia's Renewed Advances in 2024

Avdiivka, a fortified city immediately north of occupied Donetsk, had been contested since the war that began in 2014. Ukrainian positions there protected approaches to neighboring towns and lay close to Russian-held territory, making the city both militarily useful and politically symbolic. Russia intensified assaults in autumn 2023, applying artillery, aerial bombs, armored attacks and infantry pressure against a salient whose supply routes increasingly came under fire. Russian units sought to envelop the defenders rather than win every position by direct frontal attack. The Ukrainian garrison faced a growing shortage of ammunition and difficulty rotating exhausted troops. In February 2024 commander-in-chief Oleksandr Syrskyi ordered a withdrawal; Russia announced control of the city on 17 February. The evacuation occurred under pressure and not every Ukrainian soldier escaped or was accounted for immediately.

The battle illustrated a pattern that would recur: Russia assembled superior local firepower and accepted heavy losses to obtain a town after months of fighting, while Ukraine traded space for opportunities to preserve forces. The relative costs cannot be expressed confidently by a single casualty ratio from either side's official statements. Russian forces used glide bombs launched by aircraft from beyond many Ukrainian air-defense positions; Ukrainian observers described these as particularly destructive against fixed fortifications. Drones gave attackers improved opportunities to locate supply vehicles and personnel, limiting night movement that had once been safer. Yet the capture of Avdiivka did not itself open an unobstructed route to the whole of Donetsk oblast. Surrounding fields, villages and new defensive lines still had to be contested.

After Avdiivka, Russian forces advanced westward through a series of villages, while Ukrainian defenders attempted to establish more defensible lines and protect transport routes farther behind the front. The advance was uneven, shaped by local troop availability and whether fortifications had been completed. Other Russian attacks continued around Chasiv Yar, near the important high ground west of Bakhmut, and toward the industrial town of Toretsk. Ukraine's shortages of artillery rounds and interceptors were sharpened by delays in American assistance during early 2024; the U.S. Congress approved a new major security package in April. Delivery and deployment of ammunition still took time, so a legislative decision did not immediately resolve a battalion's shortage. Russian command sought to exploit this temporary imbalance before supplies arrived.

In parallel, Russian forces increased attacks against Ukrainian energy installations in spring 2024. Power stations and substations were damaged in several regions, adding repair burdens to those accumulated from earlier winters. This pressure affected military industry, civilian life and the government's ability to maintain services. Ukraine answered with attacks on Russian oil facilities and military installations, seeking to limit supplies and impose costs on Russian rear areas. The two campaigns illustrate how a war of attrition extends beyond troops facing one another in trenches: electricity, transport, petroleum processing and industrial production shape the capacity to conduct operations. International governments debated the conditions attached to weapons they supplied, sometimes limiting use across Russia's internationally recognized border even when Russian forces fired from that territory.

The term 'renewed Russian advances' must be understood as a description of changes in tempo, not the recovery of rapid armored maneuver. Military observers following geolocated footage saw gradual Russian gains in portions of Donetsk oblast through 2024, at considerable apparent cost. Open-source mapping could confirm positions occupied by particular units at particular moments but rarely established exact casualties or stable control across entire settlements. Russia also used smaller infantry groups and infiltration rather than relying exclusively on massed armored columns, adapting to widespread drone observation. The defender's exposure to attack during withdrawals made timing difficult, yet attempting to hold an isolated position could be costlier still. These dilemmas were often debated publicly in Ukraine without access to complete operational data.

For civilians the capture and attempted defense of these towns had devastating consequences. Avdiivka had already lost much of its prewar population; remaining residents faced bombardment, damaged housing and severe shortages of basic supplies. Families who evacuated left behind homes that might no longer be accessible, and people under new occupation encountered changed administrative and legal circumstances. Municipal archives, school records and local businesses were disrupted along with physical infrastructure. The chapter should neither infer public preference from the existence of people who stayed nor assume everyone was free to leave. Avdiivka's wider historical significance lies in showing how territorial gains, even limited ones, can require prolonged destruction and can leave the successful attacker responsible for governing a devastated landscape.

Section 71.27

The 2024 Kharkiv Offensive and the Fighting Around Vovchansk

Russia opened a renewed offensive across parts of the international border in Kharkiv oblast on 10 May 2024, directing attacks toward Vovchansk and settlements north of Kharkiv city. Russian officials described a need to create a buffer zone that would push Ukrainian forces and weapons farther from Belgorod oblast, where attacks from Ukraine had caused casualties and destruction. Ukrainian officials described the operation as a diversionary and territorial assault that required forces to be transferred from other threatened sectors. Russian troops crossed at several points and initially captured border villages. The Ukrainian response included reinforcements, artillery, drones, defensive operations in urban areas and emergency civilian evacuation. The frontier's political significance was different from that of the 2022 invasion: both states had already been fighting for more than two years, but a new thrust created immediate uncertainty for settlements that had begun rebuilding.

Vovchansk lay close to the border and offered roads, river crossings and built-up areas that could be used by advancing or defending forces. Fighting rapidly became destructive and localized. Russian units took positions in northern portions of the city and sought to move farther south, while Ukrainian forces defended crossings and streets, often in conditions of poor visibility and heavy aerial bombardment. Official Russian statements about full control of particular settlements sometimes exceeded what independent imagery established. Conversely, Ukrainian claims that attacks had been repelled could refer to one assault while positions elsewhere remained contested. The difference between a village occupied briefly, a military position under fire and an area administered securely is central to accurate maps of a fast-changing border front.

The offensive intersected with the debate over the conditions imposed on Western-supplied weapons. Ukrainian officials argued that restrictions on striking Russian military targets across the border enabled artillery and aircraft to attack Ukrainian settlements with reduced risk. During 2024 some supporting governments modified the circumstances under which their weapons could be used against forces attacking from Russian territory, but restrictions varied by country and system. These changes did not produce a single uniform policy governing Ukraine's entire arsenal. Military effectiveness depended on identifying launch sites, obtaining authorization, having appropriate weapons available and striking before units moved. Russian forces also employed electronic warfare and adapted deployment patterns. As elsewhere, political announcements were easier to date than their battlefield consequences.

Kharkiv city did not fall, and the Russian advance did not achieve a deep breakthrough toward the metropolis in the initial phase. Fighting settled into more limited positional contests around Vovchansk and other sections of the frontier. Russian command nevertheless achieved some of the pressure it sought by compelling Ukraine to allocate reserves and protect a wide border. Whether the expenditure of Russian troops and equipment justified the results remained disputed among military analysts. An offensive may produce operational effects without seizing its largest stated objective, but those effects must be assessed against what the attacker sacrificed elsewhere. The distinction is particularly important in 2024, when Russia was also trying to advance toward Ukrainian strongpoints in Donetsk oblast.

Civilian consequences were immediate. Evacuation buses collected residents from villages near the border, while rescue workers retrieved older people who could not travel alone. Hospitals and shelters faced sudden demands, and some agricultural communities lost access to fields during planting season. The war had already damaged many homes in Kharkiv oblast in 2022; another offensive forced households to weigh the possibility of further displacement against an uncertain ability to return. Missiles and drones also struck Kharkiv city independently of direct ground assaults, creating danger far beyond the contested villages. Stories of school closures, family separation and repeated evacuation reveal why a military map cannot adequately measure the human experience of a renewed campaign.

The episode also illustrates the difference between security arguments and the legality or effectiveness of military action. Russian officials framed the incursion as protecting Belgorod, but such a statement is evidence of the government's rationale and does not alone establish the legality of crossing an international border or occupying foreign territory. Ukrainian authorities invoked their right of defense and responsibility to protect communities. Civilian deaths on either side of the border deserve careful documentation, and casualties reported by authorities in Russian regions should be attributed when outside monitors lack access to verify them. By late 2024 the northern front remained active but had not resolved the wider contest of manpower and resources. Its importance lay as much in the diversion of forces and renewed displacement as in territorial change.

Section 71.28

Ukraine's Kursk Offensive, August 2024, and Cross-Border Warfare

On 6 August 2024 Ukrainian units crossed into Russia's Kursk oblast in a surprise operation that challenged assumptions about the geographical limits of the war. Ukrainian forces moved through border defenses, encountered Russian units and advanced into a number of settlements, including the area around Sudzha. Kyiv initially gave limited public explanation, while Russian authorities acknowledged fighting, mobilized reinforcements and introduced emergency arrangements. The incursion differed from isolated drone strikes and brief raids because Ukrainian regular forces attempted to hold territory within the Russian Federation. At the same time, describing an occupied border district as evidence of control over all of Kursk oblast would be inaccurate. The operation's changing footprint must be reconstructed from dated mapping and clearly differentiated from claims by either command.

Several possible purposes were advanced by Ukrainian officials and commentators: forcing Moscow to redeploy troops from other fronts, disrupting Russian planning for border attacks, demonstrating Ukraine's ability to seize initiative and gaining bargaining leverage. These explanations overlap but should not be treated as proof of a complete operational plan that remains partly undisclosed. Russia characterized the operation as terrorism and an attack on civilians; Ukraine presented it as part of defending against a state that had invaded its territory. International law's rules for protecting civilians, detainees and property apply regardless of which state's internationally recognized territory is involved. The fact that Ukraine entered Russia did not transfer sovereignty over the captured land or create an exemption from humanitarian law.

The geographic and logistical challenges changed as the operation developed. Initial surprise allowed more movement than entrenched fronts in eastern Ukraine, but holding a salient required supplies across a limited number of roads, protection against artillery and aviation, and reserves sufficient to meet counterattacks. Russian forces gradually assembled more formations, improved surveillance and attempted to isolate Ukrainian positions. Ukraine sought to retain useful ground while resisting pressure elsewhere. The operation coincided with renewed Russian activity in the Pokrovsk sector, illustrating that a diversion may not automatically remove enough enemy strength to halt attacks in another theater. Military analysts disagreed over whether the allocation of Ukrainian formations to Kursk represented a valuable initiative or came at excessive cost to the defense of Donetsk oblast.

By autumn and winter, the fighting had become more attritional. Reports of North Korean military personnel supporting Russia brought an additional international dimension, but early descriptions differed in their numbers, command relationships and precise battlefield roles. U.S., South Korean and Ukrainian officials reported deployments; Moscow and Pyongyang's public positions evolved. Later confirmation of participation should be dated to the statement or evidence establishing it rather than silently projected backward into initial unconfirmed reports. The presence of a foreign contingent does not make it possible to attribute every action in an engagement to that contingent. Analysts must examine the different circumstances of regular Russian units, border forces, volunteer formations and foreign soldiers.

Civilian residents in Kursk oblast experienced evacuation, damage to homes, interrupted services and uncertainty over relatives left in contested areas. Russian regional authorities reported civilian casualties and displacement; independent monitors had less access than in many parts of Ukraine, requiring careful attribution. Ukrainian troops operating in occupied settlements were subject to the obligations of a force exercising effective control, and allegations against them also deserve investigation. The historian should avoid equating lack of access with proof of no abuse, or unverified official accounts with established fact. The humanitarian story includes residents who remained, those who fled, and communities that continued to face shelling after front lines shifted.

As Russia progressively contested and reduced the Ukrainian-held salient in subsequent campaigning, the operation's legacy became a matter of strategic debate. A temporary territorial gain could expose weaknesses in Russian border defense, consume Russian reserves and raise Ukrainian morale, yet also tie down Ukrainian units and create new vulnerabilities. Its value depends partly on military consequences elsewhere and on whether any durable negotiating advantage resulted, not solely on the maximum area briefly occupied. The case demonstrates why modern territorial warfare cannot be understood as a simple tally of colored map polygons. It combines surprise, supply, political signaling, civilian harm and the difficult transition from making an incursion to sustaining it.

Section 71.29

Crimea, the Black Sea, and Long-Range Operations in 2024

Crimea remained central to Russian military operations in 2024 even though Ukraine had not mounted a conventional ground campaign onto the peninsula. Russia used the occupied territory for air-defense systems, aircraft, ammunition facilities, naval logistics and connections to southern Ukraine. Ukraine's long-range missiles and unmanned systems aimed to make those facilities less secure and to reduce the freedom of Russian aircraft and ships to operate. Attacks were reported against airfields and air-defense installations, including around Sevastopol and other military sites, but the effects of individual operations often remained difficult to determine independently. Russian authorities reported interceptions and damage; Ukrainian officials described destroyed systems and disrupted logistics. Without geolocated imagery or corroborating evidence, such competing claims should be left attributed rather than converted into a definitive tally.

The Black Sea campaign had already altered the military balance in 2022 and 2023. The sinking of the cruiser Moskva, the battle for Snake Island, strikes on naval facilities and the emergence of Ukrainian explosive surface drones forced Russian ships to adapt their operating patterns. By 2024 Russian units had shifted significant naval assets away from Sevastopol toward ports farther east, including Novorossiysk. This did not mean Russia had ceased to possess a navy or lost every ability to launch missiles from the sea. Rather, the cost and risk of operating near the western Black Sea had increased. Ukraine, a state without a comparable large surface fleet, demonstrated that shore-based missiles, intelligence and unmanned vessels could deny an adversary some uses of maritime space.

Navigation and trade were equally important. After Russia ended participation in the UN- and Turkey-brokered Black Sea Grain Initiative in July 2023, Ukraine developed a separate maritime corridor using ports near Odesa and routes whose risks depended on coastal security and insurance. Merchant vessels still faced mines, missile threats, inspection politics and higher costs. Restored exports were not proof that the sea had become safe in a normal peacetime sense. Shipping volumes also depend on grain harvests, global prices, terminal capacity and weather, so a change in monthly tonnage cannot automatically be attributed to naval action. Statistics for 2025 and 2026 require the month, commodity classification and source to be stated, particularly when reports cover grain and oilseed exports rather than all maritime cargo.

The Kerch Strait Bridge and transport links through occupied southern Ukraine gave Russia alternatives for supplying Crimea. Ukraine attacked the bridge in 2022 and 2023 and targeted depots, ferries and air defenses in later operations, seeking to raise the costs of occupation. Russia improved protection, dispersed valuable equipment, used defensive barriers and adapted shipping routes. Military success at damaging a bridge or radar does not establish that a logistical system ceased functioning for the long term. Repair crews and alternate routes can restore capacity, while repeated attacks may keep the defender committed to expensive protection. The long-term outcome of the campaign should be measured by sustained supply efficiency, freedom of maneuver and the scale of resources absorbed in defense.

The danger to civilians and maritime workers remained a significant part of the story. Air-defense launches and explosions in populated Crimean areas created uncertainty for residents, and Russian authorities imposed information and travel restrictions. Civilian vessels, dockworkers and emergency personnel faced hazards from missiles, drones and explosive devices. International legal questions also arose concerning the status of military targets in occupied territory, the distinction between military and civilian objects, and the obligation to consider foreseeable incidental harm. Ukraine and most states continued to recognize Crimea as Ukrainian territory despite Russian control and annexation claims. A historical narrative must not confuse an attack on a military installation in Crimea with an invasion of internationally recognized Russian territory.

By the end of 2024 the Black Sea campaign offered a broader lesson about asymmetry. A state with fewer ships could impose costs on a larger navy through innovation, intelligence and long-range strike, while the larger naval power could adapt through dispersal, coastal defenses and new tactics. Neither side could assume permanent success. Continued Russian strikes against Ukrainian ports showed that maritime denial remained contested. The campaign connected strategic infrastructure, commercial resilience and international food security with military operations, and it helped explain how the war widened geographically even while the main ground front moved slowly.

Section 71.30

The Eastern and Southern Campaigns of 2025

In 2025 the main ground struggle continued across Donetsk oblast and neighboring areas, where Russia sought to wear down Ukrainian defenses and seize transport centers that connected the eastern front. The geography of Pokrovsk, Toretsk, Chasiv Yar, Kostiantynivka and the wider Sloviansk-Kramatorsk urban belt mattered more than a narrative of uniform advance across an empty map. Roads, railway junctions and industrial settlements provided Ukrainian forces with defensive opportunities and supply routes, but also made them objectives for Russian artillery, infiltration and encirclement attempts. Russian units advanced in some sectors while Ukrainian counterattacks and prepared defenses limited progress in others. Analysts using geolocated imagery documented repeated local changes; these should not be confused with a final verdict on control of an entire district.

The military pattern combined infantry infiltration, drones, aerial bombs and selective concentrations of fire. Russian formations often attacked through small groups that sought concealed approaches, followed by reinforcement if they obtained a foothold. Ukraine increased the use of aerial reconnaissance and small strike drones but continued to face shortages of trained infantry and the difficulty of defending a lengthy line. Russian forces also had casualties and equipment losses and needed to replace experienced personnel. These developments are better understood as adaptation under severe pressure than as evidence that one army had solved the problem of modern combined-arms warfare. Breakthroughs remained uncommon, and apparent advances sometimes represented prolonged fighting over a cluster of streets or agricultural fields.

The relative importance of settlements changed over time. A town could matter initially because it protected a road, later because it served as a staging position, and eventually because each side had committed resources and political prestige to holding it. The Russian advance around Vuhledar, Kurakhove and other strongpoints during the preceding year shaped positions inherited in 2025, while continuing pressure toward Pokrovsk and the western Donbas affected Ukrainian supply planning. Reports of control over towns such as Toretsk or Chasiv Yar differed between governments and monitoring organizations; when dates and evidence remain disputed, the chapter should describe the contested campaign rather than assume a single announcement ended all organized resistance in the surrounding area. Villages outside a city can remain crucial even after its administrative center falls.

Southern sectors also remained strategically consequential. Russia sought to protect the land corridor linking occupied Crimea to the Donbas and to prevent Ukrainian forces from threatening transport routes through Zaporizhzhia oblast. Ukrainian forces used long-range strikes and drones against ammunition and fuel facilities, air-defense sites and logistical links, while the front around Orikhiv and other positions continued to experience local assaults. A river or minefield might be of greater operational consequence than a headline-generating village. Defense in depth required reserves and routes for moving them; attacks aimed at these systems even when they did not produce conspicuous territorial gains. Ukrainian command had to balance the defense of heavily populated eastern towns against protecting the south and the long northern border.

The year also displayed the importance of international decisions. Changes in U.S. policy after January 2025, European debates about ammunition financing, and the availability of air-defense interceptors affected planning without dictating every battlefield development. Russia's continuing ability to recruit, produce ammunition and purchase or otherwise obtain foreign components helped sustain offensive pressure, though it also faced high budgetary and human costs. Neither country's military was insulated from the wider economies and political systems that supplied it. Measures such as shell expenditure, training capacity and repair rates provided more analytical value than isolated claims about an enemy's daily losses, which were often released for morale or political purposes.

By the end of 2025 the overall pattern was of continued Russian offensive pressure, sustained Ukrainian resistance and a front whose local details changed more rapidly than the war's strategic balance. To assess the year responsibly, historians should use month-dated operational maps rather than one composite map supposedly valid for twelve months. ISW assessments are one useful secondary series, but they reflect the institute's analytical methods and should be compared with other geolocated reporting, official statements and documented losses. The main conclusion is not that a particular offensive guaranteed the war's outcome. It is that protracted attempts to capture towns, disrupt logistics and exhaust reserves came to shape military choices and civilian displacement across eastern and southern Ukraine.

Section 71.31

Escalating Long-Range Strikes and Infrastructure Warfare, 2025–2026

The war increasingly reached far beyond the immediate front through missiles, long-range drones, sabotage allegations and strikes against energy and industrial facilities. Russia expanded the number and variety of one-way attack drones launched against Ukrainian cities and infrastructure, often combining them with cruise or ballistic missiles to complicate air defense. Ukraine increased its own strike capacity against facilities in Russia, including military airfields, fuel processing sites and logistical nodes that Kyiv said supported the invasion. Both sides sought to impose costs on the opponent's ability to sustain war. The distinction between attacking an objective directly used by the military and attacking civilian economic life remained legally and morally consequential. Neither government's assertion that a target supported war production is sufficient without examining the actual object and the expected effects.

The growing scale of drone warfare changed the economics of defense. A relatively inexpensive unmanned aircraft could force the defender to employ a much more costly interceptor, but the cost balance also depended on detection, mobile gun teams, electronic jamming and whether drones actually reached valuable targets. Ukraine developed layered protection using Western systems, domestic technologies and improvised teams; Russia adapted flight profiles, launch patterns and payloads. A large salvo might seek to overload response capacity rather than rely on each vehicle's ability to penetrate defenses independently. Conversely, a defender's reported percentage of interceptions was not a complete measure of success because debris and the small number that escaped interception could still cause major harm. This is why an infrastructure report should accompany any launch statistics.

Russian attacks against power plants, substations, heating installations and other critical systems continued through 2025 and into 2026. The UN human-rights mission documented rising civilian deaths and injuries, with long-range weapons increasingly causing harm far from the battlefield. As of its September 2026 reporting, the mission verified 2,570 civilian deaths and 15,211 injuries in Ukraine during January–September 2026. These are verified conflict-related cases within a defined monitoring territory and period, not a complete count of all people harmed or of losses among soldiers. The pattern reflected both large strikes on cities and numerous drone attacks near the front. The effect on heating and water systems became especially consequential as winter approached, while repairs to equipment were repeatedly interrupted by subsequent attacks.

Ukraine's ability to strike targets inside Russia also expanded, but reports of individual attacks require differentiation. Some drones reached oil refining or storage sites; others were intercepted or caused fires whose extent could not be immediately established. Russian regional governors provided casualty and damage statements that were not always independently verifiable, just as Ukrainian military statements could overstate effects on defended installations. Geolocation, satellite imagery and later commercial data can establish whether a fire occurred or an installation's output changed, but not necessarily who ordered a strike or whether every explosion caused a loss of production. Attacks on energy infrastructure in both countries raise similar legal questions about the distinction, proportionality and feasible precautions governing wartime operations, even when the strategic context and evidence differ.

One distinguishing feature of the 2025–2026 phase was the continuing competition between strike production and countermeasures. Domestic factories, foreign component supply chains and technical specialists determined whether a state could replace expended drones and missiles. The attacking side modified navigation and electronic protection while defenders developed new interceptors and electronic warfare techniques. Industrial learning was iterative: a successful strike did not prove that the same route would work again after the defender adjusted. The war stimulated innovation but also demanded ordinary skills such as maintenance, radar management and logistics. Technical change should therefore be integrated into institutional history, not celebrated as a succession of allegedly decisive inventions.

By early November 2026 the pressure on Ukraine's energy system had intensified again. A November 4 military assessment described large Russian drone strikes overnight on November 3–4 against facilities in central and western Ukraine, with Ukrainian authorities reporting widespread emergency power outages; claims about numbers launched and facilities hit must remain attributed. The significance lies in the repetition of attacks against a network already damaged by earlier winters, and in the ability of civilian engineers and public services to continue functioning under that pressure. The long-range war had become a sustained contest over national resilience, the costs of maintaining infrastructure and the resources required for both attack and defense. It was not separate from the ground war, but neither could it be explained by movements at the front alone.

Section 71.32

The Front Lines and Operational Situation Through November 2026

A defensible account of the front on 4 November 2026 must begin by defining what a front line is. It is not an unbroken fence separating two uniformly administered territories. The zone contains trenches, reconnaissance patrols, drone-controlled spaces, mines, abandoned villages and positions whose status can change repeatedly. A military report that an assault occurred does not demonstrate that land changed hands; a geolocated video of one patrol does not prove stable control over an entire settlement. This chapter therefore uses a dated evidence cutoff and describes contested sectors without inventing precise daily territorial totals. The war was still active, and neither the ongoing negotiations nor the distribution of territory established a final political settlement.

On the northern axis, operations affected Sumy oblast and the Kharkiv borderlands, where Russian forces claimed to seek buffer zones and Ukrainian forces defended settlements and transport routes. Around Kupiansk and the Oskil River, attempts to control crossings and supply networks remained important. The security of Kharkiv city was not solely a matter of the nearest infantry position: long-range weapons could strike far behind ground forces, and civilians remained vulnerable even where the line of contact moved little. Reporting by the Institute for the Study of War on 4 November described Russian offensive operations in northern Sumy and Kharkiv areas but did not establish advances in several of the sectors discussed. As always, the institute's findings are analytical assessments based on observed evidence and reported claims, not an official cadastral survey.

In Donetsk oblast, Russian forces continued efforts against the Ukrainian defensive network commonly described as the Sloviansk-Kramatorsk fortress belt, including approaches around Lyman, Sloviansk, Kramatorsk, Druzhkivka and Kostiantynivka. The 4 November assessment described evidence consistent with Russian positions consolidating in northwestern Kostiantynivka while recording Ukrainian counterattacks in neighboring areas. It also noted offensive operations toward the Dobropillia direction without verified advance that day. Such language matters: infiltration, consolidation and a full breakthrough are not synonyms. Historical prose should identify the observation's date and degree of confidence, and should not convert a claimed gain into verified possession merely to provide a tidy narrative ending.

Farther south and west, the approaches toward Dnipropetrovsk oblast and fighting in Zaporizhzhia continued to matter for the protection of Ukrainian transport routes and the defense of occupied southern territory. The November 4 assessment recorded operations around Hulyaipole and western Zaporizhzhia without confirming advance in the relevant reporting window. The Kherson front remained divided by the Dnipro, and the occupied parts of the south were exposed to strikes on transport and energy infrastructure. Crimea continued to serve Russian military logistics and air-defense functions while facing Ukrainian attacks. These statements describe the sectors observable at the evidence cutoff, not every daily contact. Local conditions often change more quickly than an independent report can confirm them.

A complete assessment must also include civilian circumstances. The UN's September 2026 civilian-protection update, issued in October, verified at least 328 civilians killed and 2,105 injured in that month and documented harm across 22 Ukrainian regions and Kyiv. Attacks by long-range weapons remained the leading category, while short-range drones caused a large share of casualties near the front. Infrastructure strikes damaged electrical systems as colder months approached. These figures establish a documented scale of harm but do not measure displacement, untreated illness, loss of schooling or the cost of repeated evacuation. They also do not authorize an estimate of future deaths after the cutoff. Civilian experience is an essential component of the military situation because it determines how communities survive, whether local services can operate and where families can remain.

The strategic balance on 4 November 2026 remained unresolved. Russia retained occupation of substantial Ukrainian territory and continued attacks, while Ukraine maintained functioning state and military institutions, defended key areas and conducted strikes against Russian assets. Outside support, wartime budgets, personnel recruitment and the strength of air defense were critical but not fixed. Contemporary observers could explain why certain positions were threatened and why an attritional campaign continued; they could not establish the final borders of a future settlement or know whether scheduled talks would succeed. The responsible historical ending of this division is therefore a dated inventory of verified conditions, uncertainty and competing priorities. The story proceeds to occupation, law, social experience and diplomacy because those histories cannot be reduced to a final front-line map.

A further difficulty in assessing the 2026 campaign was that observers often described different levels of military reality with the same language. A Russian statement that a village had been “liberated” might mean that a detachment had entered part of its built-up area, that Ukrainian defenders had withdrawn from the center, or that the surrounding roads had become unusable. Conversely, a Ukrainian statement that a position was held might refer to defensive fire on an approach rather than control of every street. Geolocated videos provide strong evidence of presence at a particular time but generally cannot establish an uncontested line along its entire length. Historians must identify whether a report concerns an isolated assault, a tactical position, an operational penetration, or a durable shift of territorial administration. The distinction has consequences for civilian evacuation, negotiations and the validity of a map reproduced in a textbook.

Section 71.33

Russia's September 2022 Annexation Claims and International Responses

In September 2022, after Ukrainian counteroffensives had already recaptured substantial territory, Russian occupation authorities organized votes in parts of four Ukrainian oblasts: Donetsk, Luhansk, Zaporizhzhia and Kherson. Voting took place under conditions of war and occupation, without the procedural independence or security required for a free territorial referendum. Russian officials reported overwhelming approval for joining Russia and used these figures to justify formal annexation announcements at the end of the month. On 30 September President Putin signed documents claiming incorporation of the territories into the Russian Federation. The claims covered whole administrative oblasts, including areas Russia did not actually control. This fact is essential: an announcement by a government about territorial extent is not evidence that its armed forces exercised control over every district named in the announcement.

The international response emphasized the prohibition on acquiring territory by force. On 12 October 2022 the United Nations General Assembly adopted Resolution ES-11/4 by 143 votes in favor, five against and 35 abstentions. It rejected the purported referendums and called on states not to recognize the attempted annexations. A General Assembly resolution cannot physically reverse occupation, but it is strong evidence of the international legal position concerning Ukraine's sovereignty and territorial integrity. Russia maintained that residents had exercised self-determination and that the territories had become part of Russia. The legal and political claims must be distinguished from the empirical question of which streets or villages Russian forces actually controlled at a given date.

The divergence between claimed annexation and control became especially visible in Kherson. Russia's documents asserted sovereignty over the entire oblast, yet in November its troops withdrew from Kherson city and much of the western bank of the Dnipro. Russian law and official maps continued to claim territory that was under Ukrainian administration. In Zaporizhzhia oblast, the administrative capital remained under Ukrainian control despite Russia's annexation claim. The chapter must consistently use terms such as 'Russian-occupied part of Kherson oblast' or 'territory claimed by Russia' rather than labels that silently transfer recognized sovereignty. This discipline becomes increasingly important in a multi-year narrative, because otherwise geography, politics and legal status are confused in one phrase.

Russian authorities incorporated the claimed territories into state administrative structures, appointed officials, applied Russian legislation, organized elections under occupation, and sought to integrate economic systems and public institutions. These acts were consequential for residents regardless of their international legal validity. An individual might be required to accept Russian documents to receive medical care or a pension, while a school might change curriculum and language policy. Businesses encountered new licensing and taxation systems. Local police and security services enforced rules governing demonstrations and speech. Administrative change should therefore be studied as a coercive set of practices as well as an assertion about borders. It was neither simply paperwork nor evidence that residents freely consented.

The occupation also altered the experiences of communities that had already lived through the 2014–2021 conflict. In portions of Donetsk and Luhansk, de facto authorities had governed for years with Russian support; in newly occupied parts of Kherson and Zaporizhzhia, the abrupt replacement of Ukrainian administration was more immediate. Comparing these regions reveals variations in institutions, resistance, property disputes, and the speed with which Russian authorities imposed systems. Local collaborators existed, as did people who complied under pressure or avoided public political expression to protect families. The historian must avoid equating participation in an occupation bureaucracy with an unqualified political preference, while still analyzing the responsibilities of officials who participated in abuses.

The annexations did not produce a durable military settlement. Russian authorities invoked their newly asserted borders in political arguments and threats, but Ukrainian forces continued operations to restore control of internationally recognized Ukrainian territory. Diplomats faced the question whether a ceasefire line might be negotiated without recognizing conquest as lawful. This remained one of the central obstacles to peace through the 2026 cutoff. The historical lesson is the distinction among the act of claiming sovereignty, the actual exercise of power, recognition by other states, and the legal status of territory. These categories can diverge for years, and they must be documented separately throughout this chapter.

Section 71.34

Administration and Everyday Life Under Russian Occupation

Military occupation changes a civilian's life through institutions as well as armed patrols. In areas of Ukraine occupied by Russia after February 2022, local authorities were removed or pressured to cooperate, Russian-appointed administrators took office and Russian security forces established systems for surveillance, detention and public order. Documents, social benefits, employment, health care and transport increasingly depended on compliance with the new system. People who had not been politically active before the invasion encountered political choices when schools reopened under a different curriculum or when public offices required a Russian passport. The relevant legal framework is the law of occupation, which places obligations on a power exercising effective territorial control regardless of whether that power claims annexation. International recognition of Ukrainian sovereignty did not make coercive administrative measures any less consequential in practice.

The United Nations Human Rights Monitoring Mission's March 2026 thematic report describes discriminatory occupation policies that produced forcible displacement and barriers to return. Residents who lacked Russian documents could encounter restrictions affecting medical treatment, housing, pensions and employment. Persons believed to hold pro-Ukrainian opinions faced questioning, detention or expulsion, while families trying to leave sometimes found exit routes dangerous or subject to screening. The mission identified policies compelling residents toward Russian citizenship and, in some circumstances, military registration or conscription. Such findings rest on interviews, documents and corroboration but also have limits: access to occupied territory is often restricted, and abuses not reported or verified are not included in published totals. The absence of a full census of victims cannot be mistaken for evidence that coercion was rare.

Education was an especially important site of social transformation. Occupying authorities introduced Russian school standards, revised historical narratives and programs of patriotic and military-patriotic instruction. Ukrainian-language teaching and materials diminished or disappeared in many locations. Parents could face conflicting pressures: refusing the new system might interrupt a child's education, while attending could require Russian identity documents or expose children to indoctrination. The experience varied by age and place. A teenager approaching graduation faced different administrative requirements from a young child, and displaced pupils required support to continue study remotely or in schools farther west. The historical evidence includes official curricula, testimony from families, photographs of classrooms and monitoring reports, each with different limits. Education was therefore both a public service and a means through which occupation authorities attempted to reshape identity.

Property and economic relationships also changed. In depopulated settlements, abandoned homes and businesses could be registered, appropriated, reassigned or damaged beyond practical use. Residents reported pressure to obtain Russian passports as a condition for dealing with real estate, utilities or social benefits. Agricultural producers were affected by shifting rules on ownership, markets and transport, while industries needed new supply and payment channels. Disrupted water and electricity infrastructure compounded difficulties. In some occupied Donetsk communities, serious water shortages by 2025 meant that residents received limited supplies at intervals, according to UN monitoring. Such experiences complicate any description of occupation as stable administration merely because authorities displayed official flags and issued regulations.

Occupation did not produce a uniform public response. Some residents supported Russian rule, some opposed it openly, others resisted quietly, and many attempted to survive without taking conspicuous positions. Security conditions made reliable polling difficult or impossible; a public celebration filmed under occupation cannot establish the preferences of a whole town. A person who accepted a passport or worked at a hospital may have been acting under economic necessity. International humanitarian law distinguishes actions that an occupying power may lawfully require to maintain civilian life from forced political allegiance and unlawful conscription. Historians should use this distinction when evaluating individuals whose options were constrained. Local identities also vary: a Russian-speaking Ukrainian citizen need not favor Russian rule, and the language spoken at home cannot be treated as a referendum on borders.

By the end of 2025 and into 2026 international monitors described extensive barriers to return for displaced people from occupied territory. Missing identity documents, property claims, screening procedures and fear of detention made return difficult even for residents who still had relatives and homes there. Occupation thus generated a history that outlasted any individual battle: school records, citizenship status, housing rights, family relationships and local archives could be altered or lost. A postwar account will require testimony from those who remained and those who fled, alongside property and administrative records; it cannot rely solely on decrees issued by the occupation authorities. The longer occupation continued, the more deeply these administrative transformations affected future possibilities for return and recovery.

Section 71.35

The Transfer of Ukrainian Children and the International Criminal Court

One of the most consequential questions raised by Russia's occupation concerned Ukrainian children transferred from occupied regions to the Russian Federation or other occupied locations. Children were moved through a variety of circumstances, including institutions, evacuation programs, medical treatment and camps. Russian officials described many movements as protective evacuations from dangerous areas and publicized adoption or guardianship arrangements. Ukrainian authorities and independent investigators raised concerns that children were removed without valid parental consent or adequate opportunity to return, and that adoption or citizenship practices obstructed reunification. These categories cannot be flattened into a single story: the legality of a particular transfer depends on the child's circumstances, the presence or absence of consent, the measures taken to trace family, the duration and purpose of movement, and the obligations of an occupying power.

On 17 March 2023 the International Criminal Court issued arrest warrants for President Vladimir Putin and Russian children's commissioner Maria Lvova-Belova. The court stated there were reasonable grounds to believe they bore responsibility for alleged war crimes involving unlawful deportation and transfer of Ukrainian children from occupied territory. An arrest warrant is not a conviction, and the warrants themselves do not resolve each child's individual history. The court's procedural language matters because some public discussion mistakenly presents an accusation as a final judgment while other commentary treats the absence of a final conviction as evidence that no relevant conduct occurred. The historically appropriate position is to explain the court's findings, their legal threshold, the documented underlying conduct, the defense positions and what remains to be adjudicated.

Families separated by the invasion faced difficult tracing problems. Records of institutions could be inaccessible, children could have been moved multiple times, and Russian or occupation authorities sometimes changed identity documentation. Some parents or guardians had fled abroad; others remained in places where reliable communication was impossible. Civil society groups, Ukrainian officials, international organizations and mediating states tried to identify children and facilitate reunions. Each successful return can be documented as an individual outcome, but counts of children identified, reported missing, transferred or returned often measure different populations and cannot simply be subtracted from one another to produce a reliable total. Responsible data studies should specify the original record keeper, cutoff date, definition and whether cases were independently corroborated.

The legal regime governing children under occupation differs from discretionary domestic child-protection policy. The Fourth Geneva Convention and related international humanitarian law provisions contain safeguards concerning the protection, identity, care and movement of children and civilians. The transfer of children for temporary safety does not automatically authorize permanent changes to nationality, family relationships or identity. Investigators therefore examine whether authorities sought genuine return, permitted family contact, preserved documentation and avoided unnecessary separation. The existence of danger during fighting may explain an emergency evacuation but does not by itself answer whether an ensuing placement or adoption was lawful. These distinctions enable a more serious inquiry than political slogans on either side.

Children's own perspectives are essential but difficult to record responsibly. Public interviews may place them at risk of stigma or retaliation, while memories of traumatic events can change and require careful corroboration. Investigative practice protects identities and avoids leading questions; photographs and social-media posts must be verified before being used as evidence of location or consent. Schools and camps may have used political instruction that promoted Russian narratives about Ukraine. A child who repeats an official position after relocation is not a substitute for an independent account of how the relocation occurred or whether a family has been given a genuine path to reunion. The historian must distinguish adult institutional intentions from experiences that children were free to express.

By November 2026 the issue remained unresolved. The full number and location of transferred children were uncertain, and ongoing identification and return efforts did not establish that all cases had been accounted for. The ICC warrants also belonged to a wider body of legal proceedings about occupation and civilian harm. The chapter should present this subject as both a question of individual family histories and a test of the international legal protections governing occupation. It must neither reduce children to symbols in a geopolitical argument nor omit the substantial institutional documentation that has accumulated. The appropriate measure of progress is not an unverified headline total but identifiable safe returns, restored family contact, accessible records and accountable procedures.

Tracing a child across wartime jurisdictions poses difficulties that cannot be solved by choosing the largest reported total. Ukrainian authorities, Russian institutions, humanitarian agencies and independent investigators have counted children using different definitions: identified transfers, placements in institutions, movement with parents, adoption or guardianship arrangements, and cases in which a family cannot make contact. Identification records may have been changed or made inaccessible. Where the ICC warrant concerns alleged unlawful deportation and transfer, it does not make every cross-border movement automatically criminal; the legal evaluation depends on the child's status, the circumstances of movement, the safeguards that should apply and the possibility of return. A child's experience also continues beyond transport. Schooling, personal names, citizenship papers, language, communication with relatives and access to independent advocates shape the possibilities of reunification. The documentary record should therefore be read both as a question of legal responsibility and as the history of individual lives disrupted by war.

Section 71.36

Detention, Filtration, Prisoners of War, and Allegations of Torture

Prisoner-of-war status is defined by international humanitarian law, not by the language chosen by captors. Soldiers captured in an international armed conflict are entitled to humane treatment, protection from violence and intimidation, necessary medical care and communication through established channels. Captured persons must not be tortured, publicly humiliated or punished simply for having fought for their state. These principles applied to both Russian and Ukrainian prisoners throughout the conflict, including members of units whose political symbols or prior conduct were controversial. Neither side's claims about the other removed the obligation to treat detainees lawfully. At the same time, civilians detained because of alleged political loyalties are not automatically prisoners of war and may fall under different legal protections. Accurate terminology is a prerequisite for understanding reports of abuse.

The UN human-rights office documented extensive accounts of torture and ill-treatment of Ukrainian prisoners in Russian custody, including beatings, electric shocks, threats, sexual violence, denial of medical care and severe conditions of confinement. Some released prisoners gave consistent descriptions of routines across different facilities. Monitors also documented summary executions of captured or surrendering Ukrainian personnel in particular incidents, subject to the evidentiary limitations of each inquiry. Russian officials denied widespread wrongdoing and challenged some findings; their denials must be reported as such, but they do not create an equal evidentiary basis when numerous independent interviews and forensic records corroborate abusive practices. The problem of access is fundamental: investigators often met captives only after exchange, when some evidence had disappeared and memories had been affected by trauma.

The same monitoring bodies documented violations involving Russian prisoners in Ukrainian custody, including accounts of abuse during initial capture, unlawful interrogation and isolated detention arrangements. The UN distinguished the pattern and scale of abuses reported in the different systems, rather than assuming a strict symmetry. Ukrainian authorities announced investigations in some cases, and international monitors had greater access to certain facilities under Ukrainian government control than to Russian sites. This difference in access affects what can be verified, but it does not justify ignoring evidence of misconduct by Ukrainian personnel. A credible textbook reports the applicable law, the documented evidence and the scope of the finding for each side, without using a generic phrase such as 'both sides committed abuses' to conceal major differences in documented conduct.

Russian forces and occupation authorities also operated screening and 'filtration' procedures affecting civilians, particularly during the capture of places such as Mariupol. Residents seeking evacuation or movement could be questioned, searched, fingerprinted or required to provide phones and documents. Some reported detention, disappearance or transfer to facilities in Russia or occupied territory after suspicion of allegiance to Ukraine. Russian authorities described the processes as security measures intended to identify combatants and threats. The lawfulness of screening depends on necessity, safeguards and treatment; it does not create a general right to hold or transfer civilians arbitrarily. Independent reports linked some filtration practices to forced movement, ill-treatment and family separation, emphasizing that not every person processed had the same experience.

Prisoner exchanges became one of the few continuing forms of direct contact between the governments. Exchanges returned soldiers and civilians in different arrangements, sometimes involving mediation by third states. Announcements of equal or unequal numbers concerned particular transactions; they did not measure all captives remaining. Families often had no confirmed information about a missing relative's status, and identification of remains could take months. Humanitarian organizations sought access and tracing information but could not always reach detention sites. The exchange of prisoners is not evidence that prior treatment was lawful, nor is refusal to publish a complete list necessarily proof of a particular allegation. Proper historical reconstruction requires attention to the institutions that record captives, the dates of transfers and the difference between missing, detained and confirmed dead.

This history connects individual suffering to the structure of an ongoing war. Detention systems need guards, transport, medical services, interrogation procedures and government oversight; abuses can become systematic when institutions tolerate or direct them. International investigations have described both discrete incidents and wider patterns, which require different evidence. The chapter should distinguish a witness's credible personal account from a finding about an entire system, and distinguish the identification of a responsible unit from proof of a commander's criminal liability. The purpose is not to avoid judgment. It is to build conclusions that remain sound when courts, archives and later testimony add evidence after the initial publication cutoff.

Section 71.37

Civilian Protection, Atrocities, and Documentary Investigations

The discovery of killings and other abuses in Ukrainian communities after Russian withdrawals in spring 2022 transformed the international understanding of the invasion. Investigators visited Bucha, Irpin and other Kyiv-region towns, documented bodies in streets and graves, interviewed surviving witnesses and examined photographs and satellite images. Evidence concerning particular killings included ballistic and medical findings, records of troop movements, intercepted communications and visual material taken before withdrawal. Russian authorities denied responsibility and alleged fabrication or staging, claims that independent investigations evaluated against evidence of bodies present while Russian forces still controlled the areas. A historically rigorous account should not repeat mutually incompatible claims as though they were equally supported when the available chronology and physical evidence strongly favor one explanation.

Civilian killings were not confined to the Kyiv region. In occupied and contested areas, monitors examined summary executions, unlawful detention, torture, sexual violence, forced disappearances and attacks causing civilian deaths. Evidence quality varied with access, witness safety and the survival of records. Where investigators documented a pattern of abuse, they identified the individuals interviewed, the dates and locations, and the degree of corroboration needed for their conclusions. The UN Commission of Inquiry on Ukraine, for example, reported systematic Russian practices of torture and enforced disappearance and in 2025 characterized specified conduct as crimes against humanity. Such legal characterizations are the commission's findings based on its mandate and evidence; they are not the same thing as convictions of named defendants by a criminal court.

Civilian harm from bombardment presents another investigative challenge. A strike may be attributable to a particular weapon through fragments, crater analysis, trajectories, imagery and known deployment sites. Identifying the weapon does not alone establish that its use was unlawful, because the location of the military objective, expected civilian harm, available alternatives and precautions also matter. In other cases, the deliberate attack on a civilian object or an indiscriminate method may be apparent from credible evidence. Monitors should distinguish deaths verified to their standard from reasonable estimates of additional deaths in inaccessible areas. The same rule applies to attacks within Russia, where official civilian-casualty claims may be difficult for international investigators to confirm. A comparison of verified figures must acknowledge differences in access.

Atrocities are also recorded through survivor testimony, which carries both necessity and vulnerability. People subjected to violence may recall fragments rather than a neat chronology, and interviewers must avoid creating further trauma. Witnesses may also face threats if identities become public. Forensic examinations can confirm injuries or the approximate timing of death while being unable to identify a perpetrator without other evidence. Digital open-source material can establish that people were present at a location but may omit activity outside the camera's field of view. Robust investigations combine these types rather than relying on a single dramatic video or anonymous quotation. This is a historical-method lesson as much as a legal one.

Accountability processes remained uneven as the war continued. Ukrainian investigators opened numerous criminal inquiries, international bodies collected evidence, and journalists reconstructed specific incidents, while Russian authorities rejected many investigations as politically motivated. Some allegations concerning Ukrainian conduct also attracted scrutiny, particularly the treatment of prisoners and military operations affecting civilians. The relevant principle is evidence-based differentiation: investigators should neither excuse conduct because it was committed by a defending state nor ignore the scale and characteristics of Russia's documented invasion and occupation violations. Individual responsibility must be established on its own facts. The illegality of an invasion and the lawfulness of an individual battlefield act are related but analytically distinct questions.

The longer-term challenge is preserving evidence during an unfinished conflict. Burial sites may be disturbed, civilian records destroyed and witnesses displaced across borders. Digital material may disappear as platforms remove accounts; investigators need copies with provenance and verification of dates and locations. Courts may decide only a small subset of the incidents documented by monitors. A serious textbook therefore needs an evidence register that identifies each report's date, scope and limits, so later editions can update conclusions without obscuring what was known in 2026. The history of civilian harm cannot be reduced to totals; it must account for the people affected, the institutions responsible for protection, and the methods through which claims about violence become documented findings.

Section 71.38

International Accountability and the Limits of Wartime Evidence

International accountability for the war operates through institutions with different mandates. The International Criminal Court investigates and prosecutes individuals for specified international crimes within its jurisdiction. The International Court of Justice hears disputes between states under legal instruments that provide jurisdiction. The European Court of Human Rights, domestic courts and investigative commissions have different functions again. A UN report documenting a violation does not mean the same institution has convicted an individual; an ICC arrest warrant means judges found a threshold of evidence for alleged responsibility, not that a trial has established guilt. Students must learn the differences because public debate often treats every announcement involving 'The Hague' as if it were the same legal decision.

The ICC opened a Ukraine investigation after the full-scale invasion and issued its first public warrants in March 2023 for Vladimir Putin and Maria Lvova-Belova concerning alleged deportation and transfer of children. In March and June 2024 it issued additional warrants involving senior Russian commanders in relation to alleged unlawful attacks on electrical infrastructure and related conduct. The court identified the charged conduct and applicable legal standards; it did not issue a general verdict on every Russian military operation or the guilt of every official in the state. Russia rejected the warrants and disputed the court's jurisdiction. Ukraine supported the proceedings and cooperated with international investigators. The arrest and trial of a person subject to a warrant depend on further political and legal steps, including whether states execute warrants when suspects travel.

The ICJ's March 16, 2022 order in the genocide-convention dispute directed Russia to suspend the military operations begun on February 24. That order concerned provisional measures within a specific case; it was not a final adjudication of every question concerning aggression or war crimes. The case's later procedural history and jurisdictional disputes must be described accurately rather than implying that the provisional measure concluded the whole case. The UN General Assembly condemned the invasion and rejected annexation claims, while the Security Council's ability to act was constrained by Russia's veto. General Assembly resolutions are powerful evidence of state positions and international principles but are not interchangeable with criminal judgments.

National jurisdictions contribute another layer. Ukrainian police and prosecutors investigated attacks, detention and other alleged violations, sometimes with international technical assistance. Certain foreign jurisdictions considered proceedings based on international crimes or other principles of jurisdiction. Evidence collected for one purpose may not meet a different court's admissibility rules, and witnesses must be protected. Command responsibility requires proof of relevant relationships and knowledge or failure to exercise control as specified in the applicable law; broad political responsibility does not automatically establish an individual's criminal guilt. Conversely, the difficulty of obtaining custody of a suspect does not prove that the underlying conduct never happened. These distinctions protect the integrity of both legal process and historical scholarship.

International investigative commissions, UN monitoring missions, human-rights organizations and journalists publish findings on broader patterns of violence. Their methods differ: some rely on confidential survivor interviews and documentary corroboration, others on open-source imagery, forensic examination or government records. A claim supported by several independent methods is stronger than one repeated by many outlets from a single unverified statement. Some documents may remain confidential to protect witnesses or preserve evidence for trials. Students should therefore evaluate not only an institution's reputation but also the precise question investigated, its access, the standard of proof and whether the report explains uncertainty. A careful source window should give readers enough bibliographic information to find the original record.

Through November 2026 accountability remained an unfinished process, just as the war remained unfinished. The existence of warrants, national prosecutions and extensive documentary investigations reflected substantial institutional efforts, but final legal judgments on many incidents were absent or still subject to future proceedings. A history chapter should record what those processes had established by its cutoff and not anticipate the outcome of trials or settlements. Its enduring lesson is methodological: distinguish what happened, what investigators can substantiate, how the law classifies conduct, who may be individually responsible, and what courts have actually decided. Keeping these layers separate does not weaken an account of abuse; it makes its conclusions more precise and defensible.

Legal time also differs from historical time. A commission may issue a report when it has sufficient corroboration to describe an incident, while a criminal prosecutor must establish individual responsibility under a particular statute and the court must apply still more exacting proof requirements before conviction. Courts may lack custody over an accused person; witnesses may be displaced or afraid; physical evidence may be destroyed; and the authority to prosecute can be disputed. None of those limitations makes a documented killing disappear, but they matter when explaining why a historical conclusion and a judicial judgment may be reached at different times. For students, the useful method is to assemble a matrix of actor, alleged act, location, date, evidence, legal classification and procedural status. This prevents a common error of converting a report's serious allegation into an already completed criminal conviction, or treating the absence of a conviction as proof that no offense occurred.

Section 71.39

Russian Command, Logistics, Military Leadership, and Institutional Adaptation

Russia entered February 2022 with a large army that had undergone reforms after the 2008 Georgia war and had acquired expeditionary experience in Syria. Its initial attack on Ukraine nevertheless exposed serious limitations in operational planning, logistics and coordination. Units advancing toward Kyiv sometimes depended on narrow road networks, lacked secure communications and failed to synchronize air, ground and support operations. Some formations encountered resistance stronger than expected, while commanders apparently planned for a political collapse that did not occur. A large inventory of tanks and aircraft could not compensate for insufficient reconnaissance or unreliable assumptions about Ukrainian behavior. Historians cannot know every private decision made by Russian commanders in early 2022, but the mismatch between objectives, available forces and operational results is demonstrable from the campaign's chronology.

The Russian force structure evolved as the war lengthened. Battalion tactical groups, once prominent in descriptions of Russian land power, proved difficult to sustain at authorized strength and were increasingly supplemented or reorganized by larger formations, assault detachments and mobilized personnel. The army relied on regular ground units, airborne forces, naval infantry, artillery and support from Rosgvardia and other institutions in different phases. Command arrangements changed, including the appointment of Sergei Surovikin to command Russian forces in Ukraine in October 2022 and Valery Gerasimov to a senior campaign command role in January 2023. The significance of these appointments lies not only in names but in changes in coordination, responsibility, air-defense priorities and attempts to stabilize a front after reverses.

Logistics became both a persistent weakness and an area of adaptation. Ammunition had to travel from factories and depots through railheads, roads and distribution points, all vulnerable to attack. Ukraine's strikes against ammunition facilities in 2022 encouraged Russia to disperse stocks and move some depots farther from front lines. Vehicle repair and recovery mattered greatly because a tank damaged by a mine might be returned to service if transported to a workshop, whereas an intact tank abandoned without fuel might be captured. Battlefield success thus depended on fuel, bridges, rails, spare parts, mechanics and reliable orders. Neither side could rely on prewar peacetime supply methods while consuming ammunition and vehicles at sustained wartime rates.

Command problems also included the relationship between formal hierarchy and informal political influence. Russia's Ministry of Defense, security institutions, regional governors and irregular armed organizations competed or cooperated in supplying troops and maintaining public support. The prominence of Wagner and its eventual rupture with military leadership made the institutional problem especially visible. The appointment of new defense leadership in 2024 also occurred amid debates about procurement, corruption and the priorities of a prolonged war. Official reshuffles should not automatically be interpreted as proof of battlefield success or failure; their meaning depends on what administrative authority changed and whether material conditions on the front improved. A history centered only on generals neglects the lower-level officers whose decisions determine unit cohesion and adaptation.

Training and leadership at the small-unit level became increasingly important. Replacement personnel needed instruction in drone awareness, communications discipline, trench systems, first aid and coordinated movement. When commanders relied heavily on rapid replacements, the loss of experienced soldiers could degrade performance even if overall manpower increased. Conversely, units that developed effective reconnaissance and fire coordination might perform well despite shortages of equipment. Russia also adapted defensive construction, electronic warfare and the use of glide bombs and unmanned systems. Studying those improvements does not erase early failures or establish eventual success. The proper question is which capabilities were improved, which limits endured, and how adaptation altered the costs and possibilities of operations.

The Russian military's transformation was inseparable from changes in the state. Recruiting contracts, distributing bonuses, allocating factory orders, keeping casualty data confidential and managing public narratives all required civilian institutions. War production and command decisions thus crossed boundaries between national government, regions, private firms and households. Some reforms increased the capacity to continue fighting while also increasing long-term financial and demographic obligations. By the 2026 evidence cutoff the Russian armed forces were more practiced in aspects of attritional warfare than in February 2022 but continued to encounter significant operational challenges. The chapter should evaluate these changes as institutional history under continuing war rather than as a simplistic narrative of either total incompetence or inevitable mastery.

Section 71.40

The September 2022 Partial Mobilization and Its Consequences

On 21 September 2022 President Putin announced what the Russian government called partial mobilization after setbacks in northeastern Ukraine and amid preparations for annexation claims. Defense Minister Sergei Shoigu publicly cited a target of 300,000 reservists. The announcement followed months in which Russia had described its campaign as a limited 'special military operation' and had initially avoided a general call-up. Mobilization changed the war's relationship to households far from the battlefield. Men received summonses through military offices, employers and, in some circumstances, other administrative channels. The scope of exemptions, the medical condition of recruits and the application of rules varied across regions. Reported procedural abuses and erroneous call-ups led authorities to announce corrections, but problems persisted in local implementation.

The decree's language and the government's explanations created uncertainty over the duration and limits of service. Some mobilized men had previous military experience, while others had long been civilians and required basic retraining. Availability of uniforms, protective equipment, medical supplies and adequate instruction differed. Early reports documented overcrowded training facilities and rapid deployment of inadequately prepared personnel, though conditions varied by unit and time. An individual mobilized in a region with experienced instructors could have a different preparation from someone sent quickly to reinforce a depleted formation. The phrase '300,000 troops' was a policy target and official count, not a direct measure of combat-ready personnel available on a particular date. Military effectiveness depended on training, leadership, integration with units and timely logistical support.

Public response revealed both resistance and compliance. Men left Russia for neighboring countries including Kazakhstan, Georgia, Armenia and others, producing substantial flows at border crossings. Exact totals were difficult to determine because temporary travel, permanent relocation and repeated entries were recorded differently. Some Russians protested or objected on legal and moral grounds; authorities detained demonstrators and enforced restrictions on public assembly. Others reported for service, whether from support for the war, fear of penalties, family obligation, economic necessity or lack of realistic alternatives. Polling under political pressure could not unambiguously separate these motives. Regional differences in incomes and opportunities meant that military pay and compensation could exert especially strong influence in poorer communities.

Mobilization had uneven effects on Russian regions and ethnic communities. Recruitment patterns, local administrative practices and the distribution of military casualties prompted scrutiny in places including Buryatia, Dagestan and rural districts far from Moscow. Such variation should be documented with reliable regional records rather than converted into broad assumptions about ethnic identity or loyalty. Regional governments were tasked with locating personnel, providing equipment and supporting families. Volunteer collections sought clothing, drones or medical supplies, suggesting gaps that the centralized supply system had not consistently filled. An apparent act of voluntary donation might reflect genuine patriotic support, pressure from local employers or a combination of motives. Historians should attend to the local institutions that mediated the state's demands.

The mobilization contributed to Russia's ability to stabilize some defenses after the 2022 reverses, but it also created long-term obligations. Newly recruited soldiers needed rotation, medical treatment, pensions and support for dependents. Families organized to seek information about missing men or to petition authorities over deployments and leave. Public discourse became more tightly controlled, and the state sought to prevent independent organizations from turning individual grievances into larger political campaigns. Mobilization also affected civilian labor markets as some men entered military service or left the country. In sectors already experiencing worker shortages, employers had to reorganize staffing, sometimes relying on older workers, women, migrants or higher wages to fill positions.

The initial September decree should be distinguished from subsequent contract recruitment, financial incentives, legal changes to registration and renewed regional recruitment drives. Russia increasingly combined a formal mobilized cohort with continuing efforts to attract new personnel into contracts. These mechanisms had different administrative and political costs. By November 2026 mobilization had become part of a larger system for sustaining the war, but public evidence did not provide a comprehensive and independently verified count of every serving soldier, death or permanent disability. The central historical significance of September 2022 was the end of the pretense that the war could be sustained indefinitely with only the forces originally committed. It widened the conflict's direct reach into Russian society and exposed the constraints of the state's military and administrative capacities.

Section 71.41

Wagner, Irregular Forces, and the June 2023 Mutiny

The Wagner organization occupied a distinctive position in Russia's war-making system. It emerged in the 2010s as a network of armed personnel operating in places including Ukraine, Syria and parts of Africa, associated publicly with businessman Yevgeny Prigozhin and linked to Russian state interests. Before the full-scale invasion the Russian authorities often obscured or denied formal relationships with such armed formations. In 2022 Wagner became prominent in the Donbas, especially during the campaign for Bakhmut, recruiting from former soldiers and, increasingly, from penal colonies. Its fighters operated alongside Russian regular forces while maintaining a separate command structure, recruiting system and publicity apparatus. The arrangement offered the state manpower and a degree of organizational flexibility but also created problems of accountability and competing authority.

Prigozhin built a public profile through videos and statements criticizing the Ministry of Defense. During the long Bakhmut battle he accused military leaders of withholding ammunition and sacrificing his forces through incompetence. Defense officials made counterclaims and sought to bring irregular formations more tightly under military contracts. The dispute was not simply a personal quarrel: it concerned access to resources, chains of command, operational authority and the state's monopoly over organized coercion. A private military organization that could recruit prisoners and deploy large armed units had acquired leverage that ordinary civilian businesses did not possess. The war made this arrangement both useful to the Kremlin and increasingly unstable.

On 23–24 June 2023 Wagner fighters seized military facilities in Rostov-on-Don and advanced northward toward Moscow, while Prigozhin announced a confrontation with the military leadership. Putin denounced the action as betrayal and warned of national danger. The columns stopped after negotiations associated with Belarusian president Aleksandr Lukashenko, and Prigozhin announced a withdrawal. The mutiny did not result in the overthrow of Russia's government, but it revealed how far a military organization with its own leadership and armed personnel could challenge the state during an external war. Casualty and equipment figures from the confrontation were limited, and many details of negotiations remained undisclosed. The episode should be analyzed without inventing a coherent political program or assuming it was a disguised operation orchestrated by the Kremlin.

In August 2023 Prigozhin and several Wagner leaders died in a plane crash in Russia. The circumstances generated extensive speculation, but a scholarly chapter must distinguish confirmed deaths and official investigative statements from unproved allegations about responsibility. After the mutiny and crash, the Russian government sought to absorb personnel and overseas activities through more direct state-controlled military structures or alternative contracting arrangements. Some fighters continued service in various formations. The institutional effect was to narrow the autonomy of an organization that had previously operated with unusual freedom, although forms of irregular or semi-official military activity did not disappear from Russian foreign policy.

Wagner's history also raised legal and ethical questions. Prisoner recruitment created concerns about consent, training, military discipline and treatment of those placed in exceptionally dangerous assaults. Allegations concerning violence against civilians and prisoners require evaluation against specific evidence and jurisdiction. The labels 'private military company' and 'mercenary' carry different legal and institutional meanings; Russian domestic law did not simply create a normal regulated market for private armies. The state sometimes benefited from plausible deniability while exercising influence over funding, transportation and strategic objectives. Understanding these relationships is necessary to explain why Wagner could be both an extension of Russian policy and a challenge to its official hierarchy.

The June mutiny became a moment of political revelation. It showed that wartime military performance could not be separated from the distribution of political authority and financial resources inside Russia. The failure of the rebellion also demonstrated the persistence of central state control over security institutions and elite alliances. The historian should avoid two simplifying conclusions: that a brief challenge proved the state was on the verge of collapse, or that its rapid suppression proved internal relationships were entirely stable. Wagner's trajectory from battlefield prominence to confrontation and reorganization illustrates how prolonged war can generate institutions whose interests eventually conflict with the government that enabled them.

Section 71.42

Military Recruitment, Contract Soldiers, Prisoners, and Foreign Participation

Russia and Ukraine sustained the war through recruitment systems that differed in law, resources and political incentives. In Russia, mobilized reservists served alongside contract soldiers, professional volunteers, personnel recruited from prisons and people from Russian-held Ukrainian territories. The government and regional authorities offered increasingly substantial signing payments and salary packages in some areas to attract recruits without announcing a new nationwide mobilization of the same public visibility as September 2022. For an individual in a poorer locality, a contract could represent extraordinary income; for others it was driven by political conviction, previous service or pressure. These motives cannot be inferred from a recruit's uniform or from a government's celebratory account of enlistment. Records on age, region, education and prior occupation are needed for a representative social history.

The wartime military labor market created competition between the armed forces and civilian employers. Recruiting a skilled mechanic, driver or telecommunications specialist might improve a military unit while leaving a factory or regional service with a vacancy. Defense industries themselves needed experienced workers who could not be replaced quickly. Exemptions and deferments therefore became economic as well as political decisions. Regions also competed to attract recruits through bonuses and benefits, producing variation in the fiscal cost of each contract. Official announcements of how many people signed agreements in a calendar year did not necessarily disclose how many were already serving, how many completed training or how many remained medically fit. Those differences matter when discussing 'force generation' as a military capability.

Prison recruitment was especially controversial. Wagner's prison-based campaign expanded in 2022 and involved promises of pay and possible release after service. Later state structures and other formations recruited from detention systems under changing legal arrangements. Participation in dangerous combat under threat or constrained alternatives raises difficult questions about meaningful consent. Military institutions also faced the challenge of training and supervising people with varied backgrounds and often minimal preparation. Reports of punishments, coercion and abuse require source-specific verification rather than accepting anonymous viral accounts as representative. These practices cannot be separated from Russian criminal-justice institutions and the pressure to fill manpower gaps in an attritional conflict.

Foreign participation added another layer. People from neighboring and more distant states served on both sides in different legal capacities, sometimes as volunteers motivated by ideology and sometimes under contracts or inducements. Reports concerning North Korean military support to Russia became significant in late 2024 and afterward, especially in connection with fighting in Kursk oblast. U.S., South Korean and Ukrainian officials described the presence and activities of North Korean personnel, and further evidence and public statements accumulated later. The date of each confirmation matters: a claim that became well supported in 2025 should not be represented as independently established on the day it was first reported. Figures for the numbers deployed, killed or injured are particularly uncertain and require dated attribution.

Russian foreign recruitment claims also need differentiation between military aid, formal deployment and private enlistment. Iran supplied weapons and technology associated with Russia's drone program, while North Korea's assistance involved ammunition and, according to later corroborated reporting, personnel. Belarus provided territory and support for Russia's 2022 attack, but it should not be described as having sent its regular army into sustained ground combat in Ukraine without documentary evidence. China maintained substantial economic relations with Russia while disputing allegations concerning the provision of lethal military assistance. The level and kind of participation differ among these states, and an analytical table should not collapse them into one category called 'allies.'

Over time the war exposed the limits of using financial recruitment to solve a sustained military problem. Contracts could bring in personnel but did not guarantee competent junior leaders, effective unit cohesion or sufficient time to train. Pay and survivor benefits created obligations for regional and federal budgets. Foreign personnel needed interpretation, specialized integration and clear command arrangements. Prisoner recruitment posed additional disciplinary and legal questions. By 2026 the competing requirements of manpower, training, economic production and political manageability had become structural elements of the war. The most important historical question is how these recruitment systems altered Russian state institutions and society, not merely how many contracts officials announced each month.

Section 71.43

Drones, Electronic Warfare, Artillery, Air Defense, and Battlefield Innovation

The extensive use of unmanned systems was one of the defining military developments of the war. Commercial quadcopters initially adapted for reconnaissance and dropping small explosives operated alongside larger reconnaissance aircraft, one-way attack drones, loitering munitions and unmanned maritime vessels. Both armies learned that a relatively inexpensive drone could expose artillery positions, locate vehicles, observe trench movements or strike targets that would previously have required more elaborate weapons. Yet drones were not independent actors: operators needed batteries, launch sites, secure communications, replacement parts, intelligence and coordination with infantry and artillery. Their effectiveness varied with weather, terrain, countermeasures and training. A video of one successful strike could be powerful propaganda without revealing the proportion of missions that failed.

First-person-view systems changed how short-range attacks were conducted. Operators guided explosive drones toward personnel, vehicles or equipment, while both sides built networks for procurement, assembly, repair and operator instruction. A successful small drone could destroy expensive machinery, but the apparent price ratio omitted the cost of locating targets, replacing lost drones and maintaining radio links. Electronic warfare teams jammed frequencies, disrupted navigation and searched for operators. This created an iterative contest: an improvement in signal control might work briefly until the opponent changed frequencies, antennas or tactics. Soldiers adapted with concealment, dispersion, camouflage nets and changes in movement time. The result was not that traditional weapons became obsolete, but that more activities had to be organized under continuous observation.

Artillery remained decisive, particularly when paired with reconnaissance drones that corrected fire. Modern counter-battery radar, acoustic detection and digital command systems could reduce the interval between identifying a gun and firing back. Precision artillery and guided rockets allowed strikes against bridges, ammunition depots and command positions, while older unguided systems retained value in suppressing defensive areas. Both sides encountered shortages of particular rounds and replacement barrels. Russian forces increasingly employed glide bombs launched by aircraft outside some Ukrainian air-defense envelopes, adding substantial destructive power against fortified positions. Ukraine's defenders needed air-defense interceptors while also protecting cities and industry, forcing hard decisions about where scarce systems should be located. The technological history therefore includes not only inventions but the allocation of limited defenses among competing tasks.

Electronic warfare was an almost invisible part of the battlefield. Radio jamming could disrupt a drone or communications network, but indiscriminate interference might also impede friendly units and civilian signals. Drones increasingly used alternative navigation and, in some cases, wired or fiber-optic connections designed to resist radio jamming. These systems brought their own limitations in range, terrain and logistics. Both armies developed practices for rapidly analyzing enemy equipment and distributing countermeasures, making technical knowledge a form of organizational learning. A battlefield advantage depended on how quickly an institution could move from a local invention to training, procurement and maintenance across many units. A device that worked for an experimental detachment might perform differently under mass production and field conditions.

Unmanned naval systems provided another example of asymmetrical innovation. Ukraine used explosive boats and other systems against Russian naval assets, contributing to changes in Russian basing and operating patterns without possessing a comparable conventional surface fleet. The advantages were context-specific: a coastal sea with access to intelligence and target information presented opportunities that would not necessarily translate to every maritime theater. Similarly, long-range aerial drones allowed both countries to strike industrial and logistical targets far from trenches, but air defenses and geography determined results. The central continuity with earlier wars was the competition between attack, protection and logistics; the novelty lay in the affordability, availability and rapid adaptation of systems that made observation and precision attack more widespread.

Technology altered soldiers' experience. Movement under an observed sky became dangerous, casualty evacuation required new concealment, and a damaged vehicle could attract successive attacks. Troops had to learn to recognize drones by sound, shelter in protected positions and avoid unnecessary electronic emissions. Civilian communities near the front were also affected by short-range attacks that could reach streets and buses. The UN's 2026 monitoring separated long-range missiles and drones from short-range drone casualties, showing that different weapons created different patterns of harm. By November 2026 technological adaptation remained ongoing, and no single system had ended the underlying need for trained infantry, secure supply, political decision-making and the protection of civilians. The chapter should therefore treat military innovation as part of social and institutional history, not as a catalog of equipment specifications.

The growing availability of relatively inexpensive unmanned systems also changed the meaning of civilian technological capacity. Small software firms, volunteer workshops and improvised repair networks could contribute designs and components, while states still had to produce explosives, batteries, radios and communications equipment at scale. Drone videos circulating online created unusually abundant visual evidence, but the camera's view was selective: footage emphasized successful strikes, omitted failed launches, and rarely established the larger operational result. Countermeasures themselves evolved quickly. Jamming could interrupt navigation and control, prompting changes in signal management and some fiber-optic-guided systems; protective nets, decoys and dispersed equipment could reduce damage at the cost of mobility or labor. The result was not a simple replacement of artillery and infantry by drones but a continually changing interaction of surveillance, maneuver, firepower, industrial supply, improvisation and institutional learning. Technical innovation had profound human consequences, including persistent exposure for soldiers and civilians near the front.

Russian and Ukrainian Societies During the War

Section 71.44

Ukraine's Wartime Government, Martial Law, and Democratic Institutions

Ukraine entered the full-scale invasion with functioning elected national institutions: the presidency, Verkhovna Rada, cabinet, courts and local councils. President Volodymyr Zelenskyy declared martial law as Russian forces attacked, and parliament subsequently approved and renewed the legal framework. The government continued operating from Kyiv even during the battle for the capital, while local officials coordinated evacuations, public services and civilian defense. This continuity helped demonstrate that the Russian assumption of a rapid collapse was wrong. It should not, however, be confused with the absence of political conflict: opposition parties, journalists, municipalities and anticorruption organizations continued to debate decisions under extraordinary wartime conditions. The historical question is how institutions that had been contested in peacetime maintained functions while exercising emergency authority.

Martial law introduced major restrictions and responsibilities. Some movement and assembly rights were limited, military requirements expanded and particular media systems became more centralized. In 2022 Ukrainian authorities established a common television news broadcast widely known as the United News telethon, intended to coordinate information during the emergency. Critics later argued that the arrangement privileged government voices and reduced the diversity of television coverage, while officials cited the dangers of disinformation and the need for timely public instructions. Ukraine also restricted several political parties accused of pro-Russian ties through judicial or legislative processes. These policies require evaluation against national security needs and legal safeguards rather than an assumption that wartime democracy must operate exactly like peacetime politics.

Scheduled parliamentary and presidential elections did not occur while martial law remained in effect. The Ukrainian constitutional and statutory framework restricted elections under wartime conditions, and officials argued that polling would be unsafe and unfair with millions displaced, soldiers on the front, occupied regions inaccessible and substantial uncertainty over voter registers. Political opponents and civil organizations debated accountability, legitimacy and possible future arrangements. The absence of an election under martial law must not be equated automatically with a completed constitutional transfer of power or proof that officials enjoyed unlimited support. Neither does an assertion that elections would be difficult eliminate the importance of parliamentary oversight, courts, local councils and investigative reporting. A precise history explains the legal basis, renewals and public debate as of each date.

Decentralization reforms before 2022 had created local governments with expanded responsibilities, and their performance became critical during the invasion. Mayors, municipal workers, emergency services and volunteer organizations organized shelters, maintained water and heat, repaired roads and distributed assistance. Some local leaders were killed, abducted or displaced in occupied areas. In government-controlled regions the relationship between civilian administrations and military authorities evolved, sometimes creating tension over resources or public communications. The state did not function through the president alone. Ukraine's resilience depended on thousands of routine administrative decisions made by officials who had to manage budgets, schools and hospitals while responding to aerial attacks.

Corruption and procurement remained subjects of public controversy despite wartime unity. Journalists exposed allegations concerning military supplies, reconstruction contracts and the conduct of officials, prompting investigations and changes in government personnel in several periods. Such controversies must be treated according to the evidence available: an allegation is not a conviction, while the existence of an investigation shows that accountability systems continued to operate under pressure. International donors also placed importance on public financial controls and reform, especially as Ukraine sought closer integration with the European Union. The tension between rapid wartime decision-making and transparent oversight was genuine, and the historical analysis should not deny it because Ukraine was defending itself against invasion.

By 2026 Ukraine remained a state under martial law with civilian government, parliament, judiciary and an extensive network of municipalities, but also with severe constraints imposed by occupation, displacement, security requirements and the demands of military mobilization. Citizens' attitudes to the continuation of elections, negotiations and wartime governance varied and changed over time. Polls can illuminate opinion only when respondents can be reached safely and questions are interpreted carefully. The appropriate assessment is neither that the invasion suspended all democracy nor that emergency measures were costless. The war tested how a constitutional state could preserve public authority, civil liberties and democratic accountability while fighting an existential military conflict.

Section 71.45

Ukrainian Mobilization, Voluntary Service, Labor, and Social Organization

Ukraine's armed forces grew enormously after February 2022 as professional soldiers, reservists and volunteers joined mobilization. Territorial Defense Forces, established as a distinct component before the invasion, initially protected localities, checkpoints and infrastructure and later participated in front-line operations. Civilian volunteers supplied food, vehicles, medical equipment, protective clothing and increasingly drones. Women served in the armed forces, medical services, logistics, intelligence, communications and extensive civilian support networks; they also assumed additional responsibilities for families, displaced relatives and public services. The popular image of a nation united in immediate volunteering captures an important part of 2022 but cannot stand for every person's circumstances across five years of war. Service arrangements, risk and ability to contribute differed by age, occupation, region and family situation.

Ukraine faced persistent personnel pressures as the war continued. Heavy casualties, long deployments and the requirements of defending an extended front placed strain on experienced brigades. Mobilization rules determined who could be called up, who received deferments and how medical fitness was assessed. In 2024 the authorities reduced the general mobilization age threshold for men from 27 to 25 and introduced additional procedures concerning registration and contact with recruitment institutions. Administrative implementation varied and generated disputes about fairness, enforcement, corruption and the treatment of persons with disabilities or caregiving obligations. The statutory categories should be stated precisely: not every Ukrainian male adult had the same obligations, and voluntary enlistment by younger people was distinct from ordinary mandatory call-up rules.

Military service created competing claims on social labor. A medical worker might be needed in a military hospital or a municipal clinic; an engineer could maintain power infrastructure or work in a defense plant. Farmers, railway workers, teachers and construction workers were essential to civilian survival even when their work did not involve carrying weapons. Governments therefore used reservations and deferments to preserve critical sectors. Such decisions could be controversial when well-connected employers obtained protection or when poorer families believed they bore disproportionate costs. The historical analysis should examine the public rules and documented practices, not assume that every exemption was corrupt or every mobilization order lawfully issued. Maintaining national life required more than filling combat units.

The longer the war continued, the more difficult the balance between service and rotation became. Soldiers and families asked when those mobilized in 2022 could receive extended leave or demobilization; commanders argued that predictable replacement was impossible without adequate reserves. Post-traumatic stress, physical disability, rehabilitation and the social reintegration of injured personnel created further obligations. Women and other relatives frequently handled childcare, household income, military welfare inquiries and searches for missing people. Associations of families and veterans became important civic actors. Medical rehabilitation was not merely a postwar task: it had to operate throughout the conflict and affected whether injured individuals could work, study or return to civilian communities.

Voluntary civilian organization evolved from emergency response into specialized supply and advocacy systems. Groups raised funds for particular units, developed drone assembly networks, supported internally displaced people and monitored local public spending. They could operate faster than formal institutions but also faced problems of transparency, uneven funding and dependence on charismatic organizers. Relations between volunteers and military procurement departments sometimes involved cooperation and criticism. This provides a valuable institutional comparison with Russia: both sides relied on informal supplies, but their political environments and degrees of public criticism differed. The existence of volunteer networks is evidence of social capacity, not necessarily proof that the state performed every function effectively.

As of November 2026 the manpower challenge remained one of the central constraints on Ukraine's ability to hold territory and conduct operations. Judging mobilization solely from numbers recruited would neglect training quality, command, medical support, unit cohesion and the social consequences of prolonged service. Students should examine how military requirements affected education, labor markets, family responsibilities and the distribution of political rights. The war did not create a simple division between combatants and a passive home front. Ukrainian civilians supplied, repaired, cared, criticized and sometimes protested decisions while soldiers held positions under sustained fire. Their interconnected experiences form the social history of wartime national defense.

Section 71.46

Displacement, Refugees, Families, Education, and Civilian Survival

Russia's full-scale invasion caused one of Europe's largest displacement crises since the Second World War. In the first weeks of 2022 millions crossed borders or moved westward inside Ukraine, often leaving with limited possessions and uncertain information about relatives. Poland, Romania, Moldova, Slovakia, Hungary and other neighboring countries became major points of arrival, while European Union temporary-protection arrangements offered residence and access to services for many displaced Ukrainians. People did not all follow the same route. Some fled from occupied territory through Russia or Belarus; others relocated within Ukraine or moved multiple times as fighting and air attacks reached successive places of refuge. The categories 'refugee,' 'internally displaced person,' 'returnee' and 'person under occupation' describe different legal and statistical circumstances.

By 2026 UNHCR's operational overview estimated approximately 3.7 million internally displaced people in Ukraine and more than 5.9 million refugees abroad, predominantly in Europe. These were estimates within specific reporting frameworks and should not be added to cumulative border-crossing figures, which count journeys rather than unique displaced people. The same person could cross a border several times, move within Ukraine before leaving or return temporarily without establishing permanent residence. Many residents of occupied areas were difficult to register. Data collection also differs among host governments and humanitarian agencies. An instructional table should therefore identify the date, source, definition and whether counts refer to stocks of people displaced at a particular time or cumulative movements across several years.

Displacement reshaped family life. Children changed schools or studied remotely while one parent remained in Ukraine for military service or employment. Older relatives could be unwilling or unable to travel, leaving families divided among cities and countries. Women, who made up a substantial share of adult refugees, navigated childcare, employment and language requirements, while some men faced legal limits on departing Ukraine under wartime mobilization policy. These broad patterns conceal varied individual experiences, including men who qualified for travel exemptions and women who served in the military. A household's ability to find accommodation depended on savings, family networks, host-country policies, disability access and local housing shortages. People arriving with relatives or specialist skills might integrate more quickly than those who had lost documents or required continuous medical treatment.

Education illustrated both adaptation and inequality. Ukrainian teachers continued online classes, host-country schools enrolled displaced children, and some pupils combined two curricula while uncertain whether they would return. Damage to school buildings and repeated air alerts disrupted teaching inside Ukraine, even in areas far from the front. Universities relocated departments or protected archives, while laboratories and cultural institutions struggled to retain personnel. A child's interrupted schooling is not adequately measured by the number of destroyed classrooms alone; access to devices, safe internet, language support and psychologically stable conditions also matters. The war affected successive cohorts, and the longer separation lasted, the more complicated school transitions and professional qualifications became.

Housing and employment posed major problems for internally displaced persons. Families moving from eastern industrial communities to western Ukrainian towns faced differences in available jobs, rental prices and public services. Municipal governments and volunteers converted buildings into shelters and sought longer-term accommodation, but funds and land were limited. People who had fled with disabilities, chronic illnesses or dependents often encountered inaccessible housing and difficulty obtaining consistent treatment. At the same time, displaced workers opened businesses, joined local labor markets and supported host communities. A social history must record agency and contribution alongside vulnerability. Some people returned to damaged homes between periods of bombardment because employment or family ties made remaining elsewhere impossible.

The end of displacement could not be dated simply by the return of a town to Ukrainian control. Mines, damaged schools, lost water systems, insecurity and ruined housing prevented safe return. Those from occupied regions faced additional barriers concerning documents, property and threats from occupying authorities. Humanitarian organizations distinguished emergency shelter from durable solutions and long-term reconstruction. By November 2026 the length of the war meant that questions of residence, identity, pensions, education and professional careers had become inseparable from future recovery. The historical consequences extended beyond immediate refugee totals to demographic changes and the distribution of skills among Ukraine and its neighbors, whose permanent outcomes remained uncertain.

The social meaning of displacement cannot be recovered from a single registration total. An individual may leave a city for a nearby rural district, move again to a regional center, seek protection abroad and later return temporarily to check property; administrative systems may record some of these movements without identifying one continuous life history. Families could be separated by military service, disability, age and access to transportation, while host communities faced pressure on schools, rents and health services. Protection systems sometimes enabled displaced people to study and work, but legal status did not guarantee equivalent professional recognition, housing security or family reunification. Historians should therefore complement UNHCR's comparable aggregates with dated household interviews and local administrative records, observing consent and privacy. The experiences of internally displaced Ukrainian residents, refugees abroad and civilians unable to leave occupied territory are related but should not be collapsed into a single category of migration.

Section 71.47

Political Control, Media, and Public Expression in Wartime Russia

The Russian state responded to the invasion by intensifying restrictions on independent reporting and public opposition. On 24 February 2022 the media regulator Roskomnadzor instructed outlets to rely on official Russian sources when describing the operation. In early March parliament enacted measures criminalizing the dissemination of information that authorities classified as knowingly false about the armed forces and penalizing actions described as discrediting them. These laws were broadened and enforced through criminal prosecutions, administrative fines and restrictions on publications and platforms. The government presented the measures as defense against disinformation and hostile propaganda; rights organizations described them as instruments for suppressing factual reporting and criticism of the invasion. The historian must explain the legal provisions and documented enforcement rather than treat the label 'fake news' as an objective description of defendants' statements.

Independent newspapers, broadcasters and websites faced closure, blocking, forced relocation or severe pressure to change editorial practices. Some journalists continued reporting from outside Russia, while others remained and tried to preserve limited space for investigation. Audiences could seek information through encrypted messaging, virtual private networks, social platforms and foreign services, but access depended on technical knowledge, language and personal risk. State-aligned television continued to frame the invasion in official terminology and to emphasize threats from the West and alleged Ukrainian extremism. The resulting media environment was not a total absence of alternative information, but it made reaching a broad domestic audience and publishing dissent significantly more difficult. Studies of public opinion must account for the informational and legal environment in which responses were given.

Public demonstrations against the war occurred in several Russian cities in 2022 despite arrests and restrictions. Individual protesters used signs, social-media messages, performances and local political meetings to register opposition. Rights organizations such as OVD-Info documented detentions, prosecutions and the use of new criminal laws; their records are an important source but depend on reports received and verified within their methodology. Some defendants faced severe prison sentences for criticism of the military or allegations about civilian harm. The forms of dissent changed as the risks increased: public rallies became rarer, while informal support networks and independent media abroad continued. The decline in visible protest cannot be read as direct evidence that everyone supported the war.

Political opposition operated under constraints that preceded 2022. Organizations associated with Alexei Navalny had been declared extremist in 2021, and many activists had left Russia or faced prosecution. Navalny, imprisoned when the invasion began, died in a Russian prison in February 2024; his death and disputes over his treatment became important issues in Russia's human-rights and political history. The chapter should distinguish documented prison conditions and official statements from allegations of specific responsibility that require separate evidence. Other opposition figures, including local activists, journalists, trade unionists and political representatives, experienced restrictions in different ways. A narrative focused exclusively on one national figure would obscure the dispersed character of dissent.

State institutions also used patriotic education, commemorations and public events to legitimize the war. Schools and cultural organizations introduced lessons emphasizing official descriptions of the invasion, and some local governments organized support campaigns. Participation could represent belief, institutional obligation or pressure from employers and schools. Russian society was never a single ideological category: relatives of serving soldiers, pensioners, urban professionals and rural communities could interpret the same events through different experiences. Polls conducted when criticism carried risks require analysis of question wording, nonresponse and sampling. The historian should avoid ungrounded claims that either total enthusiasm or universal quiet opposition prevailed.

By 2026 restrictions on public expression had become part of the war's institutional landscape, affecting universities, courts, media businesses and everyday conversation. Legal repression had material consequences: fines, imprisonment, job loss, exclusion from educational institutions and decisions to leave the country. The continued existence of online criticism and expatriate news outlets does not negate the scale of domestic restrictions. The core historical question is how the Russian government managed information and public mobilization while sustaining a costly war, and how citizens navigated the incentives and risks created by that system. This is a distinct subject from battlefield censorship or ordinary secrecy around military operations and requires its own documentary history.

Section 71.48

Russian Families, Mobilization, Emigration, and Regional Social Costs

War reached Russian families through military service, economic pressure and the search for information. A household might receive a mobilization summons, experience a relative's departure for the front or learn of death from a commander, hospital or online report. Official casualty figures were sparse and politically sensitive, while independent researchers used obituaries, cemetery records and public notices to document identified deaths. These named-death records are minimum counts of persons independently identified, not comprehensive estimates of all Russian military losses. Families also faced long periods when relatives were missing, hospitalized or held prisoner. The uncertainty itself became a social cost, affecting employment, childcare and decisions about seeking assistance from the state.

Regional inequality shaped the impact. Defense contracts and mobilization requirements were administered through local offices, and signing bonuses or survivor compensation could be substantial relative to wages in poorer districts. Some regions reported higher levels of recruitment and publicly identified deaths than large wealthy cities, though comparisons are sensitive to population, disclosure practices and local economic conditions. It would be irresponsible to convert regional patterns into assumptions about an entire ethnic community's willingness to fight. Individuals from Buryatia, Dagestan, central Russia or Moscow had varied motives and circumstances. Regional governance, economic opportunities and military recruitment networks provide better explanatory variables than stereotypes about identity.

Migration was another consequence. After February 2022 and the September mobilization announcement, many Russians traveled abroad for temporary safety, political reasons or employment. Neighboring Armenia, Georgia, Kazakhstan and other states experienced substantial arrivals, while some migrants later returned or moved again. Host countries confronted changes in housing markets and labor demand as well as domestic debates about accepting citizens of a state conducting a war. Estimates of the Russian exodus differed because border crossings could be counted multiple times and residence records did not consistently distinguish temporary visitors from permanent emigrants. The economic effects likewise varied: some skilled workers continued working remotely for foreign employers, while others faced banking restrictions, immigration uncertainty and family separation.

Military compensation became a major interface between the state and households. Families of killed or wounded personnel could seek federal and regional payments, medical care, rehabilitation and educational benefits. Access depended on records and classification of service, and disputes arose when families had difficulty confirming a death or establishing eligibility. Disabled veterans required prosthetics, ongoing treatment and employment opportunities. These costs persisted long after an individual unit rotated away from a front. The growth of veteran populations also created new civic and political constituencies whose future influence could not be known at the November 2026 cutoff. A historian should examine enacted benefit rules and documented experiences instead of assuming that every promised payment reached recipients promptly.

The war altered labor markets and household budgets. Defense production raised demand for workers in certain industrial towns, while emigration, military recruitment and demographic trends created shortages elsewhere. Inflation and interest rates affected housing and consumption, and state subsidies or bonuses could temporarily raise income in households connected to war-related employment. Economic indicators therefore produced mixed experiences: a factory worker might gain wages while another family faced higher prices or loss of a breadwinner. The interpretation of national averages requires attention to the distribution of benefits and costs among regions, generations and occupations. This social differentiation helps explain why political support could coexist with complaints about specific policies.

By 2026 families remained central actors in the war's domestic history. Some petitioned for rotation or information, others organized aid to units, and many managed bereavement privately. The state's limits on political organizing meant that family grievances did not automatically become visible national opposition. Military recruitment, industrial output and official messaging could continue even as private costs accumulated. The chapter should resist converting this tension into a predicted political outcome. It should instead document the institutions through which military obligations entered everyday life and the varied choices families made under constraint. The long-term effect of death, disability, displacement and reduced trust in institutions would require evidence from future years beyond the present edition.

Section 71.49

National Identity, Historical Memory, Information Warfare, and Public Opinion

The war intensified a struggle over national identity and historical memory that had developed over centuries but acquired new political urgency after 2014. Russian government speeches invoked the medieval polity of Rus', the Second World War, NATO expansion and supposed obligations to protect Russian-speaking populations. Ukrainian officials and intellectuals emphasized sovereign statehood, resistance to imperial domination and the right to choose political and cultural affiliations. These arguments were not merely rhetorical decoration: they influenced school curricula, commemoration, laws on language and monuments, and the ways people understood sacrifice. Yet an historical interpretation should not assume that medieval state boundaries or nineteenth-century imperial claims supply legal entitlement to modern territory. The contemporary war resulted from modern decisions taken by governments and military leaders.

Public commemorations reflected the competing narratives. In Russia, official ceremonies increasingly framed the invasion as part of an ongoing struggle against fascism or Western hostility, incorporating older symbols of victory in 1945. In Ukraine, memorials to the dead, community fundraising and ceremonies for returning prisoners emphasized present-day national defense, while public debate reconsidered the place of Russian imperial and Soviet symbols. Changes to street names, monuments and school programs were controversial in some communities and welcomed in others. A process of cultural reorientation cannot be treated as proof that every Ukrainian held identical opinions about language, heritage or the appropriate relationship with Russia. Identity was lived differently among refugees, soldiers, residents of occupied areas and people speaking Russian at home.

Information warfare operated through state broadcasting, social media, messaging channels, online video and forged or misleading material. Governments disseminated claims about enemy casualties, troop movements and alleged abuses, and supporters amplified material that served political objectives. Some posts contained genuine documentation; others were misleading because images were undated, taken elsewhere or stripped of context. Open-source investigators developed techniques to compare landmarks, shadows, satellite records and metadata, but they still required caution about manipulation or incomplete coverage. Students should practice identifying a video's first known publication, whether it can be located, and the extent of what it proves. Viral popularity is not evidence of accuracy, and widespread repetition can conceal dependence on one original unverified source.

Public opinion was difficult to interpret in both countries for different reasons. In Ukraine, displacement, occupied territory, mobilization and security concerns could exclude substantial populations from ordinary surveys. In Russia, fear of state consequences could affect willingness to respond or express criticism, especially when questions concerned the military. Question wording matters: 'support the armed forces' may elicit a different response from 'support continuing the war under present conditions' or 'support negotiations.' Poll results should identify the survey organization, sampling frame, fieldwork date and limits. The historian should also distinguish public confidence in leaders from agreement with every war policy, and a person's criticism of mobilization from rejection of the invasion itself.

Experiences of combat and occupation could change identities as much as official programs. Ukrainian soldiers from different regions and linguistic backgrounds served together, and Russian speakers were often prominent in resistance to the invasion. Families who had once maintained close ties across the international border found travel and communication increasingly difficult. Residents under Russian occupation faced coercive policies affecting passports, education and public symbolism, creating circumstances in which outward behavior could not be assumed to reveal genuine belief. In Russia, some families embraced patriotic messages while others avoided discussing the war in public. Such diversity is important precisely because governments often claim to speak for an entire nation or language community.

A historical assessment through November 2026 should therefore identify identity politics as both a cause employed in justifications and a consequence transformed by war. Claims about historic unity did not explain why a particular military invasion began on a particular day; they supplied ideas used by decision-makers and public institutions. War experiences, educational policies, personal losses and migration then reshaped how communities interpreted those ideas. The ultimate effects on future Russian and Ukrainian generations cannot be known from the present cutoff. The chapter must document the processes visible in sources without declaring national identity fixed, uniform or permanently determined by the conflict.

Section 71.50

Russia's Wartime Budget, Defense Production, and Economic Mobilization

Russia's wartime economy was reshaped by a rapid increase in military expenditure, procurement and public employment connected to defense. Early Western expectations of economic collapse after the 2022 sanctions underestimated Moscow's ability to redirect trade, use fiscal buffers and support domestic demand, but resilience did not mean that the war was costless. The federal budget allocated a growing share to national defense and related security priorities, while manufacturing sectors producing ammunition, vehicles and military equipment expanded. State orders supported jobs and wages in some industrial regions, even as civilian projects faced higher interest rates, labor shortages or postponed investment. A history of the economy should therefore separate the level of aggregate output from the composition of output and the opportunity costs of directing resources to war.

The first economic shock included a sharp movement in the ruble, restrictions on foreign transactions and the departure or restructuring of many Western businesses. Russia used capital controls, altered payment arrangements and shifted supply chains toward countries that continued trading with it. Over time the challenge moved toward inflation, competition for labor, equipment replacement and the financing of a prolonged military effort. The Bank of Russia raised interest rates aggressively in response to price pressures, with tight monetary policy affecting household credit and business investment. The central bank's October 2026 survey recorded actual real GDP growth of about 4.1 percent in 2023 and 4.9 percent in 2024, followed by about 1.0 percent in 2025; survey participants expected only modest growth in 2026. These figures come from a specified statistical series and are not measures of how comfortable households felt.

Defense production combined expansion with constraint. Russia repaired older tanks and artillery systems, increased ammunition and drone output, and modified procurement to integrate new battlefield lessons. Factories needed machine tools, electronic components, skilled labor and reliable finance. Restrictions on some foreign technology prompted domestic substitution and purchasing through alternative markets, but substitutions could be expensive or less efficient. The availability of imported goods through third countries did not mean sanctions had no effect; it often meant that access required more complex intermediaries and higher transaction costs. Conversely, economic models predicting that every sanction would immediately halt production proved too mechanical. Understanding wartime industrial capacity requires studying inventories, replacement rates, product quality and the ability to distribute finished equipment to units.

Fiscal sustainability created trade-offs. Increased defense spending could support near-term economic activity while consuming labor, materials and public revenue that might otherwise have improved health care, housing or transport. Regional authorities funded signing bonuses, support payments and infrastructure related to military facilities, but their revenues and borrowing conditions differed. Continued energy exports provided income even as prices and destinations changed. Budget figures are complicated by classification and secrecy, especially when military expenditures are spread across several line items. It is therefore inappropriate to invent precise comprehensive war costs from a single public defense-budget number. Analysts should distinguish direct appropriations, classified expenditure, compensation to families and the wider economic losses caused by the war.

Inflation and labor shortages affected the civilian economy. High demand from defense contracts and recruitment drew workers toward particular industries and regions, while emigration and demographics reduced available labor elsewhere. The October 2026 Bank of Russia survey projected consumer-price inflation of around 7.1 percent for 2026 and reported a 2025 average key rate above 19 percent, illustrating the monetary constraints under which the economy operated. These are dated estimates and historical series that may be revised. A rising nominal wage does not guarantee rising purchasing power when housing, services and food become more expensive. Households receiving military payments might experience income gains, while others were burdened by high credit costs or lost relatives. Distribution matters as much as growth.

By the evidence cutoff Russia had built a substantial apparatus for sustaining war but faced questions about the efficiency and long-term effects of that apparatus. The state could command production and recruit labor, yet industrial expansion in ammunition or vehicles did not automatically increase civilian productivity or create durable prosperity. The economy continued functioning while becoming more dependent on military demand and state direction in key sectors. A scholarly evaluation should neither deny the demonstrated capacity to adapt to sanctions nor mistake continued output for an absence of structural cost. The central historical relationship is between military strategy, fiscal priorities, labor markets and the capacity of institutions to redirect resources over several years.

Section 71.51

Sanctions, Financial Restrictions, Energy Exports, and Trade Realignment

International sanctions against Russia expanded rapidly after February 2022. The United States, European Union, United Kingdom, Canada, Japan and others restricted transactions with selected banks, access to technology, financing, exports and the assets of designated individuals and companies. Some Russian central-bank reserves held abroad became immobilized, and governments imposed restrictions on aviation, shipping, insurance and business activity. The scope was extensive but not universal: many states did not adopt the sanctions or applied different rules. Russia responded with capital controls, restrictions on certain exports and attempts to use alternative currencies and financial channels. The historian must distinguish sanctions legally adopted by a government from voluntary corporate withdrawal and from secondary commercial effects caused by fear of legal or reputational risk.

Energy trade was a central focus. European buyers reduced pipeline gas purchases from Russia, while Russian oil exports shifted increasingly toward Asian customers and other markets. The price-cap coalition introduced a cap on seaborne Russian crude oil from December 2022, with separate measures for petroleum products in February 2023. The mechanism targeted access to shipping and insurance services provided by participating jurisdictions, not every purchase of Russian oil worldwide. Russia and traders developed alternative transport, insurance and intermediary arrangements, often described collectively as a shadow fleet, though vessels differed in ownership, condition and legal status. Oil revenues depended on the global price, discounts offered to buyers, transport costs, output and enforcement. A single total for oil exports cannot establish whether a particular sanction succeeded in reducing revenue.

The European Union continued adopting packages through 2026. Its July 2026 twenty-first package included restrictions affecting financial entities, additional vessels, and trade in sensitive technology. The EU also modified its oil price-cap mechanisms over time. These measures should be described according to their operative dates, because a future prohibition announced in 2025 was not necessarily in force during the period being discussed. Sanctions in energy also created costs for importing countries that had depended on Russian supply, leading to diversification, higher storage investment and changes in demand. The geopolitical impact was therefore shared, although Russia's invasion supplied the immediate cause of many of the policy changes. Europe's energy transition was not a single instantaneous switch from Russian fuel to other sources.

Technology and dual-use goods posed enforcement challenges. Components used in civilian electronics, vehicles or industrial equipment can also enter military supply chains. Western governments sought to restrict sensitive semiconductors, machine tools and manufacturing technology, while intermediaries in non-sanctioning jurisdictions could sometimes facilitate transfers. Investigations tracing components recovered from weapons revealed the complexity of global supply chains but did not imply that every foreign-made part was shipped knowingly for military use. Effective enforcement required customs records, corporate due diligence, cooperation with transit states and attention to re-export practices. Russia increased substitution and repair while remaining dependent on some imported components and equipment.

Sanctions did not operate independently of Russian economic policy or the global business cycle. Ruble movements reflected capital controls and export receipts as well as sanctions; inflation reflected wages, fiscal demand and supply problems; changes in trade volumes reflected both legal restrictions and evolving buyer preferences. Some sectors gained protection from competition, while others faced higher costs or lost technology partnerships. Economists use counterfactual models to estimate what would have happened without sanctions, but those estimates depend on assumptions about energy prices and policy responses. Students should be taught to distinguish a sanction's objective, its legal coverage, its observed effects and the uncertainty of attributing an entire economic trend to it.

By November 2026 Russia had not been isolated from the world economy, but its trade and financial networks had been reorganized and made more costly in important areas. The war accelerated Russia's commercial ties with China and other buyers while reducing some long-established European relationships. The continued flow of goods through new channels and the growing burdens of compliance were both real. The appropriate historical conclusion is nuanced: sanctions imposed significant constraints and stimulated adaptation, their effects varied by sector and over time, and their ultimate contribution to the war's outcome remained unknowable at the cutoff. Evaluating them demands dated official regulations, independent trade data and careful attention to what each statistic actually measures.

Section 71.52

Ukraine's Wartime Economy, Public Finance, and International Assistance

Ukraine's economy experienced a severe shock in 2022 as fighting, occupation, damaged infrastructure, disrupted ports and mass displacement interrupted production. Agriculture, manufacturing, mining, retail and services were affected differently according to location and supply chains. The government maintained payments to public employees and the armed forces while tax receipts fell and urgent military spending increased. The National Bank of Ukraine imposed emergency financial measures to stabilize banking and the currency. Ukrainian businesses adapted by relocating machinery, shifting operations westward, working with backup power and using foreign markets when access to traditional routes failed. Aggregate output statistics describe a national recession but conceal striking regional variation between businesses destroyed at the front and firms that survived or expanded in safer cities.

Public finance depended heavily on assistance from international partners. Governments and multilateral institutions provided grants, concessional loans, guarantees and other support, helping Ukraine pay for pensions, teachers, health care and administrative functions while domestic revenue supported defense and other needs. The distinction between military equipment and budget support is important: receiving a weapon does not give a ministry funds to pay a hospital worker, and a budget grant cannot directly substitute for a missing artillery round. Aid announcements also differ from funds appropriated, contracted, delivered or spent. A credible account should identify the dates and categories of assistance rather than add headlines from different programs without correcting for overlap.

Ukrainian monetary authorities confronted difficult choices between defending the currency, financing government operations and containing inflation. Interest-rate policy, international reserves, banking supervision and exchange-rate management affected household savings and business decisions. Restrictions on foreign exchange in wartime created costs for commerce but could also reduce immediate financial instability. The return of Ukrainian grain and other exports through evolving transport corridors supported foreign-currency earnings, though port attacks and rising insurance costs disrupted trade. Labor shortages grew as people joined the armed forces, relocated abroad or moved internally, while employers tried to retain staff. A manufacturer's survival might depend as much on the reliability of electricity as on nominal access to a loan.

Energy damage increasingly constrained the economy. Electricity interruptions reduced output at factories, disrupted refrigeration and raised operating costs for hospitals, shops and service providers. Enterprises bought generators, batteries or other backup systems, but those investments diverted resources from expansion and created fuel costs. Schools and clinics worked around outages while struggling with damaged buildings and displaced personnel. Infrastructure repairs and air-defense systems were therefore productive economic investments as well as security measures. Some businesses adopted innovative digital and remote-work arrangements, and technology sectors continued operating in altered forms; such adaptation should be documented without romanticizing the difficulties facing workers who lacked reliable housing or power.

International economic integration continued to change. Ukraine became an EU candidate country in 2022 and advanced accession-related reform efforts under wartime constraints, while trade agreements and temporary market-access measures affected exporters and farmers. These developments generated disputes both inside Ukraine and with some EU neighbors over grain and transport competition. Governance reforms, public procurement oversight and anti-corruption enforcement became central to securing long-term assistance and rebuilding institutions. A nation fighting for survival still faced political choices over how grants and loans were managed. The historian must not treat foreign assistance as a uniform gift without conditions, nor assume that ordinary democratic accountability was impossible because the government was under attack.

By the 2026 cutoff Ukraine had maintained functioning public finance, banking and essential services despite massive damage and extraordinary military expenditure. This capacity depended on domestic taxes, the efforts of households and businesses, and substantial external assistance. Its sustainability remained vulnerable to continued attacks, labor shortages and changes in donor policy. Recovery statistics were snapshots under continuing war rather than final bills. The most useful historical analysis compares the economic mechanisms that preserved state capacity with the losses and inequalities they could not prevent. Ukraine's wartime economy was neither simply destroyed nor unambiguously thriving; it was being continually reorganized under coercive military pressure.

Section 71.53

Destruction, Reconstruction Needs, and Economic Recovery

The physical destruction caused by the war affected housing, roads, bridges, energy facilities, schools, hospitals, farmland and industry. In some towns the damage was concentrated along streets subject to artillery fighting; elsewhere a single missile could disable a major substation or destroy an apartment block. The costs were more than the replacement value of buildings. Enterprises lost production, workers lost income, governments lost tax receipts and communities lost services or population. The most important analytical distinction is therefore between direct physical damage, economic losses over time, and the resources required for recovery and reconstruction. These are separate categories and cannot be summed or compared without checking the methodology used to measure them.

The joint Rapid Damage and Needs Assessment published in February 2026 by the Ukrainian government, World Bank, European Commission and United Nations presented an evidence-dated benchmark covering conditions through December 31, 2025. It estimated approximately $195.1 billion in direct damage, approximately $666.7 billion in socioeconomic losses and approximately $587.7 billion in recovery and reconstruction needs over a ten-year horizon. Those figures represent different analytical constructs; the reconstruction figure is not the arithmetic sum of the two other categories. The assessment also covered urgent repairs already undertaken and continuing priorities for housing, energy, transport and public services. It did not include all destruction caused after its cutoff, and subsequent figures must not be retroactively inserted into its 2025-based accounting.

Housing recovery was a particularly difficult problem because buildings could be physically repaired while neighborhoods remained unsafe or depopulated. Owners needed proof of title, damage documentation, contractors, materials and access to insurance or public compensation. In occupied territory these prerequisites were often unavailable, and displaced families faced the choice between waiting for return and establishing new lives elsewhere. Municipalities needed functioning water, schools, transport and health services before large-scale repopulation could occur. A restored apartment is not a durable solution if surrounding streets remain mined or if the local economy has collapsed. Rebuilding therefore required coordination across sectors rather than a list of isolated construction projects.

Energy infrastructure posed urgent and repeated demands. Power lines and substations were repaired under threat of renewed attack, while hospitals, water facilities and heating systems needed reliable backup. Engineers sometimes restored basic service rapidly using temporary equipment, but long-term resilience required redesigned networks, protection of key installations and sufficient generation capacity. Similar issues affected railway bridges and ports whose restoration had direct military and economic value. Such investments were often expensive even where the visible physical damage was modest. The difference between a replaced component and a resilient system matters when assessing whether recovery has actually occurred.

Land contamination created an additional long-term burden. Mines, unexploded shells and damaged industrial sites made fields, roads, forests and riverbanks hazardous after fighting moved away. Demining requires specialized personnel, surveys and time, and the area suspected of contamination is not the same as the area confirmed to contain explosive hazards. Agricultural recovery depends on access to land and transport as well as the safety of workers. Flooding after the Kakhovka Dam's destruction changed water systems and damaged infrastructure across the lower Dnipro region. Environmental restoration may take years, and uncertainty about responsibility for particular damage must not prevent documentation of its existence and consequences.

Reconstruction is also a problem of institutions and trust. Public procurement, ownership records, anti-corruption controls, donor coordination and the participation of local residents influence whether projects serve displaced and surviving communities. Builders need labor, and thousands of trained workers are serving in the military or living abroad. Private investment may return to some regions earlier than others, raising concerns about unequal recovery. As of November 2026 no final reconstruction bill could be stated because hostilities continued. The chapter should present the 2026 assessment as a benchmark for planning, with explicit cutoff and categories, and treat the future pace and political arrangements of rebuilding as unresolved historical questions.

Reconstruction estimates are especially sensitive to the difference between replacement and transformation. Replacing a destroyed school at its former capacity may be cheaper than constructing a safer, accessible facility with shelters and modern energy systems; restoration of an electric substation may require redesign because the threat of repeated strikes changes engineering requirements. A community whose residents have scattered may face a different long-term need from an intact city that has absorbed evacuees. Financial pledges, committed budget appropriations, contracted projects and completed construction are separate stages and should not be totaled as though they were finished recovery. Landmines, unexploded ordnance, damaged land records and disputed ownership complicate the return of farming and housing even after fighting moves elsewhere. The joint RDNA estimates are therefore planning tools based on a dated inventory and explicit methodology, not a final price for restoring all future losses. The question of who can safely return and participate in decisions is as important as the national financial estimate.

Section 71.54

Agriculture, Global Food Security, Energy Markets, and Environmental Damage

Ukraine and Russia occupy important positions in world markets for grain, edible oils, fertilizers and energy. The invasion disrupted planting, transport and exports, raising concern among countries dependent on Black Sea supply. Ukrainian farmers faced mined fields, labor shortages, damaged storage and uncertainty about whether ports would be accessible. Russian exports of agricultural products and fertilizers also became entangled in shipping, payment and insurance difficulties. Global food prices respond to harvests elsewhere, currency movements and government policy as well as war, so a price spike cannot be attributed to a single event. Nonetheless, uncertainty about Black Sea routes and the destruction of Ukrainian agricultural infrastructure were major factors in the international response during 2022.

The Black Sea Grain Initiative, agreed in July 2022 under UN and Turkish involvement, provided a monitored system for exports from three Ukrainian ports. The UN reported that more than 32 million metric tons of grain and foodstuffs moved during its operation, which ended when the agreement was not renewed in July 2023. This was an important success in wartime commercial coordination, not a general peace treaty or a guarantee that every Ukrainian port was secure. The Joint Coordination Centre included representatives of Ukraine, Russia, Turkey and the United Nations and oversaw procedures for vessels. Russian objections concerning its own agricultural exports and Western restrictions formed part of the negotiation, while many importing states wanted predictable supply regardless of the political disagreement.

After the initiative ended, Ukraine developed other export routes through the Black Sea and along land and river corridors. Grain and oilseeds also traveled through neighboring European states, generating political tension when local producers faced new competition or transport bottlenecks. Insurance premiums, port capacity, harvest size and navigation risks affected shipment volumes. Reports about 2026 monthly exports should specify whether they cover grain alone, oilseeds, all cargo or a particular port grouping. Comparing a September figure with the previous year's September may show a real change in flow but does not by itself prove that naval attacks caused every difference. Maritime transport was an economic and diplomatic system as well as a target of military operations.

Energy markets underwent parallel changes. Russia redirected substantial oil exports toward non-European buyers as European states reduced their dependence on Russian supplies. Gas trade required different infrastructure, and pipeline changes created distinct effects from oil shipments carried by tankers. Sanctions and price caps sought to reduce Russian revenues while limiting disruption to global supply, but the outcome depended on enforcement, discounts and the availability of alternative routes. European countries invested in LNG terminals, storage and diversified suppliers, while the war intensified debate about energy security and the transition away from fossil fuels. These changes affected household bills and industrial production beyond the battlefield and formed part of the war's wider economic history.

Environmental damage occurred through fires, damaged chemical plants, oil spills, contaminated water, minefields and the destruction of military equipment. The June 2023 collapse of the Kakhovka Dam flooded communities and altered reservoir conditions important for agriculture, industry and nuclear-plant cooling arrangements. Investigators should distinguish observed environmental consequences from disputed attribution of particular causes. Some damaged ecosystems may recover with time, while chemical contamination or explosive hazards require active remediation. The conflict also disrupted conservation work and access to scientific monitoring stations. Environmental histories are necessarily provisional while access to occupied or dangerous areas is limited.

The wider world's response reflected both humanitarian concern and national interests. Countries importing wheat or vegetable oil sought affordable supplies; shipping firms assessed risk; international organizations coordinated assistance; farmers in neighboring states worried about competition; energy exporters gained or lost market share. Ukraine's agricultural and maritime economy could not be separated from diplomacy with Turkey, the European Union and other partners. The war's global effects were therefore uneven, with some consumers exposed to higher prices and some producers benefiting from altered trade. The chapter should establish documented mechanisms and avoid presenting every movement in international food or energy prices as a direct consequence of Russia's invasion alone.

Section 71.55

The United Nations, International Law, and Responses to the Invasion

The invasion forced international institutions to confront the limits of collective security when a permanent member of the United Nations Security Council was itself the attacking state. Russia could veto substantive Security Council resolutions directed against its conduct, but the General Assembly held emergency sessions and adopted resolutions describing the invasion as aggression and calling for withdrawal. On March 2, 2022 a resolution demanding Russia end the invasion received 141 votes in favor, five against and 35 abstentions. The vote was evidence of a broad international legal and political position, not a declaration that all states had identical interests or agreed on particular sanctions. Later resolutions condemned annexation claims and addressed humanitarian consequences. Students should distinguish the legal standing of sovereign states from the practical limits of resolutions that cannot directly compel battlefield compliance.

The UN Charter's prohibition on the threat or use of force against another state's territorial integrity is fundamental to the case. Ukraine invoked its right of self-defense and requested military and humanitarian assistance. Russia offered legal and political arguments invoking the defense of Donbas populations, recognition of self-proclaimed entities, NATO threats and allegations of genocide. These arguments must be attributed and evaluated against legal evidence rather than repeated as neutral descriptions. The International Court of Justice, in provisional measures ordered March 16, 2022 in a dispute concerning the Genocide Convention, directed Russia to suspend the operations begun on February 24. That ruling had a specified procedural status; it was not a final adjudication of every legal issue raised by the war.

The international response was far from uniform beyond agreement on particular principles. Many European and North American states imposed sanctions and supplied weapons to Ukraine. Others, including several large Asian, African and Latin American states, sought to preserve trade with Russia or avoid adopting unilateral sanctions while voting differently on UN resolutions. Abstention could reflect concerns about the wording of a resolution, economic interests, diplomatic traditions or unwillingness to endorse punitive measures. It is historically misleading to group every abstaining country into a single pro-Russian bloc. Equally, the absence of sanctions did not imply approval of annexation or rejection of Ukrainian sovereignty. Each government's votes, statements, trade relationships and concrete policies should be examined separately.

Humanitarian agencies performed another function. UNHCR coordinated refugee assistance, while other UN agencies and the International Committee of the Red Cross supported efforts involving displaced people, medical care, food distribution and detention tracing. Their mandates did not provide authority to settle a war or compel access to every occupied area. Restrictions imposed by fighting forces, damage to transport routes and shortages of funding limited relief operations. International law gives humanitarian organizations a framework for seeking access but cannot eliminate physical dangers or political obstruction. The experiences of local Ukrainian aid organizations were equally important because they often reached communities that international workers could not access directly.

Legal institutions operated on their own timelines. The ICC issued arrest warrants in specific investigations, while national courts and UN investigative bodies documented other incidents. Some governments called for additional mechanisms addressing the crime of aggression, raising questions about jurisdiction, immunities and international cooperation. An arrest warrant could be issued while a suspect remained beyond the court's reach. The existence of a legal process did not mean immediate punishment, just as the absence of completed prosecution did not prove that the relevant violence was lawful. Historians should distinguish the evidence-gathering, diplomatic and judicial stages of international response. This helps students avoid treating 'the international community' as a single organization capable of one automatic decision.

Through the 2026 cutoff the UN remained a forum for diplomacy and humanitarian reporting rather than an institution that had ended the war. On February 24, 2026 the General Assembly adopted another resolution supporting lasting peace, reaffirming Ukraine's sovereignty and calling for an immediate ceasefire and exchanges of prisoners and transferred civilians. The vote differed from earlier resolutions, reflecting the changing political environment and contested approaches to settlement. This record can be studied to explain continuity in the territorial-integrity principle alongside change in diplomacy. International institutions shaped evidence, legitimacy and aid, but the military conflict continued under the decisions of states and armed forces.

Section 71.56

NATO, European Security, and Military Assistance to Ukraine

Russia's full-scale invasion transformed NATO's role in European security. Before February 2022 many European governments combined concern about Russian military power with continued energy and diplomatic relationships; afterward alliance members strengthened their eastern deployments and increased military assistance to Ukraine. NATO itself did not enter the war as a combatant alliance, and individual members retained responsibility for many arms-supply decisions. Training programs, intelligence sharing, logistical coordination and air-defense deliveries developed in overlapping national and multilateral arrangements. Military aid ranged from small arms and anti-tank missiles in the opening weeks to artillery, armored vehicles, long-range systems, air-defense batteries and eventually aircraft-related support. These categories had different training, maintenance and ammunition requirements; an announcement that a system would be delivered was not evidence that it was immediately operational.

The invasion produced an outcome opposite to one of Moscow's publicly stated strategic objectives: Finland and Sweden sought and obtained NATO membership. Finland joined on April 4, 2023, and Sweden on March 7, 2024, after national decisions and alliance ratification. Their accession changed the alliance's geography in northern Europe and the Baltic region. It should not be described as automatic or inevitable, because both states underwent substantial domestic political debate and formal accession procedures. The decision was related to Russia's invasion but also reflected their own defense assessments and institutional histories. Russian officials described the enlargement as worsening their security, while NATO governments presented it as the sovereign choice of states seeking collective defense.

European defense policy also changed within national political limits. Governments increased military budgets, sought to expand ammunition production and coordinated procurement, but industrial capacity could not expand overnight. Factories required investment, machinery, components and skilled labor before new production lines could meet the consumption of a long land war. Different states possessed different inventories and placed different restrictions on how supplied weapons could be used. Germany, Poland, the Baltic states, Britain, France and other governments debated levels of support and the risks of escalation in distinct political settings. An effective chapter compares actual appropriations, deliveries and training capacities rather than grouping all European countries into a single homogeneous donor.

Ukraine's requirements evolved. Anti-tank weapons were crucial during early Russian advances, while the positional war demanded enormous amounts of artillery ammunition and counter-battery equipment. Repeated long-range attacks on cities made air defenses especially important, and the arrival of Western systems improved protection in some locations but did not create a nationwide shield. Advanced weapons required sustainment: replacement parts, trained crews, secure communications and maintenance facilities. Some weapons were transferred from existing stocks that supporting countries then had to replenish. As the war lengthened, logistical cooperation and industrial planning became as strategically consequential as diplomatic statements. The speed of decisions sometimes reflected legislative negotiations far from Ukraine.

NATO governments also faced questions about direct confrontation with Russia. Alliance members generally avoided deploying regular combat forces to fight Russian troops in Ukraine, while Russian officials repeatedly warned against Western involvement and sometimes framed aid as participation in a proxy war. This rhetoric should be identified as political argument, not adopted as a legal conclusion that assistance automatically made donor states combatants. Nuclear deterrence, escalation management and the protection of alliance territory were recurring concerns. Incidents involving missiles near borders, sabotage allegations or airspace violations required caution because public reporting could precede full investigation. The distinction between assistance to Ukraine and a NATO-Russia war remained fundamental through the cutoff.

By November 2026 European security institutions had been reshaped but a stable settlement had not emerged. The alliance was geographically larger, European states had invested more in defense and Ukraine had deepened operational relations with supporting countries without becoming a NATO member. The unresolved question of future Ukrainian security guarantees continued to dominate diplomacy. Some proposals envisioned bilateral commitments, training arrangements or monitoring mechanisms, while others raised more extensive obligations that governments had not agreed to accept. The chapter should treat these proposals as proposals, not institutions already established. The historical achievement of European states in aiding Ukrainian resistance coexisted with ongoing debates over cost, risk and the political conditions for a durable end to fighting.

Section 71.57

The United States and the War, 2022–2026

The United States was a principal provider of military and financial support to Ukraine after the invasion, but American policy changed across administrations, congresses and political debates. Under President Joe Biden the government supplied weapons, intelligence assistance, training and funds through several statutory channels, often coordinating with European allies. Initial aid focused on anti-tank and anti-aircraft weapons, followed by artillery, rockets, armored vehicles and air-defense systems as the war changed. The administration also imposed sanctions on Russia and supported efforts to isolate Moscow diplomatically. Decisions about particular weapons involved escalation concerns, U.S. inventories, training requirements and negotiations with Congress. The tempo of approval and delivery mattered as much as the public announcement of a new package.

Congress exercised an independent role through appropriations, oversight and conditions on assistance. Arguments over domestic spending, accountability, border policy and U.S. strategic interests delayed some support, notably before the April 2024 funding legislation. Such delays affected Ukrainian expectations and procurement schedules without mechanically determining the outcome of a particular engagement. Inspector-general reports and congressional research documents are important sources because they identify different aid categories and distinguish funds obligated from equipment actually delivered. A student should not add the face value of all announced packages to produce one reliable total of weapons supplied. Budget support, loans, humanitarian assistance and the replacement of American equipment in U.S. inventories serve different functions.

Donald Trump's return to the presidency in January 2025 introduced a more overt emphasis on a negotiated settlement and changes in the presentation of U.S. priorities. Administration officials conducted talks with Russian and Ukrainian representatives, while assistance policies and diplomatic language shifted. Some public proposals placed pressure on Kyiv to consider territorial compromises or accelerated negotiations, provoking debate in Ukraine and Europe. Relations between the U.S. government and Ukraine also involved agreements on economic cooperation and reconstruction, including a 2025 framework concerning future investment. The historical record should distinguish signed instruments, proposed commitments and political statements: a proposal for sharing future revenues or rebuilding infrastructure was not evidence that postwar reconstruction had already begun on a national scale.

The Trump-Putin summit in Alaska in August 2025 produced no agreement ending the war, despite public statements presenting the meeting as constructive. Reuters reported competing positions over a ceasefire, territorial control and security conditions. Ukrainian officials opposed recognizing Russia's annexations or withdrawing from important defensive areas merely to meet Moscow's demands. European governments sought inclusion in discussions affecting their security and Ukraine's sovereignty. The summit illustrated how high-level diplomacy could change expectations without changing facts on the ground. A meeting between presidents may be a significant political event, but whether it altered military decisions or produced binding obligations must be assessed separately from the imagery of leaders appearing together.

In early 2026 U.S. envoys mediated talks involving Russia and Ukraine in Abu Dhabi and Geneva, as documented in congressional reporting. The parties remained divided over the sequencing of a ceasefire, territorial arrangements, the size and alignment of Ukraine's armed forces, and guarantees against renewed attacks. The United States also had to coordinate with European governments that might fund or implement any future security arrangement. Negotiations proceeded in an international environment shaped by other crises and competing American priorities. Public reports sometimes described a settlement as close, while official statements and continuing fighting showed major issues unresolved. A history textbook must distinguish the record of actual meetings from speculation about what participants might ultimately accept.

At the November 4, 2026 cutoff Reuters reported that another U.S.-facilitated round in Abu Dhabi was planned for November 10–11. Because those dates fell after the cutoff, the chapter may describe an announced plan but must not claim the meetings occurred or produced any result. This chronological discipline applies equally to future weapons deliveries or proposed sanctions. By the cutoff the United States had influenced the war through military support, economic restrictions and diplomatic initiatives, but no final settlement had been reached. The chapter's task is to explain changes in the instruments and priorities of American policy without reducing Ukraine's decisions to instructions from Washington or imagining that American leaders could determine the outcome unilaterally.

Section 71.58

China, India, Iran, North Korea, Belarus, and the Wider International System

States outside the principal Western sanctions coalition pursued differing policies toward Russia's invasion, reflecting their own economic interests, security calculations and diplomatic traditions. China maintained close political and commercial relations with Russia, criticized some Western sanctions and presented itself as favoring negotiation while also emphasizing sovereignty in official statements. Chinese firms and financial institutions faced complex decisions about trade and potential exposure to restrictions. Western governments alleged that some entities facilitated access to dual-use goods; such claims require evidence about the particular firms and transactions. China's government disputed many accusations and did not adopt the full sanctions package imposed by the United States and European Union. It is inaccurate either to describe Beijing as a neutral bystander unaffected by the war or to equate its policy automatically with direct participation by Chinese regular forces.

India increased purchases of discounted Russian oil and maintained defense and diplomatic contacts with Moscow while emphasizing the need for dialogue. Its policy reflected energy needs, long-established military relationships, strategic autonomy and relationships with Western partners. Other states in Asia, Africa and Latin America made varied choices concerning UN votes, trade, sanctions and humanitarian aid. These positions should not be labeled generically as a uniform 'Global South' response. Some governments condemned the invasion while opposing sanctions imposed outside the Security Council; others abstained on resolutions while supporting humanitarian assistance. The difference between a country's public diplomatic position and the practical behavior of its companies or security institutions deserves attention.

Iran became a significant participant through military technology associated with Russia's long-range drone program. Iranian-origin Shahed-family designs were used extensively in strikes on Ukraine, and the relationship later involved adaptation and production arrangements. Iranian officials sometimes denied particular transfers or disputed their timing. Establishing how weapons reached a battlefield requires looking at physical debris, serial markings, manufacturing analysis and intelligence reporting rather than relying only on government statements. North Korea supplied munitions according to extensive Western and South Korean reporting, and its reported personnel involvement became especially consequential in connection with fighting in Kursk oblast. The historical account should specify which assertions were corroborated by later public evidence and which numerical estimates remained uncertain.

Belarus played a different role. Russian forces used Belarusian territory to stage part of the February 2022 invasion, and the governments maintained military and political cooperation thereafter. Belarusian president Aleksandr Lukashenko supported Moscow rhetorically and through state arrangements, but the chapter must not imply that the Belarusian regular army was continuously deployed in Ukraine without evidence. Belarusian society was also divided, and the government restricted dissent; some Belarusian volunteers fought on the Ukrainian side. The country's position demonstrates that the government of a neighboring state, its armed forces and its citizens cannot be treated as identical actors. Geographic proximity made Belarusian territory important even without the same pattern of direct combat participation as Russian units.

Turkey pursued overlapping interests. A NATO member with economic relations with both belligerents, it supplied some weapons to Ukraine, declined to join the full Western sanctions regime and participated in negotiating the Black Sea Grain Initiative. Ankara also hosted talks and used its control of the Turkish Straits under international agreements as a significant element of Black Sea security. The war affected the Caucasus and Central Asia through trade rerouting, migration and changing regional diplomacy. Governments in those areas often sought to avoid becoming dependent on a single great power, and their ability to balance Russia, China, Europe and others differed. The same state could maintain commerce with Russia while taking positions in the United Nations unfavorable to annexation claims.

The wider international system therefore cannot be mapped as two neatly bounded camps. Military aid, commercial trade, diplomatic mediation, sanctions compliance and UN voting are separate dimensions. China and India were major economic partners without identical positions; Iran and North Korea were implicated in particular military-supply relationships; Belarus provided territorial and political assistance; Turkey combined NATO membership, diplomacy and independent commercial policy. In 2026 these patterns continued evolving under pressures from global energy markets and crises beyond Ukraine. The chapter should teach students to compare observable policies and interests, not to assign ideological labels that obscure actual conduct. Russia's ability to sustain war was affected by these external relationships, but none of them made the eventual settlement predetermined.

Section 71.59

Early Negotiations, Proposed Settlements, and Diplomacy, 2022–2024

Negotiations began within days of the full-scale invasion. Russian and Ukrainian representatives met in Belarus on February 28, 2022 and continued discussions through video conferences and later a meeting in Istanbul on March 29. The early contacts occurred while Russian troops were advancing toward Kyiv and while Ukrainian officials were seeking ways to stop the fighting without surrendering the country's sovereignty. Published drafts and later accounts indicated discussion of Ukrainian neutrality, security guarantees, restrictions on foreign military presence and arrangements concerning contested territories. However, drafts were not signed settlements, and participants later disagreed about what had been seriously accepted. Scholars must avoid repeating the claim that a completed peace agreement was ready and then simply abandoned without accounting for unresolved provisions and the war's rapidly changing military and political circumstances.

The relationship between military events and diplomacy was crucial. Russia's failure to capture Kyiv, withdrawal from northern Ukraine and the discovery of civilian killings in formerly occupied communities transformed the context in which negotiations occurred. Public opinion in Ukraine hardened against concessions, and political leaders faced the responsibility to protect citizens and maintain trust. Russia continued to seek security and political conditions that Ukrainian officials regarded as incompatible with independence. A proposal for neutrality might have been meaningful only alongside credible guarantees against renewed invasion, yet potential guarantor states disagreed about the obligations they would accept. Without enforceable terms, the same words could carry very different expectations for Kyiv, Moscow and external participants.

Humanitarian and technical arrangements offered limited cooperation even as a settlement proved elusive. Prisoner exchanges and the return of remains occurred through multiple channels. The July 2022 Black Sea Grain Initiative, brokered with Turkish and UN involvement, allowed monitored shipments from designated Ukrainian ports until its termination in July 2023. The agreement did not suspend combat or resolve territorial disputes; it addressed a narrower shared interest under conditions of conflict. International agencies also sought localized access for evacuations and the delivery of aid, with mixed results. These episodes show that diplomatic success is not all-or-nothing: parties can agree on a particular operational procedure while remaining fundamentally divided over sovereignty and war termination.

Ukraine's diplomatic proposals evolved as the war continued. President Zelenskyy presented a peace formula emphasizing territorial integrity, withdrawal, nuclear and energy safety, food security, release of captives and accountability. Ukrainian officials sought support from states outside Europe and North America, recognizing that UN votes and economic relationships mattered to diplomacy. Russia rejected elements that required surrender of occupied territories and insisted that Western security policy and its own asserted territorial claims be addressed. Outside observers debated whether a ceasefire without a political settlement could reduce deaths or instead allow renewed attack after rearmament. The historical answer cannot be assumed: the risks depend on enforcement, monitoring, force dispositions and the commitments of states willing to guarantee an arrangement.

Diplomatic summits and statements in 2023–2024 produced additional proposals but no general cessation of fighting. Governments sometimes supported broad principles without agreeing on detailed borders or enforcement mechanisms. The public record contains declarations, negotiating notes, speeches and accounts from participants, all written for different purposes. Memoirs and later interviews can illuminate intentions but must be compared with contemporaneous drafts where available. A press conference may show what leaders wanted audiences to believe without establishing everything discussed privately. This distinction is particularly important in the 2022 Istanbul talks, whose retrospective political interpretations became intertwined with later debates about whether the war could have been prevented or ended sooner.

By the end of 2024 the war had entered another phase of territorial and industrial attrition, and negotiations remained subordinate to unresolved security and sovereignty questions. Neither Russia nor Ukraine could impose its maximal preferred outcome through talks alone. The absence of a comprehensive agreement did not prove diplomacy was futile; prisoner exchanges and limited arrangements offered practical benefits. Nor did the existence of talks establish that either side had agreed to stop the war. The chapter should reconstruct the sequence of proposals, meetings and verified results, noting when documents remain unavailable, and avoid teleological claims that one particular moment would necessarily have produced a durable peace. The later diplomacy of 2025–2026 belongs to the next section precisely because the political actors and negotiating framework changed.

Section 71.60

Diplomacy in 2025–2026: Ceasefire Proposals and Unresolved Demands

The United States renewed a direct mediating role under President Donald Trump after January 2025, while Turkey, European governments and other states continued their own contacts. Trump administration officials sought discussions with Russian and Ukrainian leaders and emphasized achieving a settlement, sometimes pressing for rapid agreement despite divergent positions on territory and guarantees. Russia continued to require acceptance of conditions involving Ukraine's military alignment and territories Moscow claimed to have annexed, including areas not under Russian control. Ukraine argued that surrendering defensible territory or accepting weak guarantees could invite renewed attacks. These positions were not merely rhetorical: control of fortified towns, access to the Black Sea, military force levels and the operation of international sanctions affected what each government regarded as an acceptable deal.

Russian and Ukrainian representatives met directly in Istanbul in May and June 2025, with additional contacts during the year, but the initial rounds did not produce a ceasefire. They contributed to prisoner and remains exchanges, which were meaningful for affected families even without changing the strategic dispute. Russian representatives repeated demands for Ukrainian neutrality, restrictions on military strength and recognition or acceptance of territorial changes; Ukrainian negotiators sought a cessation of attacks and security arrangements that preserved sovereignty. The parties also disagreed about whether a ceasefire should precede broader negotiations or whether political terms must be settled first. These procedural differences mattered because a pause in hostilities might alter incentives and allow troop regrouping, while continuing fighting imposed immediate civilian costs.

The August 2025 summit between Presidents Trump and Putin in Alaska received substantial attention but yielded no binding settlement. Public accounts and subsequent reporting indicated continuing disagreement over a possible ceasefire and the territorial conditions Russia sought. Ukrainian authorities opposed demands to withdraw from additional territory in the Donetsk region, including parts of the defensive belt that were still under Kyiv's control. European governments pressed for participation in negotiations affecting their security and continued to debate future military support and guarantees. The chapter should describe the reported terms as reported, because some details came from anonymous sources and not from an agreed text made public. Diplomatic spectacle does not replace a signed agreement or verified change in military orders.

In January and February 2026 Russian, Ukrainian and American officials held trilateral discussions in Abu Dhabi and Geneva. U.S. congressional reporting described the negotiating tracks as involving possible ceasefire arrangements, territorial questions, military restrictions and security guarantees, with substantial differences still unresolved. Other international crises competed for diplomatic attention, and formal talks did not continue uninterrupted through the year. Public discussion often conflated humanitarian exchanges with agreements about final borders; these are separate issues. A verified prisoner swap establishes the exchange of identified people, not acceptance of a proposed peace framework. Similarly, a joint statement that participants will meet again does not demonstrate agreement on what they will decide.

Any prospective settlement raised difficult enforcement questions. Who would monitor a ceasefire line, and with what powers? Could foreign guarantees be activated automatically after a new attack, or would they depend on political decisions by supporting governments? How would civilian returns, demining, property claims and detainee access be handled? Could sanctions be relaxed in stages without removing incentives for compliance? Russia and Ukraine placed different weight on these issues. The position of occupied and displaced Ukrainians also matters: diplomatic arrangements about land and institutions affect people whose consent is not established merely because governments negotiate. Humanitarian obligations do not disappear during negotiations, and temporary ceasefires require verification separate from the politics of final recognition.

As of November 4, 2026 Reuters reported that another U.S.-facilitated round involving delegations from Russia and Ukraine was planned for November 10–11 in Abu Dhabi. The chapter's cutoff precedes those dates. It may record the announcement and the stated topics of security guarantees, de-escalation and humanitarian measures, but it must not report that the meeting happened or predict what it produced. The war continued and the negotiating positions remained far apart. The section's historical conclusion is therefore deliberately provisional: several years of talks yielded important limited exchanges and a substantial documentary record of rival objectives, yet no comprehensive ceasefire or peace settlement by the evidence cutoff. Future developments must be added in a subsequent edition, not anticipated in this one.

The language of a ceasefire requires careful institutional distinction. A political announcement promising to stop attacks is not the same as a written agreement defining coordinates, weapons restrictions, monitoring access, investigation procedures and consequences for violations. Exchanges of prisoners or remains can be negotiated under narrower humanitarian understandings while commanders continue offensive operations in other sectors. The status of occupied populations presents another problem: diplomats may discuss territorial arrangements while people living there have limited ability to express free preferences, and a military line of contact does not automatically become a legitimate international boundary. Economic sanctions, reconstruction financing and the future placement of foreign military assistance can be either bargaining instruments or necessary elements of a durable settlement, depending on the precise conditions. A history ending in November 2026 should therefore judge diplomatic efforts by the documents they produced, the actions they changed and the people they affected, not by the public description of a meeting as a 'breakthrough' or a 'failure.'

Section 71.61

Understanding the War: Strategy, Institutions, Resources, and Society

Understanding the war requires analysis at several levels rather than one explanation that attributes every event to a single political leader, weapon or outside government. Russia's decision to invade followed identifiable political choices made by its leadership, shaped by beliefs about Ukraine, perceptions of security and an authoritarian decision-making system. Those explanations do not transfer responsibility for the invasion to Ukraine or make war historically inevitable. Once the attack began, the course of fighting depended on Ukraine's military resistance, Russian logistical and organizational weaknesses, the geography of towns and rivers, and later changes in recruitment, industry and technology. A cause of the decision to invade is not the same thing as a cause of a failed operation or a prolonged war. Students should ask which process a proposed explanation actually addresses.

The first phase highlighted miscalculation and resilience. Russian planners apparently expected rapid political and military results, but Ukrainian institutions did not collapse and defenders exploited the vulnerabilities of advancing formations. Russia captured territory more quickly in the south than around Kyiv, showing that local conditions and deployment patterns mattered. Ukraine then retook important areas in 2022, but failed to secure a comparable strategic breakthrough in the summer of 2023. From late 2023 the war increasingly became a struggle to supply infantry, shells, air defense and drones while defending fortified positions. These shifts cannot be reduced to an unchanging balance of power based on national population or GDP. Both states adapted, and adaptation itself created new costs and vulnerabilities.

Economic capacity and international relationships explain part of the conflict's endurance. Russia redirected energy trade, increased defense production and drew on recruitment and foreign supply networks while bearing inflationary, fiscal and social costs. Ukraine sustained national institutions through domestic resilience and large-scale international assistance, but suffered occupation, displacement and immense infrastructure damage. Outside governments made choices about weapons, sanctions and diplomacy without controlling every decision of the belligerents. A material comparison should therefore distinguish available stockpiles, usable weapons delivered, trained crews, industrial replacement and political willingness to continue financing. Military outcomes are produced through institutions that convert resources into action, not through raw economic totals alone.

Civilian history challenges a purely strategic narrative. Residents of Mariupol, Bucha, Kherson, Kharkiv and numerous smaller communities experienced siege, occupation, displacement, bombardment, detention and the loss of family members. Russian families experienced mobilization, bereavement, migration and political restrictions; Ukrainian communities organized emergency services and military support while facing prolonged danger. These experiences were not symmetrical in cause or institutional setting, but each requires precise documentation. The law of occupation, protections for prisoners and prohibitions on unlawful attacks provide standards by which conduct can be evaluated. Independent reports and courts have produced substantial evidence, while unresolved cases, inaccessible territory and inconsistent casualty methods remain significant limitations.

The war was also fought through information. Both governments presented accounts intended to sustain support and influence external audiences, while independent journalists and investigators attempted to verify claims using documents, imagery and testimony. A responsible historian neither dismisses official sources automatically nor treats them as self-validating. It is necessary to ask who created a statement, which audience it addressed, whether its central claims can be corroborated and how later evidence changes the conclusion. Causal language must also remain disciplined: an event that occurred after another was not necessarily caused by it, and a publicly asserted aim may differ from an operational objective inferred from military movements. Comparative exercises should test these distinctions.

By November 2026 the available evidence supported some firm conclusions: Russia launched the full-scale invasion; Ukraine maintained independent state institutions and defended major cities; the war caused large-scale civilian suffering and displacement; Russia occupied and claimed Ukrainian territory; and international assistance and sanction policy altered the material conditions of fighting. Other issues, including comprehensive military casualty totals, private decision-making and the eventual settlement, remained uncertain. The chapter should resist an ending that turns provisional trends into destiny. Historical rigor means identifying both the established record and the questions that future access to archives, courts and wartime documents may allow researchers to answer more fully.

Section 71.62

Russia, Ukraine, and European Security at the November 2026 Cutoff

As of November 4, 2026 Russia's invasion of Ukraine had become a prolonged war affecting European security and the international economy. The Russian military still occupied substantial Ukrainian territory and conducted ground assaults and long-range attacks, while Ukraine maintained a functioning government and armed forces, defended key positions and struck Russian military and logistical targets. Territorial control varied across front-line sectors and could change between successive reports; no single unqualified map can represent all conditions throughout 2026. The Institute for the Study of War's assessment dated November 4 documented continuing Russian operations near Kostiantynivka and in several other directions, while distinguishing observed consolidation, contested positions and unsuccessful reported advances. Its assessments must remain attributed and should not be confused with a comprehensive official record of every battlefield action.

The civilian situation was severe. The UN Human Rights Monitoring Mission's report for September 2026, issued October 16, verified at least 328 civilians killed and 2,105 injured during that month and recorded a much higher casualty rate in January–September 2026 than during the corresponding periods of earlier years. Long-range missiles and drones harmed civilians in cities distant from the front, while short-range drones and bombardment continued to endanger people in front-line communities. Millions remained displaced, and repeated attacks on electricity and heating infrastructure increased risks as winter approached. These observations described verified harm; they did not capture all deaths in inaccessible areas, the full burden of psychological trauma or the complete costs of lost education, housing and health care.

The economic consequences were equally enduring. A February 2026 joint assessment estimated roughly $195.1 billion in direct physical damage and $587.7 billion in recovery and reconstruction needs through a December 2025 damage cutoff. Continued attacks after that date meant the estimate could not be treated as a final account. Russia's economy had adjusted to important sanctions and greater military spending, but faced inflation, labor shortages and trade-related constraints. Ukraine continued operating public services and defense institutions partly through external budget and military assistance. European governments had reorganized parts of their energy supply and defense planning, while international food and maritime trade remained affected. These changes created obligations for future generations, but their permanent political consequences were not yet knowable.

European security had been transformed without a settled framework replacing the prewar order. Finland and Sweden had joined NATO, Russia's relations with many European governments were severely damaged, and Ukraine had deepened its cooperation with supporting states while remaining outside the alliance. China, India, Turkey, Iran, North Korea, Belarus and other states pursued different roles rather than forming a simple two-bloc international system. Negotiations concerning a ceasefire, territory, security guarantees and sanctions continued in various formats but had not yielded a comprehensive settlement. Reports of planned meetings after November 4 must not be presented as completed history. The unresolved question of how to guarantee security for Ukraine remained central to international diplomacy.

A history written at this cutoff must also acknowledge gaps in knowledge. Casualty figures differ according to whether they count verified civilians, estimates of military losses, deaths, wounds or missing people. Some territories are inaccessible to independent observers. Criminal investigations and court proceedings concerning alleged atrocities remain open or incomplete. Governments withhold operational records and may release politically selective statistics. Even when a reliable report identifies an advance or strike, it may not establish the long-term effect. The historian's obligation is to document dates and sources, distinguish proven observations from claims, and revise conclusions when new evidence becomes available. A provisional account can be rigorous without pretending to be final.

The chapter ends with no prediction of victory, defeat or borders. Its enduring subject is how decisions to use force produced a conflict that reorganized military systems, households, state institutions and international relationships. Ukraine's statehood, Russia's wartime political transformation, the experiences of displaced civilians and the long-term effects on European security are connected but not identical histories. Chapter 72 will examine Russia's broader institutional, social, economic and cultural development across the twenty-first century rather than replay battlefield chronology. Whatever follows the November 2026 cutoff belongs to later documentary records and future editions. The proper concluding statement is not that history has reached an outcome, but that enough evidence exists to explain major causes and consequences while identifying the uncertainty that remains.

Section 71.63

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

The review checkpoint asks students to establish an evidence-dated chronology of a war still in progress, distinguish military control from recognized sovereignty, compare institutional and social experiences in Russia and Ukraine, and evaluate the limitations of statistics and official statements. It is not a prediction exercise. Written arguments must identify the exact date, issuing organization and evidentiary basis for each disputed contemporary claim.

Students should be able to trace the 2021–2022 escalation, the campaigns of 2022–2024, the prolonged fighting of 2025–2026, the expansion of sanctions and external military aid, and the unresolved diplomatic arrangements by November 4, 2026. They should also explain why international legal findings, battlefield reporting, casualty verification and economic damage estimates require different interpretive methods. Chapter Summary

The February 2022 full-scale invasion widened a conflict that had begun in 2014 into a continental European war. Russian forces attacked from multiple directions, but the attempt to capture Kyiv failed and Ukraine retained its national government. Russian forces occupied much southern territory, destroyed Mariupol through a prolonged siege, and fought Ukrainian forces in the Donbas. Ukrainian counteroffensives in the Kharkiv and Kherson regions later in 2022 reversed some of those territorial gains. At every stage civilian survival, the functioning of Ukrainian institutions, and the decisions of ordinary people shaped the war’s course.

By 2023–2024 the conflict had become increasingly characterized by entrenched positions, dense mines, artillery, missiles, drones, electronic warfare and competing capacities to replace personnel and equipment. The campaigns of 2025–2026 extended this attritional pattern while long-range attacks on energy and industrial infrastructure broadened the consequences away from the front. The exact military balance depended on sector, date and source. As of November 4, 2026 neither claims of complete victory nor projections of a final political settlement were established historical facts.

The war transformed both states. Russia increased defense production and military spending, mobilized additional forces, restricted public dissent and redirected trade under sanctions. Ukraine adapted military and civilian institutions to survive attacks while relying on domestic resources, international assistance and extensive volunteer participation. Millions were displaced; occupied communities faced coercive administrative systems and restrictions on rights; prisoners and civilians suffered serious violations. Humanitarian statistics, court documents and independent investigations revealed substantial harm while also making the limits of access and proof visible.

The wider international response encompassed UN resolutions, economic sanctions, NATO enlargement, military assistance, energy-market change, trade realignment and repeated diplomatic efforts. These processes were neither identical nor equally successful. Some negotiations yielded humanitarian exchanges or limited arrangements without producing a comprehensive settlement. The historical lesson of this chapter is methodological as well as substantive: an unfinished war demands precise dates, attributed sources, clear legal categories, sustained attention to civilian agency and disciplined refusal to convert uncertainty into false certainty.

Chapter Reflection: History Without a Known Ending

The lesson of this chapter is not that the war’s eventual outcome was determined by Russia’s size, Ukraine’s resistance, Western assistance or any single event. The historical record shows changing military conditions, persistent civilian agency and choices made under uncertainty. No government controls every consequence of its actions.

Historical inquiry also requires a distinction between compassion and verification. The suffering of civilians is documented, but particular counts, chains of command and legal responsibilities must be established through evidence. The obligation to be precise is especially important when those affected are still living through the events under study.

The evidence cutoff is November 4, 2026. The war had not ended. A responsible later edition should append verified developments, correct errors and revise interpretations transparently rather than silently substitute hindsight for the contemporaneous record.
