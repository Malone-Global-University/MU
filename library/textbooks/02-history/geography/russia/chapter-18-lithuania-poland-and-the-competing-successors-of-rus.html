Part III — Fragmentation and the Mongol Era

Lithuania, Poland, and the Competing Successors of Rus'

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

By the middle of the fourteenth century, a ruler based not in Moscow, Vladimir, or Novgorod, but in the Baltic town of Vilnius, governed a far greater expanse of the former Rus' lands than any prince of northeastern Rus'.

A student who has just finished Chapters 16 and 17 might reasonably assume that the story of Rus' after the Mongol conquest is essentially a northeastern story: the destruction of Ryazan and Vladimir, the long relationship between the princes of the Rus' lands and the khans of the Golden Horde, and the gradual, still-uncertain emergence of Moscow as one competitor among several for regional preeminence. That assumption, however, describes only part of a much larger picture. West and southwest of the principalities examined in Chapter 17, an entirely different political trajectory was unfolding among lands that had also once belonged to the Rus' world. By the second half of the fourteenth century, the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, a state whose ruling dynasty and core population were not East Slavic and, for much of this period, not even Christian, had absorbed enormous stretches of territory in what is now Belarus and Ukraine, including the ancient city of Kyiv itself.

This chapter examines that other trajectory. Its purpose, following directly from Chapter 17's account of Rus' principalities under Golden Horde supremacy, is to show that the political and cultural inheritance of medieval Rus' did not funnel automatically, or exclusively, toward Moscow. Large parts of the former Rus' world entered the orbit of Lithuania, and, through Lithuania's evolving relationship with the Kingdom of Poland, of the wider Latin Christian and Central European political system. These territories did not simply lose their Rus' character upon incorporation. Orthodox Christianity, Rus' legal custom, princely and boyar elites, and a distinctive East Slavic chancery language continued to shape political and cultural life within the Grand Duchy for generations, producing a Rus' world that looked and functioned differently from the northeastern principalities examined in the previous chapter, but that had at least as strong a claim to constitute a genuine continuation of the medieval Rus' inheritance.

Readers should resist two temptations as this chapter proceeds. The first is to see Lithuanian expansion as a foreign conquest that simply extinguished a preexisting "Russian" civilization in the west and southwest. The evidence does not support so simple a picture: Lithuanian rulers frequently governed through existing elites, adapted Rus' legal and administrative practice, and depended on Orthodox, East Slavic-speaking populations for the ordinary business of government across most of their realm. The second temptation is the opposite error: to see Lithuania's Rus' territories as simply another chapter in a continuous "Russian" national story that happened to occur under foreign political administration. Both temptations flatten a genuinely complicated situation in which multiple political centers, each with legitimate claims on aspects of the Rus' inheritance, coexisted, competed, and changed over more than two centuries.

The chapter also introduces Poland as more than a peripheral actor. The dynastic union first forged between Poland and Lithuania in the 1380s, sealed through the conversion and coronation of the Lithuanian grand prince Jogaila as king of Poland, set in motion a long process of Polish-Lithuanian association that would eventually reshape the political and religious life of the Rus' lands under Lithuanian rule. That process unfolded gradually and unevenly across more than a century and a half; students should be careful not to confuse the fourteenth-century dynastic union examined in this chapter with the much closer federal union, the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, created by the Union of Lublin in 1569—which nonetheless left Poland and Lithuania as legally distinct states in important respects—an event belonging to a much later period of this textbook.

This chapter further insists on regional differentiation within the western Rus' lands themselves. The former principality of Galicia-Volhynia, examined in Chapter 13 as one of the major successor states to Kyivan-era political fragmentation, did not pass as a single unit into either Lithuanian or Polish hands. Galicia and Volhynia followed distinguishable paths, contested by Poland, Lithuania, and Hungary across the fourteenth century, and their eventual incorporation into different political systems illustrates precisely the chapter's central argument: the medieval Rus' world fragmented into multiple successor trajectories rather than reconverging on a single center.

By the chapter's end, Moscow and Lithuania will appear side by side as two of the major political inheritors of the Rus' world, neither one yet dominant over the other, neither one a foregone historical winner. This positioning is deliberate. Chapter 19 will ask why Moscow, rather than Lithuania, Novgorod, or some other center, eventually became the political nucleus of the state that this textbook will later call Russia. That question can only be asked responsibly once students understand, as this chapter aims to show, that the outcome was not given in advance. Multiple centers, multiple elites, and multiple institutional traditions all had legitimate claims on the memory and inheritance of Rus', and the historical record of the fourteenth century gives no reason to assume that Moscow's eventual preeminence was inevitable.

Finally, this chapter asks students to hold in mind a lesson introduced already in Chapter 12's treatment of Rus' fragmentation and reinforced in Chapter 13: political fragmentation is not merely a story of decline or failure. The Lithuanian-Rus' world examined here produced its own vigorous institutions, its own legal traditions, its own religious and cultural life, and its own historical significance, quite apart from whatever eventually became of Moscow. Modern Belarusian, Ukrainian, Lithuanian, and Polish historical traditions all draw, in different ways, upon the history this chapter examines, and none of them should be read backward into the fourteenth century as though the modern nation already existed in medieval form.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the consolidation of Lithuanian political power under rulers such as Mindaugas and Gediminas as a Baltic, initially pagan, dynastic process rather than an anti-Mongol crusade or a predetermined liberation of Rus'.
  2. Trace the methods—military conquest, princely succession, dynastic alliance, and negotiated submission—by which Lithuanian rulers acquired authority over Rus' territories, particularly under Gediminas and Algirdas.
  3. Analyze the reliance of Lithuanian rulers on existing Orthodox, East Slavic princely, boyar, and clerical elites in governing their Rus' territories.
  4. Describe the development of the Polish-Lithuanian dynastic relationship, particularly the 1385 Union of Krewo and Jogaila's 1386 baptism and coronation, and distinguish this union from the later 1569 Union of Lublin.
  5. Compare the distinct fourteenth-century trajectories of Galicia and Volhynia as former Galicia-Volhynian territory was contested among Poland, Lithuania, and Hungary.
  6. Explain the political and legal diversity of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, including the persistence of Rus' customary law, princely authority, and the East Slavic chancery tradition later associated with the term "Ruthenian."
  7. Assess the religious landscape created by Lithuanian expansion, including the coexistence and competition of paganism, Orthodoxy, and Latin Catholicism before and after Jogaila's conversion.
  8. Evaluate Moscow and Lithuania as two coexisting, competing political centers with claims upon the inheritance of Rus', without treating either as historically predetermined to prevail.
  9. Interpret medieval Rus' as a source of multiple later political and cultural inheritances rather than a single national line running directly to any one modern state.
  10. Critique nationalist historical narratives that project modern Russian, Ukrainian, Belarusian, Lithuanian, or Polish identity backward onto fourteenth-century political communities.
  11. Connect this chapter's account of competing Rus' successors to Chapter 19's subsequent explanation of Moscow's rise.
  12. Distinguish among overlapping and sequential political-legal developments—the Union of Krewo, the beginnings of the Lithuanian Statutes, and the Union of Lublin—that belong to different points in this textbook's chronology.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What conditions in thirteenth- and fourteenth-century eastern Europe allowed a relatively small Baltic polity to expand into one of the largest states in Europe?
  2. By what methods, rather than by conquest alone, did Lithuanian rulers acquire authority over Rus' principalities?
  3. Why did Lithuanian rulers generally preserve, rather than replace, existing Rus' elites, law, and administrative practice?
  4. What did the Polish-Lithuanian dynastic union of 1385–1386 actually create, and what did it not yet create?
  5. Why did Galicia and Volhynia, both once part of a single Rus' principality, follow different political paths after the mid-fourteenth century?
  6. How did paganism, Orthodoxy, and Catholicism coexist and compete within the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, and how did that balance shift after 1386?
  7. Why is it historically inaccurate to describe fourteenth-century Muscovy and Lithuania as locked in an inevitable contest for a single "Russian" future?
  8. In what senses can modern Belarusian, Ukrainian, Lithuanian, Polish, and Russian historical traditions each legitimately draw upon the medieval Rus' inheritance examined in this chapter?

Historical Context

Chapter 13 traced the fragmentation of Kyivan-era Rus' into multiple regional principalities, among them Galicia-Volhynia in the southwest, a powerful and, for a time, internationally significant Rus' state whose princes maintained active diplomatic and military relationships with Poland, Hungary, and the papacy. Chapter 16 examined the Mongol conquest that devastated much of the Rus' world in the 1230s and 1240s, and Chapter 17 explained how the surviving northeastern and, to varying degrees, other Rus' principalities entered a tributary relationship with the Golden Horde, a relationship that shaped princely politics, taxation, and church affairs for more than two centuries. Nothing in that account, however, implied that Golden Horde supremacy extended uniformly across every part of the former Rus' world, nor that the princes of the northeast were the only political actors positioned to inherit aspects of the Rus' political and cultural tradition.

To the northwest of the Rus' lands, along the southeastern shore of the Baltic Sea, a cluster of Baltic-speaking peoples—including the Lithuanians, Samogitians, and related groups, distinct from the Slavic-speaking populations of Rus'—had by the twelfth and thirteenth centuries developed increasingly consolidated forms of political and military organization. These peoples faced sustained pressure from German crusading orders, chiefly the Teutonic Order and the Livonian Order (the Livonian branch of the Teutonic Order after 1237), which had established fortified territories along the Baltic coast in the name of Christianizing the pagan Baltic and Finnic peoples of the region. Political consolidation among the Lithuanians was shaped in important part by this external military pressure, as smaller Baltic communities banded together under increasingly centralized leadership capable of mounting sustained resistance and, eventually, expansion of their own; it also depended on internal dynastic competition, control over military resources and tribute, and evolving alliances among Lithuanian elites, so that crusading pressure should be understood as one major spur among several rather than the single cause of Lithuanian state formation.

At almost exactly the same historical moment that Mongol armies were devastating the Rus' principalities to the east, therefore, Lithuanian political consolidation was proceeding under different pressures to the northwest. The resulting Grand Duchy of Lithuania emerged not as a response to Mongol conquest, and not as a self-conscious project of liberating Rus', but as an independent process of Baltic state formation that happened to unfold on the doorstep of a politically fragmented and, in its eastern and southern reaches, tribute-paying Rus' world. As Lithuanian military and dynastic power grew across the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, Lithuanian rulers found themselves drawn, through a combination of opportunity, invitation, marriage, and conquest, into direct rule over large stretches of that same Rus' world.

Understanding this chapter therefore requires holding two separate historical processes in mind simultaneously: the internal consolidation of a Baltic, pagan Lithuanian polity, and the fragmented, tribute-paying condition of the Rus' principalities under Golden Horde overlordship examined in Chapter 17. Their convergence, examined across this chapter's sections, produced one of medieval Europe's most religiously, linguistically, and legally diverse states, and set the stage for a genuinely open contest, extending across the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, over who could most legitimately claim the political and cultural inheritance of Rus'.

Section 18.1

The Rise of Lithuania

The Lithuanians were, and remain, a Baltic-speaking people, linguistically and culturally distinct from the East Slavic-speaking populations of Rus' examined throughout the preceding chapters of this textbook. Chapter 5 introduced the Baltic peoples as one of several distinct linguistic and cultural groups inhabiting Eastern Europe before the formation of Rus', alongside Slavic, Finnic, and Turkic populations, and cautioned against assuming that any single ethnic group possessed exclusive claim to the region. Lithuanian political consolidation in the thirteenth century should be understood within that same framework: it was the state formation of one Baltic people among several, occurring under specific and severe external pressure, rather than the emergence of a preordained regional hegemon.

That pressure came chiefly from German crusading orders. The Teutonic Order, invited into the eastern Baltic region in the early thirteenth century to Christianize and subdue pagan Baltic and Finnic peoples, and the closely associated Livonian Order, established fortified territories, castles, and eventually substantial landed states along the Baltic coast, pressing steadily against neighboring pagan populations, including the Lithuanians, Prussians, and Livs. Facing this external pressure, smaller Lithuanian communities and local chieftains increasingly coalesced under more centralized leadership capable of mounting effective, coordinated resistance. But external warfare was only one ingredient in this process, not its sole cause. Lithuanian consolidation also depended on internal dynastic competition among rival Lithuanian dukes, control over military manpower and tribute, marriage alliances, and the gradual construction of durable relationships between a paramount ruler and subordinate local elites—developments that would have proceeded, in some form, even without a crusading frontier to contend with. Mindaugas, the first Lithuanian ruler to achieve recognition as a unifying prince over a substantial Lithuanian territory in the mid-thirteenth century, illustrates both the achievements and the fragility of this early consolidation: he was baptized around 1250–1251 and received a royal crown from the papacy in 1253, evidently as part of a diplomatic strategy that combined internal dynastic advantage with an effort to secure external recognition and reduce crusading pressure. Whether Mindaugas subsequently abandoned Christianity, as some later accounts suggest, remains genuinely uncertain; historians disagree on the point, and the surviving evidence does not allow a confident answer. His assassination in 1263 was followed by a period of renewed internal conflict before Lithuanian unity was more durably reestablished.

Students should resist reading this consolidation as an anti-Mongol project or as a deliberate campaign to "liberate" Rus'. Lithuanian state formation developed within Baltic regional dynamics—including crusading pressure, internal dynastic competition, relations with neighboring peoples and states such as Poland and the Rus' principalities, and control of military and fiscal resources—rather than as a response to Mongol conquest or a strategic calculation about the Mongol-dominated Rus' lands farther east. Only in the following generations, as Lithuanian military and political strength grew and as opportunities arose amid the continued fragmentation of the Rus' principalities, did Lithuanian rulers turn their attention systematically toward expansion in that direction, a development examined in Section 18.2.

By the early fourteenth century, under rulers whose names are less fully documented than those of Mindaugas or his successors, and then decisively under Grand Duke Gediminas from around 1316, Lithuania had become a substantial and increasingly organized state, capable of sustained military campaigning, dynastic diplomacy with neighboring Christian and pagan powers alike, and, crucially for this chapter, absorption of adjacent territories through methods considerably more varied than outright conquest. Gediminas established Vilnius as a princely seat and center of Lithuanian power, cultivated relationships with the papacy and with Christian rulers even while remaining personally non-Christian, and founded the Gediminid dynasty from which nearly all subsequent Lithuanian, and eventually Polish-Lithuanian, rulers would descend. His reign marks the point at which Lithuanian expansion into the Rus' lands began in earnest.

Section 18.2

Expansion into Rus' Territories

Lithuanian acquisition of Rus' territory did not occur through a single method, and it did not occur in a single campaign. Historians identify at least four overlapping processes at work across the fourteenth century: outright military conquest of contested towns and regions; succession of Lithuanian princes, often members of the Gediminid dynasty, to existing Rus' princely thrones through marriage, invitation, or the extinction of local ruling lines; dynastic alliance and intermarriage between Gediminid and Rurikid princely families, which frequently preceded or accompanied territorial incorporation; and negotiated submission, in which local Rus' elites accepted Lithuanian overlordship in exchange for confirmation of their existing privileges, landholding, and religious institutions. These methods were not mutually exclusive, and a single territory's incorporation might involve several of them in sequence.

Under Gediminas, Lithuanian authority extended into territories associated with the former principalities of Polotsk, Turov-Pinsk, and other western Rus' lands in the region of present-day Belarus, areas where Slavic-Lithuanian political and dynastic contact had already been developing for some time. Under his son and eventual successor Algirdas, who ruled from roughly 1345 to 1377, Lithuanian expansion accelerated dramatically and moved decisively into the Dnieper basin and the southern Rus' lands. The chronology of Lithuanian involvement in Kyiv itself is more gradual and more uncertain than a single decisive conquest. Some evidence suggests that Gediminas's brother Teodoras already held a position of authority in or connected to Kyiv earlier in the fourteenth century, though the precise nature and extent of Lithuanian influence at that stage remain debated among historians. What can be said with greater confidence is that Lithuanian authority over Kyiv became considerably firmer under Algirdas in the 1360s, when Lithuanian forces and dynastic administration were established there more durably and a Gediminid prince, his son Volodymyr, was installed to govern the city and its surrounding territory. Students should therefore understand Lithuanian control of Kyiv as a process that unfolded across several decades rather than as a single conquest achieved in one campaign, and should treat its connection to the Battle of Blue Waters, examined below, with similar caution.

This expansion brought Lithuania into direct contact and, at points, conflict with Tatar power in the southern steppe borderlands, since much of the territory Lithuania absorbed had previously fallen, at least nominally, within the tributary reach of the Golden Horde examined in Chapter 17. Later historical tradition associates a major military engagement, conventionally called the Battle of Blue Waters, with a Lithuanian victory over Tatar forces that helped secure Lithuanian control over parts of the southern Rus' lands. Students should treat the precise date, scale, and strategic significance of this battle with appropriate caution: the surviving sources are limited and later, the exact year assigned to the engagement varies among scholars, and its role in securing specific territories for Lithuania, while broadly accepted, should not be overstated as a single decisive turning point that instantly transferred all contested lands from Horde to Lithuanian control. Horde and Lithuanian claims to authority over parts of the southern Rus' borderlands appear, in fact, to have overlapped and shifted for some time rather than switching abruptly at one battle.

It is equally important to recognize what this expansion did not entail. Lithuanian rule over newly incorporated Rus' territories rarely meant wholesale displacement of existing populations, elites, or institutions. In most cases, as Section 18.3 examines in detail, Lithuanian grand dukes confirmed local princely families in reduced but still meaningful authority, preserved existing legal custom, and relied on local administrative structures to govern day-to-day affairs. The Grand Duchy of Lithuania that emerged from this process of acquisition was, by the later fourteenth century, an enormous and religiously, linguistically, and institutionally composite state. Its Rus' territories, concentrated in the east and south, covered a far greater area than the ethnically Lithuanian Baltic core and contained very large Orthodox, East Slavic populations; precise fourteenth-century population proportions between the two do not survive and should not be stated with false confidence, but the scale of the Rus' territories alone made the Grand Duchy a fundamentally composite, rather than a narrowly Lithuanian, state.

Section 18.3

Orthodox Elites Under Lithuanian Rule

Because most of the Grand Duchy's newly acquired territory was inhabited predominantly by East Slavic, Orthodox Christian populations, governing it effectively required extensive reliance on existing local elites. Rus' princely families, many of them cadet branches of the Rurikid dynasty examined throughout Chapters 7 through 17, frequently retained regional authority under a general Lithuanian overlordship, continuing to administer justice, collect revenue, and maintain local order much as they had before incorporation. Boyars, the landed aristocracy of the Rus' principalities, likewise continued to hold land, exercise local influence, and serve in administrative and military capacities, now as subjects of the Grand Duke of Lithuania rather than of an independent or Horde-tributary Rus' prince.

The Orthodox Church, similarly, continued to function largely without interruption across most of the Grand Duchy's Rus' territories throughout the fourteenth century. Bishoprics, monasteries, and parish structures inherited from the Kyivan and post-Kyivan periods carried on their religious and, in many respects, cultural and educational functions. The relationship between the Orthodox hierarchy operating within Lithuanian territory and the metropolitan see historically associated with Kyiv, examined in earlier chapters, became genuinely complicated during this period, as ecclesiastical authorities, the Byzantine patriarchate, Lithuanian rulers, and eventually Muscovite rulers all had interests in how the metropolitanate of "Kyiv and all Rus'" was organized and who exercised jurisdiction over which portion of the vast Orthodox population now divided between Lithuanian and Muscovite-oriented political spheres. This ecclesiastical dimension of the Rus' successor-state question recurs in Section 18.8 and will be examined further in later chapters treating the Muscovite church.

This pattern of governance through existing elites should not be mistaken for a simple continuation of pre-Lithuanian political life unchanged. Lithuanian grand dukes retained ultimate authority, could and did depose or replace local princes when political circumstances warranted, extracted tribute and military service from their Rus' territories, and gradually layered Lithuanian dynastic and administrative structures atop existing Rus' institutions. Nor should it be read as evidence of uniformly benevolent or cooperative relations; incorporation sometimes followed military conquest and could involve coercion, and local elites' cooperation reflected calculated accommodation to changed political realities as much as enthusiasm for Lithuanian rule. The important point for this chapter is narrower and more precise: incorporation into the Grand Duchy of Lithuania did not mean the disappearance or wholesale replacement of Rus' political, legal, religious, and cultural life. In a very real institutional sense, the Grand Duchy of Lithuania's eastern and southern territories continued to function, in local administration, law, religion, and written culture, as a Rus' political world governed by a Lithuanian dynasty rather than as a colonized or culturally erased frontier of a purely Baltic state.

Section 18.4

The Polish-Lithuanian Relationship

By the 1380s, the Grand Duchy of Lithuania faced a combination of pressures and opportunities that pushed its ruling dynasty toward closer association with the neighboring Kingdom of Poland. The Teutonic Order continued to pose a serious military threat along Lithuania's northwestern frontier, and Lithuania's continued paganism—by this point increasingly unusual among major European states—left it diplomatically isolated and vulnerable to crusading justification for continued attacks. Poland, for its part, faced a related set of dynastic and strategic considerations following the death of King Louis of Hungary and Poland in 1382, which left the Polish throne to his daughter Jadwiga and opened the possibility of a dynastic marriage that could align Polish and Lithuanian interests against the Teutonic Order while securing the Polish succession.

These considerations converged in the Union of Krewo, a dynastic agreement concluded in 1385 between representatives of the Grand Duke of Lithuania, Jogaila, and the Kingdom of Poland. Under the terms associated with this agreement, Jogaila undertook to accept Latin Christian baptism, to marry Queen Jadwiga, and to bring Lithuania into a form of association with Poland. The Krewo agreement itself set out these commitments; the union it anticipated was actually inaugurated the following year, when, in 1386, Jogaila was baptized, married Jadwiga, and was crowned King of Poland as Władysław II Jagiełło, founding the Jagiellonian dynasty that would rule both Poland and, in various arrangements, Lithuania for the following two centuries.

Students must understand precisely what this union did, and did not, accomplish. The 1385 Krewo agreement laid the foundation for the personal, dynastic union inaugurated by the events of 1386, a union centered on a shared ruler; it did not create a single centralized Polish-Lithuanian state with unified institutions, law, or administration. The Grand Duchy of Lithuania continued to exist as a distinct political entity, with its own administrative structures, nobility, and, as later sections examine, its own evolving legal tradition. This distinction became especially clear in the following decades, when Jogaila's cousin Vytautas, widely known by the Polonized form Witold, governed the Grand Duchy of Lithuania as its Grand Duke from 1392 to 1430 with substantial practical autonomy, even as the broader dynastic association with Poland continued. Vytautas pursued an active, at times independent, foreign and military policy, including further involvement in Rus' affairs and continued conflict with, and eventual major victory over, the Teutonic Order at the Battle of Grunwald in 1410, fought jointly with Polish forces.

The much closer federal union known as the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, in which the two states shared a jointly elected monarch and a common parliament (the Sejm) while each retained important separate legal, administrative, and military institutions of its own, did not come into being until the Union of Lublin in 1569, an event lying nearly two centuries beyond the period examined in this chapter and treated fully in a later chapter of this textbook. Conflating the fourteenth-century Union of Krewo with the sixteenth-century Union of Lublin is one of the most common errors in popular treatments of this period, and students should take care to keep the two events, and the very different kinds of political union they represent, clearly distinct.

Section 18.5

Galicia and Western Rus'

Chapter 13 examined Galicia-Volhynia as one of the most powerful and internationally engaged of the Rus' successor principalities following Kyivan-era fragmentation, a state whose princes maintained active diplomatic relationships with Poland, Hungary, and the Latin Christian world generally, alongside their continued Orthodox religious affiliation and Rus' political traditions. The extinction of the ruling Romanovych princely line in Galicia-Volhynia during the first half of the fourteenth century, amid disputed succession and, according to some accounts, poisoning, removed the political framework that had held the two component territories, Galicia and Volhynia, together under unified princely authority.

In the resulting vacuum, both the Kingdom of Poland and the Kingdom of Hungary, along with the expanding Grand Duchy of Lithuania, pursued claims to Galician-Volhynian territory across the middle decades of the fourteenth century, producing an extended and, at various points, militarily active contest. The outcome that gradually emerged by the later fourteenth century divided the former principality's two component regions along different political lines: Galicia came under increasingly durable Polish royal authority, formally incorporated into the Kingdom of Poland, while Volhynia was drawn substantially into the orbit of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, generally along the pattern of elite accommodation and institutional continuity examined in Section 18.3.

This divergence is historically significant for reasons that extend well beyond the immediate fourteenth-century contest. It demonstrates concretely that the former Rus' lands did not experience a single, uniform post-Mongol political trajectory even within a single formerly unified principality. Galicia's incorporation into the Kingdom of Poland exposed its population to a different long-term set of religious, legal, and social pressures than those experienced by Volhynia under Lithuanian rule, or by the northeastern principalities under Golden Horde tributary supremacy and eventual Muscovite consolidation. Students should be cautious about applying modern national geography to this period: describing this development as "Poland conquering western Ukraine" imports twentieth-century national categories into a fourteenth-century contest among dynastic states, none of which conceived of themselves in modern Ukrainian, Polish, or Russian national terms. The population living through these events understood themselves primarily through religious affiliation, local and princely loyalty, social estate, and regional identity rather than through the national categories that would develop much later.

Section 18.6

Political and Legal Diversity

The Grand Duchy of Lithuania that had emerged by the close of the fourteenth century bore little resemblance, institutionally, to the increasingly centralized princely authority that Chapters 19 through 22 will trace in Muscovy. Rather than a single, unified legal and administrative system radiating outward from one political center, the Grand Duchy functioned as a composite state in which regional princely authority, local boyar privilege, town self-governance, Orthodox ecclesiastical institutions, and customary law inherited from the Kyivan and post-Kyivan Rus' periods continued to operate alongside the overarching authority of the Grand Duke and his court at Vilnius.

Central to this legal and administrative diversity was the continued use, throughout most of the Grand Duchy's Rus' territories, of an East Slavic chancery language for government documents, legal records, and correspondence. Contemporaries within the Grand Duchy commonly called this written language "ruski"; "Ruthenian" is the conventional English scholarly label this textbook uses for it, and different modern national historiographies—Belarusian, Ukrainian, Russian, Polish, and Western—apply their own terms to the same historical written tradition. An organized grand ducal chancery using this language is well documented from the reign of Vytautas onward, beginning in 1392. This written tradition drew directly on the literary and legal culture of Kyivan-era and post-Kyivan Rus', including the linguistic and documentary heritage examined in earlier chapters treating the Russkaya Pravda and Rus' chronicle writing. Its continued official use across the Grand Duchy's Rus' territories provides strong evidence for the institutional and cultural continuity examined throughout this chapter: a Lithuanian dynasty presided over a state whose ordinary business of government was conducted, for a large majority of its territory, in an East Slavic language rooted in Rus' tradition rather than in Lithuanian.

This chapter's period should be understood as building toward, rather than already containing, the more systematic legal codification associated with the Grand Duchy of Lithuania in later centuries. The First Lithuanian Statute, a substantial written code addressing criminal law, civil law, and administrative procedure across the Grand Duchy, was not promulgated until 1529, well beyond the period covered by this chapter, and later statutes followed in 1566 and 1588. These codifications drew on, and to a considerable extent formalized, legal traditions and customary practices that had developed across the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, including much of the Rus' legal inheritance examined in this section. Students should therefore understand the Lithuanian Statutes as a later culmination of tendencies visible already in this chapter's period, not as institutions already governing fourteenth-century Lithuanian-Rus' society.

The term "Ruthenian" itself requires careful historical definition rather than casual use. In the context of this chapter and the following centuries of Lithuanian and Polish-Lithuanian history, "Ruthenian" refers to the East Slavic-speaking, historically Rus'-descended population of the Grand Duchy's territories, and to their written chancery language, legal tradition, and, in religious contexts, their predominantly Orthodox (and later, following developments treated in later chapters, partly Uniate) religious affiliation. It should not be understood as a precise ethnic category equivalent to any single modern nationality; the populations later described by this term are understood by historians as ancestral, in complex and much-debated ways, to modern Ukrainian and Belarusian national identities, but fourteenth-century Ruthenians did not think of themselves in modern Ukrainian or Belarusian national terms, a point examined further in Section 18.9.

Historical Vocabulary
Ruthenian

Ruthenian is the conventional English scholarly label used for the East Slavic written chancery language, and, by extension, the historically Rus'-descended, Orthodox (and later partly Uniate) population, of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania and, in later centuries, of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth. It derives ultimately from the Latin term for "Rus'." Contemporaries within the Grand Duchy itself typically called the written language "ruski" rather than "Ruthenian," and modern national historiographies—Belarusian, Ukrainian, Russian, Polish, and Western scholarship generally—use differing names for essentially the same historical written and cultural tradition. Students should therefore treat "Ruthenian" as this textbook's chosen scholarly convention rather than as a term that itself drew a sharp, universally recognized boundary in medieval usage.

The term matters historically because it names a genuine, historically documented East Slavic cultural and administrative tradition distinct from, though descended from the same Kyivan-era Rus' roots as, the northeastern Rus' tradition that developed into Muscovy. It must be handled with care in the classroom: "Ruthenian" is not a modern ethnicity, and its populations should not be identified simply as early Ukrainians, early Belarusians, or early Russians. Historians generally regard the Ruthenian tradition as an important, complex ancestral strand within the later development of both modern Ukrainian and modern Belarusian national identities, but this ancestral relationship developed over centuries and should never be treated as though it already existed in fully formed national terms in the fourteenth century.

Section 18.7

Religion and Cultural Competition

The religious landscape of the fourteenth-century Grand Duchy of Lithuania was exceptionally complex by the standards of medieval Europe. Ethnic Lithuanians in the Baltic core territories of the state remained predominantly adherents of indigenous Baltic pagan religious traditions until Jogaila's baptism in 1386, making Lithuania one of the last major pagan polities in Europe. A large share of the Grand Duchy's subjects, however, concentrated in the Rus' territories examined throughout this chapter, were Orthodox Christians, whose religious institutions, as Section 18.3 explained, continued to function largely without interruption under Lithuanian rule. Precise fourteenth-century population figures for the Grand Duchy do not survive, and students should be cautious about stating exact demographic proportions; what can be said with confidence is that the territory inhabited by East Slavic, Orthodox populations vastly exceeded the ethnically Lithuanian core in area, and most historians consider it likely that Rus' subjects also substantially outnumbered ethnic Lithuanians, particularly as the Grand Duchy's eastern and southern holdings grew across the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries.

Jogaila's baptism and the Union of Krewo introduced a third major religious tradition into this landscape: Latin Catholicism, now associated with the ruling dynasty itself and, following the 1386 union, increasingly favored through royal patronage, the establishment of Catholic bishoprics in ethnically Lithuanian territory, and, over subsequent generations, growing legal and political privileges attached to Catholic noble status. This shift should not be read as producing immediate or uniform religious conflict. Orthodox institutions and Orthodox elites continued to hold significant landed wealth, administrative authority, and social standing across most of the Grand Duchy's Rus' territories well beyond the period examined in this chapter, and outright persecution of Orthodox Christianity was not a general feature of fourteenth-century Lithuanian governance.

Nevertheless, the introduction of Catholicism as the dynasty's preferred faith set in motion longer-term processes of religious competition, elite conversion, and shifting legal privilege that would intensify significantly in later centuries, as subsequent chapters of this textbook will examine. Students should resist projecting the sharper Catholic-Orthodox conflicts of later Polish-Lithuanian history backward onto this chapter's period. In particular, the Union of Brest of 1596, which created a Uniate church combining Orthodox liturgical practice with recognition of papal authority and produced significant and lasting religious division within the Ruthenian population, belongs to a much later period of Polish-Lithuanian and Rus' history and should not be treated as though it already shaped fourteenth-century religious life. The fourteenth-century religious landscape examined in this section is better understood as one of genuine coexistence and gradual, uneven competition for royal favor and legal privilege, rather than as an already-polarized confessional conflict.

Section 18.8

Moscow, Lithuania, and Rival Claims to Rus'

By the closing decades of the fourteenth century, two substantial political centers, each governing significant Rus' populations and territory, existed side by side within the wider former Rus' world: the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, examined throughout this chapter, and the emerging complex of northeastern Rus' principalities in which Moscow, as Chapter 19 will examine in detail, was gradually asserting growing but still contested regional influence under continued, if evolving, Golden Horde suzerainty. Both political centers governed large Orthodox, East Slavic populations. Both maintained relationships, of very different kinds, with the institutions and memory of Kyivan-era Rus'. And both, over the following century and beyond, would develop political language asserting some form of claim to the broader Rus' inheritance.

It would be a serious historical distortion, however, to describe the situation at the close of this chapter's period as an already-mature, two-sided contest between a future Russia and a future Lithuania for the singular mantle of Rus'. Neither political center had yet articulated, let alone achieved, dominance over the other, and neither northeastern nor southwestern Rus' populations experienced their situation in the fourteenth century as participants in a binary national competition. Moscow itself remained, as Chapter 19 will make clear, only one among several competing northeastern principalities, still substantially subordinate to Golden Horde tributary authority; Lithuania, for its part, remained oriented as much toward its evolving relationship with Poland and its continued conflicts with the Teutonic Order as toward any programmatic contest with Moscow specifically.

The ecclesiastical question introduced in Section 18.3—the jurisdiction of the metropolitan see historically associated with "Kyiv and all Rus'"—provides one useful window onto the genuinely unsettled character of rival claims during this period. Because the Orthodox population of the former Rus' world was now divided between Lithuanian and northeastern Rus' political spheres, and because ecclesiastical jurisdiction did not map cleanly onto either state's political boundaries, the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries witnessed repeated disputes, negotiations, and occasional formal divisions concerning metropolitan authority, involving Lithuanian rulers, Muscovite rulers, and the Byzantine patriarchate as interested parties. This unsettled ecclesiastical landscape mirrors the unsettled political landscape examined throughout this chapter: institutions inherited from Kyivan Rus' did not automatically or exclusively attach themselves to any single fourteenth-century successor state.

This section deliberately stops short of resolving the question of which center "won" the contest for the Rus' inheritance, because posing the question in those terms already assumes an outcome that had not yet occurred, and arguably assumes a framework—winner-take-all national succession—that misrepresents how contemporaries themselves understood their political and religious world. Chapter 19 will take up directly the question of why Moscow, rather than Lithuania or some other center, eventually achieved the preeminence that hindsight makes appear, misleadingly, inevitable.

Section 18.9

Multiple Legacies of Medieval Rus'

This chapter's central interpretive conclusion follows directly from the evidence examined in its preceding sections: medieval Rus' did not produce a single, linear national successor. It produced multiple, genuinely distinct political and cultural trajectories, each drawing legitimately on aspects of the Kyivan-era and post-Kyivan Rus' inheritance, and each eventually contributing, in complex and much-debated ways, to the historical development of more than one modern nation.

Northeastern Rus', examined in Chapters 15, 17, and, beginning in the next chapter, 19 through 24, developed under sustained Golden Horde tributary supremacy into the political nucleus from which Muscovy, and eventually the Russian Empire, would emerge. Western and southwestern Rus', examined throughout this chapter, developed within the Grand Duchy of Lithuania and, in Galicia's case, the Kingdom of Poland, preserving Orthodox religious life, Rus' legal custom, and an East Slavic chancery tradition under Lithuanian and Polish dynastic authority. These territories and their populations contributed substantially, across the following centuries, to the historical development of what would become modern Belarus and modern Ukraine. Novgorod, examined in Chapter 14, followed yet another distinct trajectory for a considerable period, maintaining its own republican political institutions and extensive Baltic and Hanseatic commercial connections before its eventual incorporation into the Muscovite state, an event covered in Chapter 21.

Recognizing this multiplicity carries a direct historiographical consequence. Modern Russian, Ukrainian, and Belarusian historical traditions have each, at various points, claimed Kyivan Rus' as their own foundational inheritance, sometimes in ways that assert exclusive national ownership over a shared medieval past. This textbook's position, consistent with the methodological caution established in Chapter 1 and reinforced in Chapter 12's treatment of Rus' fragmentation, is that all three modern traditions draw legitimately on genuine historical connections to the Kyivan-era and post-Kyivan Rus' world, but that none of them should be understood as the sole, exclusive, or predetermined continuation of that world. The medieval evidence examined across Chapters 7 through 18 supports a picture of multiple, overlapping, and evolving inheritances rather than a single unbroken national line.

This conclusion also extends, in a more limited sense, to Lithuania and Poland themselves. The Grand Duchy of Lithuania that emerged from the processes examined in this chapter was not simply a Baltic state that happened to rule over Rus' territory; by the close of the fourteenth century, it was arguably better described as a composite Lithuanian-Rus' polity, in which Lithuanian dynastic authority governed, and was in turn substantially shaped by, a predominantly East Slavic, Orthodox population and its inherited institutions. Modern Lithuanian and Polish historical traditions, in different ways, also draw upon aspects of the history examined in this chapter.

Primary Source Window

The Act of Krewo (14 August 1385)

Creator: Issued in the name of Grand Duke Jogaila of Lithuania and his brothers · Date: 14 August 1385, at the castle of Krewo (Kreva) · Type: A diplomatic act recording commitments made in connection with negotiations for Jogaila's marriage to Queen Jadwiga of Poland and his prospective accession to the Polish throne.

Context: The surviving document, preserved and studied by historians of medieval Poland and Lithuania, records commitments made by Jogaila to Polish representatives as part of the negotiations that would lead, the following year, to his baptism, marriage to Jadwiga, and coronation as King of Poland. Historians generally treat the act as an authentic diplomatic record of these negotiations rather than as a fully worked-out constitutional treaty establishing a merged state; its precise legal meaning, especially the exact commitment implied by its Latin term for "attaching" or "joining" (applicare) Lithuania to the Polish Crown, has been debated by generations of Polish and Lithuanian historians.

Paraphrase of the act's central commitments (not a verbatim translated quotation):

The act records Jogaila's undertaking to accept Latin Christian baptism together with his still-pagan relatives and subjects, to marry Jadwiga, and to bring Lithuania and its associated lands into a lasting connection with the Kingdom of Poland, in terms whose precise scope—full incorporation, alliance, or something in between—remains a matter of scholarly interpretation.

Analysis: The Act of Krewo is valuable evidence precisely because it captures a moment of negotiation rather than a finished outcome: it records intentions and conditions rather than describing an already-functioning union. Historians disagree over how literally to read its language of "attaching" Lithuania to Poland, since the actual relationship that developed after 1386—as Section 18.4 explains—remained a personal union under a shared monarch rather than an institutional merger. The act should therefore be read alongside, not as a substitute for, the events of 1386 themselves: Jogaila's baptism, marriage, and coronation, which actually inaugurated the relationship the 1385 act had anticipated. As a source, the act also reflects the interests and perspective of the negotiating parties—Polish nobles and clergy seeking a Catholic monarch and a secure succession, and a Lithuanian ruling dynasty seeking to resolve external pressure from the Teutonic Order—rather than the perspective of the overwhelmingly Orthodox, East Slavic population who made up so much of the Grand Duchy's own territory and who are not addressed in the act at all.

Questions for students:

  1. What specific commitments does the Act of Krewo record, and whose interests do those commitments primarily reflect?
  2. Why do historians treat the act as a record of negotiation rather than as evidence of an already-completed political union?
  3. Why is the precise meaning of the act's language for "attaching" Lithuania to Poland historically contested?
  4. What does the absence of any reference to the Grand Duchy's Orthodox, East Slavic population in this document suggest about whose interests shaped high-level Polish-Lithuanian diplomacy in 1385?
Source Method

Reading Lithuanian Grand Ducal Confirmation Charters

Source genre: Grand ducal confirmation charter · Language: the Grand Duchy's East Slavic chancery language, called "ruski" by contemporaries and "Ruthenian" in this textbook's scholarly convention · Surviving corpus: a substantial body of such charters survives in archives and published document collections, with an organized grand ducal chancery well documented from the reign of Vytautas beginning in 1392.

Rather than presenting a single named charter here—since responsibly selecting and translating one specific surviving document is a task better suited to a dedicated sourcebook than to a general textbook feature—this section teaches the method historians use to read this well-documented genre as a class of evidence. This is a lesson in how to read a type of source, not a substitute for reading an actual example.

What the genre typically contains: Surviving Lithuanian grand ducal charters of this kind generally confirm existing landholding, religious foundations, or municipal privileges for communities and individuals within the Grand Duchy's Rus' territories. They frequently invoke established custom or privileges traced to earlier Rus' princely rule as the basis for confirmation, while also specifying obligations of loyalty, military service, or tribute owed in return, and asserting the Grand Duke's own authority to grant, confirm, or withhold such privilege.

Analysis: Read as a class of evidence, this charter genre illustrates concretely the pattern examined in Section 18.3: Lithuanian rulers did not seek to erase existing Rus' landholding and privilege but rather positioned themselves as the new confirming authority over continuing local arrangements. The recurring references to earlier Rus' princely custom suggest that Lithuanian authority was frequently exercised, at least rhetorically, through claimed continuity with the Rus' past rather than through its repudiation. At the same time, the charter format makes clear that ultimate authority to grant, confirm, or potentially revoke privilege rested with the Grand Duke, illustrating the real, if often subtly exercised, dimension of Lithuanian sovereignty over its Rus' territories. As with any genre of official document, historians must also ask what such charters leave out: they record grants and confirmations that succeeded, not disputes that failed, denials, or the perspective of those without standing to obtain a charter at all.

Questions for students:

  1. Why might a historian prefer to study many charters of this genre together rather than treating any single example as representative on its own?
  2. What does the recurring practice of confirming rights by reference to earlier Rus' princely custom suggest about how Lithuanian rulers sought legitimacy in their newly acquired territories?
  3. What kinds of obligations typically accompanied confirmation of privilege, and what does that reveal about the relationship between grand duke and subject?
  4. What historical questions can this genre of evidence answer well, and what questions does it leave largely unanswered?
Historical Debate

Was Lithuania a "Rus' State"?

The question: Given the very large Orthodox, East Slavic population and the predominance of Rus'-derived law, language, and institutions across much of its territory, should the fourteenth-century Grand Duchy of Lithuania be understood primarily as a Lithuanian state that ruled over Rus' lands, or as, in essential respects, a continuation of Rus' political and cultural life under a Lithuanian dynasty?

The "Lithuanian state" position: Some historians emphasize that ultimate sovereign authority, the ruling dynasty, the state's founding political core, and its military organization remained distinctly Lithuanian throughout this period, and that describing the Grand Duchy as a "Rus' state" risks obscuring the specifically Lithuanian political achievement of building and defending an enormous multiethnic realm against determined external pressure, particularly from the Teutonic Order.
The "Lithuanian-Rus' composite state" position: Other historians argue that sovereignty and dynasty are only part of the picture, and that the Grand Duchy's demographic composition, administrative language, legal custom, and religious life were so predominantly Rus'-derived across most of its territory that the state is better understood as a genuinely composite Lithuanian-Rus' (and, in later periods, increasingly Polish-influenced) polity, in which Rus' institutional and cultural continuity was at least as historically significant as Lithuanian dynastic rule.

Evidence: Historians weigh the ethnic and religious composition of the population, the language of surviving administrative and legal documents, the treatment of existing Rus' elites, the degree of continued Orthodox institutional autonomy, and the relative political weight of ethnically Lithuanian versus Ruthenian elites within the Grand Duchy's governing structures.

Points of agreement: Most historians agree that the Grand Duchy of Lithuania cannot be understood, even in its Baltic core, in isolation from its Rus' territories, and that describing it simply as "Lithuania" without qualification risks significant distortion.

Points of disagreement: Historians differ over how much interpretive weight to place on dynastic sovereignty versus demographic and institutional composition, and over how this balance shifted across the fourteenth, fifteenth, and sixteenth centuries as Polish and Catholic influence grew.

Student task: Using evidence from Sections 18.2, 18.3, and 18.6, construct a short argument for one of the two positions above, explicitly identifying which category of evidence—dynastic, demographic, linguistic, legal, or religious—you find most persuasive and why.

Multiple Perspectives

Governing a Divided Inheritance

A Gediminid grand ducal official at Vilnius: For an official at the Lithuanian court, the incorporation of vast Rus' territories represented an enormous expansion of resources, tribute, and prestige, but also a genuine administrative challenge: governing a realm in which the ruling dynasty's own language and, until 1386, religion differed from those of a large share of its subjects required continual accommodation, negotiation, and reliance on local elites and their existing institutions.
An Orthodox boyar in Kyiv or Volhynia: For a Rus' boyar whose family had held land and local authority for generations, incorporation into the Grand Duchy of Lithuania could mean a change of distant sovereign more than a change in daily life, so long as landholding, religious practice, and local custom continued to be respected; loyalty to a Lithuanian grand duke did not necessarily feel like a rupture with Rus' identity or tradition.
An Orthodox metropolitan or bishop navigating divided jurisdiction: For a senior Orthodox churchman responsible for a see whose historic jurisdiction now spanned both Lithuanian and northeastern Rus' political territory, the fragmentation of the Rus' political world created genuine practical and theological difficulty: maintaining a single ecclesiastical structure across politically divided and increasingly rival territories required constant negotiation with Lithuanian rulers, Muscovite rulers, and the Byzantine patriarchate alike.

These perspectives illustrate that the political fragmentation examined throughout this chapter was experienced very differently depending on one's position within the Grand Duchy's governing elite, its Rus' landed nobility, or its Orthodox ecclesiastical hierarchy—and that none of these positions maps neatly onto a modern national viewpoint.

Cause and Effect

Lithuanian Expansion into Rus'

Long-Term Conditions

Baltic political consolidation driven by a combination of sustained crusading pressure from the Teutonic and Livonian Orders, internal dynastic competition among Lithuanian rulers, and control over military manpower and tribute; the political fragmentation of Rus' examined in Chapters 12 and 13; Golden Horde tributary supremacy over the Rus' principalities examined in Chapter 17, which left many Rus' territories politically exposed.

Intermediate Developments

Dynastic state-building under Mindaugas and, more durably, Gediminas; growing Lithuanian military and diplomatic capacity; increasing dynastic and marital connections between Gediminid and Rurikid princely families.

Short-Term Triggers

Opportunities created by weak or contested succession in specific Rus' principalities; military campaigning under Algirdas into the Dnieper basin; assertion of Lithuanian authority against Tatar claims in the southern borderlands.

Effects

Lithuanian incorporation of Kyiv and much of present-day Belarus and Ukraine by the later fourteenth century; the emergence of a religiously and institutionally composite Lithuanian-Rus' state; the eventual Polish-Lithuanian dynastic union of 1385–1386.

Historical complexity: No single cause—not Baltic state formation alone, not Rus' fragmentation alone, not Horde weakness alone—fully explains Lithuanian expansion; the process depended on the interaction of all three.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Kyivan Rus' to Two Successor Worlds

What Stayed the Same

  • Orthodox Christian religious institutions and practice continued largely uninterrupted across most former Rus' territory under Lithuanian rule.
  • Rus' customary law, princely and boyar landholding, and local administrative practice persisted under a new sovereign dynasty.
  • The East Slavic Ruthenian chancery language continued as the ordinary language of government and law across most of the Grand Duchy's territory.

What Changed

  • Ultimate sovereignty passed from Rurikid Rus' princes to the Gediminid Lithuanian dynasty across most of the western and southwestern Rus' lands.
  • The political and cultural inheritance of Kyivan Rus' became divided among at least three distinct trajectories: Lithuanian-ruled western and southern Rus', Polish-ruled Galicia, and the still Horde-tributary northeastern principalities.
  • After 1386, the Grand Duchy's ruling dynasty adopted Latin Catholicism, introducing a new religious dimension absent from the earlier, exclusively Orthodox-versus-pagan landscape.

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing simultaneous continuity and change equips students to understand the Lithuanian-Rus' world as a genuine historical continuation of Rus' institutions under new sovereignty, rather than either an unbroken Rus' polity or a wholly foreign imposition.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Ruthenian Chancery Scribe

Beyond the grand dukes, princes, and bishops who dominate the surviving narrative sources for this period, the ordinary functioning of Lithuanian-Rus' government depended on a class of literate scribes and clerks trained in the Ruthenian chancery tradition, who drafted charters, recorded land transactions, maintained court records, and conducted correspondence in the East Slavic written language examined in Section 18.6. Such a scribe's professional training drew directly on the literary and legal culture of Kyivan-era and post-Kyivan Rus', including formulaic legal language with roots extending back toward the Russkaya Pravda examined in earlier chapters, even as the political sovereign issuing the documents was now a Lithuanian grand duke.

For an ordinary Rus' townsperson, monastery official, or minor landholder interacting with Lithuanian grand ducal administration, daily and legal life would likely have felt substantially continuous with earlier Rus' practice: disputes were adjudicated according to familiar customary law, land transactions were recorded in familiar language and format, and religious life continued through familiar Orthodox institutions. The most significant change for most such individuals would have come not through any dramatic cultural rupture but through the more distant fact of ultimate political authority: tribute, military obligation, and final judicial appeal now flowed toward Vilnius rather than toward a Rurikid Rus' prince or, in the more purely tributary northeastern lands, toward the khan of the Golden Horde.

Historical Vocabulary
Union of Krewo

Union of Krewo refers to the 1385 dynastic agreement between representatives of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania and the Kingdom of Poland, under which Grand Duke Jogaila undertook to accept Latin Christian baptism and to marry Queen Jadwiga of Poland, leading to his 1386 coronation as King Władysław II Jagiełło of Poland and founding the Jagiellonian royal dynasty.

The term matters historically because it names the beginning, rather than the completion, of the Polish-Lithuanian political relationship: the 1385 agreement laid the foundation for the personal, dynastic union actually inaugurated by Jogaila's baptism and coronation in 1386, a union centered on a shared monarch, not an immediate merger of Polish and Lithuanian institutions, law, or administration. Students should distinguish the Union of Krewo sharply from the much later Union of Lublin of 1569, which created a much closer federal union—the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, with a jointly elected monarch and a common parliament—while Poland and Lithuania nonetheless remained legally distinct states retaining separate law, administration, and armies; conflating the two events, separated by nearly two centuries, is a common and significant historical error.

Regional Context

Galicia and Volhynia

Galicia and Volhynia, examined together in Chapter 13 as the two component territories of a single powerful Rus' successor principality, diverged sharply in political fate during the period examined in this chapter. Galicia, centered on the city of Lviv, came under increasingly durable Polish royal authority by the 1380s and 1390s, formally incorporated into the Kingdom of Poland's administrative structure in ways that would shape its subsequent religious, legal, and social development along lines closer to those of the Polish crown lands generally. Volhynia, by contrast, was drawn substantially into the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, following the broader pattern of elite accommodation, institutional continuity, and Ruthenian chancery administration examined throughout this chapter.

This divergence should not be understood as a clean, one-time partition; the fourteenth-century contest over Galicia-Volhynia involved shifting military campaigns, contested claims by Hungary as well as Poland and Lithuania, and gradual rather than instantaneous consolidation of authority on each side. The lasting significance of this regional divergence lies in what it demonstrates about the fragmentation of the Rus' political and cultural inheritance: a single former Rus' principality could split into components following genuinely different long-term historical trajectories, each shaped by a different neighboring power, without any of those trajectories representing a more "authentic" or exclusive continuation of the Rus' past than the others.

Russia and the World

The Teutonic Order and the Baltic Frontier

The history examined in this chapter cannot be fully understood without attention to the Teutonic Order and the closely associated Livonian Order, German crusading military organizations whose sustained campaigns against the pagan Baltic and Finnic peoples of the southeastern Baltic coast directly shaped Lithuanian political consolidation, as Section 18.1 explained. These orders represented a broader medieval European pattern of crusading warfare directed not only toward the eastern Mediterranean but toward pagan populations along Latin Christendom's northeastern frontier, and their sustained military pressure gave Lithuanian state-building an urgency and external orientation quite distinct from the internal, Horde-tributary politics shaping the northeastern Rus' principalities during the same decades.

The joint Polish-Lithuanian military victory over the Teutonic Order at the Battle of Grunwald in 1410, achieved under the continued dynastic association established by the Union of Krewo and under the practical leadership of Vytautas as Grand Duke of Lithuania, illustrates one significant consequence of the union examined in Section 18.4: it provided Lithuania with a powerful ally capable of helping check a threat that had shaped Lithuanian politics for nearly two centuries. This episode also illustrates the wider theme, introduced in earlier chapters and recurring throughout this textbook, that the history of the Rus' lands cannot be separated from the history of neighboring Baltic, Central European, and Latin Christian political and military systems.

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has traced the emergence of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania from a Baltic, initially pagan dynastic polity into one of the largest and most religiously and institutionally diverse states in fourteenth-century Europe, governing enormous stretches of the former Rus' world alongside its Baltic core territory. Before proceeding to Chapter 19's examination of why Moscow specifically rose to preeminence among the northeastern Rus' principalities, students should apply this chapter's lessons to four short problems.

Problem 1 — Methods of Expansion.

Using evidence from Sections 18.1 and 18.2, explain why Lithuanian acquisition of Rus' territory should be understood as a combination of conquest, succession, dynastic alliance, and negotiated submission rather than as conquest alone.

Problem 2 — Continuity Under New Sovereignty.

Using evidence from Sections 18.3 and 18.6, explain how Rus' law, language, religion, and elite authority persisted within the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, and why this continuity complicates a simple "foreign conquest" narrative.

Problem 3 — Union, Not Merger.

Using evidence from Section 18.4, explain the difference between the 1385 Krewo agreement and the dynastic union inaugurated in 1386, and then explain how both differ from the later 1569 Union of Lublin, and why conflating any of these events distorts the history of this period.

Problem 4 — Multiple Successors.

Using evidence from Sections 18.5, 18.8, and 18.9, explain why historians resist describing either Moscow or Lithuania as the single, predetermined heir to medieval Rus'.

These problems reinforce the habits of mind—attention to multiple causal factors, recognition of institutional continuity amid political change, precise use of terminology, and skepticism toward linear national narratives—that Chapter 19 will apply directly to the specific question of Moscow's rise.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the incorporation of large portions of the former Rus' world into the Grand Duchy of Lithuania and, through Poland, into the wider Latin Christian political system of Central Europe, arguing that this development demonstrates conclusively that Moscow was only one of several plausible political heirs to medieval Rus'.

The chapter began with Lithuania's own state formation, tracing the consolidation of Baltic political power under Mindaugas and, more durably, Gediminas, as a process shaped by sustained crusading pressure from the Teutonic and Livonian Orders alongside internal dynastic competition, control over military resources, and shifting alliances among Lithuanian elites—rather than as any deliberate project of Rus' liberation or anti-Mongol resistance. It then examined the methods by which Lithuanian rulers, particularly Gediminas and Algirdas, acquired authority over Rus' territories, emphasizing conquest, princely succession, dynastic alliance, and negotiated submission as complementary rather than mutually exclusive processes, and treating the chronology of the Battle of Blue Waters with appropriate scholarly caution.

A central section examined the reliance of Lithuanian rulers on existing Orthodox, East Slavic elites—princes, boyars, and clergy—in governing their newly acquired territories, and the continued functioning of Rus' law, administration, and religious life largely without interruption, an ecclesiastical and institutional continuity complicated by ongoing disputes over jurisdiction of the metropolitan see of "Kyiv and all Rus'."

The chapter then turned to the Polish-Lithuanian dynastic relationship, using the Act of Krewo (1385) as primary-source evidence of the negotiations that preceded it, and explaining Jogaila's 1386 baptism and coronation as the events that actually inaugurated a personal, dynastic union rather than an immediate institutional merger. This distinction was reinforced by Vytautas's substantially autonomous rule of Lithuania between 1392 and 1430, and it was carefully distinguished from the much closer federal union established by the Union of Lublin in 1569, in which Poland and Lithuania nonetheless remained legally distinct states in important respects even as they came to share a monarch and a common parliament.

The chapter examined the divergent fourteenth-century fates of Galicia and Volhynia, formerly united under Galicia-Volhynian princely authority, as Galicia passed toward Polish royal authority and Volhynia toward the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, illustrating that even a single former Rus' principality could split into genuinely different long-term trajectories. It then analyzed the political and legal diversity of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania itself, emphasizing the continued official use of the Ruthenian chancery language and Rus' customary law, while distinguishing this chapter's fourteenth-century period clearly from the later codification represented by the Lithuanian Statutes beginning in 1529.

Religious developments received sustained attention: the chapter traced the coexistence of Baltic paganism, Orthodox Christianity, and, after 1386, Latin Catholicism within a single composite state, cautioning against projecting the sharper confessional conflicts of later centuries, including the 1596 Union of Brest, backward onto the fourteenth century's comparatively fluid and uneven religious competition.

The chapter's central historiographical sections examined Moscow and Lithuania as two coexisting, competing, but not yet decisively ranked centers of political authority over Rus' populations by the close of the fourteenth century, using the unsettled ecclesiastical jurisdiction of the metropolitan see of "Kyiv and all Rus'" as evidence of the genuinely open character of rival claims during this period. The chapter concluded that medieval Rus' produced multiple, legitimate successor trajectories—northeastern, Lithuanian-Rus', and Galician-Polish, alongside Novgorod's distinct republican path—each contributing, in complex ways developed across later centuries, to the historical inheritance claimed by more than one modern nation.

These lessons—attention to multiple causal mechanisms behind territorial expansion, recognition of institutional and cultural continuity beneath changing sovereignty, precise historical terminology, and explicit resistance to linear national narratives—prepare students directly for Chapter 19's examination of why Moscow specifically, rather than Lithuania, Novgorod, Tver, or any other center, eventually rose to preeminence among the successors of Rus'.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Grand Duchy of Lithuania
The Baltic-founded state that, by the later fourteenth century, governed enormous portions of the former Rus' world alongside its Lithuanian core territory.
Gediminid dynasty
The ruling dynasty of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania founded by Gediminas, from which nearly all later Lithuanian and Polish-Lithuanian rulers descended.
Dynastic union
A political arrangement in which two states share a common ruler while retaining separate institutions, as distinguished from a full institutional merger.
Union of Krewo
The 1385 agreement in which Jogaila committed to baptism and marriage to Jadwiga, laying the foundation for the Polish-Lithuanian dynastic union inaugurated in 1386; distinct from the later 1569 Union of Lublin.
Ruthenian
The conventional English scholarly label for the East Slavic chancery language (called "ruski" by contemporaries) and, by extension, the Rus'-descended Orthodox population of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania and later the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth.
Chancery language
The official written language used for government administration, law, and correspondence within a state.
Customary law
Legal norms based on established local practice and precedent rather than centralized written codification, characteristic of the Grand Duchy before the sixteenth-century Lithuanian Statutes.
Teutonic Order
A German crusading military order established along the Baltic coast whose pressure on pagan Baltic peoples spurred Lithuanian political consolidation.
Livonian Order
The Livonian branch of the Teutonic Order, active along the northeastern Baltic coast.
Boyar
A member of the landed aristocracy of Rus' society, whose authority and landholding frequently persisted under Lithuanian rule.
Orthodox Christianity
The predominant religion of the East Slavic population incorporated into the Grand Duchy of Lithuania.
Latin Catholicism
The Western Christian tradition adopted by the Lithuanian ruling dynasty following Jogaila's 1386 baptism.
Baltic paganism
The indigenous pre-Christian religious tradition of the Lithuanians and related Baltic peoples, practiced by most ethnic Lithuanians until 1386.
Galicia-Volhynia
The former Rus' principality whose two component territories diverged politically in the fourteenth century, with Galicia passing to Poland and much of Volhynia to Lithuania.
Lithuanian Statutes
A series of legal codes issued for the Grand Duchy of Lithuania beginning in 1529, later than this chapter's period but building on traditions examined here.
Union of Lublin (later)
The 1569 union creating the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, a much closer federal union with a jointly elected monarch and common parliament, though Poland and Lithuania remained legally distinct states in other respects; distinct from and much later than the 1385 Union of Krewo.
Battle of Blue Waters
A mid-fourteenth-century engagement traditionally associated with Lithuanian assertion of authority against Tatar claims in the southern Rus' borderlands; precise dating debated.
Successor state
A political entity that inherits territory, institutions, or legitimacy from a previously existing state; this chapter identifies multiple successor states to Kyivan-era Rus'.
Elite continuity
The persistence of existing ruling, landholding, or clerical elites under a new sovereign authority, characteristic of Lithuanian governance of Rus' territories.
Political fragmentation
The division of a formerly unified political entity into multiple, often competing, successor centers, examined for Rus' beginning in Chapter 12.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has carried students from the Baltic political consolidation of thirteenth-century Lithuania through the incorporation of vast Rus' territories, including Kyiv itself, into a composite Lithuanian-Rus' state, and into the beginnings of Lithuania's long dynastic association with Poland. Far from a marginal episode in the history of medieval Eastern Europe, the developments examined here shaped the political, legal, religious, and cultural life of enormous East Slavic populations for centuries, and they laid foundations that later historical traditions in Belarus, Ukraine, Lithuania, and Poland would each draw upon in different ways.

The chapter's central lesson—that medieval Rus' produced multiple, coexisting successor trajectories rather than a single national line converging inevitably on Moscow—should stay with students as this textbook turns, in Chapter 19, to the specific question of Moscow's rise. That chapter will not treat Moscow's eventual preeminence as a foregone conclusion; rather, it will ask what specific combination of geography, dynastic strategy, relations with the Golden Horde, and ecclesiastical politics allowed one northeastern principality among several, including Tver and others, to outpace its rivals, at a moment when Lithuania, as this chapter has shown, controlled far more territory and, quite plausibly, a larger population of Rus' subjects.

As the textbook proceeds into Part IV's sustained treatment of Moscow's rise and the Muscovite state, students should carry forward this chapter's insistence on contingency, its careful terminological distinctions among overlapping political unions and legal codes, and its recognition that institutional and cultural continuity with the Rus' past took multiple, simultaneously legitimate forms across a divided medieval world.
