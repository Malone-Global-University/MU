Part II — The Formation of Rus'
9

Vladimir the Great and the Christianization of Rus'

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

According to the Primary Chronicle, when Vladimir's envoys returned from observing the Byzantine liturgy at Constantinople, they reported that they no longer wished to remain in paganism, for they had witnessed a form of worship unlike any other on earth. Whatever the episode's literal accuracy, later Rus' Christians told this story about themselves to explain why their princes had chosen Byzantium.

In the closing years of the tenth century, on a bluff above the Dnieper River at Kyiv, the wooden and stone images of Perun, Khors, Dazhbog, Stribog, Simargl, and Mokosh—the deities that the Primary Chronicle reports Prince Vladimir Sviatoslavich had himself erected there only a few years earlier—were reportedly thrown down, dragged through the streets, beaten, and cast into the river. The same chronicle reports that the prince who had ordered their construction now ordered their destruction, and that he summoned the people of Kyiv to be baptized in the waters of the Dnieper. No contemporary Rus' document independently confirms the precise sequence of events that later Kyivan clergy set down, decades afterward, in the compilation historians call the Primary Chronicle. What can be established with confidence is that Vladimir Sviatoslavich, prince of Kyiv from the early 980s until 1015, adopted Byzantine Christianity for himself, his court, and his realm in a process conventionally dated to the year 988, and that this adoption set in motion one of the most consequential transformations in the history examined by this textbook.

This chapter is not a saint's life. It does not ask students to accept that Vladimir, a ruler who had seized power through fratricidal war, kept, according to the Chronicle's own hostile earlier portrait, numerous wives and concubines, and ruled through the same instruments of tribute and coercion examined in Chapters 7 and 8, simply discovered spiritual truth and set his realm on a predetermined path toward Christian civilization. Instead, this chapter treats Vladimir's conversion as a historical problem: a decision embedded in dynastic politics, Byzantine diplomacy, competing regional religious traditions, and the practical needs of a still-young and only loosely integrated princely state. It asks what evidence survives, how reliable that evidence is, and what changed—immediately and over subsequent generations—once the prince of Kyiv began sponsoring a single, institutionally organized faith.

Chapter 8 left Rus' with Sviatoslav's death in 972 amid war along the Danube and a fragile succession among his sons. That chapter also established an important precedent: Vladimir's grandmother Olga had personally adopted Christianity a generation earlier, although the precise date and place of her baptism remain disputed, without any comparable transformation of Rus' society following from her individual choice. Olga's baptism was personal and dynastic. It changed her own status and connections but did not make Rus' a Christian polity. Chapter 9 examines how her grandson's adoption of the same faith became something categorically different: a ruler-sponsored transformation of an entire political community's official religious identity, backed by institutions, art, marriage alliance, and law, even though—crucially—the underlying society did not change its beliefs and practices overnight.

The stakes of getting this history right are considerable. Christianization gave Rus' princes a new vocabulary of legitimate rulership grounded in scripture and Byzantine political theology; it opened durable channels of contact with Constantinople, the wealthiest and most sophisticated state in the medieval Christian world; it introduced an expanded literate culture built on an already-existing Slavic ecclesiastical language; and it left behind churches, icons, and chronicles that still shape how Russians, Ukrainians, and Belarusians understand their shared and separate inheritances from medieval Rus'. At the same time, later centuries built elaborate national and religious narratives on top of these events, narratives that this chapter must examine critically rather than repeat uncritically.

This chapter therefore moves along two tracks at once. One track follows Vladimir's political career: his violent rise to sole rule over Rus', his political and military relationship with the Byzantine Empire, and his sponsorship of a new religious establishment. The other track follows the deeper, slower history of Christianization as a social process—a process that began before Vladimir, accelerated dramatically under his patronage, and continued for generations after his death in 1015, unevenly, incompletely, and in constant negotiation with older beliefs and practices that did not simply disappear.

By the chapter's end, students should be able to distinguish clearly between the chronicle's dramatic narrative of a single decisive year and the more gradual, contested, and regionally uneven reality that modern historians reconstruct from a wider body of evidence. They should also be equipped to explain why 988 remains a genuinely important date in the history of Rus'—not because everything changed instantly on that date, but because it marks the moment when the ruling authority of Kyiv committed itself, publicly and irrevocably, to a single institutional faith with lasting consequences for politics, culture, and identity across the territories this textbook continues to examine in Chapters 10 through 12 and well beyond.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace Vladimir Sviatoslavich's rise to sole rule over Rus' following the succession crisis that followed Sviatoslav's death in 972.
  2. Describe the diverse pre-Christian religious environment of Rus', including Slavic, Scandinavian, Finnic, and steppe elements, and Vladimir's reported pagan cult at Kyiv.
  3. Explain that Christianity already existed in Rus' before 988, through Olga's conversion and Christian merchants and warriors, and why this matters for interpreting Vladimir's later choice.
  4. Analyze the Primary Chronicle's "testing of the faiths" narrative as a constructed literary and theological account rather than a literal historical record.
  5. Evaluate the political, military, and diplomatic context of Vladimir's relationship with Byzantine Emperor Basil II, including the marriage to Princess Anna.
  6. Distinguish the Chronicle's narrative of Vladimir's baptism from the disputed historical reconstructions of its date, location, and sequence.
  7. Explain the difference between Vladimir's individual conversion and the ruler-sponsored Christianization of the wider population.
  8. Describe the institutional foundations of the early Rus' church, including clergy, ecclesiastical structure, and princely patronage such as the Church of the Tithes.
  9. Assess the cultural consequences of Christianization for literacy, architecture, and sacred art, situating Rus' within an existing Slavic Christian literary tradition.
  10. Interpret Christianization as an uneven, extended historical process rather than a single completed event in 988.
  11. Compare Olga's personal conversion with Vladimir's ruler-sponsored Christianization of Rus' as a society.
  12. Connect the developments examined in this chapter to Yaroslav's later consolidation of church and law in Chapter 10 and to the broader social history of religion examined in Chapter 11.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did Vladimir's violent path to power shape the kind of ruler who would later sponsor a religious transformation of his realm?
  2. Why is it inaccurate to describe pre-Christian Rus' as possessing a single, unified pagan religion?
  3. What does the "testing of the faiths" narrative reveal about how later Christian Rus' understood its place among neighboring civilizations, and what does it fail to tell historians about actual tenth-century events?
  4. In what ways was Vladimir's conversion simultaneously religious, political, diplomatic, and dynastic?
  5. Why do historians disagree about the exact sequence of events surrounding Cherson, Anna's marriage, and Vladimir's baptism?
  6. What is the difference between a ruler adopting a new faith and a population becoming Christian?
  7. What institutions had to be built before Christianity could function as more than a princely preference?
  8. Why should Christianization be understood as a process extending over generations rather than a single event in 988?

Historical Context

Chapter 8 traced the militarized consolidation of Rus' under Oleg, Igor, Olga, and Sviatoslav: tribute collection through the polyudie system, campaigns against Constantinople and the Khazars, and Sviatoslav's ultimately fatal ambitions along the Danube frontier. That chapter also introduced Olga's conversion to Christianity as a genuinely important but strictly personal and dynastic event. Olga's conversion is securely attested, but its precise date and place remain disputed; Constantine VII independently records her reception at Constantinople in 957 without explicitly describing a baptism there, and historians continue to debate whether her baptism occurred during that visit or earlier. Whatever its precise circumstances, Olga's conversion opened a channel of prestige and contact with Byzantium and may have shaped the religious environment in which her grandson Vladimir was raised, but it did not convert Kyiv, let alone Rus' as a whole. Sviatoslav himself, according to the Chronicle, resisted his mother's urging to accept Christianity, reportedly fearing the reaction of his retinue.

By the 970s, Rus' was a loosely integrated collection of fortified towns and tributary territories linked by the river network described in Chapter 2 and unified chiefly through the person and retinue of the Kyivan prince, whose authority rested on military force, tribute collection, and control of long-distance trade along the Dnieper route toward Byzantium described in Chapter 6. Religious life across this territory was correspondingly diverse and decentralized: East Slavic agricultural communities maintained their own household and communal religious practices; Scandinavian settlers and warriors, still numerous within princely retinues in this period, brought their own gods and customs; Finnic populations across the northern forests maintained distinct religious traditions; and steppe neighbors, including Khazars whose elite had adopted Judaism and various Turkic groups with their own beliefs, added further variety along the southern frontier. Byzantine Christian merchants, captives, and diplomats moved through Kyiv regularly, and a Christian presence, including Christian members of the Rus' elite and a church at Kyiv, is attested in the mid-tenth century, well before Vladimir's conversion.

The wider Christian world that Rus' bordered was itself still institutionally undivided in the formal sense familiar to later centuries. The event historians conventionally call the Great Schism is dated to 1054, more than sixty years after Vladimir's conversion, but 1054 functions best as a symbolic and administrative milestone in a much longer, gradual estrangement rather than as the single moment communion between Rome and Constantinople was definitively and permanently severed; many historians of the church point to still later developments, including the events of 1204, as at least equally decisive for the final, hardened separation. In the 980s, meaningful differences of language, liturgy, ecclesiastical practice, and political relationship already existed between Rome and Constantinople, and Rus' rulers were aware of both traditions, but no single, final break yet divided them. This chapter therefore avoids describing Vladimir's choice as a decision "between Orthodoxy and Catholicism" in the settled, fully divided sense those terms would carry only after centuries of further estrangement, and instead uses the more historically accurate language of Byzantine Christianity, the Byzantine rite, or Greek Christianity as the tradition Vladimir adopted.

Byzantium itself, in the late 980s, was passing through a severe internal crisis. The young emperor Basil II faced a serious military rebellion led by the general Bardas Phokas, a crisis substantial enough that Basil turned to Rus' for military assistance—a decision with enormous consequences for the story this chapter tells. Understanding Vladimir's conversion requires understanding this Byzantine emergency as much as it requires understanding Rus' internal politics, since the two problems became intertwined through negotiations that combined military alliance, dynastic marriage, and religious conversion into a single, difficult-to-disentangle sequence of events.

Section 9.1

Vladimir's Rise to Power

Sviatoslav's death in 972, ambushed by Pecheneg forces while returning from his Danube campaigns as described in Chapter 8, left Rus' without a clear single successor. Sviatoslav had, in the pattern this textbook has already examined for earlier Rurikid princes, distributed authority over different parts of his realm among his sons during his lifetime: Yaropolk held Kyiv itself, the most prestigious center; Oleg held the Dereva lands, associated with the Drevlian territory whose earlier resistance to tribute collection Chapter 8 examined; and Vladimir, reportedly Sviatoslav's son by a woman of lower status within the princely household, held Novgorod in the north, the same northern trading center that Chapter 7 identified as a founding node of the Rus' political network.

Rivalry among the brothers escalated into open war within a few years of Sviatoslav's death. According to the Chronicle's account, conflict between Yaropolk and Oleg resulted in Oleg's death, whether by battle or accident during a retreat, an event the Chronicle presents with some ambiguity regarding the degree of Yaropolk's direct responsibility. Vladimir, fearing that Yaropolk's growing dominance over Kyiv and the Dereva lands threatened his own position in Novgorod, is reported to have fled north, most plausibly to Scandinavia, where existing Rus'-Scandinavian ties, examined in Chapter 7's discussion of Varangian connections, gave him access to recruit or hire additional armed followers.

Vladimir returned from this period of exile with sufficient military support to reassert and expand his position. He first recovered Novgorod and then moved south against Yaropolk's Kyiv. The details of the ensuing campaign, as the Chronicle presents them, include negotiations, betrayal by members of Yaropolk's own entourage, and finally Yaropolk's death at the hands of Vladimir's agents under circumstances the Chronicle frames as treacherous even by the standards of princely succession warfare common in this period. By approximately 980, Vladimir had eliminated both rival brothers and secured sole rule over the Rus' lands his father had once governed, becoming prince of Kyiv.

This sequence of events matters for the chapter's larger argument in a specific way: the ruler who would later present himself, and be presented by later Christian tradition, as a transformative sponsor of Christian civilization first established his authority through the same kind of fratricidal dynastic warfare, foreign military recruitment, and calculated betrayal that characterized Rus' princely politics more generally in this period. Nothing in Vladimir's rise to power in the late 970s anticipated or required his later religious choices. Students should resist reading his youth as a foreshadowing of sainthood; later Christian hagiography did precisely this kind of retrospective reshaping, and this textbook's task is to separate that later theological reconstruction from the political record of a ruler consolidating power through familiar, often brutal, methods. The succession crisis that would follow Vladimir's own death in 1015, involving his sons including the future Yaroslav, belongs to Chapter 10 and is not addressed here.

Section 9.2

Religion Before Christianization

Describing the religious world Vladimir inherited and briefly attempted to reshape before adopting Christianity requires considerably more care than the simple label "paganism" allows. Rus' in the 970s and 980s encompassed multiple overlapping religious traditions rather than one coherent belief system, reflecting the diverse populations examined in Chapters 5 and 6: East Slavic agricultural communities with their own deities, rituals, and calendar observances; Scandinavian settlers and warriors within princely retinues who maintained their own gods, including figures such as Thor and Odin, whose worship left traces in Rus' material culture and whose influence on the composition of the later Kyivan pantheon remains debated among specialists; Finnic populations of the northern forests with distinct religious traditions of their own; and steppe neighbors practicing still other beliefs, including the Judaism associated with the Khazar elite examined in Chapter 8 and various Turkic religious traditions along the southern frontier.

Within East Slavic practice specifically, sources point to a loose pantheon of deities associated with different domains of life. Perun is comparatively well attested as a thunder deity associated with martial and—most importantly for this chapter's argument—princely authority specifically. The functions and origins of the other deities named alongside him, Khors, Dazhbog, Stribog, Simargl, and Mokosh, are much less securely reconstructed; solar, wind, fertility, household, and Iranian-linked interpretations have all been proposed by different scholars, and no single account commands consensus. Beyond this named pantheon, evidence points to widespread household and local religious practice: veneration of ancestors, domestic spirits, sacred groves, springs, and stones, seasonal agricultural rituals, and divinatory practices, evidence for which survives partly in later folklore, partly in archaeological remains such as ritual deposits, and partly in the disapproving descriptions later Christian clergy included in sermons condemning continuing "pagan" practice—sources that must be read cautiously, since clerical authors had an interest in emphasizing the persistence of error they were combating.

Against this genuinely plural religious landscape, the Chronicle reports that around 980, shortly after securing sole rule, Vladimir established a princely religious center at Kyiv featuring wooden images of Perun and several other deities, with Perun's image reportedly given particular prominence, including a silver head and golden mustache. Many historians have interpreted this episode, itself known only through a later and partly oral chronicle tradition, as evidence of a deliberate political project: an attempt to construct a unified princely cult, elevating a single major deity associated with martial and princely authority, that could serve the same integrating function for princely legitimacy that Christianity would later serve far more effectively. This should not be read as evidence that Rus' previously lacked religious life requiring integration; rather, it shows Vladimir experimenting, before his conversion, with religious policy as an instrument of political consolidation, foreshadowing without predicting the more thoroughgoing transformation examined later in this chapter.

It is essential that students not conclude from this discussion that Christianity arrived in Rus' for the first time with Vladimir. As Chapter 8 established, Olga had been baptized a generation earlier, and the Chronicle itself preserves evidence—including references to a "Christians' church" in Kyiv and to Christian merchants and warriors serving within Rus' princely retinues—that a Christian minority already existed in Kyivan society well before 988. Some scholars connect earlier Rus'-Byzantine treaties, including the sworn oaths accompanying the tenth-century agreements examined in Chapter 8, with evidence that Christian Rus' subjects swore differently than non-Christian ones, implying an already-recognized Christian community with its own legal status. Vladimir's later conversion therefore did not introduce Christianity to an untouched society; it elevated an existing but minority faith into the exclusive, state-sponsored religion of the realm.

Section 9.3

The Choice of Christianity

The Primary Chronicle's most celebrated account of Vladimir's conversion is the episode historians conventionally call the "testing of the faiths." According to this narrative, Vladimir, dissatisfied with his existing religious arrangements, received in succession envoys or missionaries representing four traditions: Islam, associated with the Volga Bulgars; Judaism, associated with Khazar representatives; Latin Christianity, associated with emissaries from the German lands or Rome; and Byzantine Christianity, represented by a Greek philosopher who delivered an extended account of Christian history and doctrine. The Chronicle reports that Vladimir rejected Islam on the grounds that its prohibition on alcohol was incompatible with Rus' custom, rejected Judaism by pointing to the historical loss of Jewish political sovereignty and the scattering of the Jewish people as evidence against the truth of their faith, showed some interest in Latin Christianity but ultimately favored the Greek account, and, most famously, sent his own envoys to observe the religious practices of each faith directly, receiving from those who visited Constantinople a report describing the splendor of Byzantine liturgy in terms suggesting they had witnessed something approaching heaven on earth.

This narrative deserves careful, critical handling rather than repetition as literal historical reportage. The account as it survives was compiled by Christian clergy working in Kyiv generations after the events it purports to describe, drawing on oral tradition, theological convention, and quite possibly on earlier written models used elsewhere in the Christian world to explain the conversions of other peoples. The specific reasons the Chronicle attributes to Vladimir for rejecting Islam and Judaism reflect standard medieval Christian polemical arguments against those faiths rather than necessarily reflecting Vladimir's own tenth-century reasoning, whatever that reasoning actually was. The literary structure of the episode—four faiths presented in sequence, each evaluated and found wanting except the final, correct choice—follows conventions found in other medieval Christian conversion narratives composed to explain, after the fact, why a particular Christian tradition had triumphed among a particular people.

None of this means the episode is worthless to historians. Read not as a transcript of tenth-century diplomatic exchanges but as a later theological and political narrative, the "testing of the faiths" reveals a great deal about how educated Rus' Christians of a later generation understood their society's relationship to neighboring civilizations: it positions Rus' as a discerning, sovereign actor choosing among the major religious traditions of its world—Islam to the southeast, Judaism among the Khazars, Latin Christianity to the west, Byzantine Christianity to the south—rather than as a passive recipient of any single neighbor's influence, and it explains, after the fact, why Byzantine rather than Latin Christianity had become the established faith of Rus'. The episode should be studied, in other words, primarily as evidence for later Rus' self-understanding and for how the Chronicle's compilers constructed a usable origin story for their society's Christian identity, rather than as a reliable record of what Vladimir personally investigated in the mid-980s.

Treating it as straightforward reportage misrepresents both the nature of the source and the actual, more politically and diplomatically entangled process examined in Sections 9.4 and 9.5.

Section 9.4

Byzantium and Dynastic Diplomacy

Behind the Chronicle's theological narrative lay a harder set of political and military circumstances that historians regard as central to explaining why Vladimir's conversion took the specific form it did. In 987, the Byzantine general Bardas Phokas led a serious rebellion against the young co-emperor Basil II, threatening to overturn his rule entirely. Facing a crisis substantial enough to endanger his throne, Basil turned outward for military assistance, and Rus', already linked to Byzantium through the trade and treaty relationships examined in Chapter 8, became his chosen source of reinforcement.

According to the reconstructions most widely accepted by historians, Vladimir agreed to supply Basil with a substantial force of Rus' warriors—a force later Byzantine sources describe operating within imperial territory and playing an important part in suppressing the rebellion—in exchange for a marriage alliance of extraordinary prestige: Vladimir would receive as his wife Anna, sister of the reigning emperors Basil II and Constantine VIII, a Byzantine princess born to the purple, a status conveyed by the honorific term porphyrogenita. Such a marriage, joining a princely house widely regarded elsewhere in Christian Europe as impressive but still peripheral to one of the two ruling Byzantine emperors' own sisters, represented a diplomatic prize without precedent for a Rus' ruler, and its significance for princely prestige should not be underestimated.

Byzantine sources indicate that the emperors attached a condition to this marriage that went beyond ordinary dynastic practice: Vladimir would have to become Christian himself before the marriage could take place, since Byzantine imperial ideology did not readily countenance marrying an imperial-born princess to a non-Christian ruler regardless of his military usefulness. Conversion, in other words, functioned in this reconstruction as a diplomatic precondition for the marriage that sealed the military alliance, entangling religious, political, and dynastic considerations so closely that separating them into distinct, sequential motives—first politics, then religion, or the reverse—almost certainly misrepresents the historical reality. This does not mean Vladimir's later personal Christian commitment, well documented by his subsequent religious patronage examined in Section 9.7, should be dismissed as insincere; rulers across medieval Christian Europe regularly experienced political and spiritual motivations as inseparable rather than competing.

The precise chronological sequence connecting the rebellion, the Rus' military assistance, the marriage negotiations, Vladimir's own baptism, and a further complication—Vladimir's siege and capture of the Byzantine-controlled Crimean city of Cherson, examined in the next section—remains genuinely disputed among historians working from a fragmentary and sometimes contradictory combination of Rus' chronicle material and Byzantine, Arabic, and other outside sources. One influential reconstruction holds that Vladimir supplied the promised military assistance, then found the promised marriage delayed or withheld by Basil once the immediate military emergency had passed, and consequently besieged and captured Cherson as leverage to compel Byzantine compliance, receiving Anna and completing his own baptism only after this additional show of force. A different reconstruction places Vladimir's baptism earlier in the sequence, treating the Cherson campaign as either largely independent of the marriage negotiations or as occurring after Vladimir had already become Christian. Both reconstructions agree on the essential outline—Byzantine crisis, Rus' military aid, marriage to Anna, and Vladimir's conversion, all closely linked—while disagreeing over its precise internal order. Students should retain the essential outline while recognizing that a fully certain, single sequence of events cannot presently be established from the surviving evidence.

Section 9.5

The Baptism of Vladimir

The Primary Chronicle places Vladimir's own baptism at Cherson, the Byzantine-controlled Crimean city he had captured after a lengthy siege, associating the baptism directly with the negotiations over Anna's hand described in the previous section. This account has shaped centuries of subsequent tradition, including the later designation of Cherson, under its alternative name Korsun, as the symbolic birthplace of Rus' Christianity, and it remains the conventional account presented in general histories. It should nonetheless be presented to students as the Chronicle's narrative construction rather than as an unquestionable historical fact, since, as the previous section explained, historians continue to debate whether Vladimir's baptism preceded or followed the Cherson campaign, and even whether an earlier conversion, prior to any involvement with Cherson at all, might already have occurred amid the marriage negotiations themselves.

The year 988 functions in this textbook, as in most scholarly and popular accounts, as the conventional date of Vladimir's conversion and the associated Christianization of Kyiv—a useful anchor for organizing the chronology of Rus' history—rather than as a precisely documented, independently verified date beyond dispute. Some scholars have proposed alternative years within a range extending from the late 980s into the early 990s, based on differing readings of the Byzantine, Arabic, and Rus' evidence for the timing of the Phokas rebellion's suppression and the subsequent marriage. This textbook follows the conventional dating while flagging its approximate character, consistent with the methodological caution this textbook has applied to disputed chronology since Chapter 4's treatment of Herodotus.

Equally important for historical precision is what Vladimir's baptism did not represent. It should not be described, in the language sometimes loosely applied by later writers, as a choice of "Orthodoxy over Catholicism." The conventional date of 1054 for the Great Schism is a useful symbolic and administrative milestone, but it marks a stage within a long, gradual estrangement between Rome and Constantinople rather than a single definitive break, and it in any case postdates Vladimir's conversion by more than six decades. In 988, Vladimir adopted the Christian tradition, discipline, liturgy, and ecclesiastical connections of Byzantium specifically—what this textbook calls Byzantine Christianity or the Byzantine rite—at a moment when meaningful differences between Latin and Byzantine Christian practice already existed but had not yet hardened into the fully divided, mutually exclusive confessional identities of later centuries. Applying the later, fully developed categories "Orthodox" and "Catholic" to Vladimir's choice in 988 is anachronistically neat: Rus' rulers were choosing a specific, prestigious, geographically proximate Christian tradition rather than selecting one confessional branch of an already-divided Christendom.

Following his baptism, Vladimir is reported by the Chronicle to have married Anna and returned to Kyiv, bringing with him Byzantine clergy, relics, and, according to some traditions, artisans and craftsmen who would contribute to the architectural and artistic developments examined in Section 9.8. Whatever the precise sequence of the preceding diplomatic and military events, the outcome by the early 990s was clear and historically decisive: the prince of Kyiv had become a Christian ruler married into the Byzantine imperial family, publicly and permanently committed to sponsoring Christianity as the religion of his court and, he intended, of his realm.

Section 9.6

Christianization of the Population

The Chronicle's account of what followed Vladimir's own baptism is dramatic and compressed: the destruction of the idols he had earlier erected at Kyiv, including Perun's image, which the Chronicle describes being dragged, beaten, and cast into the Dnieper as an act of public humiliation of the old cult; and a mass baptism of the population of Kyiv, summoned by princely command to the river, where, the Chronicle reports, parents brought their children and the people entered the water while priests performed the rite and read prayers, an event the Chronicle presents with the celebratory tone of a decisive, completed transformation.

This narrative should be examined for what it actually documents rather than accepted as a description of a completed social transformation. The Chronicle strongly attests the later Rus' tradition that Vladimir sponsored and enforced a public conversion program at Kyiv, using his political authority to mandate a communal act of baptism among the population directly subject to his court and to destroy the previous state-sponsored cult he himself had established less than a decade earlier; but the precise mechanics, scale, voluntariness, and sequence of the river baptism are not independently documented beyond the Chronicle's own compressed, celebratory account. The broader conversion program was clearly ruler-sponsored and coercive in its public-policy dimension, even though the degree of compulsion involved in the specific river-baptism episode cannot be independently measured, and the account should not be mistaken for evidence of a spontaneous, voluntary, or complete conversion of belief and practice among the people ordered to participate. Later chronicle tradition and folk memory, discussed further in this chapter's Historical Debate feature, preserve hints of resistance, including reports, treated with appropriate source caution, of the destruction of pagan images and shrines meeting local opposition in some places beyond Kyiv, particularly in the more distant northern regions where princely authority was weaker and where, as Section 9.9 will explain, Christianization proceeded far more slowly and unevenly.

It is essential that students not conflate this baptismal event at Kyiv, however genuinely important as a starting point, with the completed Christianization of Rus' as a whole. Kyiv itself, as the seat of princely and now ecclesiastical authority, would become thoroughly and rapidly Christianized in its formal, public life over the following decades. The wider population of Rus'—including rural agricultural communities, more distant towns, and regions such as Novgorod in the north, where later and highly disputed traditions describe forcible resistance to Christianization—experienced a substantially longer, more gradual, and more genuinely contested process, one this chapter examines further in Section 9.9 and one Chapter 11 will revisit in its broader social-historical treatment of Orthodox Christianity and popular religion across Rus' society. The mass baptism at Kyiv should therefore be understood as Christianization's decisive beginning at the political center, not its accomplished completion across the realm.

Section 9.7

Church Organization and Clergy

Turning a prince's individual conversion and a single dramatic mass baptism into a durable, functioning religious establishment required institutions, and Vladimir's reign saw the first substantial steps toward building them. Byzantine clergy, brought to Kyiv in connection with Vladimir's marriage and baptism, provided the initial core of an ecclesiastical structure that had to be constructed largely from without, since Rus' possessed no pre-existing Christian clerical hierarchy of its own scale to draw upon. These clergy required churches to serve in, financial support to sustain them, and a jurisdictional relationship to the wider Byzantine ecclesiastical world. Vladimir's conversion placed the developing Rus' church within the ecclesiastical orbit of Constantinople, but the precise organization of the hierarchy under Vladimir himself is obscure, and students should be cautious about assuming that a fully staffed metropolitanate already existed in his reign.

The precise early chronology of this ecclesiastical structure—exactly when a Kyivan metropolitan see was formally established, who its earliest metropolitans were, and how quickly subordinate bishoprics were organized in other Rus' towns—is not documented with the same certainty for every detail, and students should understand that reconstructing the earliest institutional history of the Rus' church involves piecing together scattered and sometimes later evidence rather than working from a complete contemporary administrative record. Later Rus' certainly possessed a metropolitan see connected to the Patriarch of Constantinople, but the first securely attested metropolitan, Theopemptus, belongs not to Vladimir's reign but to the late 1030s, under Yaroslav, examined in Chapter 10. What can be said with reasonable confidence about Vladimir's own reign is that Byzantine clergy were present and active at Kyiv and that a rudimentary ecclesiastical structure connected to Constantinople was taking shape, even though its full institutional maturation—including a securely documented metropolitan and subordinate bishoprics extending to other major towns—belongs to the following generation.

Vladimir's most visible act of institutional patronage was the construction of a substantial stone church in Kyiv, the Church of the Tithes, so named because the prince reportedly dedicated a tenth—a tithe—of his princely revenues to its support, an arrangement that provided the new ecclesiastical establishment with a durable, ongoing source of income independent of ad hoc royal generosity. The church's construction, completed within roughly a decade of Vladimir's baptism, employed Byzantine architectural models and, in all likelihood, at least some craftsmen and materials connected to Byzantine building traditions, representing the first major monumental expression of Rus' Christian architecture and princely religious patronage combined. This textbook returns to the fuller institutional maturation of the Kyivan church—including monasteries, expanded learning, and the church's role as a mature religious and political center—in Chapter 10's treatment of Yaroslav the Wise, whose reign saw a far more developed ecclesiastical establishment than Vladimir's initial foundation. The present chapter's task is limited to establishing that initial foundation: clergy, jurisdictional connection to Constantinople, and princely financial and architectural patronage sufficient to give Christianity institutional permanence rather than dependence on any single ruler's personal commitment.

Historical Vocabulary
Metropolitanate

Definition: An ecclesiastical province headed by a metropolitan bishop, who held authority over subordinate bishops within a defined territory and who, in the case of Kyiv, remained under the jurisdiction of the Patriarch of Constantinople.

Historical context: A Kyivan metropolitan see connected to Constantinople gave the Rus' church, once fully established, formal institutional standing within the wider Byzantine ecclesiastical world rather than leaving Christianity in Rus' dependent solely on princely favor. This structure was only beginning to take shape under Vladimir; the first securely documented metropolitan, Theopemptus, belongs to the late 1030s, under Yaroslav.

Why it matters: The metropolitanate structure explains how Christianity in Rus' gained administrative durability across changes of ruler, and it created a lasting institutional and cultural connection to Constantinople that shaped Rus' religious, artistic, and political life for centuries. Students should not assume this structure was already fully formed in Vladimir's reign; its fuller institutional history is examined further in Chapter 10.

Section 9.8

Literacy, Architecture, and Sacred Art

Christianization's cultural consequences were substantial, but it did not require the invention of a new Slavic written tradition from nothing. By the time Byzantine Christianity reached Rus', a fully developed Slavic Christian literary tradition already existed, created in the ninth century by the Byzantine missionary brothers Cyril and Methodius, who translated scripture and liturgical texts into a literary Slavic language using the Glagolitic script they developed for that purpose, in order to make Christian worship and instruction accessible to Slavic-speaking populations who did not read Greek or Latin. Their disciples and successors, working chiefly in Bulgaria after the brothers' mission in Moravia ended, expanded this literary tradition considerably, and it was within this broader Cyrillo-Methodian and South Slavic tradition, rather than from Cyril himself, that the Cyrillic script subsequently developed and spread. This Old Church Slavonic literary tradition, already substantially developed within the Bulgarian cultural sphere by the later tenth century, provided Rus' with an immediately usable body of translated scripture, liturgical books, and religious literature upon its adoption of Christianity, sparing Rus' the much slower process of developing an entirely new written religious culture from nothing.

What Christianization did accomplish in Rus' specifically was a dramatic expansion in the practical uses, institutional support, and social reach of literacy, building upon this already-existing Slavic Christian literary foundation rather than creating writing anew. The church required literate clergy to perform liturgy, read scripture, and maintain religious instruction; princely administration increasingly employed writing for legal, diplomatic, and administrative purposes connected to the newly Christian court; and, over the following generations, a genuine Rus' literary culture began to develop, producing original chronicle writing, sermons, and religious works alongside translated Byzantine and South Slavic material. This expansion of literate culture unfolded across generations rather than within Vladimir's own reign, reaching a much fuller flowering under Yaroslav, examined in Chapter 10, but its institutional foundation—clergy trained to read and write in a Slavic literary language already suited to Christian purposes—was laid in the Christianization Vladimir sponsored.

Architecture and sacred art followed a similar pattern of transmission and adaptation rather than simple, mechanical copying. The Church of the Tithes, examined in the previous section, introduced Byzantine masonry church architecture to Kyiv, a building technology and design tradition markedly different from the wooden construction that had previously predominated in Rus' towns. Byzantine models likewise shaped the earliest Rus' icons, frescoes, and church furnishings, transmitted through craftsmen, imported objects, and the artistic conventions embedded in the liturgical books and religious instruction that came to Rus' through Christianization. Yet from the outset, this transmission involved genuine local adaptation rather than passive replication: Rus' builders and craftsmen working with Byzantine models over subsequent generations developed distinctive regional variations in church design, ornament, and artistic style, a process this textbook traces further in Chapter 10's discussion of Kyiv's churches and monasteries and in later chapters' treatment of regional architectural traditions across Novgorod, Vladimir-Suzdal, and other Rus' centers. Christianization thus set in motion a long process of cultural exchange and adaptation between Byzantine models and Rus' artistic development, a process whose most mature early results belong to the following chapter rather than to Vladimir's own reign.

Section 9.9

Conversion as Process Rather Than Event

This section gathers the chapter's central interpretive argument. Everything examined in Sections 9.1 through 9.8—Vladimir's political rise, his adoption of Byzantine Christianity through a diplomatically entangled process, the mass baptism at Kyiv, and the initial construction of ecclesiastical institutions and Christian culture—describes the beginning of Christianization, not its completion. The Christianization of Rus' as a society unfolded over generations, unevenly across geography and social position, and in constant negotiation with older religious practices that did not simply vanish because a prince had ordered idols thrown into a river.

The most basic distinction students must maintain is between elite and princely conversion, which occurred rapidly and decisively at Kyiv under Vladimir's direct authority, and popular Christianization, which proceeded far more slowly, unevenly, and incompletely across the wider population of Rus'. Kyiv itself, as the political and now ecclesiastical center, experienced the most immediate and thorough transformation. Other major towns followed at varying paces depending on princely presence, missionary activity, and local receptiveness. Rural agricultural communities, the great majority of the Rus' population as Chapter 11 will examine in detail, experienced Christianization far more gradually, often over multiple generations, and in many cases through a long process of blending rather than the wholesale replacement of older practice with new doctrine.

Regional variation compounded this social unevenness. Later and highly disputed traditions, especially the so-called Joachim Chronicle known only through the eighteenth-century historian Vasily Tatishchev, describe forcible resistance to Christianization at Novgorod, including a violent campaign associated with the figures Dobrynya and Putyata. These accounts cannot securely establish the actual details of Novgorod's conversion, since the authenticity and even the authorship of the Joachim Chronicle text have long been contested by specialists. What can be said with more confidence is that Christianization outside Kyiv clearly proceeded unevenly, consistent with the generally looser degree of princely control over more distant regions that Chapters 7 and 8 have already established as a recurring feature of early Rus' political geography. Areas with substantial Finnic, Baltic, or other non-Slavic populations, examined in Chapter 5, followed their own distinct and often considerably slower trajectories of Christian influence, trajectories this textbook will continue to trace in later chapters covering Novgorod's expansion and the broader colonization of the northern forests.

Students should also resist a second, related oversimplification: the assumption that "Christianity" and "paganism" functioned after 988 as two wholly separate, internally uniform systems standing in permanent opposition to one another. The actual historical process was considerably more entangled. Older ritual practices connected to agricultural cycles, household protection, and ancestor commemoration persisted for centuries in modified forms, sometimes reinterpreted within a Christian calendar and framework, sometimes simply continuing alongside official church practice with limited clerical interference in daily village life. Archaeological and folkloric evidence, examined more fully in Chapter 11, points to a long era of coexistence and gradual blending rather than an abrupt, total replacement of one belief system by another. This should not be mistaken for a claim that Christianity's arrival made no real difference; on the contrary, over the extended period this section describes, Christianization genuinely and permanently transformed ritual practice, burial customs, calendars, political ideology, artistic production, literacy, and the language available for expressing legitimate rulership. The point is that these transformations occurred as a process extending across generations rather than as an event concluded in a single year.

This interpretive framework—Christianization as an extended, uneven, socially and regionally differentiated process rather than a completed event in 988—prepares students directly for Chapter 11's fuller social-historical examination of Orthodox Christianity and popular religion across Rus' society, and it should govern how students read any later reference, in this textbook or elsewhere, to "the conversion of Rus'" as a supposedly finished historical accomplishment.

Primary Source Window

The Primary Chronicle on the Baptism of Rus' (compiled after 988; earliest surviving manuscripts from the 14th century)

Creator: Compiled by Kyivan monastic clergy, drawing on earlier oral and written tradition · Date of composition: The core narrative was likely assembled in the early twelfth century, describing events of the late tenth century · Type: Monastic historical-theological chronicle

Context: The Primary Chronicle (sometimes called the Tale of Bygone Years) is the principal narrative source for early Rus' history. Its account of Vladimir's conversion was composed by Christian clergy working generations after the events described, within a monastic tradition invested in presenting Christianization as a providential turning point in Rus' history.

Brief paraphrase of the account (not a verbatim translated quotation):

The Chronicle describes Vladimir ordering the wooden idols he had earlier erected at Kyiv to be cut down, with Perun's image dragged behind a horse and beaten with sticks before being cast into the Dnieper, watched, the account says, by weeping onlookers whom the prince had instructed not to rescue the idol. It then describes Vladimir summoning the people of Kyiv to the river the following day, where they were baptized en masse, the prince reportedly rejoicing that his people had received a new faith.

Analysis: This account should be read as a theologically shaped narrative of triumphant transformation rather than as a neutral eyewitness record. Its dramatic staging—public humiliation of the old cult followed immediately by joyful mass conversion—serves the Chronicle's larger purpose of presenting Christianization as a decisive, providentially guided turning point. The Chronicle strongly attests the later Rus' tradition that Vladimir sponsored and enforced a public conversion program at Kyiv, but the precise mechanics, scale, voluntariness, and sequence of the river baptism are not independently documented; it should not be read as evidence that the entire population's beliefs changed as quickly or completely as the narrative's compressed, celebratory framing suggests.

Questions for students:

  1. What does this account tell historians about Vladimir's use of political authority in religious matters?
  2. Why might the Chronicle's compilers have wanted to present Christianization as swift, decisive, and joyfully embraced rather than gradual and contested?
  3. What kind of additional evidence would help historians assess how quickly Kyiv's population actually adopted Christian belief and practice, beyond participating in a compelled public ritual?
  4. How does this source connect to the chapter's broader argument in Section 9.9 that Christianization was a process rather than a single event?
Historical Debate

Where and When Was Vladimir Baptized?

The question: Did Vladimir's baptism occur at Cherson, as the Primary Chronicle states, and did it occur before or after his military assistance to Basil II and his marriage negotiations for Anna?

Interpretation A — the traditional Cherson sequence: Following the Chronicle closely, some historians hold that Vladimir supplied Basil II with military assistance against Bardas Phokas, found the promised marriage to Anna delayed once the immediate crisis passed, besieged and captured Cherson as leverage, and received both Anna and baptism together as the campaign's outcome, making the Cherson siege the direct proximate cause of the conversion's completion.
Interpretation B — an earlier or separate conversion: Other historians, working from close comparison of Byzantine, Arabic, and Rus' sources, argue that Vladimir's baptism may have occurred earlier, closer to the initial marriage negotiations and before or independent of the Cherson campaign, with the Cherson siege functioning as a related but analytically separate episode of Rus'-Byzantine friction rather than as the campaign that directly produced the baptism.

Evidence: Historians weigh the internal consistency and probable compilation history of the Primary Chronicle against external Byzantine chronicle references to the Rus' military contingent and the Phokas rebellion, along with scattered Arabic geographical and historical sources describing Rus'-Byzantine relations in this period.

Points of agreement: Virtually all historians agree that Vladimir's conversion, his marriage to Anna, and Rus' military assistance to Basil II during the Phokas rebellion were closely connected events occurring within the same several-year period in the late 980s.

Points of disagreement: Historians disagree over the precise internal sequence of these connected events and over how directly the Cherson campaign specifically should be linked to the baptism itself.

Why it matters: The debate illustrates a recurring challenge in early Rus' history: even a genuinely well-attested cluster of interconnected events can resist confident sequencing when the surviving sources were composed at different times, in different languages, for different purposes, and with different degrees of proximity to the events described.

Student task: Explain why historians can be highly confident that Vladimir's conversion, marriage, and Byzantine military assistance were connected, while remaining considerably less certain about their exact order, and explain what kind of new evidence might help resolve the disagreement.

Multiple Perspectives

Experiencing Christianization

A member of Vladimir's princely retinue at Kyiv: For a warrior or courtier attached directly to Vladimir's household, the shift to Christianity likely arrived quickly and visibly, through participation in the new court culture, exposure to Byzantine clergy and ceremony, and the practical necessity of aligning one's public religious observance with the prince's own commitments, whatever private beliefs an individual retainer continued to hold.
A Byzantine clergyman newly arrived in Kyiv: For a Greek-speaking priest sent north to serve the new Rus' church, the task involved not only performing the liturgy but building an institution from limited resources in an unfamiliar setting, training or relying upon Slavic-speaking assistants, and navigating a religious landscape in which older practices remained visible and, in many places, largely unchallenged.
A peasant household in a village distant from Kyiv: For a rural family outside the immediate reach of princely and ecclesiastical authority, the mass baptism at Kyiv described in the Chronicle may have had little immediate effect on daily religious practice, since the more gradual, uneven process of rural Christianization examined in Section 9.9 meant that older seasonal rituals, household observances, and local sacred sites often persisted largely undisturbed for a considerable time after 988.

These perspectives illustrate why "the Christianization of Rus'" cannot be treated as a single, uniform experience: proximity to princely and ecclesiastical authority produced sharply different timelines and depths of religious change.

Cause and Effect

Vladimir's Conversion

Long-Term Conditions

Existing Rus'-Byzantine trade and treaty contact examined in Chapter 6 and 8; Olga's earlier personal conversion; a small existing Christian community at Kyiv predating Vladimir.

Intermediate Developments

Vladimir's consolidation of sole princely rule after 980; his earlier experiment with a unified pagan cult centered on Perun; growing Rus' engagement with Byzantine political and religious prestige.

Short-Term Triggers

The 987 rebellion of Bardas Phokas against Basil II; Byzantine need for Rus' military assistance; negotiations over marriage to Anna requiring Vladimir's baptism as a precondition; the Cherson campaign.

Effects

Vladimir's baptism and marriage to Anna; the mass baptism at Kyiv; destruction of the princely pagan cult; founding of the Church of the Tithes; establishment of an ecclesiastical hierarchy connected to Constantinople; long-term Christianization of Rus' society.

Historical complexity: No single motive—religious conviction alone, political calculation alone, or diplomatic necessity alone—adequately explains Vladimir's conversion; the evidence suggests these motives were historically inseparable rather than competing alternatives.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Belief in Rus', Before and After 988

What Changed

  • The princely court's official, publicly sponsored religion shifted decisively from an experimental pagan cult to Byzantine Christianity.
  • A formal ecclesiastical hierarchy, connected to Constantinople, began to take institutional shape at Kyiv.
  • Monumental stone church architecture, icons, and an expanded literate culture built on Old Church Slavonic began developing at Kyiv.
  • Rus' princely legitimacy gained a new religious and political vocabulary connected to Byzantine Christian ideology.

What Continued

  • Household and local religious practices connected to agricultural cycles, ancestors, and sacred natural sites persisted for generations, especially outside Kyiv.
  • Regional religious diversity connected to Finnic, Baltic, Scandinavian, and steppe populations continued to shape local practice well beyond 988.
  • The gradual, uneven pace of religious change in rural and peripheral areas meant that formal Christianization and everyday practice remained distinct for a very long time.

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both sharp change at the political center and slow continuity at the social margins allows students to hold two true statements at once: 988 was genuinely transformative, and Rus' society did not become uniformly or immediately Christian.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Village Beyond Kyiv

For most inhabitants of Rus' in the years immediately following 988, life continued according to rhythms only partially touched by the events at Kyiv described in this chapter. Agricultural households in villages across the forest and forest-steppe zones examined in Chapter 2 continued to mark the agricultural year with seasonal observances, tend household spirits and ancestral memory in customary ways, and rely on local knowledge of sacred springs, groves, or stones passed down through generations, largely undisturbed by any priest, since the still-thin network of clergy Vladimir's reign began to establish could reach only a small fraction of Rus' settlements in this early period.

Over subsequent generations, examined further in Chapter 11, this situation changed gradually as churches, resident clergy, and monastic institutions extended outward from major towns into the countryside, but the pace of this extension varied enormously by region, by distance from princely and ecclesiastical centers, and by the density and prior character of local religious tradition. Understanding Christianization at the level of ordinary rural life therefore requires setting aside the Chronicle's dramatic Kyiv-centered narrative and imagining, instead, a far slower, more locally negotiated process unfolding across many decades and, in some respects, centuries.

Russia and the World

The Byzantine Commonwealth

Vladimir's conversion drew Rus' into what modern historians sometimes call the Byzantine commonwealth: a wider zone of Orthodox Christian states and peoples, including Bulgaria, Serbia, and other Slavic-speaking regions, connected to Constantinople through shared liturgy, ecclesiastical hierarchy, artistic convention, and political ideology even where they remained politically independent of direct Byzantine rule. Rus' entry into this commonwealth connected Kyiv to a broad Christian cultural world stretching from the Balkans to Constantinople itself, providing access to an already-mature Slavic Christian literary tradition, as Section 9.8 explained, and to artistic and architectural models that would shape Rus' culture for centuries.

This connection did not make Rus' simply an extension of Byzantine political authority. Rus' princes remained politically independent sovereigns even as they adopted Byzantine religious and cultural forms, adapting them, as later chapters will show, to distinctly Rus' political and artistic purposes. The relationship illustrates a pattern this textbook will trace again in later contexts: cultural and religious influence moving along established trade and diplomatic routes without implying political subordination, a dynamic already familiar from Chapter 6's treatment of Rus'-Byzantine-Khazar-Islamic trade contact and one that will recur in modified form throughout this textbook's later chapters on Rus' and its neighbors.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined Vladimir Sviatoslavich's adoption of Byzantine Christianity and the beginning of the long historical process by which Rus' became a Christian society. It opened with Vladimir's violent rise to sole rule following the succession crisis that followed his father Sviatoslav's death in 972, establishing that the ruler who would later sponsor Christianization first secured power through familiar, often brutal, dynastic methods rather than through any special religious destiny.

The chapter then examined the genuinely diverse religious environment of pre-Christian Rus', including Slavic, Scandinavian, Finnic, and steppe traditions, and Vladimir's own earlier experiment with a unified princely pagan cult centered on Perun, an experiment that anticipated, without determining, his later and far more consequential religious policy. It established that Christianity already existed within Rus' before 988, through Olga's earlier personal conversion and an existing Christian minority at Kyiv, cautioning against any narrative in which Christianity simply arrived in an untouched society.

The chapter examined the Primary Chronicle's "testing of the faiths" narrative as a later theological and literary construction rather than a reliable record of tenth-century diplomatic events, valuable chiefly as evidence for how later Christian Rus' understood its place among neighboring religious civilizations. It then turned to the harder political and diplomatic context behind conversion: the Byzantine crisis created by the rebellion of Bardas Phokas against Basil II, Rus' military assistance to the beleaguered emperor, and the marriage negotiations for the Byzantine princess Anna that entangled religious, political, and dynastic motives inseparably.

Vladimir's baptism, conventionally dated to 988 and traditionally associated with the Byzantine-controlled city of Cherson, was examined as a historically significant but imprecisely documented event, with historians disagreeing over its exact sequence relative to the Cherson campaign and the marriage negotiations. The chapter cautioned against describing this choice as one between "Orthodoxy and Catholicism," since 1054, the conventional date of the Great Schism, marks a milestone within a long, gradual estrangement between Latin and Byzantine Christianity rather than a single definitive break, and in any case occurred more than six decades after Vladimir's conversion.

The chapter distinguished sharply between Vladimir's own baptism and the ruler-sponsored mass baptism and destruction of the earlier pagan cult at Kyiv, presenting this dramatic episode as the decisive beginning of Christianization at the political center rather than its completion across Rus' society. It examined the initial institutional foundations of the Rus' church, including Byzantine clergy, a developing ecclesiastical structure connected to Constantinople, and Vladimir's patronage of the Church of the Tithes.

Turning to culture, the chapter explained that Christianization drew upon an already-existing Slavic Christian literary tradition created by Cyril, Methodius, and their successors, while dramatically expanding written culture within Rus', including the institutional reach and social uses of literate culture, church architecture, and sacred art over subsequent generations.

The chapter's central interpretive argument, developed most fully in Section 9.9, held that the Christianization of Rus' must be understood as an extended, socially and regionally uneven process rather than a single event concluded in 988, with elite and princely conversion proceeding far more rapidly than popular Christianization, and with older religious practices persisting, blending, and gradually transforming over many generations rather than disappearing at once.

These lessons—critical reading of chronicle narrative, careful attention to the entanglement of religious and political motives, and insistence on process rather than event—prepare students directly for Chapter 10's examination of Yaroslav the Wise, whose reign brought Rus' Christian institutions, law, and culture to a considerably more mature stage of development, and for Chapter 11's fuller social-historical treatment of Orthodox Christianity and popular religion across Rus' society.

Metropolitanate
An ecclesiastical province headed by a metropolitan bishop, under the Patriarch of Constantinople's jurisdiction; only beginning to take shape under Vladimir, with the first securely attested Kyivan metropolitan dated to the late 1030s under Yaroslav.
Ecclesiastical hierarchy
The organized structure of clergy, bishops, and metropolitans governing church administration, examined in Section 9.7.
Perun
An East Slavic deity associated with thunder and, importantly, with princely and warrior authority; central to Vladimir's pre-Christian cult and its later destruction.
Idol
A term, often used pejoratively in Christian sources, for images of pre-Christian deities; the Chronicle describes the destruction of such images at Kyiv in the late 980s.
Tithe
A tenth of revenue dedicated to religious support; Vladimir's dedication of a tithe funded the Church of the Tithes.
Church of the Tithes (Desyatynna Church)
A stone church built in Kyiv under Vladimir's patronage, an early monument of institutionalized Rus' Christianity.
Great Schism (1054)
The conventional symbolic and administrative milestone in the long, gradual estrangement between the Latin church of Rome and the Byzantine church of Constantinople; occurring more than sixty years after Vladimir's conversion, and not itself the single moment communion was definitively and permanently severed.
Old Church Slavonic
The literary Slavic liturgical language developed by Cyril, Methodius, and their successors, used in Rus' Christian worship and literature from the outset of Christianization.
Cyrillic script
The alphabet, developed within the Cyrillo-Methodian tradition, used for Old Church Slavonic and subsequent Rus' literary culture.
Cyrillo-Methodian tradition
The ninth-century Byzantine missionary and literary project, associated with Cyril and Methodius, that created a Slavic Christian literary culture predating Rus' Christianization.
Icon
A devotional image, typically of Christ, the Virgin, or saints, central to Byzantine and Rus' Christian worship and artistic production.
Fresco
A wall painting executed on wet plaster, a technique introduced to Rus' church decoration through Byzantine influence.
Political legitimacy
The socially and institutionally recognized right to rule; Christianization gave Rus' princes a new religious vocabulary for claiming legitimate authority.
Dynastic marriage
Marriage arranged for political and diplomatic purposes among ruling families; Vladimir's marriage to Anna exemplifies this practice at exceptional prestige.
Syncretism
The blending of different religious traditions and practices; relevant to understanding the long coexistence of Christian and older practices in Rus'.
Popular religion
The everyday religious beliefs and practices of ordinary people, often distinct from official doctrine, examined further in Chapter 11.
Cherson (Chersonesus, Korsun)
A Byzantine-controlled Crimean city besieged by Vladimir and traditionally, though not certainly, associated with his baptism.
Porphyrogenita
An honorific Byzantine title meaning "born to the purple," designating a child born to a reigning emperor; applied to Princess Anna, Vladimir's wife.

Reconstructing the Sequence of 987–989

This project asks you to investigate the disputed sequence of events surrounding Vladimir's conversion, marriage, and the Cherson campaign, comparing how different types of sources support different reconstructions.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has carried students from Vladimir's violent seizure of sole princely power through his diplomatically entangled adoption of Byzantine Christianity to the extended, uneven social process by which Rus' gradually became a Christian society. Vladimir was neither a saint discovering truth nor a cynical schemer using religion as a mere instrument; the evidence suggests a ruler whose political, dynastic, and religious motives were genuinely inseparable, operating within a diplomatic crisis not of his own making and a religious landscape considerably more plural than later tradition often acknowledges.

The chapter's central methodological lesson—that a ruler's conversion and a society's Christianization are related but fundamentally different historical processes, unfolding on very different timelines—will recur throughout this textbook, particularly in Chapter 11's fuller treatment of popular religion and in later chapters examining how state-sponsored religious change interacted with persistent local practice across Rus', Muscovy, the Russian Empire, and beyond.

As the textbook proceeds to Chapter 10's examination of Yaroslav the Wise, law, dynasty, and statecraft, students should carry forward this chapter's critical approach to chronicle narrative, its insistence on distinguishing event from process, and its recognition that Christianization gave Rus' princes powerful new tools of legitimacy and cultural connection to the Byzantine world without instantly or completely transforming the beliefs of the society over which they ruled.
