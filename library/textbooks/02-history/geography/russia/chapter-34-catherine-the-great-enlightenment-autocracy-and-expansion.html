Part VI — Peter the Great and the Eighteenth-Century Empire
34

Catherine the Great: Enlightenment, Autocracy, and Expansion

A History of Russia: From the Eurasian World to the Russian Federation

Chapter Opening

On June 28, 1762 (Old Style; July 9, New Style), a thirty-three-year-old German-born grand duchess appeared before Guards regiments in St. Petersburg who had already declared for her. By nightfall she was being proclaimed sovereign of the Russian Empire, while her husband's position as reigning emperor was collapsing; he abdicated and was taken into custody shortly thereafter. She had no hereditary Romanov blood claim of her own. Her son possessed a stronger dynastic right to the throne than she did. She would nonetheless rule Russia for the next thirty-four years, one of the longest reigns in the history of the Russian Empire and the longest since Peter I, and she would become one of the most consequential rulers in the empire's history.

Chapter 33 traced the thirty-seven years after Peter I's death in 1725, when the throne passed rapidly among Catherine I, Peter II, Anna Ivanovna, the infant Ivan VI, Elizabeth Petrovna, and finally Peter III, and when Guards regiments, court factions, and noble interests repeatedly determined who would rule and how long they would remain in power. That chapter ended with Peter III's brief and politically vulnerable reign, his unpopular foreign policy, his abrupt withdrawal of Russia from a war it had been winning, and the Manifesto on the Freedom of the Nobility, which released the service nobility from Peter I's compulsory state service without asking anything comparable of the peasants who supported them. Peter III's isolation from the political coalitions that had sustained his predecessors created exactly the kind of vulnerability this textbook's account of palace politics had already identified as characteristic of the post-Petrine decades.

This chapter asks how his wife, born Sophie of Anhalt-Zerbst and received into Orthodoxy as Grand Duchess Catherine Alekseevna, converted the seizure of power into one of the eighteenth century's most durable and, in some respects, most successful reigns. The question is not simply how Catherine took the throne — Chapter 33 has already prepared students to recognize the mechanics of a Guards-backed palace coup — but how a ruler whose claim to rule rested on force and circumstance rather than hereditary right built a government that a broad cross-section of the Russian nobility, the imperial bureaucracy, and eventually much of educated Europe came to regard as legitimate, capable, and even enlightened.

Catherine is often remembered through two competing, oversimplified images. In one, she appears as a genuine philosophe on the throne, a correspondent of Voltaire and Diderot who tried to bring the rule of law, religious toleration, and rational administration to a backward empire. In the other, she appears as a cynical autocrat who used fashionable Enlightenment language to flatter European opinion while ruling through force, expanding serfdom, and rewarding the nobility whose support had made her empress. This chapter argues that neither image, taken alone, withstands close examination of the evidence. Catherine engaged seriously and consistently with Enlightenment ideas about law, education, toleration, and rational government, and she pursued genuine institutional reforms across nearly every branch of the state. At the same time, she governed within, and deliberately reinforced, an autocratic political order that depended on the cooperation of a privileged noble elite and that extended, rather than loosened, the bondage of the enserfed peasantry who formed the great majority of the empire's population. The tension between reforming language and autocratic practice that runs through this chapter was not simply hypocrisy grafted onto an otherwise coherent program; it was structural, arising from the basic political arithmetic of governing a vast, thinly administered, socially stratified empire through the willing cooperation of a landowning elite.

The chapter also follows Catherine's reign outward from the court and the chancery into the wider Eurasian world. Her reign coincided with two major wars against the Ottoman Empire, the annexation of the Crimean Khanate, the final partitions and extinction of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth as an independent state, and a substantial reorientation of Russia's position in the Black Sea and Central European political systems. These developments cannot be told simply as chapters in Russian territorial growth. The Ottoman Empire, the Crimean Khanate, and the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth were themselves complex political societies with their own institutions, interests, and internal debates, and their peoples experienced Russian expansion in ways that a purely Russian-centered narrative would miss. This chapter accordingly gives sustained attention to Crimean Tatar society, to Polish and Lithuanian political life on the eve of partition, and to the diplomatic calculations of Russia's rivals, rather than treating these societies only as territory awaiting incorporation.

Finally, this chapter establishes a boundary with the chapter that follows it. Chapter 35 examines eighteenth-century Russian society from below: the expansion of serfdom, the texture of noble provincial life, the peasant household economy, women and family, religious and ethnic diversity within the empire, and the Volga-Ural and Yaik Cossack world that erupted into the massive rebellion led by Emelian Pugachev between 1773 and 1775. This chapter, by contrast, is primarily a chapter about government from above: about how Catherine built, justified, and used state power. Readers should therefore expect Pugachev's rebellion to appear here chiefly as a pressure that helped shape Catherine's provincial administrative reforms, not as a fully narrated event; the rebellion itself, and the frontier society and peasant grievances that produced it, belong to Chapter 35.

By the chapter's end, students should be able to explain how Catherine transformed an insecure, coup-born accession into a durable autocracy; assess the substance and limits of her Enlightenment-influenced reforms; explain how her policies toward the nobility reinforced rather than checked autocratic power; and evaluate the causes, course, and consequences of Russian expansion into the Black Sea region and Poland-Lithuania during her reign.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the political circumstances, participants, and legitimacy problems surrounding the coup of 1762.
  2. Analyze Catherine's engagement with European Enlightenment thought and distinguish "enlightened government" from liberal or democratic government.
  3. Evaluate the Nakaz and the Legislative Commission of 1767–1768 as evidence for both the ambitions and the limits of Catherine's reform program.
  4. Describe Catherine's provincial administrative reforms and explain how they addressed problems of governance across a vast empire.
  5. Assess Catherine's educational and cultural policies and identify who gained access to their benefits.
  6. Explain the Charter to the Nobility of 1785 and analyze why strengthening noble privilege could also strengthen autocratic monarchy.
  7. Trace the causes, course, and consequences of the Russo-Ottoman wars of 1768–1774 and 1787–1792.
  8. Explain the annexation of Crimea and evaluate its consequences for the Crimean Khanate and Crimean Tatar society.
  9. Analyze the partitions of Poland-Lithuania as an interaction between Commonwealth politics and the interests of neighboring powers, rather than as a purely Russian initiative.
  10. Compare Peter I and Catherine II as reforming autocrats with different methods and priorities.
  11. Critically evaluate the historiographical category of "enlightened absolutism" as applied to Catherine's reign.
  12. Connect the contradictions of Catherine's reign to the social history of serfdom, estate society, and frontier rebellion examined in Chapter 35.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did a ruler who gained power through a palace coup convert possession of the throne into durable political legitimacy?
  2. What could "enlightened government" mean inside an eighteenth-century autocracy that had no intention of limiting monarchical power?
  3. Why did the Legislative Commission of 1767–1768 fail to produce a new law code, and does that failure mean the undertaking was meaningless?
  4. How did Catherine's provincial reforms attempt to close the gap between formal central authority and effective local government?
  5. How could granting nobles greater legal privilege, through the Charter of 1785, simultaneously strengthen the autocratic state?
  6. What did Russia gain, and what did neighboring peoples lose, through the wars against the Ottoman Empire and the annexation of Crimea?
  7. How did intervention in the internal politics of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth evolve into the destruction of that state?
  8. To what extent can Catherine's reign be understood as both enlightened reform and autocratic consolidation at the same time, rather than as one or the other?

Historical Context

Chapter 33 traced the "Age of Palace Revolutions" that followed Peter I's death in 1725: a period in which the absence of a stable, broadly accepted succession mechanism and fallback procedure — rather than the absence of any law at all, since Peter I's 1722 statute had formally given the reigning sovereign the right to designate a successor — combined with the political weight of the Guards regiments Peter himself had created, allowed a rapid succession of rulers — Catherine I, Peter II, Anna Ivanovna, the infant Ivan VI, Elizabeth Petrovna, and Peter III — to come to power through coalition politics, court intrigue, and, on more than one occasion, outright military coup. That chapter established several themes essential to understanding Catherine's reign: that the throne's security depended less on formal hereditary right than on the ruler's ability to build and hold a coalition of court favorites, Guards officers, and noble interests; that noble service obligations, imposed by Peter I as the price of the state's continued claim on landholding families, had become an increasing source of noble grievance across the intervening decades; and that Peter III, in his brief reign of 1761–1762, alienated important military and court constituencies through his abrupt peace and alliance with Prussia, his preparations for a campaign against Denmark-Norway over his ancestral Holstein-Gottorp claims to Schleswig, and controversial military and ecclesiastical policies, even as his Manifesto on the Freedom of the Nobility of February 1762 granted the service nobility a substantial concession by releasing them from compulsory state service.

Catherine entered this political world as an outsider by birth. Born Princess Sophie Friederike Auguste of Anhalt-Zerbst in 1729, she arrived in Russia in 1744 as a fourteen-year-old bride selected by the Empress Elizabeth for her nephew and heir, the future Peter III. She converted to Orthodoxy, adopted the name Ekaterina Alekseevna, and spent nearly two decades at the Russian court cultivating relationships, mastering the Russian language, and reading widely in French Enlightenment literature while her marriage to Peter deteriorated. By the time of Elizabeth's death in December 1761, Catherine had built a substantial network of supporters among Guards officers, court figures, and foreign diplomats who regarded her as a far more capable and popular figure than her husband.

Beyond the immediate court context, Catherine's reign unfolded within a broader European intellectual and political moment. The mid- and late eighteenth century saw a number of continental rulers — Frederick II of Prussia, Joseph II of Austria, and, to varying degrees, others — associate their governments with the vocabulary of the Enlightenment: reason, utility, toleration, codified law, and the image of the monarch as a rational reformer governing for the wellbeing of the state rather than merely by inherited right or religious sanction. Historians commonly group these rulers under the label "enlightened absolutism" or "enlightened despotism," a category this chapter's Historical Debate feature examines critically. Catherine corresponded with major Enlightenment figures, subscribed to their journals, and drew self-consciously on their vocabulary in her own writing, while simultaneously presiding over an empire whose social structure — an autocratic monarchy resting on a landholding nobility and an enserfed peasantry — differed sharply from the constitutional monarchies and republics that many Enlightenment writers imagined as ideal.

Russia's international position at Catherine's accession also shaped the priorities of her reign. The Seven Years' War, examined in Chapter 33, had demonstrated Russian military capability against Prussia even as Peter III's peace threw away its fruits. To the south, the Ottoman Empire still controlled the northern Black Sea coast and exercised suzerainty over the Crimean Khanate, blocking Russian access to warm-water ports that earlier rulers, from Peter I onward, had sought. To the west, the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, though formally independent, had become progressively more vulnerable to the intervention of its more centralized and militarily stronger neighbors — Russia, Prussia, and Austria — as internal political structures such as the liberum veto made effective Commonwealth governance increasingly difficult. These conditions set the stage for the wars, annexations, and partitions examined in the second half of this chapter.

Section 34.1

The Coup of 1762

By the winter of 1761–1762, Peter III's brief reign had alienated several constituencies whose support any eighteenth-century Russian ruler required. His decision to withdraw Russia from the Seven Years' War and return conquered Prussian territory to Frederick II, discussed in Chapter 33, struck many officers as a betrayal of hard-won military gains. His open admiration for Prussian military style, his preparations to commit Russian forces to a campaign against Denmark-Norway in pursuit of his ancestral Holstein-Gottorp claims to Schleswig, and controversial military and ecclesiastical policies further eroded support among the Guards regiments and the court; the coup itself forestalled the Danish campaign before Russian forces engaged. Catherine, by contrast, had spent nearly two decades cultivating relationships across the court, learning Russian, and presenting herself as a devoted adherent of Orthodoxy and Russian custom — a calculated performance of belonging that stood in deliberate contrast to her husband's foreign sympathies.

The coup itself, organized chiefly through the Guards regiments with the active participation of Grigory Orlov and his brothers, unfolded rapidly in late June 1762. Catherine, informed that an associate's arrest threatened to expose the conspiracy, traveled to St. Petersburg, secured the allegiance of the Izmailovsky and Semenovsky Guards regiments, and was proclaimed autocrat. Peter III, isolated at his residence at Oranienbaum, offered little resistance and signed a formal abdication within days. He was moved into custody at Ropsha, where he died about a week later. The official announcement attributed his death to a hemorrhoidal colic; the precise circumstances remain disputed, and historians have not established with certainty whether his death resulted from illness, from violence at the hands of his guards independent of direct instruction, or from an order that cannot now be firmly documented. Students should recognize this as an area of genuine historical uncertainty rather than settled fact in either direction.

The coup created an unusual and, in strict dynastic terms, precarious situation. Catherine held no blood claim to the Romanov succession; her son Paul, born in 1754, possessed a considerably stronger hereditary right to the throne than his mother did. That Catherine ruled in her own name as sovereign empress, rather than merely as regent for her son, reflected both the practical realities of Guards politics — the coup had made her, not the eight-year-old Paul, the person to whom the Guards regiments had sworn loyalty — and Catherine's own determination to rule outright. This arrangement left Catherine with a lasting political problem: how could a ruler who had acquired power through force, against a still-living and later deceased predecessor and in preference to a legitimate hereditary heir, convert bare possession of the throne into durable legitimacy?

The answer Catherine constructed across the following decades combined several elements examined throughout this chapter: an active cultivation of European intellectual opinion through correspondence and patronage; a program of legal and institutional reform presented in the vocabulary of reason and the public good; sustained cultivation of noble support through concrete privileges, culminating in the Charter of 1785; and substantial military and territorial success against the Ottoman Empire and in Poland, which allowed Catherine to present her reign as bringing glory and security to the Russian state. Legitimacy, in other words, was built rather than inherited, and nearly every major policy examined in this chapter can be understood, in part, as contributing to that larger project.

Section 34.2

Catherine and the Enlightenment

Catherine's engagement with the European Enlightenment was neither superficial nor merely performative, though it also never extended to accepting the constitutional or republican conclusions that some Enlightenment writers drew from their premises. From her years as grand duchess onward, she read extensively in French philosophical and political literature, including the works of Montesquieu, whose Spirit of the Laws she would draw upon extensively in the Nakaz examined in Section 34.3, and Cesare Beccaria's treatise on crime and punishment, which argued against torture and disproportionate cruelty in criminal justice. As empress, she corresponded directly with Voltaire and, later, with Denis Diderot, whom she invited to visit St. Petersburg and whose personal library she purchased, allowing him to retain use of it during his lifetime — an arrangement that generated favorable publicity across educated Europe while providing Catherine with a tangible connection to the era's most celebrated critical intellect.

Understanding what Catherine meant by enlightened government requires distinguishing her project sharply from later liberal or democratic political thought. Enlightenment political philosophy in the eighteenth century was not a single, unified program; while some writers, including Montesquieu, expressed admiration for institutions that divided or checked political power, and others moved toward republican or proto-democratic conclusions, a great deal of Enlightenment political writing focused instead on questions of rational law, administrative efficiency, religious toleration, the reduction of arbitrary cruelty, and the cultivation of a well-informed, productive population — goals that a sufficiently determined monarch could pursue without relinquishing ultimate authority. Catherine consistently articulated her project in these latter terms: she sought a Russia governed by clear, consistently applied law rather than by the arbitrary whim of officials; she favored religious toleration for the empire's Muslim, Protestant, Catholic, and other non-Orthodox subjects, within limits; she supported education and the circulation of useful knowledge; and she took a personal interest in the reform of criminal punishment. None of this implied any intention to share sovereign authority with a legislature, to establish constitutional limits on her own power, or to extend civic equality to the enserfed peasantry who made up the large majority of her subjects.

Catherine also used her Enlightenment credentials instrumentally, in ways that served the legitimacy project examined in Section 34.1. Publicized correspondence with Voltaire and Diderot, patronage of the arts and sciences, and participation in the vocabulary of reasoned, humane government helped present Catherine to European elite opinion as a modern, capable ruler rather than as the beneficiary of a violent palace coup. This instrumental dimension does not mean her intellectual engagement was insincere; rather, it illustrates how personal conviction and political utility could reinforce one another. Catherine appears to have genuinely believed that rational law and administrative reform would strengthen the Russian state, and she also understood, accurately, that such reforms and their public presentation would strengthen her own political position.

Section 34.3

The Legislative Commission

The clearest test of Catherine's enlightened political language came early in her reign, with the drafting of the Nakaz, or Instruction, and the convening of the Legislative Commission of 1767–1768. Russia's existing law code, the Sobornoe Ulozhenie of 1649, examined in Chapter 27, was by the 1760s more than a century old and had been supplemented by an accumulating, often contradictory mass of subsequent decrees. Catherine determined to replace this patchwork with a single, rationally organized code and, toward that end, drafted the Nakaz between 1765 and 1767 as a set of governing principles to guide the commission's work, drawing heavily, and in places very closely, on Montesquieu's Spirit of the Laws and on Beccaria's writing on criminal justice.

The Legislative Commission itself was a genuinely unusual consultative representative body for its time and place, though it was not a parliament with independent legislative authority: a gathering of several hundred elected deputies drawn from the nobility, townspeople, state peasants, Cossacks, and various non-Russian and non-Orthodox populations of the empire, each bringing formal statements, called nakazy in the plural, cataloguing local grievances and requests. Privately owned serfs, however, had no deputies of their own — an important limitation given their enormous place in the empire's population and economy, and a silence that this chapter's Primary Source Window examines further in connection with the Nakaz itself. The Commission's proceedings opened in Moscow in 1767 and continued, with the body later relocating to St. Petersburg, into 1768, when the outbreak of war with the Ottoman Empire, examined in Section 34.7, provided the occasion for its indefinite adjournment. No new comprehensive law code resulted from its work.

Historians have long debated how to weigh this outcome. One reading treats the Commission as a failed or even insincere undertaking, arguing that Catherine used the appearance of consultation to enhance her European reputation without ever intending to accept binding constraints on her authority, and that the war's convenient timing masked a decision to abandon a process that had begun to expose how little agreement existed among Russia's disparate estates and peoples. A different reading emphasizes what the Commission did accomplish: it produced an extraordinarily rich body of evidence about local conditions and grievances across the empire, evidence that later informed Catherine's provincial reforms examined in Section 34.4; it demonstrated, to Catherine and to later historians alike, the genuine difficulty of reconciling the interests of nobles, townspeople, state peasants, and non-Russian populations within a single legal framework; and it associated Catherine's government, in the eyes of European observers, with a serious and unusually participatory attempt at rational law reform. Both readings contain real evidence, and a sound historical judgment should draw on each rather than adopting either in isolated form: the Commission was a genuine, ambitious undertaking whose failure to produce a finished code reflected both the scale of the task and the political convenience its abandonment offered once war began.

Section 34.4

Provincial Reform

If the Legislative Commission tested Catherine's ambitions for codified law, her provincial administrative reforms tested her ability to translate governing principles into effective institutions reaching across an enormous empire. Chapter 32 examined how Peter I's Senate, colleges, and provincial governorates had attempted to impose a more systematic administrative structure on Muscovite government, and how the gap between formal central authority and actual local control remained wide throughout the early and mid-eighteenth century. Catherine confronted the same underlying problem: a thinly staffed bureaucracy, provinces of enormously uneven size and population, and local government that depended heavily on the cooperation, or the indifference, of resident noble landowners.

Pugachev's Rebellion, examined fully in Chapter 35, erupted in 1773 along the Volga-Ural frontier and exposed serious weaknesses in provincial administration, security, and the state's capacity to respond quickly to unrest far from the capital. The rebellion's scale and initial success demonstrated, in the starkest possible terms, the practical costs of an undergoverned periphery. This chapter's task is not to narrate the rebellion itself, which belongs to Chapter 35's examination of the frontier and society from below, but to note that the rebellion's exposure of administrative weakness helped make thoroughgoing provincial reorganization politically urgent in a way that abstract principle alone might not have achieved.

The resulting Statute for the Administration of the Provinces, issued in 1775, restructured Russia's territorial administration into a larger number of more uniformly sized provinces, or gubernii, each subdivided into districts, with clearer hierarchies of governors, courts, police officials, and fiscal administrators than had existed before. The reform also established a more elaborate structure of local courts organized partly along estate lines, with separate tribunals for nobles, townspeople, and state peasants, and it created institutions of local noble self-administration, including elected noble marshals, that gave the provincial nobility a formal, continuing role in local governance. The reform did not create a modern professional civil service in any developed sense; it continued to rely heavily on unpaid or lightly compensated noble service and on the cooperation of local landowners. It did, however, represent a substantial and durable reorganization of Russian provincial government that remained largely in place, with modification, into the nineteenth century, and it illustrates a recurring theme of this textbook: successive rulers, from Peter I through Catherine II and beyond, repeatedly confronted the same structural problem of governing a vast territory with limited administrative capacity, and each generation's solution built upon, without fully resolving, the problem inherited from its predecessors.

Section 34.5

Education and Culture

Catherine's reform agenda extended beyond law and administration into education and cultural patronage, both understood as instruments of enlightened government and as means of cultivating a more capable, productive population and a more sophisticated court culture. Her first major educational initiative, the Smolny Institute, founded in 1764, was the first state educational institution for women in Russia, reflecting Catherine's particular interest, shared with some Enlightenment writers, in the moral and intellectual formation of women as mothers and household managers, though its student body and reach remained narrowly limited to the nobility.

A more ambitious and broader effort followed in the 1780s. Catherine appointed a Commission on National Schools in 1782 to design a state system of general education, drawing on Austrian educational models and advice from foreign experts, and the resulting Statute of National Schools, issued in 1786, established a graded system of "main" public schools in provincial capitals and smaller district schools in county towns, with a standardized curriculum and teacher training. This statute represented Russia's first attempt at a coordinated, state-directed system of general public education reaching beyond the nobility to include, in principle, children of the clergy, merchantry, and other free urban and rural estates — though, significantly, these public schools did not admit the children of privately owned serfs, whose education remained entirely at their landlords' discretion.

Catherine also patronized the arts, sciences, and literature on a substantial scale, expanding the Imperial Academy of Sciences, founding the Russian Academy for the study of the Russian language in 1783, sponsoring theater and architecture, and assembling one of the era's great art collections, which formed the nucleus of the later Hermitage Museum. She herself wrote extensively, including comedies, historical works, and educational texts for her grandchildren, and she supported a lively, if closely watched, periodical press and literary culture among the educated elite, including for a time the satirical journalism of Nikolai Novikov, though her relationship with independent-minded writers grew more restrictive later in her reign, particularly after the shock of the French Revolution in 1789 sharpened her concern about the political implications of unchecked publication.

The reach of these educational and cultural initiatives, however, remained narrow even after the 1786 statute. Implementation was gradual and uneven, actual enrollment fell well short of the system's design, and, as noted above, privately owned serfs remained excluded from the public schools by design rather than by mere oversight; the enserfed peasantry, who composed the great majority of Russia's subjects, gained essentially no access to the schools, academies, or court culture that Catherine's government sponsored. The educational and cultural achievements of Catherine's reign should therefore be understood as a genuine, and in the case of the 1786 statute genuinely national in ambition, expansion of Russian institutional and intellectual life, closely tied to the Enlightenment vocabulary examined in Section 34.2, rather than as evidence of broad social transformation reaching the whole population. A fuller portrait of noble provincial culture, peasant household life, and the social diversity of Catherine's empire is provided in Chapter 35.

Section 34.6

Nobility and the Charter of 1785

Chapter 33 traced how Peter III's Manifesto on the Freedom of the Nobility of 1762 released the service nobility from Peter I's requirement of compulsory lifelong state service, granting nobles the choice to serve or to retire to their estates. Catherine, who had displaced Peter III only months after that manifesto's issuance, needed to demonstrate to the nobility whose support had installed and now sustained her that their new freedoms would be preserved and, ideally, expanded rather than reversed. The Charter to the Nobility, issued in 1785 alongside a companion Charter to the Towns, accomplished this by codifying, in a single comprehensive legal document, the corporate rights and privileges of the Russian nobility as an estate.

The Charter confirmed nobles' exemption from compulsory state service and from personal taxation, secured their rights to own populated estates and to dispose of their property freely, protected them from corporal punishment and from loss of noble status, title, or property except through due legal process and judgment by their peers, and formally established provincial and district noble assemblies with the right to elect noble marshals and certain local officials, reinforcing the local self-administration structures created by the 1775 provincial reform examined in Section 34.4. Together, these provisions gave the Russian nobility a more secure and legally defined corporate status than it had ever previously possessed.

The apparent paradox — that an autocratic empress strengthened the legal privileges of a social estate rather than the power of the central state alone — dissolves once the underlying political logic is made explicit. Catherine's government depended on the nobility to staff the officer corps, to administer the provinces under the 1775 reforms, to manage the agricultural economy that generated state revenue, and to maintain order over the enserfed peasant population in the countryside. A nobility whose privileges rested on secure, codified law, rather than on the shifting favor of whichever ruler currently occupied the throne, had every incentive to support the continuation of that same autocratic order, since only the autocracy could guarantee those privileges against future reversal. Strengthening noble corporate rights therefore bound the empire's most powerful social group more securely to the political system Catherine headed, reinforcing rather than checking her authority.

This same settlement, however, sharpened a contradiction that this chapter has traced since its opening pages. The Charter to the Nobility secured unprecedented legal protection and corporate self-government for perhaps one percent of the empire's population, at the very moment when, as Chapter 35 examines in detail, the legal and practical condition of the enserfed peasantry who worked noble estates was becoming more restrictive, with expanding landlord authority over peasant labor, mobility, and even sale. Greater corporate security for the nobility coincided with intensified dependence and diminished protection for the peasants beneath them. This chapter identifies that contradiction; Chapter 35, beginning with its examination of the expansion of serfdom and estate society, explains its social consequences in full.

Section 34.7

Wars Against the Ottoman Empire

Catherine's reign coincided with two major wars against the Ottoman Empire, reflecting a long-standing Russian ambition, traceable at least to Peter I's Azov campaigns examined in Chapter 31, to secure access to the Black Sea and to check Ottoman and Crimean Tatar power along Russia's southern frontier. The first war, fought from 1768 to 1774, began after Ottoman authorities, alarmed by Russian intervention in Polish affairs examined in Section 34.9, declared war following a border incident. The conflict saw substantial Russian military and naval success, including a notable naval victory at Chesme in 1770, achieved by a Russian fleet that had sailed from the Baltic around Western Europe into the Mediterranean, and sustained land campaigns that carried Russian forces into the Danubian principalities and across the northern Black Sea steppe.

The war concluded with the Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca in 1774, a settlement of major long-term significance. The treaty granted Russia territory along the Black Sea coast, including the outlet of the Dnieper River, and, crucially, secured Russian merchant shipping rights in Ottoman waters and a vaguely worded provision concerning Russian protection of Orthodox Christian subjects within the Ottoman Empire, a clause that later Russian governments would invoke, with contested interpretations, in subsequent diplomatic crises examined in later chapters. Most immediately significant for this chapter, the treaty declared the Crimean Khanate independent of Ottoman suzerainty, formally ending centuries of Ottoman overlordship over the Crimean Tatars while in practice opening the Khanate to intensified Russian political influence, a development examined fully in Section 34.8.

A second Russo-Turkish war, fought from 1787 to 1792, followed the definitive Russian annexation of Crimea in 1783 and reflected continued rivalry over the Black Sea littoral and Ottoman hopes of reversing recent losses. Russian forces, under commanders including Alexander Suvorov, achieved further significant victories, and the war concluded with the Treaty of Jassy in 1792, which confirmed Russian control of Crimea and extended Russian territory to the Dniester River, consolidating the gains of the preceding quarter-century. Throughout both conflicts, the Ottoman Empire should be understood not merely as an obstacle to Russian ambition but as a major imperial power in its own right, pursuing its own strategic interests, allied at various points with other European powers, and experiencing these wars as a genuine and costly challenge to its own territorial integrity and prestige in southeastern Europe.

Section 34.8

Crimea and Black Sea Expansion

The Crimean Khanate, a Turkic Muslim polity descended in part from the successor states of the Golden Horde examined in Chapter 17, had ruled the Crimean peninsula and adjacent steppe territory under Ottoman suzerainty since the late fifteenth century, maintaining a distinctive political and religious life connected to the wider Ottoman and Islamic world. The Küçük Kaynarca settlement of 1774, examined in Section 34.7, declared the Khanate formally independent of Ottoman authority, but this "independence" proved to be a transitional stage rather than a stable outcome. Freed from Ottoman overlordship, the Khanate became increasingly exposed to Russian political interference, including support for rival claimants to the khanate's throne and the stationing of Russian forces within its territory. In 1783, Catherine's government formally annexed the Khanate outright, incorporating Crimea directly into the Russian Empire.

This chapter's central caution regarding imperial expansion applies with particular force here: describing these events as "Russia gained Crimea" captures only the perspective of the expanding state and omits the experience of the Crimean Tatar population who had governed and inhabited the peninsula for centuries. Annexation brought substantial demographic and social disruption. Significant numbers of Crimean Tatars emigrated to Ottoman territory in the years following annexation, whether from religious and political conviction, fear of the new government, or economic pressure, while Russian authorities encouraged settlement of the newly acquired territory by Russian and Ukrainian peasants, by foreign colonists recruited from German-speaking Europe and elsewhere, and by demobilized soldiers, fundamentally altering the region's demographic composition over subsequent decades. Russian authorities promoted this settlement across the wider northern Black Sea steppe, an area often designated Novorossiya, or "New Russia," in official and popular usage; Crimea itself, administratively, was organized as a separate Taurida Oblast in 1784. Only after Catherine's death did her successor, Paul I, abolish the Taurida Oblast and incorporate it into a revived New Russia gubernia by decree in December 1796.

Grigory Potemkin, Catherine's close collaborator, directed much of the practical work of settling and developing the newly annexed southern territories until his death in 1791, founding new port and administrative centers including Kherson, Mykolaiv, and Sevastopol as a naval base, promoting agricultural settlement, and organizing a famous, and famously embellished in later legend, tour of the new territories for Catherine in 1787, an episode from which the phrase "Potemkin village," describing an impressive but hollow façade constructed to create a false impression, entered common usage — though historians caution that the most extreme versions of this legend, describing entirely fake painted settlements, exaggerate a genuine but more modest practice of selective presentation during the tour. Odessa, founded on the site of the former Ottoman fort of Khadzhibei, was established somewhat later, in 1794, after Potemkin's death, as part of the continued development of the region under Catherine's government. The Black Sea acquisitions of Catherine's reign gave Russia secure naval bases and commercial navigation rights connecting Black Sea trade to the wider Mediterranean; the Küçük Kaynarca settlement had granted Russian merchant vessels passage rights through the Ottoman-controlled straits, but military passage through the straits remained a distinct and far more constrained diplomatic question that continued to shape Russian-Ottoman relations for more than a century afterward, even as Black Sea expansion imposed profound and often destructive change on the Crimean Tatar society that had previously made the peninsula its home.

Section 34.9

The Partitions of Poland

The Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, formed by the union of the Kingdom of Poland and the Grand Duchy of Lithuania and examined in earlier chapters as a major regional power and rival claimant to the inheritance of medieval Rus', possessed by the eighteenth century a distinctive political system centered on an elective monarchy and a parliament, the Sejm, in which the principle of the liberum veto allowed a single deputy's objection to nullify legislative proceedings. This system, prized by the Commonwealth's szlachta, or noble class, as a guarantee against royal absolutism, had by the eighteenth century made effective, coordinated governance extremely difficult, leaving the Commonwealth increasingly vulnerable to the intervention of its more centralized and militarily capable neighbors: Russia, Prussia, and Austria.

Catherine's government intervened directly in Commonwealth politics from the beginning of her reign, securing the 1764 election of Stanisław August Poniatowski, a former favorite of Catherine's, to the Polish throne, and subsequently pressing for guarantees protecting the legal status of religious minorities within the Commonwealth, including Orthodox Christians, ostensibly on grounds of toleration but also as a lever for continued Russian influence over internal Commonwealth affairs. Resistance to this intervention among segments of the Polish nobility, combined with instability along the Commonwealth's borders, contributed to the tensions that helped trigger the first Russo-Turkish war examined in Section 34.7. Amid this ongoing instability, Russia, Prussia, and Austria agreed in 1772 to the First Partition of Poland-Lithuania, each power annexing a portion of Commonwealth territory and reducing the remaining state to a substantially smaller and more vulnerable rump.

The First Partition provoked serious internal reflection within the surviving Commonwealth, leading to a significant reform movement that culminated in the Constitution of 3 May 1791, one of Europe's earliest modern written constitutions, which abolished the liberum veto, strengthened royal executive authority, and attempted to modernize Commonwealth government along lines influenced by contemporary constitutional thought. This reform effort demonstrates clearly that the Commonwealth's eventual destruction was not a predetermined outcome awaiting only the convenience of its neighbors; Polish and Lithuanian political actors actively sought to reform and preserve their state, and the reform's genuine promise is essential to understanding why its neighbors, and conservative factions within the Commonwealth itself allied with Russia, moved quickly to reverse it. Russian and allied Polish conservative forces intervened militarily against the new constitutional order, and a Second Partition, between Russia and Prussia, followed in 1793, further reducing Commonwealth territory. A national uprising led by Tadeusz Kościuszko in 1794 sought to reverse these losses and defend what remained of Commonwealth sovereignty but was ultimately defeated by Russian and Prussian forces, and a Third Partition in 1795, dividing the Commonwealth's remaining territory among Russia, Prussia, and Austria, extinguished the Polish-Lithuanian state entirely as an independent political entity for more than a century.

Russian gains from the partitions extended Russian territory deep into what had been Commonwealth Lithuania, Belarusian lands, and right-bank Ukraine, incorporating substantial new populations of Poles, Lithuanians, Belarusians, Ukrainians, and, notably, a very large Jewish population previously subject to distinct Commonwealth legal arrangements. Their incorporation contributed to a series of residence restrictions Catherine's government imposed beginning in 1791 and extended through the following years, which laid the foundations of what became known as the Pale of Settlement, the designated territory within which most Jewish subjects of the empire were required to reside; this system was subsequently maintained and further elaborated by later imperial governments well into the nineteenth century, a policy examined further in later chapters treating nineteenth-century imperial minority policy. The partitions therefore did not simply add land to Russia; they added new peoples, languages, religions, and legal traditions whose incorporation created lasting administrative challenges and historical grievances extending well beyond Catherine's own reign.

Primary Source Window

Catherine II, the Nakaz (Instruction to the Legislative Commission), 1767

Creator: Catherine II · Date of composition: 1765–1767 · Type: Programmatic legal-political treatise

Context: Catherine drafted the Nakaz as a guiding statement of legal and political principle for the Legislative Commission examined in Section 34.3, drawing extensively on Montesquieu's Spirit of the Laws and Beccaria's writing on criminal justice.

Brief paraphrase of the document's themes (not a verbatim translated quotation):

The Nakaz argues that Russia's vast territorial extent requires a single sovereign authority capable of acting decisively for the common good, and that this authority should govern through clear, consistently applied laws rather than arbitrary decree. It calls for moderation in criminal punishment, opposes torture, favors legal equality of subjects before the law within their respective estates, and speaks broadly of the sovereign's obligation to promote the welfare and security of the people.

Analysis: The Nakaz is invaluable as the fullest statement of Catherine's political vocabulary, but it requires the same critical reading this textbook applies to every governing document. It borrowed extensively, at times very closely, from Montesquieu and Beccaria, raising questions about how much reflects Catherine's own original thought versus her selective adaptation of Western sources to Russian circumstances. It also explicitly assumes the necessity of autocratic government for a territory of Russia's size, a premise many of its European sources did not share in the same form, and it says relatively little about the legal status of the enserfed peasantry, an omission historians consider highly significant given that group's size within the empire's population.

Questions for students:

  1. Which ideas in the Nakaz draw most directly on Enlightenment political philosophy, and which assumptions remain distinctly autocratic?
  2. Why might Catherine have drawn so extensively on Montesquieu and Beccaria rather than composing an entirely original document?
  3. What does the Nakaz's relative silence on serfdom suggest about the limits of Catherine's reform ambitions?
  4. How does the Nakaz's argument for autocracy as a practical necessity compare with the arguments for territorial administration examined in Section 34.4?
Historical Debate

Was Catherine "Enlightened"? The Problem of "Enlightened Absolutism"

The question: Historians commonly describe Catherine, along with rulers such as Frederick II of Prussia and Joseph II of Austria, as an "enlightened absolutist" or "enlightened despot." How useful is this category, and does it accurately capture Catherine's reign?

The skeptical position: Some historians argue that "enlightened absolutism" is a misleading or even contradictory label, since genuine Enlightenment political thought, at least in some of its strands, emphasized limits on arbitrary power that Catherine never accepted. On this view, Catherine's Enlightenment language served chiefly to cultivate European elite opinion and to legitimize a coup-born reign, while her substantive policies — expanding noble privilege, tightening serfdom, suppressing the Pugachev rebellion, and later restricting the press after 1789 — reveal a ruler whose commitment to reform was calculated and limited rather than principled.
The corroborationist position: Other historians argue that dismissing Catherine's Enlightenment commitments as mere performance underestimates real, sustained achievements: a serious attempt at legal codification through the Legislative Commission, substantial administrative reform, religious toleration extended further than under most predecessors, and genuine cultural and educational patronage. On this view, "enlightened absolutism" accurately describes rulers who applied Enlightenment methods and vocabulary to strengthen, rather than limit, monarchical government, and Catherine fits this pattern more consistently than almost any other eighteenth-century ruler.

Evidence: Historians weigh the content and originality of the Nakaz, the outcomes of the Legislative Commission, the substance of provincial and educational reform, the treatment of serfdom and the peasantry, and Catherine's responses to the French Revolution after 1789.

Points of agreement: Most historians agree Catherine engaged seriously, not merely cosmetically, with Enlightenment ideas, and that her reign combined genuine reform with unwavering commitment to autocratic and noble-privileged government.

Points of disagreement: Historians differ over how much weight to give the gap between Catherine's stated principles and the condition of the peasantry, and over whether "enlightened absolutism" is best treated as a coherent historical phenomenon or as a retrospective label imposed by later historians on a diverse group of rulers.

Student task: Using evidence from Sections 34.2, 34.3, and 34.6, construct an argument for whether "enlightened absolutism" is a useful category for understanding Catherine's reign, addressing at least one piece of evidence that complicates your position.

Multiple Perspectives

Annexing Crimea

A Russian imperial official: For an official serving Catherine's government, the annexation of Crimea in 1783 represented the fulfillment of a long-standing strategic goal reaching back to Peter I: secure Russian access to the Black Sea, the elimination of a source of Tatar raiding that had troubled Russia's southern frontier for centuries, and a major addition to imperial territory and prestige achieved with comparatively little direct fighting after the 1774 declaration of Crimean "independence."
A Crimean Tatar notable: For a member of the Crimean Tatar elite, the years after 1774 brought escalating Russian interference in Khanate politics, the loss of Ottoman protection that had structured Crimean political and religious life for centuries, and, after 1783, the definitive loss of Khanate independence, followed by substantial emigration of Tatar populations, land dispossession, and the arrival of new settlers on land the Tatars had long inhabited.
An Ottoman official: For an official of the Ottoman government, the loss of suzerainty over Crimea in 1774 and its outright annexation in 1783 represented a significant strategic and religious setback, removing a longstanding Muslim tributary state from the Ottoman sphere and exposing the empire's northern Black Sea frontier to further Russian pressure, concerns that helped motivate the second Russo-Turkish war examined in Section 34.7.

These perspectives illustrate why annexation cannot be adequately understood only as a Russian territorial acquisition; it was simultaneously a strategic triumph for one government, a geopolitical setback for another, and a profound and disruptive transformation for the society that had inhabited and governed the peninsula.

Cause and Effect

Building a Durable Autocracy

Long-Term Conditions

The post-Petrine pattern of Guards-dependent succession and noble political influence examined in Chapter 33; Russia's underdeveloped provincial administration inherited from the early eighteenth century.

Intermediate Developments

Peter III's alienation of Guards and noble support; Catherine's two decades of court relationship-building and intellectual self-cultivation before 1762; the 1762 Manifesto on the Freedom of the Nobility.

Short-Term Triggers

The 1762 coup itself; the pressing need, after an irregular accession, to secure noble, European, and administrative legitimacy through visible reform and military success.

Effects

The Nakaz and Legislative Commission; the 1775 provincial reform; the 1785 Charter to the Nobility; military victories over the Ottoman Empire and the partitions of Poland, together producing a thirty-four-year reign of unusual stability and international prestige.

Historical complexity: No single policy—legal reform alone, noble privilege alone, or military victory alone—secured Catherine's throne; these were interlocking sources of legitimacy that developed over thirty-four years of changing circumstance, war, rebellion, and shifting priorities, and that often reinforced one another without necessarily reflecting a single plan conceived at the outset.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Peter I to Catherine II

What Stayed the Same

  • Both rulers pursued state-directed modernization understood chiefly as strengthening Russia's military, administrative, and international standing.
  • Both drew self-consciously on Western European models while insisting on Russia's need for strong, centralized monarchical authority.
  • Both expanded Russian territory substantially and sought secure maritime access as a strategic priority.

What Changed

  • Peter pursued reform chiefly through coercion, compelling noble service and imposing sweeping institutional change from above; Catherine relied far more on cultivating noble cooperation through expanded privilege.
  • Peter's modernization emphasized military-fiscal transformation and forced Westernization of elite manners; Catherine's emphasized codified law, education, and Enlightenment political vocabulary.
  • Peter's service-state model bound nobles tightly to compulsory state duty; Peter III's Manifesto of 1762 released nobles from that duty, and Catherine's Charter of 1785 confirmed that freedom and codified noble corporate privileges by law.

Why the pattern matters: Comparing the two rulers shows that "modernizing autocracy" was not a fixed program but a flexible strategy adapted to changing political circumstances and elite expectations across the eighteenth century.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Provincial Governor's Office

The 1775 provincial reform examined in Section 34.4 was experienced, in practice, through the daily operation of a new provincial bureaucracy: governors and their staffs processing petitions, managing tax collection, adjudicating disputes through newly organized estate courts, and coordinating with local noble marshals elected under the structures the reform created. For provincial nobles, service in these new local institutions offered social prestige, local influence, and a continuing connection to state authority even after the compulsory service obligation had been lifted, while for townspeople and state peasants, the reform's separate estate courts meant that legal status, rather than uniform citizenship, continued to determine which institutions governed one's daily affairs.

For merchants and townspeople, the companion Charter to the Towns of 1785 similarly codified municipal self-government and merchant corporate privileges, though, as with the nobility, these institutions functioned within, rather than against, the framework of autocratic oversight, and actual local influence often depended heavily on wealth, connections, and the cooperation of provincial governors.

Regional Context

The Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth on Its Own Terms

Before its partition, the Commonwealth was one of early modern Europe's largest states, with its own political traditions, religious diversity encompassing Catholic, Orthodox, Protestant, and Jewish communities, and a distinctive noble republican political culture that, whatever its practical difficulties, reflected genuine constitutional experimentation, most visibly in the Constitution of 3 May 1791 examined in Section 34.9. Polish and Lithuanian political actors were not passive subjects awaiting absorption; they debated, reformed, resisted, and, under Kościuszko, fought to preserve their state's independence.

The Commonwealth's disappearance from the map after 1795 shaped the modern political history of Poland, Lithuania, Belarus, and Ukraine for more than a century, and the memory of partition became a central element of nineteenth-century Polish national identity, examined further in Chapter 39's treatment of the Polish uprisings of the following century.

Russia and the World

The Black Sea and Ottoman Balance

Catherine's Black Sea ambitions unfolded within a wider European diplomatic system in which Britain, France, Austria, and Prussia each had strategic interests in the balance of power between Russia and the Ottoman Empire. European governments watched Russian gains at Küçük Kaynarca and after the Crimean annexation with a mixture of alarm and calculation, since a weakened Ottoman Empire and a strengthened Russia altered the strategic balance in the Mediterranean and southeastern Europe. This dynamic, sometimes called the Eastern Question in later nineteenth-century diplomacy and examined further in Chapter 41's treatment of the Crimean War, had its decisive eighteenth-century origins in the wars and annexations examined in this chapter.

Chapter Review and Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has traced Catherine II's thirty-four-year reign from its irregular origins in a Guards-backed palace coup to its conclusion as one of the most territorially successful and internationally prestigious reigns in Russian imperial history. Before proceeding to Chapter 35's examination of serfdom, estate society, and the peoples of the eighteenth-century imperial order, students should apply this chapter's lessons to four synthesizing problems.

Problem 1 — Legitimacy from Force.

Explain how Catherine converted an insecure, coup-based accession into a durable and internationally respected reign, drawing on evidence from Sections 34.1, 34.2, and 34.6.

Problem 2 — Enlightenment and Autocracy.

Using evidence from Sections 34.2 and 34.3, explain how "enlightened government" could coexist with, and even reinforce, autocratic rule rather than limiting it.

Problem 3 — Reform and Privilege.

Using evidence from Sections 34.4 and 34.6, explain why strengthening noble corporate privilege could serve to strengthen, rather than weaken, the autocratic state.

Problem 4 — Expansion and Its Costs.

Using evidence from Sections 34.7, 34.8, and 34.9, explain what Russia gained through Black Sea expansion and the partitions of Poland, and what the Crimean Tatar and Polish-Lithuanian peoples lost in the process.

To what extent can Catherine's reign be understood simultaneously as Enlightenment reform, autocratic consolidation, noble privilege, and imperial expansion? These four problems reinforce the habit of mind central to this chapter: recognizing that these four dimensions of Catherine's rule were not contradictory alternatives requiring a single choice, but interacting features of her long reign that frequently reinforced one another as circumstances changed, whose social costs—especially for the enserfed peasantry and for the diverse peoples brought under Russian rule—form the subject of Chapter 35.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the reign of Catherine II, from the palace coup of 1762 that brought her to power through her death in 1796, as a sustained project of building political legitimacy through the combination of Enlightenment-influenced reform, autocratic consolidation, noble partnership, and territorial expansion. Catherine's accession, examined in Section 34.1, left her without a hereditary claim to the throne, a problem that shaped nearly every subsequent policy examined in this chapter.

The chapter traced Catherine's genuine, sustained engagement with European Enlightenment thought, examined in Section 34.2, while distinguishing her project sharply from later liberal or democratic political thought: Catherine sought rational law, administrative efficiency, and cultural patronage without ever intending to share sovereign authority. The Nakaz and the Legislative Commission of 1767–1768, examined in Section 34.3, tested these commitments directly; the Commission's failure to produce a finished law code should be read as evidence of both genuine ambition and genuine difficulty, not as proof that the undertaking was insincere.

Catherine's provincial reforms of 1775, examined in Section 34.4, addressed a structural problem — the gap between formal central authority and effective local governance — that had troubled Russian rulers since Peter I, and were shaped in part by the administrative weaknesses exposed by Pugachev's Rebellion, fully treated in Chapter 35. Her educational and cultural patronage, examined in Section 34.5, substantially expanded elite Russian intellectual and artistic life while leaving the great majority of her subjects untouched by its benefits.

The Charter to the Nobility of 1785, examined in Section 34.6, resolved the apparent paradox of an autocrat strengthening noble privilege by showing how secure, legally codified noble rights bound the empire's most powerful social group more closely to the autocratic order that alone could guarantee those rights — a settlement that simultaneously deepened the divide between noble privilege and peasant subjection examined fully in Chapter 35.

The chapter's second half traced Catherine's territorial expansion: two wars against the Ottoman Empire, examined in Section 34.7; the annexation of the Crimean Khanate, examined in Section 34.8 with attention to its consequences for Crimean Tatar society; and the three partitions of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, examined in Section 34.9, which this chapter presented as an interaction between Commonwealth politics — including the genuine reform effort of the Constitution of 3 May 1791 — and the calculated interests of Russia, Prussia, and Austria, rather than as an inevitable outcome.

Throughout, the chapter emphasized that Catherine's reign should not be reduced to either uncritical celebration of enlightened reform or dismissal of that reform as mere propaganda; genuine reform and unwavering autocratic and noble-privileged government coexisted as interacting, mutually reinforcing features of her rule across thirty-four years of changing circumstance, rather than as the execution of one fixed plan conceived at her accession.

These lessons—attention to the structural logic connecting reform and privilege, critical evaluation of Enlightenment vocabulary within an autocratic context, and sustained attention to the experience of peoples affected by imperial expansion—prepare students directly for Chapter 35's examination of serfdom, estate society, and the diverse peoples of Catherine's empire, and for Chapter 36's turn to Russia's confrontation with revolutionary and Napoleonic France.

Key Terms

Palace coup
The seizure of political power through court and military conspiracy rather than hereditary succession, the mechanism by which Catherine came to power in 1762.
Enlightened absolutism
A historiographical category describing eighteenth-century monarchs who applied Enlightenment vocabulary and methods to strengthen, rather than limit, autocratic government; examined critically in this chapter's Historical Debate.
Nakaz
Catherine's Instruction of 1767, drafted to guide the Legislative Commission, drawing heavily on Montesquieu and Beccaria.
Legislative Commission
A consultative representative body convened 1767–1768 to draft a new Russian law code; adjourned without completing that task.
Gubernia (province)
The reorganized territorial administrative unit established by the 1775 provincial reform.
Charter to the Nobility
The 1785 charter codifying noble corporate rights, privileges, and local self-government.
Charter to the Towns
A companion 1785 charter codifying municipal self-government and merchant corporate privileges.
Noble corporate rights
Legal privileges belonging to the nobility as a defined social estate rather than to individuals alone.
Liberum veto
A Polish-Lithuanian Sejm procedure allowing a single deputy to nullify legislative proceedings, contributing to Commonwealth governmental paralysis.
Partition (of Poland)
The division of Commonwealth territory among Russia, Prussia, and Austria in 1772, 1793, and 1795.
Suzerainty
A relationship in which a subordinate state retains internal autonomy while acknowledging the overlordship of a dominant power, as the Crimean Khanate did under the Ottoman Empire until 1774.
Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca
The 1774 treaty ending the first Russo-Turkish war of Catherine's reign and declaring the Crimean Khanate independent of Ottoman suzerainty.
Warm-water port
A port that remains ice-free and navigable year-round, a strategic goal underlying Russian Black Sea expansion.
Novorossiya (New Russia)
A shifting administrative and settlement designation applied to the northern Black Sea steppe; Crimea itself was organized separately as the Taurida Oblast in 1784 and was joined with mainland Novorossiya only after Catherine's death, by decree of Paul I in December 1796.
Toleration
The Enlightenment-associated principle of permitting religious and, to varying degrees, ethnic diversity within a single political order, applied selectively under Catherine.
Codified law
Law organized into a single, systematic, rationally structured body, the unrealized goal of the Legislative Commission.
Estate (soslovie)
A legally defined social category, such as nobility, clergy, townspeople, or peasantry, possessing distinct rights and obligations under Russian law.
Constitution of 3 May 1791
A Polish-Lithuanian constitutional reform abolishing the liberum veto and strengthening royal authority, reversed by Russian-backed intervention.
Potemkin village
A phrase, derived from Grigory Potemkin's development of Novorossiya, denoting an impressive but hollow façade; historians caution against accepting its most exaggerated popular versions uncritically.
Pale of Settlement
A system of Jewish residence restrictions begun under Catherine in 1791, following Russia's acquisition of large Jewish populations through the First Partition of Poland, and subsequently maintained and further developed by later imperial governments.

Comparing the Nakaz and Its Sources

This project asks you to investigate the relationship between Catherine's Nakaz and its principal Western European sources, evaluating how closely it borrowed from them and what its adaptations reveal about Russian political circumstances.

  1. Select passages. Choose two or three sections of the Nakaz addressing law, punishment, or sovereignty.
  2. Identify sources. Research which passages of Montesquieu's Spirit of the Laws or Beccaria's writing on crime and punishment most closely correspond to your selected passages.
  3. Analyze adaptation. Identify what Catherine changed, added, or omitted in adapting these sources to Russian circumstances.
  4. Evaluate silence on serfdom. Research what, if anything, the Nakaz says about the condition of the enserfed peasantry, and compare this with its treatment of noble and urban estates.
  5. Write your findings. In a 3–5 page essay, explain what the Nakaz's borrowings and adaptations reveal about the possibilities and limits of enlightened government in autocratic Russia.

Library resources to use: The MGU History Library's eighteenth-century Russian history collection, translated editions of the Nakaz, and academic editions of Montesquieu and Beccaria.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has traced Catherine II's transformation of an insecure, coup-born accession into one of the most stable, prestigious, and territorially successful reigns in Russian imperial history. Catherine was neither simply a philosophe on the throne nor simply a cynical autocrat wielding Enlightenment language as propaganda; she was a ruler who genuinely engaged with reforming ideas while never doubting that only unshared autocratic authority, supported by a securely privileged nobility, could govern an empire of Russia's scale.

The chapter's central tension—between reforming ambition and autocratic practice—shaped nearly every policy examined here, from the Legislative Commission's unfinished law code to the Charter that bound the nobility more closely to the throne even as it left the peasantry more firmly bound to their landlords. That same tension extended outward into Catherine's wars and annexations, which brought Russia lasting strategic gains in the Black Sea and in Poland-Lithuania while imposing profound disruption on the Crimean Tatar and Polish-Lithuanian peoples whose homelands those gains absorbed.

As the textbook proceeds to Chapter 35's examination of serfdom, estate society, and the diverse peoples of the eighteenth-century imperial order—including the Pugachev rebellion that this chapter has deliberately left for fuller treatment there—students should carry forward this chapter's central lesson: that Catherine's reign cannot be reduced to a single label, enlightened or autocratic, reforming or repressive, but must be understood as the changing, often mutually reinforcing interaction of all of these elements across a long and eventful reign.
