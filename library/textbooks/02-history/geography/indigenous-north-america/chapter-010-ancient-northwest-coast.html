The Ancient Northwest Coast

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

On Haida Gwaii, an archipelago off the northern coast of present-day British Columbia, archaeologists working at a site called Kilgii Gwaay have documented evidence of a coastal way of life reaching back more than ten thousand years. The tools, the food remains, and the location of the site, on a shoreline shaped by sea levels very different from today's, testify to a fundamental fact about the ancient Northwest Coast: people had already built lives around the sea, its rivers, and its forests very early in the region's postglacial history.

Chapter 9 closed with a question that this chapter now takes up directly. Along the coast of California, Indigenous communities that did not depend on domesticated-crop agriculture nonetheless achieved dense populations, long-term settlement, economic specialization, and, in places, marked social distinctions, challenging any assumption that only farming societies could become populous or complex. The Northwest Coast, stretching from northwestern California through Washington, British Columbia, Haida Gwaii, and southeastern Alaska, carried these same processes further and, in places, earlier. By the time European ships began entering the region's waters in the eighteenth century, Northwest Coast societies were famous, and remain famous today, for enormous cedar-plank houses, oceangoing canoes, elaborately carved and painted art, hereditary systems of rank and privilege, and ceremonial institutions such as the potlatch that redistributed wealth while affirming social position. None of this depended on domesticated-crop agriculture of the kind examined in Chapter 13. It depended instead on an extraordinarily productive, if uneven and seasonal, marine and riverine environment, on technologies of storage, woodworking, and water travel, and, as Section 10.4 discusses, on deliberate management and cultivation of certain plant resources, all developed and refined by Indigenous peoples of the region over thousands of years.

It is tempting, and it is historically wrong, to explain this entire achievement with a single sentence: the Northwest Coast had abundant salmon, and abundant salmon produced complex societies. This chapter argues that such a sentence badly oversimplifies a much longer, more regionally varied, and more historically interesting story. Salmon mattered enormously, but so did herring, halibut, shellfish, sea mammals, land game, berries, roots, and other resources whose relative importance differed sharply from one stretch of coastline to the next. Storage mattered enormously, and some archaeologists interpret the evidence from the site of Namu, on the central coast of British Columbia, as showing storage-based, substantially sedentary village life developing there by roughly seven thousand years ago, an interpretation examined critically in Section 10.5, well before the historically documented systems of hereditary rank, elaborate house-front art, and specialized whale hunting that outside observers later took as defining the entire region. Different components of what is sometimes carelessly called "the Northwest Coast pattern" therefore developed at different times, in different places, for different reasons, and this chapter's central task is to trace that uneven, cumulative history rather than to assume it appeared as a single finished package.

The evidence for this history comes overwhelmingly from archaeology, supplemented where possible by the oral traditions and continuing knowledge of Indigenous nations whose ancestors built these communities and who remain present on this coast today, including the Haida, the Heiltsuk, the Nuu-chah-nulth, the Kwakwaka'wakw, the Coast Salish peoples, the Makah, and many others. Shell middens, the accumulated remains of generations of shellfish harvesting and other domestic waste, preserve extraordinarily long and detailed records of diet, settlement, and, when carefully excavated, changing social organization. Waterlogged and mud-sealed sites occasionally preserve wood, basketry, and cordage that would otherwise have decayed entirely, offering rare glimpses of a technological world that ordinary preservation conditions almost entirely erase. The chapter's closing case study, the Makah village site partially buried by a mudslide at what is now called Ozette, illustrates this preservation problem vividly: because that slide sealed part of a village beneath oxygen-poor mud, tens of thousands of wood, fiber, and other perishable objects survived there that have vanished from almost every other site on the coast.

This chapter also asks students to hold two ideas together that can seem to pull in opposite directions. On one hand, Northwest Coast communities became remarkably settled: substantial villages of planked houses, occupied across many generations, appear at various points across the archaeological record, and some places show deep continuity of occupation reaching back thousands of years. On the other hand, settlement did not mean staying still. Households moved, by canoe, to fishing camps, shellfish beds, hunting grounds, and berry patches at different times of year, and the entire coastal economy depended on this seasonal rhythm of movement organized around a permanent home base. Sedentism and mobility, in other words, were not opposites on the Northwest Coast; they were two sides of the same successful adaptation to a demanding, productive, and highly seasonal environment.

Finally, this chapter treats the Northwest Coast as a region of real internal diversity rather than a single culture. Haida Gwaii, the central British Columbia coast, the Salish Sea and Fraser River system, western Vancouver Island, the Olympic Peninsula, and the lower Columbia River each combined marine, riverine, and terrestrial resources somewhat differently, and each region's archaeological sequence tells a somewhat different story about the timing and character of change. A history that flattens these differences into one generic "Northwest Coast culture" misrepresents both the historical record and the political and cultural distinctiveness of the many nations whose homelands make up this coast today.

By the chapter's end, students should understand the Northwest Coast neither as a place where nature simply handed people prosperity, nor as a region whose famous features can be projected backward, unchanged, across ten thousand years. They should instead understand it as a region where successive generations of Indigenous peoples built, tested, refined, and regionally adapted an extraordinarily successful set of institutions and technologies for living well, and unevenly, from a rich but variable sea.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Define the Northwest Coast as an analytical cultural and archaeological region and identify its major sub-regions.
  2. Explain why the region's economy cannot be reduced to "salmon abundance" alone, and identify the range of marine and terrestrial resources it depended upon.
  3. Describe the earliest known evidence of coastal occupation, including sites such as Kilgii Gwaay on Haida Gwaii, and the challenges posed by changing relative sea levels.
  4. Trace the development of salmon storage economies and early sedentism, using the site of Namu as a central case study, and evaluate the scholarly debate over how confidently this evidence establishes early storage-based sedentism.
  5. Analyze the role of woodworking, fiber technology, and watercraft in the coastal economy and explain why preservation conditions distort the surviving evidence.
  6. Explain how plank houses functioned as economic, social, and ceremonial institutions rather than simply as architecture.
  7. Evaluate the archaeological evidence for the emergence of social rank and wealth differentiation, and explain why this evidence points to a gradual, regionally uneven process.
  8. Describe how seasonal mobility operated within a broadly sedentary settlement system, rather than treating mobility and sedentism as opposites.
  9. Compare at least four regional sub-areas of the Northwest Coast and identify meaningful differences in their resource base and historical trajectory.
  10. Analyze the Ozette site as an example of exceptional archaeological preservation and its relationship to Makah oral history.
  11. Critique the idea of a single, unified "Northwest Coast package" of cultural traits, and explain why its components developed at different times.
  12. Assess the strengths and limitations of shell-midden archaeology, ancient DNA analysis, and waterlogged-site preservation as forms of historical evidence.

Historical Context

Chapter 9 examined the Indigenous peoples of ancient California, whose linguistic and ecological diversity, dense population, and, in some areas, notable social differentiation demonstrated that societies not dependent on domesticated-crop agriculture could achieve levels of settlement and complexity historians once assumed required farming. Northwestern California, home to peoples whose economies and, later, whose material culture overlapped considerably with practices found farther north, served as a natural bridge between that chapter and this one. This chapter continues north along the coast, into the region conventionally called the Northwest Coast: the temperate, island-studded, forested shoreline running from northwestern California through Oregon, Washington, British Columbia, Haida Gwaii, and into southeastern Alaska.

The deep human history of this coast begins, as Chapter 2 through Chapter 6 established for the continent as a whole, with the peopling of the Americas at the end of the Pleistocene. What makes the Northwest Coast especially significant within that larger story is the strong possibility, actively investigated by archaeologists and geneticists, that early movement along the Pacific coastline, sometimes called a coastal migration route, allowed people to reach parts of the Americas earlier, or by a different path, than an exclusively interior route across the retreating ice sheets would have allowed. This chapter does not resolve that larger debate, which belongs to Chapters 3 and 4; it instead picks up the story once coastal communities are archaeologically visible in their own right, beginning in the terminal Pleistocene and earliest Holocene.

A crucial complication runs throughout this chapter: sea levels along much of the Northwest Coast have changed substantially since the end of the last glaciation, and not in a single uniform direction. In some areas, land that was depressed by the weight of glacial ice rebounded upward after the ice melted, while sea level itself rose as glaciers worldwide released meltwater into the oceans. The balance between these two processes differed from place to place, meaning that some ancient shorelines now lie underwater while others now lie well inland or well above modern sea level. This has a direct historical consequence: the earliest coastal sites are not evenly distributed across the modern landscape, and their apparent absence in some areas may simply reflect that the shorelines people actually used are no longer accessible to archaeologists, rather than reflecting an actual absence of ancient coastal life.

Section 10.1

What Is the Northwest Coast?

"Northwest Coast" is, like "Great Basin" or "California" in the preceding chapters, an analytical category rather than a single Indigenous political or cultural identity. Archaeologists and anthropologists use the term to describe the temperate, deeply indented, island-rich coastline running roughly from northwestern California through Oregon, Washington, British Columbia, Haida Gwaii, and southeastern Alaska. For the purposes of this textbook, northwestern California has already been introduced in Chapter 9 as a transitional zone sharing important features with the region examined here, including its dependence on salmon runs and its use of plank construction, while the present chapter concentrates on the coastal region extending from Washington and Oregon northward through southeastern Alaska.

Within this large region, dozens of distinct nations, speaking languages from several unrelated language families, including Salishan, Wakashan, Tsimshianic, and others discussed further in Chapter 33, built their own particular histories, economies, and political systems. No single "Northwest Coast people" ever existed, any more than a single "European people" existed. What justifies treating the region together in a single chapter is not cultural uniformity but a shared set of environmental conditions, a temperate rainforest climate, extensive island and fjord coastlines, and productive marine and riverine fisheries, that gave rise to broadly comparable, though far from identical, economic strategies and technological solutions across many separate societies.

Historical Vocabulary
Complex Hunter-Gatherers

Definition: Archaeologists use the term "complex hunter-gatherers" to describe societies that supported themselves primarily through hunting, fishing, gathering, and intensive management of wild and semi-managed resources, without depending on domesticated-crop agriculture, while nonetheless achieving substantial population density, long-term or permanent settlement, economic specialization, storage of surplus food, and, in many cases, hereditary social rank.

Historical context: The Northwest Coast is one of the world's most frequently cited examples of this pattern, alongside societies such as those of ancient California examined in Chapter 9 and some Jōmon-period communities of ancient Japan. These societies challenge an older assumption in anthropology that population density, sedentism, and social hierarchy required farming.

Why it matters: Using this term carefully allows historians to explain Northwest Coast achievements without implying that its societies were an exception to normal rules of human development, or, conversely, that they were somehow "almost agricultural." As Section 10.4 explains, some Northwest Coast communities also practiced deliberate cultivation of specific plants, such as wapato, without this cultivation resembling the field-crop agriculture examined in Chapter 13. The category "complex hunter-gatherer" therefore describes a broad economic strategy that could include some cultivation, not a strict, uniform absence of any plant management.

Potential misunderstanding: "Complex" here refers to social and economic organization, not to some measure of moral or cultural superiority over more mobile or less populous societies. All human societies are, in the relevant historical sense, complex.

Section 10.2

Between Rainforest and Sea

The Northwest Coast's defining environmental features are its temperate rainforest, its intricate coastline of fjords, islands, and estuaries, and its network of salmon-bearing rivers connecting the interior to the sea. Heavy rainfall supports dense stands of western red cedar, Sitka spruce, western hemlock, and Douglas fir, forests that, as Section 10.6 explains, supplied the raw material for the region's distinctive woodworking traditions. The coastline itself, shaped by glaciation, offers countless sheltered bays, narrow channels, and productive intertidal zones, all of which could be reached and exploited efficiently by canoe.

It is important to state precisely what made this environment valuable to human communities, because loose talk of general "abundance" obscures the real historical pattern. The Northwest Coast's productivity was not uniform, constant, or automatic. It was concentrated in specific places, salmon rivers at spawning time, herring spawning grounds in early spring, particular shellfish beds, particular sea-mammal haul-outs, and it was highly seasonal, meaning that different resources became available, in large quantities, at different points in the year and in different specific locations. Exploiting this pattern successfully required not passive reliance on natural bounty but detailed, transmitted knowledge of when and where resources would appear, together with the technology, labor organization, and storage methods needed to convert a short, intense harvest into food that could last through leaner months. The chapters that follow trace how coastal communities built exactly this kind of knowledge and technology over thousands of years.

Section 10.3

Early Holocene Coastal Communities

The archaeological site of Kilgii Gwaay, on Haida Gwaii, provides some of the earliest well-documented evidence of coastal life on the Northwest Coast. Materials recovered there indicate a maritime way of life, one that Parks Canada and Haida researchers describe as reflecting Haida presence on these islands, extending back more than ten thousand years. This places the earliest known coastal occupation on Haida Gwaii solidly within the terminal Pleistocene and earliest Holocene, the same broad era treated in Chapters 3 through 6 for the continent's initial peopling.

Interpreting this evidence requires taking seriously the complication introduced in the Historical Context section above: relative sea levels along this coast have changed substantially and unevenly since the end of the last glaciation. Along the central coast of British Columbia, research using shell-midden cores has shown that the known chronology of settlement correlates strongly with long-term patterns of local relative sea-level change, meaning that where archaeologists find old sites, and how old those sites can be, depends heavily on where the ancient shoreline actually was at a given time, not solely on where people chose to live. In some areas, land rebounded upward after the weight of glacial ice was removed, raising former shorelines above the modern sea; in other areas, rising global sea levels outpaced any local rebound, submerging former shorelines beneath the modern sea, in some cases by many meters.

This has an important interpretive consequence that students should remember throughout the chapter: the absence of very early archaeological sites in a particular stretch of coastline does not necessarily mean that people were absent from that area at an early date. It may instead mean that the shoreline they used has since been submerged, eroded, or otherwise made inaccessible to archaeological survey. Conversely, well-preserved early sites such as Kilgii Gwaay survive, in part, because of particular local histories of relative sea-level change that happened to preserve rather than destroy or submerge the relevant ancient shoreline. Historians of this period must therefore read the distribution of known early sites as a partial, geologically filtered sample of a once more widespread pattern of coastal settlement, not as a complete record of where people actually lived.

Historical Vocabulary
Shell Middens and Site Formation

Definition: A shell midden is an archaeological deposit built up, often over centuries or millennia, from discarded shells, bone, ash, tools, and other domestic refuse left behind by people harvesting and processing shellfish and other foods at or near a settlement. "Site formation" refers to the full set of natural and human processes, deposition, compaction, erosion, burial, and disturbance, that shape how a site's remains come to be arranged, preserved, or destroyed by the time archaeologists excavate it.

Historical context: On the Northwest Coast, shell middens can accumulate to remarkable depth and extent where a location was used repeatedly across very long periods, and their layered structure, examined through techniques such as vibracore sampling discussed in Section 10.4, allows archaeologists to reconstruct changing diet, settlement duration, and, in some cases, environmental change across thousands of years at a single location.

Why it matters: Middens are among the most durable and information-rich archaeological deposits on the coast precisely because shell, bone, and stone resist decay far better than wood or fiber. Understanding how a midden actually forms, through repeated, sometimes seasonal deposition rather than a single event, helps historians avoid treating its contents as a single snapshot and instead read it as a long, cumulative record.

Potential misunderstanding: A large midden does not automatically indicate a large or permanently settled population; the same volume of shell could reflect either an intensively used seasonal camp occupied briefly but repeatedly over many centuries, or a smaller number of occupations by a larger, more sedentary community. Archaeologists must examine internal midden structure, artifact density, and other evidence, not shell volume alone, before drawing conclusions about settlement size or duration, a caution directly relevant to the debate over Namu discussed in Section 10.5.

Section 10.4

Marine Foods Beyond Salmon

Popular accounts of the Northwest Coast frequently reduce its entire economy to salmon, and salmon does deserve the extended treatment given in Section 10.5. But archaeological evidence increasingly shows that coastal communities depended on a much wider range of marine and terrestrial resources, whose relative importance varied considerably by location and by period. Research using vibracore sampling of shell-midden deposits on the central coast of British Columbia has documented roughly six thousand years of Indigenous fisheries and settlement, revealing sustained, long-term reliance on both herring and salmon, with the relative importance of each resource differing according to local site ecology rather than following a single coast-wide pattern.

Herring, in particular, deserve more attention than they traditionally receive in general historical narratives. Herring spawn in enormous numbers in specific nearshore locations in early spring, depositing eggs on eelgrass, kelp, and other underwater vegetation. This spawn could be harvested in vast quantities using submerged branches or other materials placed to collect the eggs, providing a nutritionally rich, storable food source at a time of year when stored resources from the previous year might be running low. Alongside herring and salmon, coastal communities relied on halibut and other groundfish, shellfish gathered from tidal flats and rocky shores, sea mammals including seals, sea lions, and, in some regions, whales, seabirds and their eggs, and a range of terrestrial resources including deer, elk, berries, and roots gathered from forest and estuary environments. No single resource, including salmon, can account on its own for the historical trajectory this chapter traces; rather, the successful, flexible combination of many resources, deployed according to detailed seasonal and local knowledge, made the Northwest Coast's demanding but genuinely productive environment work for the people who lived there.

This picture of wild-resource use should not be read as implying that Northwest Coast peoples never deliberately cultivated plants. Archaeological research in Katzie territory, in the Fraser River lowlands of the Salish Sea region, has identified an engineered wapato garden, a constructed wetland feature designed to increase the yield of wapato, an edible aquatic tuber, dating to roughly 3,800 years ago. This evidence has led archaeologists to reconsider older assumptions that Northwest Coast economies rested solely on hunting, fishing, and the gathering of unmanaged wild plants. The wapato garden indicates that at least some Coast Salish communities practiced sustained, intentional cultivation of a specific food plant over a very long span of time, even though this cultivation did not resemble, and did not replace, the field-based cultivation of domesticated grain and seed crops examined in Chapter 13. The most accurate summary is therefore that Northwest Coast economies were not dependent on domesticated-crop agriculture, while still including, in at least some places and periods, deliberate plant cultivation alongside hunting, fishing, gathering, and storage.

Section 10.5

Salmon, Storage, and the Growth of Permanent Places

Salmon nonetheless occupy a distinctive place in the region's history, and this section examines why. Pacific salmon return from the ocean to spawn in the same natal rivers and streams where they hatched, arriving in different runs at predictable, though not perfectly constant, times of year. This predictability, combined with the sheer numbers in which salmon could arrive during a strong run, made mass capture using weirs, traps, and nets an extraordinarily efficient strategy, capable of yielding, in a short period, far more food than any single household could consume immediately.

The historical significance of this pattern depended entirely on the development of effective preservation and storage technology, since a large catch was worthless beyond a few days unless it could be dried, smoked, or otherwise preserved for months of later use. Drying and smoking salmon on wooden racks, a technology requiring relatively simple tools but considerable accumulated knowledge and labor organization, allowed communities to convert a short, intense harvest into a food supply capable of sustaining a settled population through the winter months when fresh food was scarce.

The archaeological site of Namu, on the central coast of British Columbia, sits at the center of an important scholarly debate over how early this storage-based strategy developed. Archaeologists Aubrey Cannon and Dongya Yang analyzed ancient DNA extracted from salmon remains recovered at Namu and interpreted the pattern and concentration of these remains as evidence of an established salmon-storage economy supporting a substantially sedentary, permanent winter village extending back roughly seven thousand years. If correct, this interpretation would place large-scale, storage-dependent, sedentary village life at this site many thousands of years before the historically documented Northwest Coast institutions of hereditary rank and elaborate plank-house art examined in Section 10.8.

Other archaeologists, notably Gregory Monks and Trevor Orchard, have directly challenged this interpretation, arguing that the Namu salmon assemblage and its associated ancient-DNA evidence do not, by themselves, securely establish either year-round sedentism or a dedicated storage-and-preservation focus at this early date; the same evidence, in their view, remains consistent with more limited or seasonal use of the site. Cannon and Yang have in turn defended their original interpretation in published response. Namu therefore should be understood not as settled proof of a seven-thousand-year-old storage village, but as an important body of evidence at the center of an active, ongoing debate over how early storage-based sedentism actually emerged on the Northwest Coast. What both sides of this debate agree upon is more limited but still significant: sedentism and storage capacity, wherever and whenever they first developed, are separate historical questions from the emergence of hierarchical social organization, examined in Section 10.8, and the two did not necessarily appear together or at the same pace anywhere on the coast.

Primary Source Window

Reading Namu's Salmon Bones

Creator: Ancient salmon populations, recovered and analyzed by archaeological researchers · Type: Zooarchaeological and ancient DNA evidence from excavated fish remains, published in peer-reviewed archaeological research on the Namu site

Context: Excavations at Namu, on the central coast of British Columbia, recovered large quantities of salmon bone from deep, long-accumulated deposits. Because different Pacific salmon species, and even different populations within a species, can sometimes be distinguished using ancient DNA, researchers Aubrey Cannon and Dongya Yang were able to analyze which salmon populations were being caught and how consistently, over a very long span of time.

What the evidence shows, and what remains contested: Cannon and Yang interpret the pattern and concentration of salmon remains, combined with genetic analysis, as supporting the conclusion that people at Namu were catching, processing, and storing salmon on a substantial scale for a very long period, consistent with sustained, substantially sedentary village occupation extending back roughly seven thousand years. Monks and Orchard have published a direct critique arguing that this same evidence does not, on its own, securely establish either year-round sedentism or a dedicated storage economy at that early date, and Cannon and Yang have since defended their original interpretation. Students should treat the "roughly seven thousand years" figure as a debated scholarly interpretation of the evidence rather than an uncontested fact.

Analysis: This is not a written or oral primary source in the conventional sense; it is material evidence that historians and archaeologists must interpret using scientific method. It is included here because it functions, in this period, as the closest equivalent to a documentary record: a physical trace left directly by the economic activity it describes.

Limitations: Bone evidence alone cannot describe social organization, land or resource ownership, ritual practice, or political structure, and, as the Monks and Orchard critique shows, it does not unambiguously establish sedentism or storage behavior either; skilled researchers examining the same assemblage have reached different conclusions about what it demonstrates. Conclusions about social hierarchy or ceremonial life at Namu during this early period must in any case rest on separate, and generally much later, lines of evidence, discussed in Section 10.8.

Section 10.6

Woodworking, Fiber, and Canoes

The forests that blanket the Northwest Coast supplied the material foundation for much of the region's technology. Western red cedar in particular proved exceptionally valuable: its wood splits cleanly along the grain, resists rot, and can be worked with stone, bone, and, later, metal tools into planks, boxes, canoes, and house timbers, while its bark can be processed into cordage, matting, clothing, and basketry. Stone adzes, wedges, and mauls, tools that survive comparatively well in the archaeological record, provide indirect evidence for this woodworking tradition even where the wooden objects they produced have long since decayed.

This point about survival deserves emphasis, because it shapes how historians must read the archaeological record for most of the Northwest Coast's history. Wood, bark, and other plant-fiber materials generally decompose rapidly in the region's ordinary, biologically active, aerated soils, meaning that for most sites and most periods, only the stone and bone tools used to work these materials survive, not the finished wooden objects themselves. Archaeologists must therefore reconstruct an enormous share of the region's material culture, canoes, houses, boxes, baskets, mats, and clothing, indirectly, through tool evidence, through occasional impressions left in other materials, and through the exceptional cases, examined in Section 10.11, where unusual burial conditions preserved organic material directly. Watercraft deserve particular attention in this context: oceangoing canoes, essential for fishing, transport, trade, and travel across this island-studded coast, are almost never preserved archaeologically, meaning that historians must infer their early existence and importance largely from the successful settlement of offshore locations such as Haida Gwaii, which could not have been reached without capable watercraft.

Section 10.7

Plank Houses and Village Life

By the era of sustained European contact, large cedar-plank houses had long been a defining feature of Northwest Coast settlement, but the archaeological record now indicates that substantial plank-house architecture developed considerably earlier than a simple "ancient origins, late elaboration" story might suggest. Excavations at the Scowlitz site, in the Fraser River lowlands of the Salish Sea region, have documented substantial plank-house structures dating to approximately 2,600 years ago, and archaeological synthesis across the broader Salish Sea region indicates that large houses and multihouse villages had become widespread by roughly 2,000 years ago. These structures, framed with heavy timber posts and beams and sheathed with split cedar planks, could be substantial in size and were historically documented as housing multiple related families under a single roof, organized around a household head or heads with recognized authority over the house's members, resources, and ceremonial responsibilities.

Archaeological and ethnographic research on Northwest Coast plank houses emphasizes that these structures functioned simultaneously as communal living spaces and as physical expressions of social hierarchy. Interior space, hearth arrangement, storage areas, and sleeping and working areas could be organized to reflect the relative rank of different families or individuals within the household, while the house as a whole represented a corporate economic and ceremonial unit, holding rights to specific fishing locations, hunting territories, and ceremonial privileges that were inherited and defended across generations.

It remains important, however, to distinguish the deep antiquity of permanent, storage-based settlement, which some archaeologists date, in a debated interpretation discussed in Section 10.5, to sites like Namu by roughly seven thousand years ago, from the specific, substantial plank-house architecture documented at Scowlitz and across the Salish Sea by roughly 2,600 to 2,000 years ago, and from the still more elaborate plank-house village system, monumental art, and social institutions described in far greater detail in historical and ethnographic sources from the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries onward. Early permanent villages, wherever and whenever they first developed, likely used a range of structures that need not have matched the specific architectural form, scale, and social function of the later, well-documented plank houses. This chapter treats large-scale plank-house architecture, and the elaborate house-based social system historically associated with it, as a later, cumulative development that emerged roughly two to three thousand years ago, several thousand years after early sedentism and storage economies are first proposed to have developed, and further still from the final, most richly documented form recorded by outside observers after the eighteenth century. Sedentism, storage, house architecture, and social hierarchy therefore represent at least three, and arguably four, historically distinct developments, each with its own chronology, rather than a single feature that appeared all at once.

Section 10.8

Wealth, Rank, and Social Differentiation

One of the most historically significant, and most carefully debated, questions in Northwest Coast archaeology concerns when hereditary social rank and marked wealth differentiation actually emerged. The historically documented Northwest Coast, described in detail by European and American observers from the eighteenth century onward and studied intensively by anthropologists in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, featured elaborate systems of hereditary rank, in which chiefly families held recognized titles, ceremonial privileges, and rights to specific resource locations, publicly affirmed and renegotiated through ceremonial institutions such as the potlatch.

Archaeologists cannot simply assume that this historically documented system existed, in the same form, throughout the entire span of coastal settlement traced earlier in this chapter. Instead, they look for specific kinds of evidence that might indicate developing social differentiation at earlier periods: differences in grave goods and burial treatment between individuals, evidence of specialized craft production controlled by particular households, unequal house size or interior organization, and physical evidence such as labrets, lip ornaments historically associated in some Northwest Coast societies with individuals of high status, whose distribution and use can be tracked archaeologically over time.

Research in the Salish Sea region, examining the social relationships reflected in the distribution and style of traditional labrets, suggests that meaningful status differentiation was developing before the later, well-known Marpole cultural period and became more clearly entrenched during a span running roughly from about 2,400 to 1,000 years before the present. This chronology places the clear archaeological emergence of hierarchical social distinction considerably later than the early storage economy proposed for Namu, reinforcing the point made in Section 10.5: sedentism and storage capacity, wherever and whenever they first developed, did not automatically or immediately produce social hierarchy. Rank-based social systems appear to have developed gradually, over an extended period, and, importantly, this development was not uniform across the entire coast; different regions show different timing and different specific expressions of social differentiation, a point developed further in Section 10.10.

Section 10.9

Mobility Within a Sedentary World

A common misconception treats "sedentary" and "mobile" as opposite, mutually exclusive categories, with settled village life on one side and nomadic movement on the other. Northwest Coast history shows why this framing fails to capture how coastal economies actually worked. Communities with substantial, long-occupied winter villages nonetheless depended on a seasonal round of movement to exploit resources that were only available, in useful quantities, at particular places and particular times of year: spring herring spawning grounds, summer and fall salmon-fishing stations along particular rivers, shellfish beds accessible at certain tides, upland hunting grounds, and berry-gathering areas in the mountains or forest interior.

Ethnographic and archaeological research indicates that entire households, or significant portions of a household's members, might relocate to seasonal camps for weeks or months at a time, traveling by canoe along the coast's protected waterways, before returning to the permanent winter village where stored food, substantial housing, and much of the year's ceremonial and social life were concentrated. In some historically documented traditions, this seasonal movement extended even to architecture itself: research on traditional Northwest Coast building practices describes house planks, among the most labor-intensive and valuable parts of a house structure, being removed from a permanent house frame and transported by canoe to a seasonal camp, where they could be used to construct temporary shelter before being returned to the winter village. This practice illustrates vividly how thoroughly Northwest Coast societies integrated permanence and mobility into a single, coherent economic strategy, rather than treating them as separate or contradictory ways of life.

Section 10.10

Regional Northwest Coast Worlds

Because environmental conditions and historical trajectories varied considerably across the Northwest Coast, this section briefly compares several sub-regions to illustrate the internal diversity that a single generic label can obscure.

Haida Gwaii and the northern coast combine offshore island settlement, exceptionally early documented coastal occupation at sites such as Kilgii Gwaay, and later historical development of elaborate monumental woodcarving and house-front art among Haida and neighboring peoples. The central British Columbia coast, home to Heiltsuk and neighboring peoples, contains some of the strongest, though actively debated, evidence for very early sedentism and storage economies, proposed for sites such as Namu, alongside long-term shell-midden sequences documenting sustained use of both herring and salmon fisheries. The Salish Sea and Fraser River system, a large, sheltered inland sea fed by one of the coast's most productive salmon rivers, shows some of the clearest archaeological evidence for the gradual emergence of hereditary status distinctions during the last several thousand years, alongside dense, long-term settlement supported by the Fraser's exceptional salmon runs. Western Vancouver Island, facing the open Pacific, developed distinctive maritime technologies suited to more exposed waters, including, among Nuu-chah-nulth communities, specialized whale-hunting traditions documented in the historical and ethnographic record. The Olympic Peninsula and lower Columbia River region, encompassing Makah, Quileute, Chinookan, and other communities, combined open-coast marine resources, including whale and fur seal hunting in some areas, with access to one of the continent's largest river systems, and provides, at Ozette, the chapter's most vivid archaeological window into everyday household life.

These regional differences matter historically because they demonstrate that the Northwest Coast's shared environmental advantages did not produce a single uniform society. Instead, each region's particular combination of marine geography, river systems, and resource mix shaped a somewhat different historical trajectory, even as neighboring peoples traded, intermarried, and exchanged technology and ceremonial practices across regional boundaries.

Section 10.11

Ozette and the Archaeology of Everyday Life

No single site illustrates the preservation problem introduced in Section 10.6 more dramatically than Ozette, a Makah village on the outer coast of the Olympic Peninsula. Approximately five hundred years ago, a mudslide overran part of the village, burying several houses beneath a thick, waterlogged, oxygen-poor deposit of mud and debris. This burial, catastrophic for the households involved at the time, had the unintended long-term effect of sealing organic materials away from the oxygen and microbial activity that ordinarily destroy wood, fiber, and other perishable substances within a matter of decades.

When archaeologists, working closely with the Makah Tribe, excavated the site beginning in the 1970s, they recovered tens of thousands of wooden, fiber, and other artifacts that simply do not survive at almost any other Northwest Coast site: complete house planks and structural timbers, woven baskets and mats, wooden boxes, fishing gear, canoe paddles, textiles, and carved and painted objects representing artistic traditions that would otherwise be known only through much later, and far less direct, evidence. The site therefore offers an extraordinarily detailed picture of household technology and daily life shortly before the era of sustained European maritime contact.

Ozette is also historically significant for the close relationship it demonstrates between archaeological method and Indigenous oral tradition. Makah oral history had preserved knowledge of a destructive slide that buried part of a village long ago; archaeological excavation confirmed and helped date this remembered event, providing a clear example of the kind of corroboration between oral tradition and archaeological evidence discussed as a general methodological principle in Chapter 1. The excavation was also conducted as a collaborative project between archaeologists and the Makah Tribe, whose direct descent from and continuing connection to the site shaped both the excavation itself and the long-term care and interpretation of the recovered materials, now held largely at the Makah Cultural and Research Center.

Historical Method

What Wood and Fiber Hide From Ordinary Archaeology

Compare an ordinary Northwest Coast shell midden to a waterlogged, mud-sealed site like Ozette. At an ordinary midden, archaeologists typically recover shell, bone, stone tools, and occasionally fire-cracked rock, but almost never wood, basketry, cordage, or textiles, because these materials decay rapidly in the region's ordinary, aerated, biologically active soils. At Ozette, by contrast, the same categories of material survive in enormous quantity because the mudslide sealed them into waterlogged, oxygen-poor mud almost immediately.

This contrast should prompt a specific historical caution: if archaeologists relied only on ordinary sites, they would likely conclude that Northwest Coast material culture consisted mainly of stone tools and shell debris, and would have little direct evidence for the elaborate woodworking, weaving, and carving traditions that Ozette, along with historical and ethnographic documentation, show were actually central to the region's technology and art. Historians must therefore treat the ordinary archaeological record as systematically biased toward durable materials, and must use exceptional sites, and corroborating oral tradition, to correct for what that bias otherwise hides.

Section 10.12

Long-Term Development Without a Single "Northwest Coast Package"

Older popular and even some earlier scholarly accounts of the Northwest Coast sometimes described its characteristic features, salmon storage, sedentism, plank houses, hereditary rank, specialized whale hunting, elaborate monumental art, and ceremonial institutions such as the potlatch, as though they formed a single cultural package that appeared together and remained essentially unchanged for a very long span of time. The evidence surveyed in this chapter does not support that picture, even accounting for the genuine scholarly debate over the earliest dates involved. Storage-based sedentism may be established at Namu by roughly seven thousand years ago, on the interpretation favored by Cannon and Yang, though Monks and Orchard's critique shows this date should not be treated as settled fact. Substantial plank-house architecture is more securely documented at Scowlitz and across the Salish Sea by roughly 2,600 to 2,000 years ago, considerably later than Namu's proposed early village, even on the most generous reading of that debated evidence. Clear archaeological evidence for developing hereditary status distinction in the Salish Sea region appears across a similar span, becoming more entrenched within roughly the last two and a half thousand years. The specific, richly documented ceremonial system historically recorded across the region, including the potlatch itself, along with mature monumental art styles and specialized economic practices, represents a still later elaboration, resting on a deeper archaeological record of feasting, mortuary ritual, and inherited status, built upon these earlier foundations. Ozette, dating to only around five hundred years ago, captures this developed system shortly before the era of sustained European contact, not at its origin.

The historically responsible conclusion, and the argument this chapter has built toward throughout, is that Northwest Coast societies developed their characteristic features cumulatively, unevenly, and regionally, over a period spanning many thousands of years, rather than receiving them as a single completed inheritance at some early moment. Different components, storage technology, permanent settlement, woodworking sophistication, social hierarchy, and specialized ceremonial and economic institutions, emerged at different times, in different places, in response to specific historical and environmental circumstances. Treating the well-documented eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Northwest Coast as though it had always existed in that exact form erases this long, genuinely historical process of development, experimentation, and regional adaptation.

Historical Debate

Did Salmon Create Northwest Coast Complexity?

The question: Does the abundance and predictability of Pacific salmon runs provide the primary explanation for the Northwest Coast's development of storage economies, sedentism, and eventually social hierarchy, or does this explanation overstate the role of a single resource in a much more complicated historical process?

Interpretation A — Salmon abundance as the primary driver: Some researchers emphasize that the concentrated, predictable, and storable nature of salmon runs provided an unusually reliable economic foundation, allowing communities to accumulate surplus, remain settled for much of the year, and eventually support the specialized labor, ceremonial life, and social distinctions documented in the historical record. On this view, salmon's particular biological and ecological characteristics go a long way toward explaining why complex, sedentary societies developed here without dependence on domesticated-crop agriculture.
Interpretation B — A more complicated, multi-resource and historically variable process: Other researchers caution against a single-resource explanation. They point to sustained, long-term reliance on herring alongside salmon, to considerable local variation in which resources mattered most, to woodworking, storage technology, watercraft, and social organization as necessary complementary developments, and, increasingly, to evidence that salmon runs themselves varied significantly over time rather than providing a constant, unchanging surplus. A study of Salish Sea fish assemblages published in 2026 documents meaningful variability in salmon abundance across both time and location, suggesting that coastal communities had to develop institutions capable of managing an unpredictable, not merely a generous, resource.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of zooarchaeological, ancient DNA, and shell-midden evidence discussed throughout this chapter, differing chiefly in how much explanatory weight to give salmon relative to other resources, technologies, and historical contingencies.

Why it matters: The choice between these interpretations affects how historians explain the uneven regional and chronological pattern documented in this chapter: if salmon alone explained social development, all major salmon-rich regions might be expected to show the same trajectory at roughly the same time, which the archaeological record does not clearly show.

Student task: Using evidence from Sections 10.4, 10.5, and 10.8, construct an argument for which interpretation better fits the pattern of when and where storage economies and social differentiation actually developed on the Northwest Coast.

Multiple Perspectives

Living With a Rich, Uneven Coast

Note: the perspectives below are reconstructed analytical viewpoints based on archaeological and ethnographic patterns, not quotations from named individuals.

From the perspective of a household head managing a winter village: Success depended on accurately anticipating which runs, spawns, and gathering grounds would be productive in a given year, coordinating household labor for intense, short harvesting periods, and ensuring stored food lasted through the winter, while also maintaining the household's recognized rights to particular fishing and gathering locations.
From the perspective of an archaeologist excavating a shell midden: The site offers a long, detailed, but incomplete record: shell, bone, and stone tools survive in abundance, while wood, fiber, and other organic technology are almost entirely absent except under unusual preservation conditions, requiring constant caution against mistaking a durable-materials bias for the full picture of ancient technology.
From the perspective of a Makah community member connected to Ozette: The site represents both a documented historical tragedy, a slide that buried part of an ancestral village, and an invaluable, carefully stewarded record of ancestral technology and daily life, interpreted in partnership with, rather than apart from, the descendant community.
From the perspective of a member of a household without high hereditary rank: Life within a plank-house society involved real, structured inequality in status, resource rights, and ceremonial privilege, even as most members of a household shared in its stored food, its labor, and its collective identity and defense of shared resources.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From First Villages to Ozette

What Changed

  • Settlement moved from early coastal camps toward substantial, long-occupied storage-based villages, with some archaeologists dating this development at Namu to roughly seven thousand years ago, though the timing and even the sedentary character of the earliest such villages remain debated
  • Social organization developed from patterns archaeologists cannot yet clearly distinguish in the earliest period toward increasingly documented hereditary status differentiation after roughly 2,400 years ago
  • Architecture developed toward the large, elaborately organized cedar-plank houses first securely documented at sites such as Scowlitz by roughly 2,600 years ago and widespread by roughly 2,000 years ago, well before the still more elaborate versions recorded in the later historical record
  • Ceremonial and artistic traditions became increasingly elaborate over time: archaeological evidence documents increasingly prominent feasting, mortuary ritual, wealth display, and inherited-status practices in the last two to three thousand years, and monumental woodcarving and the historically and ethnographically documented potlatch itself are clearly attested by the era of sustained European contact

What Stayed the Same

  • Dependence on a combination of marine and terrestrial resources organized around a detailed seasonal round remained constant throughout the sequence
  • Canoe travel remained the essential means of connecting permanent settlements to seasonal resource locations across the entire period
  • Cedar woodworking, even where its products do not survive archaeologically, appears to have remained central to technology throughout most of the sequence
  • The combination of substantial permanence with continued seasonal mobility remained a defining feature from the earliest storage villages through the era of European contact

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the deep continuity of a resource-based seasonal economy and the very real, gradual development of storage, permanence, architecture, and social hierarchy prevents treating the historically documented Northwest Coast as either a timeless, unchanging tradition or a sudden late invention.

Cause and Effect

Storage and Its Consequences

Long-term causes

The predictable, if variable, seasonal availability of salmon, herring, and other resources at specific, defensible locations along a coast well suited to canoe travel.

Short-term causes

The development of effective drying, smoking, and storage technology capable of converting a brief, intense harvest into food usable across many months.

Effect

Substantially sedentary, storage-dependent village life, proposed by some archaeologists for Namu by roughly seven thousand years ago, though this specific date remains debated, that in any case allowed larger, more stable populations to remain settled through the winter months.

Longer-term consequence

Over subsequent millennia, sustained storage-based sedentism created conditions, in some regions, under which control over particularly productive fishing and gathering locations could become a basis for inherited status, contributing to the gradual emergence of social differentiation documented especially clearly in the Salish Sea region after roughly 2,400 years ago.

Historical complexity: The link between storage capacity and social hierarchy was not immediate, automatic, or uniform. Several thousand years separate the earliest clear evidence of storage-based sedentism from the earliest clear evidence of developing hereditary rank, and different regions moved through this process at different speeds and in different specific ways.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Winter in a Plank House

Consider a composite, illustrative reconstruction of household life in a later Northwest Coast winter village, drawn from archaeological and historical evidence rather than describing any single documented household. With the intense labor of the salmon and herring seasons behind them, household members would spend much of the winter working with stored food and materials gathered earlier in the year: preparing and consuming dried and smoked fish, repairing and constructing wooden boxes, tools, and canoe parts, weaving baskets and mats from cedar bark and other plant fibers, and carrying out the ceremonial obligations, storytelling, and social gatherings, including, by the era documented in historical and ethnographic sources, events such as the potlatch, that affirmed names, rank, and relationships between households and communities.

Children would learn practical skills, including basketry, fishing technique, and woodworking, alongside oral traditions describing the origins, rights, and history of their own family and community, transmitted by elders through structured storytelling much like the oral historical systems discussed in Chapter 1. Households of higher recognized rank bore particular ceremonial responsibilities and enjoyed particular privileges, including recognized rights to especially productive fishing or gathering locations, while all members of a household, regardless of individual status, depended on and contributed to its shared stores of food and materials. As always, historians must remember that this reconstruction describes a general pattern documented across multiple communities and periods rather than a specific, individually attested household.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the ancient Northwest Coast, the temperate, deeply indented coastline running from northwestern California through Oregon, Washington, British Columbia, Haida Gwaii, and southeastern Alaska, as a region where Indigenous communities built exceptionally productive, storage-based, substantially sedentary societies without depending on domesticated-crop agriculture, while in some places, such as Katzie territory, also practicing deliberate cultivation of specific plants. It has emphasized throughout that the region's later, famous features, monumental plank houses, hereditary rank, specialized whale hunting, and elaborate ceremonial institutions, developed cumulatively over many thousands of years rather than appearing together as a single, ancient, unchanging cultural package.

The chapter began with the deep antiquity of coastal occupation, documented at sites such as Kilgii Gwaay on Haida Gwaii, extending back more than ten thousand years, while emphasizing that changing relative sea levels, driven by the interacting processes of glacial-isostatic rebound and global sea-level rise, have unevenly preserved, submerged, or destroyed the ancient shorelines where the earliest evidence would otherwise be found.

It then examined the resource base underlying coastal life, insisting that salmon, while historically significant, cannot alone explain the region's economic and social development. Herring, shellfish, sea mammals, and terrestrial resources all played substantial roles, with their relative importance varying considerably from one location to another, as demonstrated by long-term vibracore sampling of shell middens on the central British Columbia coast.

The chapter examined salmon storage and resulting sedentism as an early, actively debated development, using ancient DNA evidence from Namu, interpreted by Cannon and Yang as indicating substantially sedentary, storage-dependent village life extending back roughly seven thousand years, and challenged by Monks and Orchard on methodological grounds. It also introduced the recently documented Katzie wapato garden as evidence that deliberate plant cultivation formed part of some Northwest Coast economies well before European contact. The chapter then traced woodworking, fiber technology, and watercraft as essential but archaeologically elusive components of the coastal economy, explaining that the region's ordinary, aerated, biologically active soils generally destroy organic materials, leaving historians dependent on indirect evidence, such as stone tools, except at rare, exceptionally preserved, waterlogged sites.

Plank houses were examined as economic, social, and ceremonial institutions rather than mere architecture, with substantial plank-house construction securely documented at Scowlitz by roughly 2,600 years ago and widespread across the Salish Sea by roughly 2,000 years ago, several thousand years after early sedentism and storage are proposed to have developed and well before the still more elaborate historically documented village system. The chapter then addressed the emergence of hereditary rank and wealth differentiation directly, using Salish Sea evidence on status differentiation and labret distribution to show that hierarchical social organization developed gradually, becoming more clearly entrenched within roughly the last two and a half thousand years, on a similar timescale to plank-house architecture and considerably later than the early storage economy proposed for Namu.

The chapter emphasized that permanent settlement did not eliminate seasonal mobility; households moved by canoe to exploit resources available only at specific places and times, in some documented cases even transporting removable house planks between permanent and seasonal locations. A regional comparison across Haida Gwaii, the central coast, the Salish Sea, western Vancouver Island, and the Olympic Peninsula and lower Columbia demonstrated that no single, uniform Northwest Coast society ever existed, and that different sub-regions followed distinct historical trajectories shaped by their particular environmental conditions.

Finally, the chapter examined Ozette, a Makah village site partially buried by a mudslide roughly five hundred years ago, as an exceptional case of archaeological preservation that reveals a wealth of ordinarily invisible wood, fiber, and other organic technology, while illustrating the value of collaboration between archaeological method and Indigenous oral tradition. The chapter's concluding argument rejected the notion of a single, ancient "Northwest Coast package," insisting instead on a historically accurate picture of uneven, cumulative, regionally varied development across many thousands of years.

These themes prepare students for Chapter 11, which turns north and inland into the Arctic and Subarctic, where seasonality, sea ice, boreal forest, caribou, marine mammals, and extreme climatic variability produced different, though comparably sophisticated, solutions to the shared historical problem of building durable societies in demanding northern environments.

Key Terms

Northwest Coast
An analytical cultural and archaeological region extending from northwestern California through southeastern Alaska, defined by temperate rainforest, an indented coastline, and marine and riverine economies, not by a single Indigenous identity.
Complex hunter-gatherer
A society that achieves substantial population density, sedentism, storage, specialization, and often hereditary rank without depending on domesticated-crop agriculture, though it may still include limited, deliberate plant cultivation.
Wapato garden
An engineered wetland feature built to increase the yield of wapato, an edible aquatic tuber; documented in Katzie territory dating to roughly 3,800 years ago as evidence of deliberate Coast Salish plant cultivation.
Scowlitz
A Fraser River lowlands site providing some of the earliest secure evidence, dating to roughly 2,600 years ago, for substantial Northwest Coast plank-house architecture.
Relative sea-level change
The combined local effect of glacial-isostatic rebound and global sea-level rise, which raised some ancient shorelines above and submerged others below their original position.
Isostatic rebound
The gradual rise of land that had been depressed under the weight of glacial ice, occurring as the ice melted and its weight was removed.
Shell midden
An archaeological deposit formed from accumulated shellfish remains and other domestic refuse, providing long-term evidence of diet, settlement, and environmental change.
Vibracore sampling
A coring method used to extract long, undisturbed sediment or midden samples, allowing archaeologists to study deep, long-term deposits with minimal disturbance.
Storage economy
An economic system in which surplus food from a short, productive harvest is preserved for later use, enabling settlement through periods of scarcity.
Sedentism
The practice of remaining settled in one place, or a small number of places, for extended periods, rather than moving frequently.
Seasonal round
A recurring annual pattern of movement between a permanent settlement and various resource-specific locations, timed to the availability of different foods.
Plank house
A large, timber-framed, cedar-plank-sheathed house historically documented as home to multiple related families and organized to reflect household rank and function.
Ancient DNA
Genetic material recovered from archaeological remains, used to identify species, populations, or relationships not always distinguishable through physical examination alone.
Labret
A lip ornament, historically associated in some Northwest Coast societies with individuals of recognized high status, whose archaeological distribution can indicate developing social differentiation.
Status differentiation
The archaeological or historical evidence of unequal social rank, wealth, or privilege among members of a society.
Adze
A woodworking tool with a blade set perpendicular to its handle, used for shaping planks, canoes, and other wooden objects; a durable proxy for otherwise unpreserved woodworking activity.
Waterlogged site
An archaeological site where oxygen-poor, water-saturated burial conditions preserve organic materials, such as wood and fiber, that would otherwise decay.
Preservation bias
The systematic tendency of archaeological evidence to overrepresent durable materials, such as stone and shell, and underrepresent perishable materials, such as wood and fiber.
Potlatch
A ceremonial institution, historically documented among many Northwest Coast peoples, involving hosted gatherings and the distribution of wealth connected to the affirmation of names, rank, and social relationships.
Winter village
A community's principal, most substantially built settlement, occupied through the winter months when stored food and indoor household activity were most important.
Resource ownership
Recognized, often hereditary, rights held by a household or lineage over specific productive locations, such as fishing sites or gathering grounds.
Marine productivity
The overall biological output of marine ecosystems, which on the Northwest Coast was substantial but concentrated in specific places and seasons rather than uniformly distributed.
Cultural region
An analytical category grouping peoples by shared environment and broadly similar economic and cultural patterns, used with the same cautions introduced in Chapter 1.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that some of the most celebrated features of Indigenous North American history, the great cedar-plank houses, the monumental carved art, the elaborate ceremonial systems of the historic Northwest Coast, were not gifts of an unusually generous nature but the cumulative product of many thousands of years of technological development, social experimentation, and regionally specific adaptation. The people who built this history did not depend on domesticated-crop agriculture, though, as the Katzie wapato garden shows, some communities did practice deliberate plant cultivation, and they built some of the most densely settled, materially rich, and socially elaborate societies documented anywhere in the world without ever depending on field-crop farming.

The chapter's insistence on chronology and regional variation matters beyond this single case. It offers students a model for resisting the temptation, common in popular history, to compress a long, uneven historical process into a single origin story or a single explanatory cause. The unresolved scholarly debate over Namu's proposed seven-thousand-year-old storage economy, the more securely dated appearance of substantial plank houses at Scowlitz some two to three thousand years later, the Salish Sea's evidence of status differentiation, and Ozette's late but extraordinarily detailed preservation together demonstrate that even a single, well-studied region can contain multiple, distinct historical stories unfolding at different paces, some of them still actively contested among specialists.

This chapter also reinforces a methodological lesson introduced in Chapter 1 and echoed throughout Volume I: the surviving evidence is never a neutral, complete window onto the past. Preservation conditions, whether the general absence of organic material at most sites or the extraordinary preservation at Ozette, shape what historians can and cannot know, and responsible historical writing must say so explicitly rather than mistaking an artifact of preservation for a fact about the past.

Finally, the chapter's collaborative relationship between archaeology and Indigenous oral tradition and community stewardship, exemplified at Ozette, models the ethical and interpretive commitments introduced in Chapter 1's discussion of authority, evidence, and the ongoing relationship between descendant Indigenous nations and the archaeological study of their own ancestral history. The Northwest Coast's ancient history belongs, in the fullest sense, to the Haida, Heiltsuk, Nuu-chah-nulth, Coast Salish, Makah, and other nations whose ancestors built it and whose descendants continue to live on this coast today.
