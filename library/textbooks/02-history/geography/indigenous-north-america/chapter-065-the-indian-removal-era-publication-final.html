The Indian Removal Era

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

In the spring of 1830 the United States Congress debated, for weeks, whether the nation that had signed treaties with the Cherokee, Muscogee, Choctaw, and Chickasaw peoples could lawfully and honorably press them to leave their homelands. The House approved the bill by a narrow margin, while the Senate supported it more decisively. How a republic that recognized Indigenous nations through treaties came to build a federal program of displacement is the subject of this chapter.

The Senate vote on the removal bill came on April 24, 1830, and the House vote followed on May 26. The bill carried the Senate by 28 to 19 and the House by 102 to 97. President Andrew Jackson signed it on May 28. Those margins matter. Congress did not treat the measure as an obvious or consensual expression of national purpose. Senators and representatives spoke for days. Petitions poured in from across the northern states, many organized by missionary societies and by women who had no vote. Delegations from Indigenous nations came to Washington to testify, in effect, against the premise on which the bill rested.

The debate exposed a contradiction that had been growing since the 1780s. The United States Constitution and a long series of treaties treated Indigenous peoples as distinct political communities with their own governments and territories. Yet the same federal government, pressed by states, settlers, land speculators, and slaveholding planters, repeatedly obtained those territories through the very treaties that acknowledged Indigenous authority. By 1830 the contradiction had become acute, because the nations most affected, notably the Cherokee, had adopted written laws, schools, newspapers, and constitutional governments of exactly the kind American policymakers had urged on them for a generation.

This chapter is a history of the construction of policy. It asks how removal developed from scattered proposals and local treaty exchanges into a legally authorized, administratively supported, and politically contested federal program. It therefore concentrates on ideology, economic interest, constitutional argument, statute, administration, and the negotiating methods through which agreements were obtained. It does not narrate the forced marches, camps, disease, and deaths that followed; those belong to Chapter 66, and the Seminole resistance belongs to Chapter 67. The Cherokee political reforms that made the Cherokee case so politically and legally charged are treated in Chapter 64, and are referenced here only where needed.

The chapter's argument has four parts. First, removal was not the invention of one president. Its roots lie in the treaty system, in Jefferson-era land policy, in the Georgia Compact of 1802, and in the military victories of the War of 1812 era. Second, removal was driven by overlapping interests, including cotton agriculture and the expansion of slavery, state ambitions, speculation, and a sustained ideology of racial hierarchy and paternalist "civilization." Third, removal was contested, in Congress, in the press, in the courts, and above all among Indigenous nations themselves, which did not respond uniformly. Fourth, the Indian Removal Act of 1830 authorized a system of land exchange, but the coercive character of many resulting agreements came from state pressure, executive negotiation, and unequal bargaining conditions rather than from explicit statutory commands.

Historians reconstruct this history from an unusually rich documentary record, which includes statutes and treaty texts, congressional debates and reports, War Department correspondence, state legislative acts, court opinions, newspapers, missionary papers, and the petitions and letters of Indigenous leaders. This record is also unusually one-sided in some respects. Federal and state officials produced most of it, and treaty councils were often recorded by the very negotiators seeking land. This chapter therefore identifies, wherever possible, whether a claim rests on a statute, a treaty text, an executive message, a court opinion, an Indigenous petition, or later historical interpretation.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the treaty system both recognized Indigenous political authority and served as the principal instrument of land cession.
  2. Trace the development of removal proposals from the Jefferson administration through the Monroe administration and distinguish them from the statutory program of 1830.
  3. Analyze the economic foundations of removal, including cotton, slavery, land speculation, state revenue, and settler demand, without reducing removal to a single cause.
  4. Assess the role of racial ideology, "civilization" policy, and paternalist argument in justifying dispossession.
  5. Compare the political strategies of the Cherokee, Muscogee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Seminole, and selected northern nations as they confronted removal.
  6. Evaluate the roles of state governments, Andrew Jackson, Congress, missionaries, and public opinion in shaping removal policy.
  7. Explain the constitutional issues of treaty power, federal authority over Indian affairs, and state jurisdictional claims.
  8. Describe the holding and the limits of Johnson v. M'Intosh, Cherokee Nation v. Georgia, and Worcester v. Georgia.
  9. Distinguish what the Indian Removal Act of 1830 formally authorized from the practices later used to obtain removal treaties.
  10. Interpret primary sources, including statutes, executive messages, Indigenous statements, and court opinions, with attention to authorship, purpose, and limitation.
  11. Evaluate historians' debate over whether the 1830 Act was a rupture or an intensification of earlier policy.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How could a government that negotiated treaties with Indigenous nations as political equals in form also pursue policies designed to displace them?
  2. Why did removal advocates insist that treaties and "voluntary" exchanges were the proper means of policy, and what did that insistence conceal?
  3. What roles did cotton, slavery, and land speculation play in the demand for Indigenous lands, and what other motives operated alongside them?
  4. Why did Indigenous adoption of schools, agriculture, and written law fail to protect nations from removal demands?
  5. Why did Indigenous nations differ in their responses to removal, and why should historians avoid treating those differences as simple division or weakness?
  6. Was the Indian Removal Act a break with earlier policy or a codification and intensification of it?
  7. What could courts accomplish when state governments and the executive branch disagreed with their rulings?
  8. How should historians evaluate the "consent" recorded in removal treaties?

Historical Context

When the United States won independence in 1783, the lands between the Appalachians and the Mississippi were not an empty frontier awaiting settlement. They were the homelands of the Cherokee, Muscogee (Creek), Choctaw, and Chickasaw peoples in the South, the Shawnee, Miami, Wyandot, Potawatomi, Ottawa, Ojibwe, and many others in the Old Northwest, and Seminole communities taking shape in Spanish Florida. Several of these nations were powerful, populous, and diplomatically experienced, and the new republic initially dealt with them cautiously, as the Cherokee and others held military leverage and European powers still contested the continent. The Constitution assigned relations with Indigenous nations to the federal government, and federal treaties in the 1780s and 1790s defined boundaries and promised protection of Indigenous lands from settlers and states.

The War of 1812 changed the strategic landscape. The collapse of Tecumseh's northern confederacy (Chapter 62 and Chapter 63), the defeat of the Red Stick Creeks, and the end of British support for Indigenous resistance south of the Great Lakes sharply reduced the diplomatic room of Indigenous nations. After 1815, Indigenous nations could no longer so readily play European empires against one another. At the same time, the southern interior was being reshaped by the spread of short-staple cotton, which made the fertile uplands and river valleys of Georgia, Alabama, and Mississippi enormously valuable, and by a domestic slave trade that moved enslaved people toward the expanding cotton districts.

During the same years, southeastern Indigenous nations were not static. The Cherokee in particular developed a written syllabary, a national newspaper, a bicameral council, and in 1827 a written constitution, as Chapter 64 explains. Other nations also built centralized councils, schools, plantations, and market connections, and some wealthy Indigenous families held enslaved people. These changes were choices made by Indigenous political communities. They also created the central irony of the removal era, since the political and economic transformation that federal "civilization" policy had encouraged did not prevent demands for land.

Students should keep the nature of the evidence in view. Treaty texts are legal documents but reflect the drafting and translation practices of federal negotiators; congressional debates reveal arguments but not the full pressures behind them; and many Indigenous voices survive through petitions, newspapers, and letters, often translated or edited. Wherever possible this chapter identifies which kind of source supports each claim.

Division I — The Origins of Federal Removal Policy · Section 65.1

Indigenous Sovereignty and the Expanding American Republic

In 1800 the political geography of the southeastern interior was Indigenous. The Cherokee held mountain and piedmont country across what are now parts of Georgia, Tennessee, Alabama, and the Carolinas. The Muscogee confederacy, made up of many towns divided broadly into Upper and Lower Creek districts, controlled a large territory across Georgia and Alabama. The Choctaw lived in central and southern Mississippi, and the Chickasaw in the north of that state and neighboring areas. Each nation had governing institutions, whether town councils, clan-based systems of law, national councils, or combinations of them, and each negotiated with the United States, with neighboring Indigenous nations, and, until 1821, with Spanish Florida.

The United States claimed several kinds of authority over these peoples, and these claims were not identical. It claimed sovereignty over the territory within its recognized international boundaries. It claimed the exclusive right, as against states and private citizens, to purchase Indigenous land and to regulate trade. And it claimed, in various ways, a protective relationship. None of these claims, in the early republic, was understood to erase Indigenous self-government. Federal officials used treaties, a form of agreement associated with relations between political communities, precisely because Indigenous nations controlled land and had to consent, formally, to its transfer.

The relationship was also one of enormous and growing imbalance. The non-Indigenous population of the United States grew rapidly, new states entered the Union, and settlers moved onto lands that treaties had reserved to Indigenous nations. The federal government sometimes tried to restrain this movement, and in some periods used troops to remove intruders, but its commitment to enforcement was inconsistent. States in turn claimed jurisdiction over all persons and lands within their borders, including Indigenous territories. This clash of claims, federal, state, and Indigenous, is the setting in which removal policy took shape.

In the Old Northwest the pattern differed in detail but not in principle. Peoples such as the Shawnee, Wyandot, Miami, Potawatomi, and Ojibwe lived within regions the United States had organized as territories and states, while the Haudenosaunee remained in New York, where the Ogden Land Company and state officials pursued their own claims. Removal pressure was therefore never exclusively a southern matter, even though the southeastern cases received the most national attention.

Section 65.2

The Treaty System and the Contradictions of American Expansion

The treaty was the central instrument of United States Indian policy. A treaty negotiation typically involved federal commissioners, a council of Indigenous leaders, interpreters, and often the distribution of presents. The treaty text might define boundaries, cede land, establish annuities, guarantee protection, provide for schools or blacksmiths, and sometimes reserve land to individuals. Under the Constitution, treaties ratified by the Senate became part of the supreme law of the land, so the form carried real legal weight.

Yet the system contained a built-in tension. Treaties recognized that Indigenous nations had to consent to land cessions, and that recognition implied political authority. At the same time, nearly all treaty negotiations after 1790 in the southeastern and Old Northwest regions aimed at land acquisition, and the negotiating conditions were far from equal. The United States chose the time and place of councils, selected which leaders to recognize, controlled annuity payments, and could create pressure through trade debt, withdrawal of protection from intruders, or the threat of state action. Formal consent therefore coexisted with coercive circumstances. A signature on a treaty was evidence of agreement, but not necessarily of freely chosen agreement.

Federal land policy made use of these pressures. Thomas Jefferson, in a private letter of 1803 to William Henry Harrison, discussed how encouraging Indigenous leaders to incur debts at government trading houses could make them more willing to cede land to settle those debts. The letter is revealing precisely because it was private. It shows an official who publicly spoke of friendship and agricultural "improvement" while privately discussing the use of indebtedness to obtain territory. Annuities also created dependence, while land cessions provided the revenue and the settlement opportunities on which federal and state governments counted.

Another recurring problem was authority to sign. Indigenous political systems did not always vest land-cession authority in a single chief, and federal negotiators sometimes recognized leaders who lacked national backing, or acted with those willing to sign. The Treaty of Indian Springs in 1825 is a clear case. William McIntosh and a small group of Lower Creek leaders signed a cession of Creek land in Georgia in violation of Muscogee law that made unauthorized cession a capital offense. The Muscogee National Council executed McIntosh, and a new treaty in 1826 annulled the Indian Springs agreement, yet still ceded much land. The episode shows both the vulnerability of Indigenous governance to selective recognition and the strength of Indigenous legal norms.

Section 65.3

Early Removal Proposals: Jefferson, Monroe, and Federal Policy

The idea of relocating Indigenous peoples west of the Mississippi predates Andrew Jackson by decades. Jefferson's expansionist administration (1801–1809) pursued extensive land cessions, and the Louisiana Purchase of 1803 gave American planners a vast region west of the Mississippi that could, in theory, accommodate exchanges. Jefferson himself expressed several hopes that did not fit neatly together: that Indigenous nations would adopt farming and eventually merge with the American population, and that those unwilling to do so might move west. Legislation for the new Louisiana territory in 1804 contemplated exchanges of land with Indigenous nations east of the Mississippi. These measures show removal as one possible option in a flexible policy toolkit, not a settled program.

Between 1817 and 1819 the first significant exchange treaties with the Cherokee produced a western settlement in Arkansas of Cherokee who chose to leave, sometimes called the Western Cherokee or "Old Settlers." In 1820, at Doak's Stand, Andrew Jackson and Thomas Hinds negotiated a treaty in which the Choctaw ceded part of their Mississippi land in exchange for territory in the West. These agreements involved communities that actually relocated, though motives varied: some left to escape pressure, others to seek better hunting and farming land, and some, certainly, because settler encroachment made staying difficult. They also established precedents that later removal advocates would cite.

President James Monroe's administration moved closer to a general policy. In 1824 Secretary of War John C. Calhoun created an Office of Indian Affairs within the War Department, and on January 27, 1825, Monroe sent Congress a special message urging that Indigenous nations be encouraged to move west of the Mississippi to a region where they could be protected, proposing that the process be voluntary, in form, while framing the settlement of Indigenous peoples in the East as incompatible with state sovereignty. The message did not produce legislation, but it fixed removal as an explicit executive goal and gave later advocates a respectable pedigree.

It is important to distinguish these proposals from the 1830 statute. Before 1830, removal was a policy preference, pursued through individual treaties, not a general legal authority backed by a dedicated appropriation and an administrative program. The difference between proposal and program is central to this chapter's argument, and to the debate examined in the Historical Debate feature later in the chapter.

Historical Vocabulary
Removal and Exchange

Definition. "Removal" is the modern historians' term for the federal policy of relocating Indigenous nations from lands in the eastern United States to territory west of the Mississippi. Contemporaries used a range of terms, including "emigration," "migration," "exchange of lands," and "colonization."

Historical context. The 1830 statute's own title referred to an "exchange of lands" and to removal west of the Mississippi. Supporters preferred euphemisms such as "emigration" because they implied choice and benefit.

Why it matters. The vocabulary reveals the political argument. To describe a program as exchange was to present it as voluntary and reciprocal.

Potential misunderstanding. Students sometimes treat "removal" as meaning only the physical journey. In this chapter the term refers to the whole policy, including legal authority, pressure, and negotiation. The journeys themselves are the subject of Chapter 66.

Section 65.4

Military Conquest, Land Cessions, and the Precedents for Removal

War and treaty-making worked together. The Creek War of 1813–1814 began as a conflict within the Muscogee confederacy between the "Red Stick" faction, which sought to resist American encroachment and cultural change, and other Muscogee leaders who favored accommodation, and it widened when Red Stick forces attacked Fort Mims in Alabama in August 1813. Tennessee militia under Andrew Jackson, with Cherokee and Lower Creek allies, ultimately defeated the Red Sticks at Horseshoe Bend in March 1814. The Treaty of Fort Jackson in August 1814 then imposed a cession of roughly twenty-two to twenty-three million acres in present-day Alabama and Georgia. The treaty took land from the defeated faction and from Muscogee who had fought alongside the United States, a feature that shows how thoroughly territorial ambition, rather than punishment for hostility, drove the settlement.

In the Old Northwest, similar patterns emerged. Treaties such as the Treaty of Fort Wayne in 1809 had already provoked Indigenous resistance (Chapter 62). After the War of 1812, a series of treaties in the late 1810s, including those negotiated at the Maumee Rapids in 1817 and in the Michigan Territory under Governor Lewis Cass, obtained large cessions, often leaving small reserves for Indigenous communities that officials expected would shrink further. Cass, who would later serve as Jackson's Secretary of War, became an important intellectual advocate for removal in print and in policy.

Jackson himself was a central figure in these precedents. Between 1814 and 1824 he negotiated or presided over a series of cessions, including the Chickasaw cession of 1818 and the Choctaw treaty at Doak's Stand. He also led the 1818 campaign into Spanish Florida against Seminole and allied communities, discussed in Chapter 67. The result was a set of habits and assumptions about how land could be obtained, which included the combination of military victory, treaty pressure, and rhetoric of Indigenous benefit, that formed the practical background for the later program.

These precedents do not prove that removal was inevitable. Each treaty was the product of particular circumstances, and Indigenous leaders sometimes negotiated reserves, boundaries, or payments that mattered to their communities. But they established that large-scale cession was achievable, that military and diplomatic pressure were mutually reinforcing, and that the federal government would give priority to settler access over the continued protection of Indigenous homelands.

Division II — The Economic and Ideological Foundations of Removal · Section 65.5

Settler Expansion and the Demand for Indigenous Lands

The fundamental material pressure behind removal was settler demand. The populations of Alabama and Mississippi, for example, more than doubled between the censuses of 1820 and 1830, and Georgia's population also grew rapidly. Settlers sought farmland, and land speculators bought and sold tracts in anticipation of future cessions. The Land Act of 1820 lowered the minimum price of public land to $1.25 per acre and the minimum purchase to eighty acres, making smaller purchases possible while still requiring cash.

States played an active role in organizing settlement. Georgia distributed ceded Indigenous land to its citizens by lottery, repeatedly in the early nineteenth century, and used lotteries again in 1832 for Cherokee land that the state claimed even before it had been formally ceded. These practices mattered politically, because each lottery created thousands of holders of expectations whose interests depended on the further extinguishment of Indigenous title. The administration of Indigenous land as a state resource shaped state politics for a generation.

Settlers did not constitute a single interest. Large planters, small farmers, and speculators had different goals and resources. Some settlers trespassed on Indigenous land without any legal claim; others acted under state authority; others purchased at federal land sales. Federal officials, meanwhile, had revenue interests, since public land sales financed government operations, and strategic interests in securing borders. The diversity of interests complicates any single account, but they converged on one demand: that Indigenous title be extinguished and Indigenous governments be displaced.

Settler demand also operated through electoral politics. Southern and western states sent representatives and senators to Congress who treated removal as a constituency issue, and the growth of white male suffrage in many states increased the political weight of settlers and prospective settlers. In this way land hunger became a lever on federal policy, not merely a local phenomenon.

Section 65.6

Cotton, Slavery, and the Political Economy of Dispossession

The expansion of cotton agriculture gave the demand for land a particular economic character. The cotton gin, patented in 1793, made the processing of short-staple cotton profitable, and the upland and river soils of the Deep South suited that crop. By the 1820s cotton was the nation's leading export by value, tying southern land to Atlantic and British industrial markets. Plantation agriculture was organized around the labor of enslaved people, and a large domestic slave trade moved hundreds of thousands of enslaved people from the Upper South into the expanding cotton districts, a forced migration that accompanied, and in many places followed, the dispossession of Indigenous peoples.

Removal and slavery were therefore linked, but in several ways, not one. Cotton planters wanted fertile land, and enslaved labor was the means by which many intended to develop it. Slaveholders in the southern states also feared federal restraint of slavery's expansion, which made states' rights arguments politically attractive and gave jurisdictional claims over Indigenous land a wider ideological significance. Some historians emphasize slavery as the engine of removal, while others emphasize land speculation or state sovereignty. This chapter treats these factors as interacting rather than competing.

The picture is further complicated by the fact that slavery was present within some southeastern Indigenous nations. Wealthy Cherokee, Muscogee, Choctaw, and Chickasaw families held enslaved Black people, and in 1835 the Cherokee Nation, with roughly sixteen thousand citizens, included roughly fifteen hundred enslaved people. This fact did not protect those nations from removal demands, and it also means that the nations' economic and social change cannot be described without acknowledging the participation of some Indigenous elites in the plantation economy. Enslaved people, meanwhile, experienced removal as a separate and often devastating displacement, discussed in Chapter 66.

Cotton cannot explain removal alone. Removal also occurred in the Old Northwest, where cotton was not an option, and where the demands came from farmers, land companies, and state governments, and in some cases from the desire to open routes and canal lands. A responsible account treats cotton and slavery as a major force in the Southeast, joined by speculation, revenue, state sovereignty claims, racial ideology, and political calculation.

Section 65.7

Georgia, State Expansion, and the Contest over Territory

Georgia was the leading state advocate of removal, and its history reveals the interplay of federal commitment, state ambition, and Indigenous sovereignty. In the Compact of 1802, Georgia ceded to the federal government its claims to western lands that became Alabama and Mississippi. In return, the federal government agreed to extinguish Indian title to lands within Georgia "peaceably" and on reasonable terms, as soon as it could. Georgia's leaders came to treat this as a debt that the United States owed, and for decades they demanded its payment.

Conflict intensified in the 1820s. After the Indian Springs affair of 1825 (Section 65.2), Georgia Governor George Troup threatened to use state authority against federal efforts to protect the Creek, and President John Quincy Adams, faced with the prospect of a clash between state militia and federal troops, retreated. By 1827 the Muscogee had ceded their remaining land in Georgia. Georgia then turned to the Cherokee. When the Cherokee adopted their written constitution in 1827, Georgia's legislature responded with resolutions treating the Cherokee as tenants at will on state land and asserting the state's right to take possession.

In December 1828 Georgia passed legislation extending state authority over Cherokee territory within its claimed boundaries, to take effect in June 1830. This law purported to nullify Cherokee law and prohibited Cherokee council meetings except to cede land. A related development was the discovery of gold in northern Georgia in 1828, which brought thousands of prospectors onto Cherokee land and gave the state an economic stake in the territory. Georgia barred Cherokee from digging for gold on their own lands and created a state guard in 1830 to police the region. The Cherokee responded through petitions, the newspaper Cherokee Phoenix, delegations to Washington, and eventually litigation, as Section 65.19 explains.

Georgia's actions posed a direct constitutional question: could a state extend its laws over Indigenous territory within its borders, notwithstanding federal treaties? The state said yes, citing its sovereignty. The Cherokee said no, citing treaties and their own sovereignty. The answer would depend, in practice, less on legal argument than on the willingness of the executive branch to enforce federal law, and Jackson's election in 1828 made it clear how that willingness would be exercised.

Section 65.8

Race, Civilization, Paternalism, and the Ideology of Removal

Removal depended on an ideology that justified it to its supporters and to the broader public. One strand was racial. Many white Americans assumed a hierarchy of peoples in which Indigenous peoples, regarded as fundamentally different, were destined to give way before white settlement. This view appeared in popular writing, in political speech, and in the assumptions of officials who regarded Indigenous presence as an obstacle.

A second strand concerned land use. Removal advocates argued that Indigenous peoples, as hunters, had not "improved" the land and therefore had a weaker claim than farmers. The argument ignored the reality that southeastern Indigenous nations were already extensive farmers, with towns, fields, orchards, and livestock, and drew on older European theories of property that tied ownership to cultivation. It also showed the flexibility of the argument: when Indigenous people did farm, advocates shifted ground to political or racial objections.

A third strand was paternalist. Federal policy since the early republic had promoted "civilization" through the Civilization Fund Act of 1819, which appropriated annual funds for missionary and educational efforts. Some officials and missionaries sincerely believed that Indigenous nations needed protection from corrupting contact with settlers and could be preserved by relocation. Lewis Cass argued this position in an influential article in the North American Review in January 1830, as did Jackson in his messages to Congress. Critics countered that the argument was self-serving and that the same government demanding relocation had failed to protect Indigenous land in place.

The deepest contradiction lay here. Federal policy encouraged Indigenous nations to adopt agriculture, literacy, Christianity, and representative government, and then treated those who did so, such as the Cherokee, as an obstacle because they were not leaving. Cherokee success undermined the claim that Indigenous peoples could not coexist with settlers, which made it politically threatening to those who wanted their land. Removal ideology therefore had to shift from the claim that Indigenous peoples were unable to change to the claim that they were unable to remain, whatever they did.

Division III — The Political Struggle over Removal · Section 65.9

Indigenous Nations Confront the Removal Question

Indigenous nations did not respond to removal pressure uniformly, and treating them as monolithic obscures the political reasoning involved. The Cherokee government, led by Principal Chief John Ross, pursued a strategy of legal and political resistance: petitions to Congress, publicity through the Cherokee Phoenix, delegations to Washington, hired counsel, and litigation. Cherokee law, reaffirmed repeatedly in the 1820s, treated unauthorized land cession as a capital offense. This was a strategy grounded in the belief that the United States' own treaties and constitutional order could be made to protect the nation, a belief that Cherokee leaders pursued with considerable skill.

The Muscogee had experienced the consequences of treaty politics directly in the McIntosh affair. Leaders such as Opothleyahola worked through the National Council to resist further cessions, then negotiated under mounting pressure as Alabama extended its laws over Muscogee territory and intruders poured in. The Choctaw were divided among three districts whose leaders differed. Greenwood LeFlore concluded that state law left no viable alternative to negotiating, while Mushulatubbee and others were more reluctant. The Chickasaw, whose leaders had long negotiated carefully, sought assurance of good western land and fair compensation, and engaged in prolonged bargaining. The Seminole, whose treatment is the subject of Chapter 67, resisted a removal agreement signed in 1832 by a limited group of leaders.

Within nations, debates were intense. Some leaders concluded that removal could not be prevented and that the best course was to obtain the best terms before conditions worsened, others believed that any agreement would simply invite further demands, and still others remained committed to staying whatever the cost. These differences reflected different assessments of the same evidence: the federal government's lack of will to enforce treaty protections, the extension of state law, and the likelihood of external help. They were not signs of political weakness or disunity in any simple sense. They were the kind of serious political disagreement found in every society facing an existential decision under duress.

In the Old Northwest, communities such as the Shawnee, Wyandot, Seneca, Potawatomi, and Ojibwe faced related pressure but with different circumstances, often as small communities surrounded by settlers, with limited reserves. The Sauk and Meskwaki leader Black Hawk resisted in 1832 in a conflict, the Black Hawk War, that ended with heavy loss and new cessions in the Mississippi Valley. Others negotiated removal or reservations. The range of responses, from litigation to armed resistance to negotiated relocation, shows the varied strategies available to nations with very different resources.

Section 65.10

State Governments, Frontier Politics, and the Campaign for Removal

State governments were the most aggressive lobbyists for removal. Georgia's legislature and governors repeatedly demanded that the federal government fulfill the 1802 Compact. Alabama and Mississippi, in late 1829 and early 1830, extended state law over Muscogee, Choctaw, and Chickasaw lands, declaring Indigenous governments abolished or ineffective within state borders and subjecting Indigenous people to state courts that excluded Indigenous testimony in cases involving white people. These laws were intended to make life in place untenable and to pressure leaders to negotiate.

Wilson Lumpkin illustrates the political career of removal. A Georgia congressman from 1827 to 1831 and then governor, he was a leading advocate in Congress and later in state office, and he promoted the surveying and distribution of Cherokee land. His career reflects how state politics, land distribution, and federal lobbying operated together.

Removal was not exclusively southeastern. In Ohio, Indiana, Illinois, Michigan Territory, and elsewhere, territorial and state authorities pressed for cessions and for the departure of remaining communities. Treaties negotiated in Ohio in 1831 involved the Wyandot, Seneca, and Shawnee. The Treaty of Chicago in 1833 obtained a cession by the Potawatomi, Ojibwe, and Odawa of roughly five million acres in Illinois and Wisconsin, with removal to follow. In New York, the Ogden Land Company pursued the Seneca land claims over several years, a contest that culminated in controversial treaties after the period of this chapter. These northern cases show that removal was a national policy shaped by settlers and states in many regions, and that the same legal and political mechanisms operated across them.

The relationship between state demands and federal policy was reciprocal. States put pressure on the federal government, and federal officials, in turn, often used state pressure as leverage in treaty negotiations, presenting Indigenous leaders with a choice between removal on negotiated terms and remaining subject to state law without federal protection. In this way state action and federal diplomacy worked together even when they appeared to be in conflict.

Section 65.11

Andrew Jackson, the Election of 1828, and Executive Expansion

Andrew Jackson's election in 1828 brought to the presidency a man with long experience of Indigenous affairs. He had commanded Tennessee militia in the Creek War, negotiated several cession treaties, and led the 1818 Florida campaign. His electoral coalition, strong in the South and West and among voters expanded by new suffrage laws, included many constituents who favored removal. Jackson's own views combined strategic assumptions about security with conviction that Indigenous nations could not maintain independent governments within the states.

It is nonetheless misleading to say that removal began with Jackson. As earlier sections showed, removal proposals, exchange treaties, and administrative machinery predated his presidency by decades. What Jackson did was alter the executive posture. His administration declined to enforce federal protection of Indigenous lands against Georgia, and in April 1829 Secretary of War John Eaton informed a Cherokee delegation that the president would not oppose Georgia's extension of its laws and that the federal government would not support an independent Cherokee state within Georgia's limits. The administration also made removal a legislative priority, using the presidency to press Congress for statutory authority and funds.

Jackson's constitutional argument, set out in his first annual message in December 1829, relied on the claim that the Constitution did not permit a new state to be formed within an existing state without that state's consent, and that Indigenous nations were therefore subject to state jurisdiction. This argument, which treated the Cherokee as a potential competing state rather than as a nation party to treaties, was contested at the time and rejected by the Supreme Court in Worcester v. Georgia.

Section 65.12

The National Debate: Removal, Opposition, and American Democracy

The removal bill provoked one of the early republic's most consequential national debates over Indian policy. Opposition drew on several sources. Jeremiah Evarts, corresponding secretary of the American Board of Commissioners for Foreign Missions, published a series of essays in 1829 under the pen name "William Penn," arguing that treaties bound the United States and that removal would violate national honor. Catharine Beecher, an educator, circulated an address in December 1829 urging women to petition Congress against removal, and women's petitions, though they carried no electoral weight, became a notable feature of the campaign. Historians have noted connections between this petitioning and later antislavery petition campaigns, though the extent of the continuity is debated.

In Congress, Senator Theodore Frelinghuysen of New Jersey delivered a lengthy speech against the bill, arguing on legal and moral grounds that the United States was bound by its treaties and that Indigenous peoples held a right to the land they occupied. Representatives such as Edward Everett of Massachusetts, Henry Storrs of New York, and Davy Crockett of Tennessee also opposed it. Supporters, among them members of Congress from Georgia and the Senate and House Indian affairs committee chairs from Tennessee, argued that states possessed jurisdiction, that removal was necessary and in the interest of Indigenous peoples, and that the government was offering generous terms. The debate did not follow party lines neatly, since opponents included northern Jacksonians as well as National Republicans, while supporters were mainly, though not only, southern and western.

Indigenous voices were central to the opposition. Cherokee delegations lobbied in Washington and submitted written memorials, and the Cherokee press supplied material to supporters in the North. These memorials appealed to treaties, to the Constitution, and to the justice and honor of the United States, often with careful attention to American political language. The debate therefore turned on competing views of what republican government demanded of its own commitments.

Division IV — Law, Congress, and the Federal Removal System · Section 65.13

Indigenous Sovereignty, Federalism, and Constitutional Authority

The constitutional framework for Indian affairs was ambiguous, and its ambiguity was exploited. The Constitution gives Congress power to regulate commerce with foreign nations, among the states, and with the Indian tribes, and it gives the president, with the Senate, the power to make treaties. Article VI makes treaties part of the supreme law of the land. The treatment of Indigenous nations in a clause separate from foreign nations and states suggested a distinct category, but the text left unresolved the status of Indigenous nations and the extent of state authority over their lands.

Congress passed a series of Trade and Intercourse Acts beginning in 1790 that regulated trade with Indigenous nations, prohibited private purchase of Indigenous land, and sought to restrain settlers from trespass. These acts reflected a federal claim of exclusive authority over relations with Indigenous peoples, which in principle excluded state interference. States disputed that claim in practice, arguing that sovereignty within their borders gave them jurisdiction over all land and people.

Three positions can be distinguished. The Indigenous position, articulated by the Cherokee and others, held that Indigenous nations were sovereign political communities whose authority was inherent and predated the United States, and that treaties with the federal government protected that authority. The state position held that no sovereign could exist within a state's limits. The federal position varied across administrations, sometimes defending treaty rights against states and sometimes yielding. Tribal sovereignty, in the Indigenous view, was not a grant from the United States, while the legal authority claimed by federal and state governments rested on assertions of discovery, treaty, and statute. The resolution of these conflicts depended not only on legal reasoning but on the exercise of political power.

Section 65.14

Land Ownership, the Doctrine of Discovery, and Johnson v. M'Intosh

In Johnson v. M'Intosh (1823), the Supreme Court addressed a dispute over land in present-day Illinois. The plaintiffs claimed title through purchases made in the 1770s directly from Indigenous nations, the Illinois and the Piankeshaw, while the defendant claimed title through a later federal grant. Chief Justice John Marshall, writing for the Court, held that private individuals could not acquire valid title from Indigenous nations, and that title ultimately came from the government of the discovering European power and its successor, the United States.

Marshall drew on a doctrine of discovery, a body of European legal and diplomatic claims that held that the first European power to claim a region held the exclusive right, as against other European powers, to acquire it from its inhabitants. In the opinion, discovery gave the United States title subject to a right of occupancy held by Indigenous nations, and Indigenous nations' power to sell their land to anyone other than the United States was thereby limited. Marshall acknowledged the artificiality of the doctrine but treated it as established by long practice and by the consequences of conquest.

The decision's scope should be stated carefully. It did not hold that Indigenous nations lacked sovereignty or governments, and it did not authorize removal. It addressed who could purchase land from Indigenous nations. Its significance for the removal era lies in the framework it supplied: Indigenous land rights were recognized as real but subordinate, and the federal government was positioned as the sole legitimate purchaser. This framework strengthened the federal government's role in the treaty system and made it easier to treat Indigenous land as something to be acquired by cession, but removal rested on many other legal, political, and economic foundations. The decision also built on earlier cases such as Fletcher v. Peck (1810), which had treated state land grants as valid while describing Indigenous occupancy as a burden on the state's title.

Historical Vocabulary
Indian Title and Extinguishment

Definition. "Indian title," or the right of occupancy, was the legal term for the Indigenous right to occupy and use land. "Extinguishment" meant the ending of that right through purchase, cession, or other means.

Historical context. Under the framework described in Johnson v. M'Intosh, the United States held an underlying title and the exclusive power to extinguish Indian title.

Why it matters. The concept explains why treaties were needed for land acquisition, and why federal officials spoke of "extinguishing" rather than "seizing" title.

Potential misunderstanding. The term does not mean Indigenous nations had no sovereignty or ownership in a common sense. It describes a legal construct created by the United States and its courts, which Indigenous nations did not accept as describing their own legal understanding.

Section 65.15

The Indian Removal Act of 1830

The legislative history of the Act reveals the intensity of the national conflict. A removal bill was introduced early in the 1829–1830 session, and the Senate debated it for weeks in the spring. Opponents offered amendments to protect treaty rights and delay action. The Senate passed the bill on April 24, 1830, by 28 votes to 19. In the House, debate was equally bitter, and the bill passed on May 26 by 102 to 97. The close margin demonstrates that the policy did not command overwhelming support. Jackson signed the act on May 28, 1830.

The statute, formally titled an act to provide for an exchange of lands with the Indians residing in any of the states or territories and for their removal west of the Mississippi River, authorized the president to set apart land west of the Mississippi, not within any state or organized territory, to which Indian title had been extinguished, and to divide it into districts for the reception of Indigenous nations. It authorized the president to negotiate exchanges with nations that chose to exchange, to guarantee the new lands to them, to compensate them for improvements on lands they left, to cover the costs of removal and provide assistance for a first year, and to protect them in their new homes. It appropriated $500,000 to carry out its provisions. A final provision stated that nothing in the act authorized or directed violation of any existing treaty.

The text matters as much for what it did not say. The statute did not order any nation to leave, did not specify a mechanism of compulsion, and spoke the language of exchange with consenting parties. It created an authority and a fund. The coercion that characterized many subsequent agreements arose from the pressure of state laws, the threat of withdrawn federal protection, the use of favored leaders, bribery, and the manipulation of negotiating conditions by executive agents. This distinction is crucial to a precise understanding of the removal regime. The Act made removal negotiations possible, and the executive's choices in carrying it out made them coercive.

Section 65.16

The Federal Machinery of Removal: Administration, Money, and Authority

A statute alone does not make policy. The removal program depended on an administrative apparatus that developed over the 1820s and 1830s. The War Department supervised Indian affairs, and the Office of Indian Affairs, created by Secretary Calhoun in 1824 under Thomas L. McKenney, handled correspondence, trade, and annuities. Congress established the post of Commissioner of Indian Affairs in 1832, with Elbert Herring as its first holder, and reorganized the department in 1834, the same year it passed a new Trade and Intercourse Act that defined "Indian country" in the West.

Implementation relied on a chain of officials: treaty commissioners appointed by the president, agents assigned to each nation, army officers who handled logistics, and contractors who supplied transport and provisions. The army's Commissary General of Subsistence played an important role in organizing removal parties in the 1830s. Surveyors mapped the ceded lands, while agents valued improvements and administered allotments or reserves that treaties provided. Appropriations flowed through the department, and treaties often used annuities and the proceeds of land sales to fund removal, so Indigenous nations sometimes bore costs of their own displacement.

The relationship between formal agreement and practical enforcement mattered. Treaties promised western land, protection, and compensation, but the machinery that carried out those promises was underfunded, vulnerable to corruption, and dependent on the integrity of individual agents. The same machinery gave the executive extensive discretion, and this discretion allowed those who negotiated removal treaties to select favored signatories and to exploit divisions within Indigenous nations.

Division V — From Removal Policy to the Displacement of Nations · Section 65.17

Treaty Negotiation, Coercion, and the Problem of Consent

Three treaties illustrate how removal agreements were pursued, and they are best treated separately, since they differed in the circumstances of negotiation and in the legitimacy of the signatures. The Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek, signed in September 1830 with the Choctaw, was the first removal treaty under the 1830 Act. Commissioners John Eaton and John Coffee met Choctaw leaders after Mississippi had extended its laws over the nation and abolished the authority of its chiefs under state law. The council's initial resistance gave way to an agreement under which the Choctaw ceded approximately 10.4 million acres in Mississippi and Alabama. A provision allowed heads of families who registered within a set period to remain and receive land, but in practice few were able to claim it. Choctaw leaders had differed in their assessments, and the treaty was ratified in early 1831.

The Treaty of Cusseta, also called the Treaty of Washington, was signed on March 24, 1832, with a Muscogee delegation, including Opothleyahola, in Washington. It ceded Muscogee land in Alabama and provided for individual allotments to heads of families, with larger tracts for chiefs, that could be sold. The context included Alabama's extension of state law and the influx of intruders. Allotments proved vulnerable to fraud as speculators bought them, and the arrangement left many Muscogee dispossessed in place, a development that contributed to the crisis of the mid-1830s discussed in Chapter 66. The Chickasaw Treaty of Pontotoc Creek (1832) followed a similar logic, providing for the sale of Chickasaw land and the use of proceeds for resettlement, with the western land still to be found.

The Treaty of New Echota, signed December 29, 1835, is the most disputed. The Cherokee National Council, under Principal Chief John Ross, had rejected removal proposals, and Ross's delegation was in Washington. Federal commissioner John F. Schermerhorn convened a gathering at New Echota attended by a small fraction of the Cherokee people, and a group of signers from the "Treaty Party," including Major Ridge, John Ridge, Elias Boudinot, and Stand Watie, agreed to terms providing for a cession of all Cherokee land in the East in exchange for $5 million and land in the West. The Cherokee government and a large majority of citizens opposed the treaty, and opponents sent a memorial carrying 3,352 Cherokee signatures to Congress. Cherokee delegates subsequently reported that more than fifteen thousand Cherokee people had protested the treaty; those figures reflect different documentary accounts and should not be treated as the count for one petition. The Senate ratified the treaty in May 1836 by 31 votes to 15, just one affirmative vote above the required two-thirds threshold. The signers were not mere dupes or villains: they judged that resistance would fail and that removal on negotiated terms offered the best prospect for the survival of the nation. Their opponents argued that the signers had no authority to cede the nation's land. Their disagreement became a lasting division in Cherokee politics.

Taken together, these cases show several recurring features: state pressure that narrowed options before negotiation, executive selection of negotiating partners, internal Indigenous disagreement exploited by federal agents, provisions for individual allotments that fragmented communal landholding, and ratification by the Senate despite doubts about legitimacy. Whether the resulting agreements are best described as consent is a question each case answers differently, and the problem of consent under duress remains central to the history of removal.

Everyday Life and Social History

Farms, Households, and the Valuation of "Improvements"

Behind the diplomacy lay households. Many southeastern Indigenous families lived on farms with cabins, fields, orchards, livestock, ferries, and mills, and communities maintained towns, schools, churches, and ceremonial grounds. When a treaty provided compensation for "improvements," federal appraisers visited and assigned values to buildings, fences, and cultivated acreage. The process reduced the places where families had lived for generations to line items in ledgers, and valuations were open to dispute and manipulation. Compensation did not cover the value of land itself, of community ties, or of ceremonial sites.

Women's roles mattered, particularly in nations where agriculture and household property followed matrilineal patterns. Intruders squatting on land, theft of livestock, and state courts that refused to hear Indigenous testimony against white people affected daily life well before any removal occurred. Enslaved people held by Indigenous families faced their own uncertainty. Because much of the surviving record was written by officials, historians draw on missionary letters, petitions, and later testimony to reconstruct these experiences, reading each with attention to who produced it.

Section 65.18

Indian Territory and the Geography of Displacement

Federal planners treated the region west of Arkansas Territory and Missouri as a destination for relocated nations. Explorers' reports, including accounts of the 1819–1820 Long expedition that popularized the label "Great American Desert," encouraged the belief that the Plains were unsuited to farming and therefore would remain beyond settlement. This assumption supported the idea of a permanent Indian country. The 1834 Trade and Intercourse Act defined the region as Indian country, and the term "Indian Territory" became its common name.

But the region was not empty. The Osage, whose homeland extended across parts of present-day Missouri, Arkansas, Kansas, and Oklahoma, ceded vast claims in an 1825 treaty and were confined to a reserve in Kansas. The Quapaw ceded Arkansas lands in treaties of 1818 and 1824 and were later assigned a small reserve. The Caddo ceded Louisiana lands in 1835. Farther west, Comanche, Kiowa, Wichita, Pawnee, and other peoples ranged over the southern and central Plains, and an army dragoon expedition in 1834 sought contact with some of them. Western Cherokee who had settled earlier, after treaties of 1817, 1819, and 1828, had fought with the Osage at Claremore Mound in 1817 and later faced the arrival of the larger body of Cherokee. The federal plan to resettle many eastern nations in this region thus created potential conflict with peoples already there and with earlier immigrants, and it assumed that distant lands could accommodate displaced nations without regard to their existing Indigenous populations.

Section 65.19

The Supreme Court, Cherokee Sovereignty, and the Limits of Judicial Protection

The Cherokee turned to the Supreme Court. In Cherokee Nation v. Georgia (1831), the nation, represented by former Attorney General William Wirt, sought an injunction against enforcement of Georgia's laws over its territory. Chief Justice Marshall's opinion held that the Court lacked original jurisdiction because the Cherokee Nation was not a "foreign state" within the meaning of the Constitution. Instead, he described Indigenous nations as "domestic dependent nations," in a relationship to the United States resembling that of a ward to a guardian. Justices Smith Thompson and Joseph Story dissented, arguing the Cherokee were a foreign nation entitled to sue. The decision was a defeat for the Cherokee, while introducing a phrase that would shape federal Indian law for generations.

The next year the Court reached the merits. Samuel Worcester, a missionary, and others had been arrested for living in Cherokee territory without a license required by Georgia law, and Worcester and Elizur Butler were sentenced to hard labor. In Worcester v. Georgia (1832), Marshall's opinion held Georgia's laws had no force in Cherokee territory, that the relationship between the Cherokee and the United States was governed by treaties and federal law, and that the states had no authority over it. The Court ordered Worcester's release. Georgia refused to comply, and the executive branch did not enforce the ruling. A story that Jackson said Marshall had made his decision and could now enforce it is of doubtful authenticity and should not be treated as a reliable quotation. What can be said is that Jackson's administration did not act to compel Georgia, and that the nullification crisis of 1832–1833, in which South Carolina challenged federal authority, made the question of federal enforcement politically delicate. Georgia's governor pardoned Worcester and Butler in January 1833 after they dropped their appeal.

The cases show both the potential and the limits of judicial protection. Worcester provided the strongest judicial statement of the early republic that Indigenous nations were political communities beyond state jurisdiction, and later courts would draw on it. But courts depended on the executive and the states for enforcement, and where those authorities were unwilling, a legal victory could not by itself secure rights. The Cherokee gained no practical protection from the ruling, and pressure for a removal treaty continued, culminating in New Echota.

Historical Vocabulary
Domestic Dependent Nation

Definition. A phrase from Cherokee Nation v. Georgia describing Indigenous nations as distinct political communities located within the United States and dependent on it in a guardian-like relationship.

Historical context. Marshall used the phrase to explain why the Cherokee were not a foreign state for purposes of the Court's original jurisdiction. The opinion in Worcester a year later emphasized the nations' distinct political character.

Why it matters. The phrase became foundational in federal Indian law, used both to support tribal sovereignty and to justify federal power.

Potential misunderstanding. The term does not mean Indigenous nations lacked self-government. In its original context it distinguished their constitutional position from that of foreign nations and states, and it has been read differently by later courts.

Primary Source Window

Chief Justice Marshall's Opinion in Worcester v. Georgia, 1832

Context: Opinion of the Supreme Court issued in March 1832 in a case arising from Georgia's prosecution of missionaries in Cherokee territory. Source type: Judicial opinion.

"a distinct community occupying its own territory"

Paraphrase: In the passage from which the phrase comes, Marshall describes the Cherokee Nation as a distinct political community with defined territory, in which Georgia's laws have no force, and whose relations with the United States are governed by treaties and federal law.

Source reference: Worcester v. Georgia, 31 U.S. (6 Pet.) 515, 561 (1832); full judicial opinion.

Analysis: The opinion rests on treaty text, on the Constitution's allocation of power over Indian affairs to the federal government, and on a view of Indigenous nations as political communities. It is the strongest judicial endorsement of Cherokee sovereignty in the period.

Limitations: The opinion is a legal argument that could not enforce itself. It depended on the executive and Georgia, which refused to comply. It also reflects the legal framework of discovery and guardianship, which limited Indigenous independence in ways the Cherokee did not accept.

Perspective: The federal judiciary. Cherokee advocates had pressed the case but did not write the opinion, and Georgia's officials rejected its premises.

Section 65.20

Removal as Federal Policy: Consequences, Contradictions, and Historical Significance

By 1835 the United States had built a removal regime. It had developed from scattered proposals and local exchange treaties into a statutory program with dedicated funds, an administrative apparatus, a body of legal argument, and a record of treaties negotiated under pressure. No single cause explains the result. Land hunger and speculation created demand, cotton and slavery gave that demand economic and political force in the South, state governments pressed claims of jurisdiction, racial ideology and paternalist argument supplied justification, and the executive chose to side with the states. At the same time, Indigenous nations, missionaries, opponents in Congress, and the Supreme Court produced resistance that shaped the course and terms of policy even where it did not prevent it.

The regime also embodied lasting contradictions. It combined the language of treaty and consent with the practice of pressure. It encouraged "civilization" and then treated civilized nations as obstacles. It promised protection in the West to nations whose western lands were already inhabited. And it invoked the Constitution while presiding over a conflict in which its highest court's ruling went unenforced.

Historical Debate

Did the 1830 Act Mark a Policy Rupture or an Intensification?

The question: Was the Indian Removal Act a fundamental break with earlier federal policy, or the codification and intensification of practices already underway?

Interpretation A — Rupture: Some historians emphasize that before 1830 federal policy, though aimed at acquiring land, still included a stated commitment to protecting Indigenous nations and to gradual "civilization" in place, and that Jackson's administration abandoned that commitment by endorsing state jurisdiction and seeking a general removal law. On this view, 1829–1830 changed the character of federal policy.
Interpretation B — Intensification: Others stress continuity: the Georgia Compact, Jefferson's land policies, exchange treaties in 1817–1820, and Monroe's 1825 message all anticipated removal, and cession pressure was constant. On this view, the 1830 Act gave legal form and funding to an existing trajectory and increased its speed and scale.

Evidence: Both views rely on the same documentary record: treaties, messages, statutes, and correspondence. The disagreement concerns weight and interpretation, rather than the existence of the evidence. Historians such as Anthony F. C. Wallace and Claudio Saunt have emphasized, in different ways, the policy's roots in earlier expansion and its links to slavery's growth, while others, including Francis Paul Prucha, have given greater attention to the stated intentions of policymakers and to the tension between humanitarian and expansionist aims.

Why it matters: The answer affects how responsibility is assigned, whether to see removal as the work of one administration or of a broader political system, and how to interpret the role of opposition.

Cause and Effect

The Construction of the Removal Regime

Long-term causes

Treaty-based land acquisition, settler expansion, the Georgia Compact, racial ideology, and the rise of cotton and slavery in the Deep South.

Short-term causes

State extension of jurisdiction over Indigenous land, the discovery of gold in 1828, and Jackson's election and executive posture.

Immediate trigger

The 1829–1830 push for a general statute, intensified by Georgia's laws taking effect in 1830.

Transformation

Enactment of the Removal Act and creation of an administrative system for exchange treaties.

Immediate effects

Treaties such as Dancing Rabbit Creek and Cusseta, deepening of Indigenous political division, and the unenforced Worcester ruling.

Long-term consequences

Displacement of southeastern and northern nations, examined in Chapter 66, and a precedent for later federal removal and reservation policies.

Historical complexity: No factor alone produced removal. Economic incentives needed political channels, ideology needed institutions, and institutions needed executive will. Indigenous resistance and national opposition meant outcomes were contested, and the final form of the regime was not predetermined.

The chapter ends where Chapter 66 begins. This chapter has traced the creation of the removal regime, the laws, officials, arguments, and treaties that made removal possible. It has not described the experience of those subjected to it: the journeys, the camps, the loss of life, the resettlement, and the survival of nations in new places. Those histories, told from the standpoint of Cherokee, Muscogee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, and Seminole communities, belong to the chapters that follow, and they should be read with the understanding that the regime described here was a human construction, assembled through choices that others contested.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has explained how the United States developed Indian removal into an organized federal policy. Its central claim is that removal was constructed over time, through economic interest, ideology, constitutional conflict, legislation, administration, and negotiation, not decided in a single moment or by a single president.

The treaty system was the foundation. Treaties recognized Indigenous nations as political communities whose consent was required, yet they were also the main tool for acquiring land, negotiated under conditions of unequal power. The Georgia Compact of 1802, Jefferson-era land policy, the Creek War cession of 1814, and early exchange treaties established precedents and expectations that later advocates invoked.

Removal proposals before 1830 were options within a flexible policy, culminating in Monroe's 1825 message and the creation of the Office of Indian Affairs. The difference between proposal and program, a statute with funds and machinery, is essential to understanding what changed in 1830.

Economic forces drove demand. Settler migration, land speculation, state land lotteries, and public land sales created pressure on Indigenous homelands, and in the South the cotton economy and the expansion of slavery gave that pressure particular force. Removal also occurred in the Old Northwest, where cotton was not the motive, which shows why single-cause explanations fail.

Ideology justified the policy. Racial hierarchy, arguments about land use, and paternalist claims that relocation would protect Indigenous peoples combined with a contradiction at the heart of "civilization" policy: nations that adopted the institutions urged on them, such as the Cherokee, were still treated as obstacles.

Indigenous nations responded in varied ways, from legal and political resistance to negotiation to armed conflict, and their disagreements reflected differing assessments of how best to preserve their communities. The Cherokee, Muscogee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Seminole, and northern peoples each faced distinct circumstances.

Georgia, Alabama, and Mississippi extended their laws over Indigenous territory, and Andrew Jackson's election aligned the executive with state demands. The national debate of 1829–1830, shaped by missionaries, women petitioners, congressional opponents, and Indigenous testimony, produced a closely divided vote, and the Removal Act became law on May 28, 1830.

Legally, the Act authorized exchanges with consenting nations and appropriated funds, but the coercion in subsequent treaties came from state pressure and executive negotiation. Johnson v. M'Intosh shaped the framework of Indian title, while Cherokee Nation v. Georgia and Worcester v. Georgia defined Cherokee status and confirmed, in principle, that states could not govern Cherokee territory, a ruling that went unenforced. The treaties of Dancing Rabbit Creek, Cusseta, and New Echota show varying degrees of legitimacy and consent.

By 1835 a removal regime existed. Chapter 66 turns from its construction to the experiences of the nations subjected to it.

Key Terms

Removal
The federal policy and process of relocating Indigenous nations from eastern lands to territory west of the Mississippi, pursued through exchange treaties and, after 1830, under statutory authority.
Land exchange
The term used in the 1830 Act for the trade of eastern Indigenous land for land in the West.
Cession
The transfer of land by treaty from one political community to another.
Annuity
A recurring payment of money or goods promised in a treaty, which could also create dependence.
Georgia Compact (1802)
Agreement in which Georgia ceded western land claims and the United States promised to extinguish Indian title within Georgia.
Indian title
The legal term for the Indigenous right of occupancy under the framework of United States law.
Extinguishment
The legal ending of Indian title by purchase or cession.
Doctrine of discovery
A body of European-derived legal claims, invoked in Johnson v. M'Intosh, giving the discovering power exclusive rights to acquire land from its inhabitants.
Domestic dependent nation
Phrase from Cherokee Nation v. Georgia describing the constitutional position of Indigenous nations within the United States.
Federalism
The division of authority between federal and state governments, a central issue in the removal conflict.
Civilization policy
Federal programs, including the Civilization Fund Act of 1819, promoting Indigenous agriculture, schooling, and Christianity.
Paternalism
The view that the government should act as guardian of Indigenous peoples, invoked to justify removal as protection.
Improvements
Buildings, fields, and other developments on land, valued by federal appraisers for compensation under removal treaties.
Reserve or allotment
An individual tract of land reserved in a treaty, often vulnerable to sale and fraud.
Intercourse Acts
Federal laws from 1790 onward regulating trade with Indigenous nations and restricting land purchase.
Indian country
A legal term for lands reserved to Indigenous nations, defined for the West in 1834.
Indian Territory
The region west of Arkansas and Missouri designated for relocated nations.
Memorial
A formal written petition to a legislature, used by Indigenous nations and their supporters.
Treaty Party
The Cherokee faction that signed the Treaty of New Echota in 1835.
Office of Indian Affairs
Agency within the War Department, created in 1824, administering Indian relations.
Intruders
Settlers and others who entered Indigenous lands in violation of treaties or federal law.
Indian Removal Act
The 1830 statute authorizing exchanges of land and removal west of the Mississippi.
Consent
Formal agreement by Indigenous representatives, a concept complicated by coercive circumstances and disputed authority to sign.
Nullification crisis
The 1832–1833 confrontation over South Carolina's claim to nullify federal law, which shaped the political context of Worcester.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a policy of displacement was built through the ordinary machinery of a republic: treaties, statutes, state legislatures, executive offices, courts, and elections. Removal was not an accident or a spontaneous movement. It was a project assembled from many parts, and understanding its construction helps explain why it was so difficult to resist and why its consequences were so far-reaching.

The chapter also reveals continuity. The treaty system, the doctrine of discovery, the language of paternalism, and the practice of dividing communities through selective negotiation did not end with removal. Later chapters will show them recurring in the wars, reservation policies, and allotment programs of the nineteenth century, and in the legal disputes over sovereignty that continue into the present.

At the same time, the chapter shows Indigenous nations as political actors. Cherokee petitions, Muscogee law, Choctaw diplomacy, and Seminole resistance were serious responses to a serious threat, and the judicial victory in Worcester became a lasting reference point in Indigenous legal claims even though it could not prevent removal at the time.

Chapter 66 takes up the human consequences of this regime, and Chapter 67 turns to Seminole resistance in Florida. Students should carry forward from this chapter the understanding that the displacement of nations was the product of decisions made by identifiable actors and institutions, and that those decisions were contested at every stage.
