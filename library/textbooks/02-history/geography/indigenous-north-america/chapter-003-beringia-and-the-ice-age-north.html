Beringia and the Ice Age North

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

Where the Bering Strait now separates Siberia from Alaska by some fifty-three miles of cold water, there was once dry land: a rolling, wind-scoured plain, hundreds of miles wide from north to south, joining Asia and North America into a single continuous landmass. For thousands of years during the last Ice Age, this land, not a narrow crossing but a subcontinent in its own right, was not a bridge that people hurried across. For long stretches of time, it appears to have been where they lived.

Modern maps mislead students in a very particular way when it comes to the region historians and scientists call Beringia. Because the Bering Strait appears today as a narrow channel of water, it is tempting to picture the first human presence in North America as a brief, single crossing: a line of families walking single file across an icy causeway, arriving in a new world within a few dramatic seasons. The physical and archaeological evidence assembled over the past several decades tells a far more complicated, and in some ways far more interesting, story. At the height of the last glacial period, when enormous volumes of the world's water were locked into continental ice sheets, global sea levels fell so far that the shallow floor of the present-day Bering and Chukchi Seas emerged as exposed land. The resulting landmass, at its greatest extent, is generally described as stretching from roughly the Lena River in eastern Siberia to the Mackenzie River in northwestern Canada, though scholars define its precise boundaries somewhat differently, encompassing an area some scientists estimate at over a million square miles, larger than the states of Texas and Alaska combined.

This chapter examines Beringia not as a passageway but as an environment: a distinctive cold, and in many areas dry, landscape that supported large grazing animals in at least some regions, may have sustained human communities for an extended period, and shaped the earliest chapters of Indigenous North American history long before descendant populations had reached the regions now called the Canadian Plains or the American Southwest. Interior Alaska itself, it should be noted at the outset, is not a destination reached only after leaving Beringia; it forms the eastern, and today the only readily accessible, sector of Beringia itself. Understanding Beringia requires thinking simultaneously about several different kinds of evidence: the physical geology of sea level change and glacial ice; the ecology of a vanished grassland biome that some scientists call the mammoth steppe; the genetic evidence embedded in the DNA of contemporary Indigenous peoples and ancient human remains; and the archaeological record of the relatively few sites, on both the Siberian and Alaskan sides of the modern strait, that document human presence in this landscape before it disappeared beneath rising seas.

The chapter also introduces one of the most significant recent developments in the scientific understanding of the peopling of the Americas: the Beringian standstill hypothesis, a genetic and archaeological argument holding that ancestral Native American populations may have experienced a period of reduced gene flow and relative isolation, plausibly located in or around Beringia, for some centuries or millennia before contributing more broadly to populations elsewhere in the Americas. This hypothesis, still actively debated and refined by researchers, reframes Beringia from a mere corridor of passage into a leading candidate for a homeland, a place where distinct biological and, potentially, cultural characteristics of later Indigenous American populations may have taken shape.

None of this evidence answers every question. Beringia's own landscape now lies mostly underwater, inaccessible to the kind of systematic archaeological survey historians can conduct on dry land, and what is known about the region depends on a combination of submerged and coastal geology, sediment cores, ancient pollen and insect remains, a small number of archaeological sites in Siberia and Alaska, and genetic research on both ancient and living populations. This chapter treats these forms of evidence honestly, identifying what they can support and where interpretation remains provisional, contested, or subject to revision as new research emerges.

This chapter does not resolve the debates introduced in Chapter 2 concerning the timing of the earliest human presence in the Americas; it instead supplies the environmental and geographic foundation necessary to evaluate those debates. Chapter 4 examines the archaeological evidence, including the Clovis tradition and pre-Clovis sites, in detail. This chapter's task is different: to explain what Beringia actually was, physically and ecologically, and to establish why so much of the scholarly argument about the earliest peopling of North America now centers on this vanished northern landscape rather than on any single moment of arrival.

Readers should also keep in mind a caution introduced in Chapter 1: archaeological and genetic evidence describes population movements and material patterns, not the specific identities, languages, or self-understandings of the people who lived in Beringia. This chapter draws where relevant on the oral traditions of contemporary Arctic and Subarctic Indigenous peoples, including accounts of ancestral movement and long habitation in northern lands, while being careful to distinguish oral tradition, genetic reconstruction, and archaeological inference as related but methodologically distinct forms of evidence, each contributing a different piece of a still incomplete picture.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the physical formation of Beringia as a consequence of glacial-period sea level change, and explain its approximate scale and duration.
  2. Explain why Beringia is better understood as a distinctive subcontinental environment than as a narrow land bridge or simple crossing point.
  3. Analyze the ecology of the mammoth steppe, including its characteristic plant communities and large mammal populations.
  4. Evaluate the Beringian standstill hypothesis and the genetic and archaeological evidence used to support and question it.
  5. Describe the technological, social, and subsistence adaptations that would have allowed human communities to live in Beringia's cold, arid environment.
  6. Compare the interior and coastal route hypotheses for movement out of Beringia into the rest of the Americas.
  7. Explain how rising sea levels at the end of the Pleistocene transformed Beringia from exposed land into the present-day Bering Strait and surrounding seas.
  8. Assess the strengths and limitations of genetic, archaeological, and paleoecological evidence for reconstructing a landscape that is now largely submerged.
  9. Distinguish between secure scientific findings and areas of continuing scholarly debate concerning Beringia's role in the peopling of the Americas.
  10. Connect the Beringian standstill hypothesis to the broader questions about Indigenous origins introduced in Chapter 2 and developed further in Chapter 4.

Historical Context

Chapter 2 introduced the broad archaeological, genetic, and Indigenous perspectives concerning the earliest human presence in North America, and it identified the deep antiquity of that presence, securely documented through fossilized footprints at White Sands, New Mexico, dated to at least twenty-one to twenty-three thousand years ago, with ongoing research into the possibility of an even earlier presence. This chapter narrows the focus to a single, pivotal region: the far northern landscape connecting Siberia and Alaska during the closing stages of the last Ice Age, formally known to geologists and archaeologists as the Bering Land Bridge or, in its fuller ecological and cultural sense, Beringia.

Understanding Beringia requires some basic familiarity with the mechanics of Ice Age climate. During the most recent glacial period, often called the Last Glacial Maximum and reaching its peak intensity roughly twenty-six thousand to twenty thousand years ago, enormous continental ice sheets, principally the Laurentide Ice Sheet covering most of present-day Canada and the northern United States, and the smaller Cordilleran Ice Sheet covering the mountainous regions of the Pacific Northwest and western Canada, locked vast quantities of the planet's water into solid ice. Because that water no longer flowed into the oceans, global sea levels fell dramatically, by more than one hundred meters, roughly three hundred and fifty feet, below present levels at the glacial maximum. Shallow continental shelves around the world, including the floor of the present-day Bering and Chukchi Seas between Siberia and Alaska, were exposed as dry land.

This exposed seafloor did not simply connect two existing landmasses along a narrow strip; because the continental shelf beneath the Bering and Chukchi Seas is unusually broad and shallow, the exposed land extended for hundreds of miles in every direction, forming a landmass of continental scale in its own right. Scientists studying this region use the term Beringia to describe not merely the strip of land connecting Asia and North America but the entire unglaciated region on both sides of the modern strait, including parts of northeastern Siberia and central Alaska that, crucially, remained largely free of glacial ice even at the height of the Ice Age, because this region was too dry, receiving too little snowfall, to support the kind of ice sheets that buried much of Canada and Scandinavia.

This combination of exposed land and unglaciated terrain is central to why Beringia matters so much to the history of the Americas. Rather than functioning only as a route connecting two already-inhabited continents, unglaciated Beringia may have functioned, for a period lasting many centuries or millennia, as a habitable region in its own right, supporting distinctive plant communities, large grazing mammals, and, according to a substantial body of genetic and archaeological evidence developed over the past several decades, ancestral populations during part of a more complex process of population isolation, branching, and dispersal, even as other populations or branches of the same broader ancestral lineage were already established, or becoming established, farther south in the Americas. This chapter examines that possibility directly, situating it within the broader debates about the peopling of the Americas that Chapter 2 introduced and that Chapter 4 develops through detailed examination of the archaeological record south of Beringia itself.

Section 3.1

A Landmass Between Two Continents

To understand Beringia, students must first set aside the modern map. Today, Asia and North America are separated at their closest point by the Bering Strait, a channel of ocean roughly fifty-three miles wide and, in most places, less than two hundred feet deep. That shallowness is the key to Beringia's existence. Because the seafloor beneath the strait, and beneath much of the surrounding Bering and Chukchi Seas, is a broad, gently sloping continental shelf rather than a deep ocean basin, even a relatively modest fall in global sea level is enough to expose enormous stretches of that seafloor as dry land.

During the Last Glacial Maximum, global sea levels stood more than one hundred meters, roughly three hundred and fifty feet, below their present level, because so much of the planet's water was locked into the Laurentide, Cordilleran, Scandinavian, and other continental ice sheets. At that sea level, the shallow shelf beneath the modern Bering and Chukchi Seas emerged almost entirely, joining Siberia and Alaska into a single, continuous, and very large landmass. Geologists and archaeologists estimate that at its greatest extent, unglaciated Beringia, including the exposed shelf and the adjoining unglaciated regions of Siberia and Alaska, covered well over a million square miles, an area substantially larger than the state of Alaska itself.

This scale matters because it changes the entire way students should picture the region. Rather than a narrow isthmus or causeway, comparable to the Isthmus of Panama, Beringia was closer in scale to a small continent, roughly comparable in size to the combined area of Texas, Alaska, and several additional states. A community living somewhere in the middle of this landmass, far from either the Siberian or Alaskan coastlines as they exist today, would have had no practical sense of "crossing" from one continent to another. They would simply have been living within a very large, continuous region, one that happened to connect two landmasses that later became separated by ocean.

Beringia's boundaries were not fixed. Because it depended directly on global sea level, itself a function of how much water remained locked in glacial ice at any given moment, the size and shape of exposed Beringian land expanded and contracted repeatedly over tens of thousands of years, tracking a series of glacial and interglacial fluctuations that geologists have reconstructed from ocean sediment cores, ice cores, and other paleoclimatic evidence. Earlier, less extensive episodes of Beringian land exposure occurred well before the Last Glacial Maximum, meaning that opportunities for movement, or at least for continuity of unglaciated habitat, between Siberia and Alaska existed at multiple points during the broader span of the last several hundred thousand years, not solely during the final glacial episode most often discussed in connection with the peopling of the Americas.

Historical Vocabulary
Beringia and the Bering Land Bridge

Beringia, as used by geologists, paleoecologists, and archaeologists, refers to the entire unglaciated region encompassing the exposed Bering-Chukchi continental shelf together with adjoining unglaciated portions of northeastern Siberia and northwestern North America during periods of lowered sea level. It is best understood as an environmental and biogeographic region, not merely a route of travel.

Bering Land Bridge refers more specifically to the exposed stretch of former seafloor connecting the two continents where open water now lies. Some scholarly and popular usage treats "Bering Land Bridge" and "Beringia" as interchangeable; this textbook, following much current scientific usage, reserves "Beringia" for the fuller unglaciated region and uses "Bering Land Bridge" or "land bridge" specifically for the exposed connecting land itself.

Why the distinction matters: Describing this region only as a "bridge" implies a narrow, temporary connection crossed quickly on the way somewhere else. Describing it as Beringia, an environment and potential homeland in its own right, better reflects the genetic and archaeological evidence, discussed later in this chapter, suggesting that ancestral populations may have experienced a period of reduced gene flow and relative isolation in or around this region as part of a broader, more complex process of population branching and dispersal, rather than in a single, uniform sequence of Beringian settlement followed by later southward movement.

Geography Study

Mapping a Vanished Landmass

Using a bathymetric map showing the depth of the modern Bering and Chukchi Seas, or a reconstructed paleogeographic map of Beringia at the Last Glacial Maximum, students should consider the following:

  • The shallow continental shelf beneath the modern Bering and Chukchi Seas, much of which lies less than two hundred feet below present sea level and would have been exposed as dry land when sea levels fell by more than three hundred feet
  • The unglaciated interior of Beringia, including central Alaska and the Yukon, which, unlike most of Canada to the south and east, remained largely free of continental ice sheets throughout the last glacial period because of low precipitation
  • The glaciated mountain ranges bordering Beringia to the south and east, including parts of the Brooks Range and the mountains of southeastern Alaska, which would have constrained but not entirely blocked movement
  • The modern coastlines of Alaska and Chukotka, which mark only the present-day edge of a landmass that once extended hundreds of miles farther in each direction

Section 3.2

Ice Sheets and the Shape of Glacial North America

Beringia's history cannot be separated from the broader story of continental glaciation. During the last glacial period, two major ice sheets covered most of what is now Canada and the northern tier of the United States. The Laurentide Ice Sheet, centered over the Hudson Bay region, was the larger of the two, eventually extending south to cover most of present-day Canada, the Great Lakes region, and New England, and reaching thicknesses of more than two miles in places. The Cordilleran Ice Sheet formed independently along the mountainous spine of western North America, covering the coastal ranges and much of the interior plateau of present-day British Columbia and the Pacific Northwest.

These two ice sheets did not simply block movement southward from Beringia; their precise extent, and the timing of their advance and retreat, directly determined which routes, if any, were passable at different moments. At the height of the Last Glacial Maximum, the Laurentide and Cordilleran ice sheets appear to have merged along much of their shared boundary, closing off any dry-land route between unglaciated Beringia and the unglaciated regions of the continent farther south. This closure is central to the chronological puzzle introduced in Chapter 2: if the ice sheets were fused at their maximum extent, and if human groups were already present south of the ice by more than twenty thousand years ago, then either those groups arrived before the ice sheets closed the interior route, or they arrived by some other path, most plausibly along the Pacific coast, a possibility examined in Section 3.6.

Critically, Beringia itself, despite lying at extremely high latitudes, was largely unglaciated. This apparent paradox has a straightforward explanation: continental ice sheets form where there is abundant snowfall that fails to melt over multiple seasons, not simply where temperatures are cold. Beringia's interior climate was cold but very dry, receiving too little precipitation to build the deep, persistent snowpack required to form glaciers. The result was a landscape of exposed, largely ice-free ground even during the coldest millennia of the last Ice Age, standing in sharp contrast to the heavily glaciated regions to the south and east.

This combination, an unglaciated Beringian interior bounded by glaciated regions to the south, helps explain why researchers increasingly treat Beringia as a potential refugium, a region where plant and animal populations, including human populations, could persist through the coldest phases of the Ice Age even as ice sheets rendered much of the rest of northern North America uninhabitable. Section 3.4 develops this idea in detail through the Beringian standstill hypothesis.

Section 3.3

The Mammoth Steppe and Its Animals

The ecosystem most closely associated with unglaciated Beringia's productive grazing grounds is known to paleoecologists as the mammoth steppe, sometimes called the steppe-tundra. Reconstructed from fossil pollen, plant macrofossils, insect remains, and the stomach contents and dung of preserved Ice Age animals, this ecosystem, where it occurred, differed markedly from the wet, mossy tundra that characterizes much of the same region today, consisting instead of a cold, dry, and, by tundra standards, surprisingly productive grassland dominated by grasses, sedges, and herbaceous flowering plants known as forbs rather than by the mosses and lichens typical of modern Arctic tundra. Students should be careful, however, not to picture all of unglaciated Beringia as a single, uniform grassland. Sediment cores recovered from the now-submerged land bridge itself indicate that much of the central and northern lowlands supported a different plant community, a moist shrub tundra dominated by dwarf birch, willow, and various tundra herbs, with comparatively little grass cover, while true steppe-tundra vegetation, more favorable to large grazing herds, appears to have been concentrated in specific upland areas, including parts of the Seward Peninsula and the eastern Beringian uplands. Beringia, in other words, was an ecological mosaic rather than a single continuous mammoth steppe, and this variation matters directly for questions about where within the region human populations could have found the most reliable subsistence base.

Within the areas where mammoth-steppe grassland did predominate, this distinctive plant community supported an assemblage of large herbivorous mammals collectively referred to as Ice Age megafauna. Woolly mammoths grazed across these grasslands in substantial numbers, alongside woolly rhinoceroses in Eurasian portions of the range, steppe bison, wild horses, saiga antelope, and, in the American sector of Beringia, caribou. Mastodons, though present in the broader Pleistocene history of eastern Beringia, are associated primarily with earlier, warmer interglacial episodes, more than seventy-five thousand years before the period this chapter otherwise examines, when a browse-based diet of woody shrubs was more widely available; they should not be treated as a characteristic member of the terminal-Pleistocene mammoth-steppe grazing community discussed here. Predators including short-faced bears, scimitar cats, and Beringian gray wolves, a form well documented in the fossil record of the region and distinguished by researchers from the more southerly dire wolf, occupied the upper levels of this ecosystem's food web in North American Beringia, while cave lions and cave hyenas occupied a comparable role in Eurasian Beringia.

For human populations, the productivity of mammoth-steppe grassland, where it occurred, mattered enormously. A grassland capable of supporting large herds of grazing animals offered a viable subsistence base for mobile hunting communities, even at high latitudes and in a cold, dry climate that might otherwise seem inhospitable, while the shrub-tundra lowlands that covered much of the rest of the exposed land bridge likely supported a different, and probably less concentrated, mix of resources, including caribou, waterfowl, and riverine and coastal foods where accessible. Archaeological sites in unglaciated Beringia, examined further in Section 3.5 and this chapter's Evidentiary Source feature, show clear evidence of hunting, butchering, and the use of animal products, including bone, antler, ivory, and hide, for tools, shelter, clothing, and fuel.

Neither the mammoth-steppe grassland nor the shrub-tundra lowlands survived the end of the Ice Age in their Pleistocene form. As the climate warmed and became wetter beginning roughly fifteen thousand years ago, the drier grassland ecosystem across much of its former range gave way to the wetter, mossier tundra and, farther south, boreal forest that characterize the region today. This ecological transformation coincided with, and likely contributed to, the extinction or dramatic range contraction of many of the large mammal species associated with the mammoth steppe, a development examined further in Chapter 6's discussion of megafaunal extinction at the close of the Ice Age.

Section 3.4

Beringia as Homeland: The Standstill Hypothesis

One of the most significant developments in recent scholarship on the peopling of the Americas is the Beringian standstill hypothesis, sometimes called the Beringian incubation model. This hypothesis proposes that the population ancestral to later Native American peoples did not move directly from Siberia into the Americas in a single, continuous migration. Instead, according to this model, that ancestral population experienced a period of reduced gene flow and relative genetic isolation, plausibly located in or around Beringia, before contributing more broadly to populations elsewhere in the Americas. This textbook treats the standstill as a model-dependent reconstruction, one supported by genetic evidence but not yet confirmed by direct archaeological discovery of the population's location, rather than as an established historical fact in every particular.

The evidence supporting this hypothesis is primarily genetic, and the precise timing proposed has varied somewhat from study to study as methods and available genomic data have improved. A widely cited 2018 study, based on a high-coverage ancient genome from central Alaska, estimated an initial split between ancestral Native American and East Asian populations beginning around thirty-six thousand years ago, with continuing gene flow between the two lineages persisting until approximately twenty-five thousand years ago; within the ancestral American lineage, a population sometimes called "Ancient Beringians" appears to have branched away sometime between about twenty-two thousand and eighteen thousand years ago, while the northern and southern branches of Native American ancestry that eventually populated the rest of the continent diverged from one another later still, roughly between seventeen thousand five hundred and fourteen thousand six hundred years ago. An earlier and influential 2015 genomic study proposed a somewhat later initial divergence, around twenty thousand years ago and no earlier than about twenty-three thousand years ago, followed by a period of isolation lasting no more than approximately eight thousand years. More recent genomic research continues to refine these estimates, with some analyses placing the formation of the ancestral Indigenous American lineage at around twenty-five thousand years ago in or around the Beringian region. Rather than treating any single figure as settled, students should understand the standstill as probably lasting several millennia, with its precise beginning, duration, and geographic center still subject to revision as genomic methods and sample sizes improve.

Beringia is a plausible location for at least part of this process for several interlocking reasons. Geographically, it lay directly between Siberia and the rest of North America, both of which were the sources of, or destinations for, the population movements genetic evidence documents. Ecologically, portions of the region, discussed in Section 3.3, offered a viable subsistence base capable of supporting human communities for an extended period, though not uniformly across the entire landmass. And chronologically, much of the proposed period of reduced gene flow overlaps with the Last Glacial Maximum, precisely the period when the Laurentide and Cordilleran ice sheets are thought to have been most extensively merged, potentially restricting interior movement farther south and thereby providing one possible environmental contribution to the pattern genetic evidence documents.

This chronology must be reconciled carefully with the archaeological evidence, examined in Chapter 2, for a human presence at White Sands, New Mexico, securely dated to at least twenty-three thousand to twenty-one thousand years ago. Because this date falls within, rather than after, the period during which genetic models propose reduced gene flow associated with an ancestral Beringian population, the relationship between the two bodies of evidence remains an active area of research rather than a settled sequence. One possibility is that the White Sands population, or its immediate ancestors, had already separated from the more isolated Beringian lineage before the period of most reduced gene flow began; another is that population structure within and around Beringia during this period was more complex, and less uniformly isolated, than a simple single-population standstill model implies. This chapter presents the tension explicitly rather than resolving it, consistent with the evidentiary caution introduced in Chapter 1.

Archaeological evidence bearing directly on the standstill hypothesis remains limited, in large part because so much of Beringia's former land area now lies beneath the Bering and Chukchi Seas, inaccessible to conventional land-based excavation. Researchers have nonetheless identified sites, discussed further in this chapter's Evidentiary Source feature, in the unglaciated interior of Alaska and in far northeastern Siberia that document human presence in the Beringian region during or after the period genetic models associate with the standstill, though none of these currently known sites is old enough to directly confirm human presence throughout the entire duration any particular genetic model proposes. Some researchers have also proposed that submerged portions of the Bering-Chukchi shelf, now underwater but potentially preserving traces of Ice Age human activity, represent an important frontier for future underwater archaeological research, though such research faces substantial logistical and financial challenges. Genetic evidence, in short, can identify a demographic pattern consistent with extended occupation somewhere in or around Beringia; it cannot by itself locate a campsite on the now-submerged shelf, and students should be careful not to treat the standstill hypothesis as equivalent to direct archaeological proof of continuous Beringian settlement.

Historical Vocabulary
Refugium and Standstill

Refugium (plural: refugia) is a term ecologists and paleoecologists use for a region where a particular plant or animal population, or a distinctive ecosystem, persists during a period of broader environmental change, such as an ice age, that renders much of the surrounding landscape uninhabitable for that population or ecosystem. Unglaciated Beringia functioned as a refugium for steppe-tundra and shrub-tundra plant communities and their associated animal populations, and, according to the standstill hypothesis, may have served a comparable function for an ancestral human population as well.

Standstill, in the specific context of the Beringian standstill hypothesis, describes a period of relative genetic isolation, meaning substantially reduced gene flow between an ancestral population and other populations, rather than a claim that the population's geographic range or way of life was literally static. Geneticists infer such a period from patterns of accumulated genetic difference between populations; the term describes a demographic and statistical pattern, not a historical account of people remaining motionless in one place.

Potential misunderstanding: Students sometimes assume "standstill" means people were trapped, stranded, or unchanging. The hypothesis instead describes a genetic pattern consistent with a population that experienced reduced contact with other populations for a period, possibly while living, moving seasonally, and adapting within a broad region rather than a single fixed location.

Historical Debate

How Long Did the Standstill Last, and Where Exactly Did It Occur?

The question: Genetic evidence indicates a period of reduced gene flow between the ancestral Native American population and other Asian and American populations, but genetic data alone cannot specify precisely where that population lived, how many people it included, or how continuously it occupied a single region during that period. Researchers differ over how confidently this process can be located specifically within Beringia as opposed to other unglaciated regions, over how long the period of reduced gene flow actually lasted, and over how it should be reconciled with archaeological evidence for people already living south of the ice sheets during at least part of the same broad window.

Interpretation A — An extended, Beringia-centered period of reduced gene flow: Several influential ancient-genome studies estimate an initial divergence between ancestral Native American and East Asian lineages beginning sometime between about thirty-six thousand and twenty thousand years ago, with reduced gene flow persisting for a period that a number of researchers estimate at several thousand years, plausibly located primarily within or around Beringia, given the region's geographic position and, in places, ecological viability.
Interpretation B — A shorter, more geographically distributed, or more complex process: Other researchers caution that genetic divergence dates carry substantial statistical uncertainty, that different genomic studies have produced meaningfully different estimates for both the timing and duration of reduced gene flow, that the population's precise geographic location cannot be established from genetic data alone, and that population structure during this period may have been more complex and geographically distributed, potentially including groups already south of the ice sheets, than a single, uniform Beringian standstill model implies.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on ancient and contemporary genomic datasets, which have produced varying divergence and isolation estimates across different studies published over the past decade, combined with the archaeological and paleoecological evidence discussed in this chapter, including the White Sands footprints discussed in Chapter 2, which fall within the period some genetic models associate with reduced gene flow.

Why it matters: Where, for how long, and how uniformly any period of reduced gene flow occurred affects how historians and scientists understand the broader chronology of the peopling of the Americas, including how directly genetic and archaeological evidence can be reconciled.

Student task: Considering both the genetic and archaeological evidence discussed in this chapter and in Chapter 2, explain what would need to be true for both bodies of evidence to be reconciled, and identify what kind of future discovery, archaeological or genetic, might help narrow the range of current estimates.

Section 3.5

Living in a Cold, Dry, Windswept World

Whatever its precise duration, human life in Beringia required substantial technological and social adaptation to an environment that combined extreme cold, low precipitation, strong winds, long winter darkness, and, for much of the year, scarce plant food, with the compensating advantage of a productive population of large grazing animals. Reconstructing the daily lives of Beringia's human inhabitants depends on a combination of archaeological evidence from the relatively few excavated sites in Alaska and Siberia, comparative evidence from later well-documented Arctic and Subarctic hunting societies, and careful, cautious inference from the physical demands of the environment itself.

Clothing and shelter would have been essential to survival. Comparative evidence from later Arctic peoples, together with the fragmentary evidence of bone needles, scrapers, and hide-working tools recovered from Ice Age sites, suggests that effective cold-weather clothing, likely including multiple insulating layers, was a technological prerequisite for year-round habitation this far north. Shelter construction likely made use of available materials, including animal skins stretched over frames of wood, bone, or mammoth ivory, since Beringia's dry, largely treeless interior offered relatively little timber compared to regions farther south.

Subsistence depended heavily on the large grazing animals of the mammoth steppe, described in Section 3.3, supplemented by smaller game, birds, fish where rivers and coastlines were accessible, and, seasonally, edible plants. Successful large-mammal hunting in this environment likely required cooperative strategies among multiple households or bands, sophisticated knowledge of animal behavior and seasonal movement patterns, and specialized weaponry. Archaeological sites in Beringia have yielded evidence of composite hunting technology, including stone, bone, and ivory projectile points, some hafted onto foreshafts designed to detach from a spear or dart upon impact, a technological pattern with parallels across the broader Paleoindian and Upper Paleolithic worlds discussed further in Chapter 5.

Mobility was almost certainly essential. Given the low overall productivity of any single location in a cold, dry grassland environment, human groups likely moved seasonally, following the movement of game herds and shifting between different resource zones as conditions changed throughout the year. This pattern of high residential mobility, organized around small, flexible kin-based groups, is consistent both with the archaeological evidence from Beringian sites and with ethnographic patterns documented among later Arctic and Subarctic hunting peoples, though this textbook is careful, as Chapter 1 emphasized, not to assume that later documented societies provide a direct, unchanged model for Ice Age communities many thousands of years earlier.

Evidentiary Source Window

The Upward Sun River Site, Central Alaska

Location: Tanana River valley, central Alaska, within the traditional homeland of Alaska Native communities represented by the Healy Lake Tribal Council · Type of evidence: Archaeological excavation and associated skeletal and genomic analysis, conducted in consultation with local Native governing bodies · Approximate age of key finds: Roughly 11,500 years ago

Context: Because Beringia's own former landmass now lies largely beneath the Bering and Chukchi Seas, archaeologists studying the region rely heavily on sites located in its unglaciated American sector, present-day interior Alaska, which remained dry land throughout the period this chapter examines. The Upward Sun River site, excavated over multiple seasons beginning in the early twenty-first century in consultation with the Healy Lake Tribal Council and the Tanana Chiefs Conference, has produced some of the most detailed evidence available anywhere in Beringia's former range, including a seasonal residential camp, hearth features, faunal remains documenting a broad-based subsistence strategy, and the remains of two infants, referred to in the scientific literature as USR1 and USR2, whose study and treatment were conducted under agreements developed with these regional Native governing bodies.

A high-coverage genome sequenced from USR1 indicated an ancestry closely related to, but genetically distinct from, other ancient and contemporary Native American populations, leading researchers to describe the population it represents as a previously unrecognized ancient Beringian genetic lineage; mitochondrial DNA recovered from both infants contributed further evidence for this interpretation.

Section 3.6

Possible Routes Out of Beringia

If ancestral Native American populations did remain within Beringia for an extended period before dispersing farther into the Americas, a key question follows directly: by what route, or routes, did people eventually move south? Two principal hypotheses have dominated scholarly discussion, and current evidence suggests that both may have played a role at different times.

The Ice-Free Corridor Hypothesis

The older and, for much of the twentieth century, dominant hypothesis proposed that people moved south through an interior ice-free corridor, a gap that opened as the Laurentide and Cordilleran ice sheets retreated from one another along the eastern flank of the Rocky Mountains, roughly following the modern boundary between Alberta and British Columbia. According to this model, this corridor would have opened sufficiently, and would have supported sufficient plant and animal life to sustain human travelers, by some point after the Last Glacial Maximum, allowing movement from unglaciated Beringia and interior Alaska into the unglaciated Great Plains south of the retreating ice.

Research over the past several decades has complicated this model considerably. Detailed reconstruction of the corridor's biological viability, using ancient DNA recovered from lake-bed sediments along the proposed route, suggests that the corridor physically opened, in the sense that the Laurentide and Cordilleran ice sheets separated from one another, by roughly fourteen thousand years ago, but that its ecological usability developed later still and remains debated: some evidence suggests usable conditions by roughly thirteen thousand five hundred to thirteen thousand years ago, while environmental DNA evidence documents steppe vegetation and large mammals present by about twelve thousand six hundred years ago. Under any of these estimates, the corridor's viability falls considerably later than the archaeological evidence for human presence at White Sands, New Mexico, discussed in Chapter 2, which places people south of the ice sheets by at least twenty-three thousand to twenty-one thousand years ago, and later even than the second Pacific coastal window discussed below. This timing problem has led many researchers to look more seriously at an alternative route, at least for the initial movement of people into the Americas.

The Pacific Coastal Route Hypothesis

The Pacific coastal route hypothesis proposes that people moved south from Beringia along the Pacific coastline, using boats, coastal foraging, and marine and estuarine resources, potentially well before the interior ice-free corridor became biologically viable. Geological and glaciological reconstruction published in recent years identifies two comparatively favorable windows for such coastal movement, both considerably earlier than the corridor's own viability: an earlier window around twenty-four thousand five hundred to twenty-two thousand years ago, and a later window around sixteen thousand four hundred to fourteen thousand eight hundred years ago. The earlier window is of particular significance because it falls before, rather than after, the White Sands dates discussed in Chapter 2, offering one plausible, though not yet directly proven, explanation for how people might have reached the interior of North America by that time. Importantly, this research also emphasizes that the coast was not simply open and easily traveled throughout this period; strong ocean currents, actively calving tidewater glaciers, and shifting ice margins would have made coastal travel difficult or impassable at various points even within these more favorable windows, meaning the coastal route should be understood as a plausible and evidence-supported hypothesis rather than a simple, continuously open pathway.

This hypothesis has gained increasing scholarly support in recent decades, aided by evidence that portions of the Pacific coastline, particularly around the Alexander Archipelago in present-day southeastern Alaska and British Columbia, remained free of glacial ice, or became ice-free earlier than the continental interior, and supported productive coastal and marine ecosystems capable of sustaining human populations with appropriate watercraft and maritime knowledge. Testing the coastal route hypothesis directly is difficult, because much of the relevant ancient coastline now lies underwater, submerged by the same post-glacial sea level rise that eventually drowned Beringia itself, discussed in Section 3.7. Underwater archaeological survey along the Pacific coast has begun to identify promising submerged landscapes and, in some cases, artifacts, but this remains a developing and technically demanding field of research.

Evaluating the Hypotheses

Current scholarly opinion increasingly treats an early Pacific coastal migration, potentially during the twenty-four-thousand-five-hundred to twenty-two-thousand-year-ago window, as a leading hypothesis for explaining how people could have reached regions south of the ice sheets by the time securely documented at White Sands, given how much later the interior ice-free corridor appears to have become ecologically usable. This does not mean the interior corridor was never used; once it became usable, somewhere in the range of roughly thirteen thousand five hundred to twelve thousand six hundred years ago depending on the criterion applied, it may well have served as a route for further movement and continued contact between northern and southern populations, and the second, roughly sixteen thousand four hundred to fourteen thousand eight hundred-year-ago coastal window may have offered a further, still-earlier opportunity for coastal movement during the same general span. Both hypotheses remain active subjects of research, and this textbook presents them as complementary possibilities operating at different times rather than as a resolved either-or choice, consistent with the evidentiary caution introduced in Chapter 1 and applied throughout Chapter 2's discussion of the broader peopling debate.

Section 3.7

The Drowning of a Continent

Beringia's disappearance was, like its formation, a consequence of changing global sea level, though the ending of its role as a continuous overland connection appears to have come more abruptly than the gradual, multi-thousand-year submergence of Beringia's lowest margins might suggest. As the Laurentide, Cordilleran, and other continental ice sheets melted following the end of the Last Glacial Maximum, the water they released flowed back into the world's oceans, causing global sea levels to rise over several thousand years. This process, sometimes called post-glacial or Holocene sea level rise, proceeded unevenly, with periods of more rapid rise, some associated with sudden, large-scale ice sheet collapse events, punctuating a broader trend of increase, and it progressively inundated the lowest-lying margins of exposed Beringian land well before the final overland connection was lost.

Geological and geophysical research on sediment cores from the floor of the Bering Strait indicates that the decisive event, the breaching of the strait itself and the end of continuous dry-land connection between Asia and North America, occurred relatively rapidly at approximately eleven thousand calibrated years ago. This does not mean the surrounding coastal and shelf environment then remained fixed; shorelines, river systems, and nearshore environments in the Bering and Chukchi Seas region continued to shift and evolve for several millennia afterward as sea level continued rising toward its modern level. Students should therefore distinguish between the breaching of the strait itself, a comparatively rapid event around eleven thousand years ago that ended overland travel, and the longer, more gradual process of shoreline change that continued to reshape the region's coastal geography over the following millennia.

The end of overland connection did not, however, end contact between the peoples of northeastern Asia and northwestern North America. This point deserves particular emphasis, because it is easy, but historically inaccurate, to assume that the disappearance of dry land meant the disappearance of any relationship between the two continents. Once the strait had formed, it became not a barrier but a different kind of connection, a maritime one. Watercraft-using populations, including communities associated with the Arctic Small Tool tradition, are documented crossing the strait by boat within the past several thousand years, and archaeological and ethnographic evidence documents centuries of continuing trade, exchange of technology and ideas, seasonal travel, and, in many documented cases, kinship and family ties that spanned the strait well into the modern era, including sustained cross-strait Native travel and family connections that persisted into the twentieth century before being substantially curtailed by later political border restrictions. The transformation of Beringia should therefore be understood as a change in the mode of connection between two continents, from a land bridge to a maritime strait, rather than as the permanent severing of contact between their peoples.

The submergence of most of Beringia's former land area did, nonetheless, have lasting consequences for the historical record available to researchers. It erased, beneath the waters of the Bering and Chukchi Seas, whatever archaeological evidence of human habitation may have existed across the vast majority of Beringia's former land area, leaving researchers dependent on the comparatively small unglaciated areas of Alaska and Siberia that remained above water, together with underwater archaeological survey of increasing but still limited scope, to reconstruct a landscape and history that is, in the most literal sense, largely submerged.

The disappearance of Beringia's dry-land connection coincided with, though should not be assumed to have directly caused, other major environmental transformations at the close of the Ice Age, including the retreat of the mammoth-steppe and shrub-tundra ecosystems described in Section 3.3 and the extinction or dramatic range contraction of many large Ice Age mammal species, developments examined in full in Chapter 6. By the time these transformations had run their course, the physical and ecological conditions that had made Beringia a distinctive, and possibly formative, environment in the early history of Indigenous North America had given way to the strait, the seas, and the two separated, though never entirely disconnected, continents that appear on maps today.

Cause and Effect

Sea Level, Ice, and the Making and Unmaking of Beringia

Long-term causes

Recurring glacial-interglacial cycles over hundreds of thousands of years, driven by long-term variations in Earth's orbit and axial tilt, repeatedly locked and released large volumes of water in continental ice sheets.

Short-term causes

The particular intensity and duration of the Last Glacial Maximum produced sea levels more than one hundred meters below present levels, sufficient to expose the unusually broad, shallow Bering-Chukchi continental shelf as dry land.

Effect

A continental-scale, largely unglaciated landmass connected Siberia and North America for a period of many thousands of years, potentially serving as both habitat and, eventually, corridor for ancestral Native American populations.

Reversal and long-term consequence

Post-glacial warming released ice-sheet water back into the oceans, breaching the Bering Strait at approximately eleven thousand years ago and ending the continuous overland connection between Asia and North America, while shoreline environments continued to evolve for several thousand years afterward as sea level kept rising toward modern levels.

Historical complexity: The same physical process, glacial-interglacial sea level change, both created and destroyed Beringia. Historians and scientists must therefore treat Beringia's existence as a temporary, climate-dependent condition rather than a permanent geographic feature, with direct consequences for how the peopling of the Americas is understood.

Multiple Perspectives

Reading the Same Landscape: Disciplinary Perspectives on Beringia

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns scholars and communities have documented. They are not quotations from any single named individual.

From the perspective of a population geneticist: Beringia is best understood through patterns of genetic divergence and isolation encoded in DNA, both ancient and contemporary, which suggest a period of separation between ancestral American and Asian populations that most plausibly, though not certainly, occurred within the Beringian region itself.
From the perspective of a paleoecologist: Beringia is a case study in how climate, sea level, and vegetation interact to create and destroy entire ecosystems, and the mammoth steppe's productivity, and its eventual collapse, offer a way of understanding how environmental change shaped the possibilities available to the humans and animals living within it.
From the perspective of an archaeologist working in interior Alaska: Beringia is frustratingly, and fascinatingly, incomplete: the richest and most direct evidence of daily human life is likely to lie either in the small unglaciated areas that remain accessible on land or beneath modern seas, requiring patient, site-by-site excavation rather than sweeping generalization.
From the perspective of a contemporary Iñupiat or Yupik community member reflecting on ancestral homelands: Long habitation in the North is not a scientific hypothesis alone but a matter of ancestral connection to a specific landscape, and oral traditions describing deep, longstanding ties to northern lands and waters offer a complementary form of knowledge alongside, and sometimes in tension with, the specific chronological claims of genetic and archaeological research.
Continuity and Change Over Time

A Landscape That Vanished, A Population That Persisted

What Changed

  • Beringia's physical extent expanded and contracted repeatedly with fluctuating sea levels, eventually disappearing entirely beneath rising post-glacial seas
  • The mammoth steppe ecosystem gave way to wetter tundra and boreal forest as the climate warmed at the close of the Pleistocene
  • Populations on either side of the former land connection lost continuous overland contact once the strait was breached, though maritime travel, trade, and kinship ties across the strait continued for thousands of years afterward, discussed in Section 3.7
  • Subsistence strategies shifted as the large-mammal-based economy of the mammoth steppe became less viable with the extinction and range contraction of megafauna, examined in Chapter 6

What Stayed the Same

  • Human communities continued to occupy the unglaciated regions of Alaska and adjoining Siberia after Beringia's core landmass disappeared, adapting their subsistence strategies to new environmental conditions
  • Genetic continuity persisted between the ancestral Beringian population and the diverse Indigenous peoples of the Americas who descended from it
  • Cold-climate technological traditions, including composite hunting weapons and skin-based clothing and shelter, continued to develop and diversify among northern peoples long after Beringia itself vanished
  • Contact between the peoples of northeastern Asia and northwestern North America continued across the strait for thousands of years after the land connection was lost, sustained by maritime travel, trade, and kinship rather than overland movement, discussed in Section 3.7

Why the pattern matters: Beringia illustrates a case in which a specific physical landscape disappeared entirely while the human population associated with it persisted and diversified, a reminder that continuity in human history does not always depend on continuity in the physical landscape a people once occupied.

Everyday Life and Social History

Daily Survival in the Beringian North

Behind the genetic and geological evidence discussed throughout this chapter lie the ordinary, largely undocumented rhythms of daily life: families constructing shelter against the wind, hunters tracking herds of bison or mammoth across open grassland, the careful maintenance of fire in an environment offering little timber, and the transmission, presumably through spoken instruction and demonstration rather than any surviving written or pictorial record, of the specialized knowledge required to survive in one of the coldest, driest inhabited environments on Earth during the Ice Age.

Comparative evidence from later, well-documented Arctic and Subarctic societies, while not a direct model for Beringian life many thousands of years earlier, suggests the likely importance of extended kin networks, cooperative hunting, careful seasonal scheduling of movement and resource use, and specialized knowledge of animal behavior, ice and snow conditions, and plant availability. Because Beringia's population likely remained small and highly mobile, archaeological evidence of these everyday patterns is sparse, concentrated in the relatively few excavated sites in unglaciated Alaska, and dependent on careful, comparative inference rather than direct documentation.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined Beringia, the vast unglaciated landmass connecting Siberia and North America during the last Ice Age, as an environment and possible ancestral homeland central to the early history of Indigenous North America. Rather than treating this region merely as a narrow bridge crossed quickly on the way to a destination farther south, the chapter has presented Beringia as a continental-scale landscape, formed by dramatic sea level fall during the Last Glacial Maximum, that may have supported human communities for a substantial period in its own right.

The chapter explained the physical mechanics of Beringia's formation: falling global sea levels, driven by the accumulation of water in continental ice sheets, exposed the shallow continental shelf beneath the modern Bering and Chukchi Seas as dry land, while the region's low precipitation kept much of its interior free of the glacial ice that covered so much of the rest of northern North America.

Students encountered the mammoth steppe, the cold, dry, and, where it occurred, surprisingly productive grassland ecosystem that supported woolly mammoths, steppe bison, wild horses, and other large mammals across parts of Beringia and northern Eurasia, and examined the evidence that unglaciated Beringia was best understood as an ecological mosaic, with steppe-tundra grassland concentrated in specific uplands and extensive shrub-tundra covering much of the now-submerged central and northern lowlands, together providing the subsistence foundation for human hunting communities in the region.

The chapter presented the Beringian standstill hypothesis as a central, though still actively debated, framework for understanding this period, examining the genetic evidence for a period of reduced gene flow and relative isolation between ancestral Native American and East Asian populations, plausibly centered in or around Beringia, and evaluating the archaeological evidence, most notably from sites such as Upward Sun River in central Alaska, that bears on this hypothesis.

Students also examined the two principal hypotheses for how people eventually moved out of Beringia into the rest of the Americas: an interior ice-free corridor route and a Pacific coastal route, weighing the evidence that increasingly favors an early coastal dispersal while acknowledging that both routes may have played complementary roles at different times.

The chapter explained how Beringia's own dry-land connection came to an end, driven by the same sea level dynamics that had created it: the Bering Strait was breached at approximately eleven thousand years ago, ending continuous overland travel between Asia and North America, even as the region's shorelines continued to evolve for several thousand years afterward. Crucially, this transformation changed the mode of connection between the two continents rather than ending contact between their peoples altogether; maritime travel, trade, and kinship across the strait continued for thousands of years, a continuity that complicates any simple narrative of permanent separation.

Throughout, the chapter has emphasized the specific character and limitations of the evidence available for this period: genetic reconstruction, paleoecological analysis, a small number of archaeological sites in the unglaciated regions that remain above water, and, where relevant, the oral traditions of contemporary Arctic and Subarctic Indigenous peoples, each offering a different and necessarily partial view of a landscape now largely inaccessible to direct study.

These themes, environmental reconstruction, genetic evidence, archaeological caution, and Indigenous perspective, prepare students for Chapter 4's detailed examination of the archaeological evidence for human presence south of Beringia, where the chronological and evidentiary tensions introduced in this chapter and in Chapter 2 receive fuller treatment.

Bering Land Bridge
The specific exposed stretch of former seafloor connecting Siberia and Alaska where open water now lies, sometimes used loosely as a synonym for Beringia as a whole.
Last Glacial Maximum
The period, roughly twenty-six thousand to twenty thousand years ago, when continental ice sheets reached their greatest extent and global sea levels reached their lowest point.
Laurentide Ice Sheet
The larger of the two major North American ice sheets, centered over Hudson Bay and eventually covering most of Canada and parts of the northern United States.
Cordilleran Ice Sheet
The smaller ice sheet covering the mountainous regions of western Canada and the Pacific Northwest during the last glacial period.
Mammoth steppe
A cold, dry grassland ecosystem, dominated by grasses and forbs rather than moss and lichen, that supported large Ice Age herbivores across parts of Beringia and northern Eurasia; much of the submerged Beringian land bridge itself was instead covered by moist shrub tundra, making the region an ecological mosaic rather than a single continuous grassland.
Megafauna
Large mammal species, including woolly mammoths, steppe bison, and wild horses, that inhabited the mammoth steppe and other Ice Age ecosystems.
Refugium
A region where a population or ecosystem persists during a period of broader environmental change that renders surrounding areas uninhabitable.
Beringian standstill hypothesis
A model proposing a period of substantially reduced gene flow and relative isolation in the ancestry of Indigenous American populations, plausibly centered in or around Beringia, with its precise location, timing, and duration still debated.
Genetic divergence
The accumulation of genetic differences between populations as gene flow between them becomes reduced or ceases.
Ice-free corridor
A proposed interior route between the retreating Laurentide and Cordilleran ice sheets, once considered the primary route of human movement out of Beringia.
Pacific coastal route
A proposed route of human movement south from Beringia along the Pacific coastline, using maritime and coastal resources, increasingly favored by current evidence.
Continental shelf
The relatively shallow seafloor bordering a continent, portions of which become exposed as dry land when sea levels fall sufficiently.
Eustatic sea level
Global sea level considered as a single worldwide measure, as distinct from local sea level changes caused by land movement.
Pleistocene
The geological epoch, lasting from about 2.6 million to about 11,700 years ago, encompassing the last Ice Age and the period this chapter primarily examines.
Holocene
The current geological epoch, beginning approximately 11,700 years ago, characterized by a generally warmer and more stable climate than the preceding Pleistocene.
Younger Dryas
A sharp, roughly 1,200-year cold interval interrupting the general post-glacial warming trend, ending around 11,700 years ago.
Paleoecology
The scientific study of past ecosystems, reconstructed from evidence such as fossil pollen, plant remains, and animal remains.
Haplogroup
A genetic population group defined by shared mutations, used by geneticists to trace patterns of descent and migration among human populations.
Composite tool
A tool assembled from multiple components, such as a stone or bone point hafted onto a separate foreshaft and spear, common in Ice Age hunting technology.
Bathymetric map
A map depicting the depth and shape of underwater terrain, used by researchers to reconstruct the former extent of exposed Beringian land.

Major People and Concepts

Identification & Significance

Beringia
The unglaciated region connecting Siberia and North America during the last Ice Age, the central subject of this chapter and a leading candidate for the location of the Beringian standstill population.
The mammoth steppe
The cold, dry grassland ecosystem that supported large Ice Age herbivores across parts of Beringia and northern Eurasia, existing alongside extensive shrub-tundra lowlands across much of the rest of the region in an overall ecological mosaic, discussed in Section 3.3.
The Beringian standstill hypothesis
A model proposing a period of substantially reduced gene flow and relative isolation in the ancestry of Indigenous American populations, plausibly centered in or around Beringia, with its precise location, timing, and duration still debated, discussed in Section 3.4 and this chapter's Historical Debate feature.
The Upward Sun River site
An archaeological site in the Tanana River valley of central Alaska providing some of the most detailed direct evidence of human life in the American sector of Beringia near the close of the Ice Age, discussed in this chapter's Evidentiary Source Window.
The ice-free corridor and Pacific coastal route hypotheses
The two principal proposed routes by which people may have moved from Beringia into the rest of North America, discussed in Section 3.6.
The Laurentide and Cordilleran Ice Sheets
The two major continental ice sheets whose extent and retreat shaped possible routes of human movement out of Beringia, discussed in Section 3.2.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to imagine a landscape that no longer exists: a vast northern ecological mosaic, combining steppe-tundra grassland in some uplands with extensive shrub-tundra across much of its lowlands, stretching between two continents that are today separated by open ocean. Beringia matters to the history of Indigenous North America not simply because it once provided a route between Asia and the Americas, but because a growing body of genetic and archaeological evidence suggests it may have served, for at least part of a longer and more complex process, as an important ancestral homeland, a place where populations experienced reduced gene flow, structured into distinct branches, and adapted to a demanding and varied environment, even as other populations or branches of the same broader ancestral lineage were already moving toward, or established in, regions farther south in the continent.

The chapter has also modeled a particular kind of historical reasoning appropriate to evidence of this kind: careful attention to what genetic, archaeological, and paleoecological evidence can and cannot establish, willingness to hold competing hypotheses, such as the ice-free corridor and coastal route models, in productive tension rather than forcing premature resolution, and recognition that much of the physical evidence relevant to this period now lies beyond direct reach, submerged beneath modern seas.

Understanding Beringia also reinforces a theme introduced in Chapter 1 and continued throughout this textbook: that Indigenous history did not begin with any single, simple point of origin. Even at the very outset of human presence in the Americas, the evidence points not to a single dramatic crossing but to a prolonged, environmentally shaped process of habitation, adaptation, and eventual dispersal, a pattern of gradual, uneven change that will recur throughout this textbook's treatment of later migrations, technological developments, and political transformations.

The story of Beringia connects directly to the chapters that follow. Chapter 4 examines the archaeological record of human presence south of Beringia in detail, testing the chronological expectations this chapter has established against the physical evidence of the Clovis tradition and earlier pre-Clovis sites. Chapter 5 follows the diverse Paleoindian societies that emerged as descendants of Beringia's population spread across the unglaciated regions of the continent, and Chapter 6 examines the environmental transformations that brought the Ice Age, and the world Beringia's peoples had known, to a close.
