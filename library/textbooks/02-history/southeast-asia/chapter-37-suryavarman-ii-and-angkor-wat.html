Suryavarman II and Angkor Wat

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Angkor Wat is the monument of a king, but it is also the monument of many later generations who worshipped, modified, and remembered it.

Sūryavarman II (r. 1113–c. 1150) ruled during a period of ambitious Khmer expansion, ceremonial diplomacy with Song China, and campaigns that met serious resistance. His reign is known from inscriptions, Chinese and Vietnamese records, Cham sources, and above all the monument itself. This chapter asks how he pursued power, how Angkor Wat expressed his ambitions, and how later people reshaped its meaning.

Two bodies of evidence make this possible, and they must be read differently. The first is textual: Khmer and Cham inscriptions, Chinese court records, and Vietnamese chronicles, each written for its own audience. The second is the monument: its architecture, its reliefs, and the later inscriptions that record Buddhist worship there. The first shows a king in his regional setting. The second shows how a court wished to present itself, and how later people used the building.

The chapter is organized in six divisions: the king and the sources, diplomacy and war, building Angkor Wat, its religious and artistic program, the debate over its purpose and its later religious life, and its memory in modern times. Students should keep three histories distinct: the reign, the idealized kingship in stone, and the monument’s long afterlife.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  2. Evaluate the sources for the reign and their limits.
  3. Trace the campaigns against Đại Việt and Champa.
  4. Distinguish occupation from integration.
  5. Describe Angkor Wat’s architecture and construction.
  6. Interpret the Vaiṣṇava program of the reliefs.
  7. Distinguish twelfth-century from later relief work.
  8. Assess the temple-or-tomb debate.
  9. Trace the monument’s later Buddhist history.
  10. Connect Angkor Wat to Cham and Vietnamese regional history.

Historical Context

By the early twelfth century Angkor had centuries of royal building behind it (Chapters 33–36), and its kings competed through elite networks, patronage, and war. Chapter 34 covers kingship, Chapter 35 the urban landscape, and Chapter 36 religion; this chapter applies them to one reign and one monument. The later Cham war of 1177 belongs to Chapter 38.

The political setting matters as much as the religious one. The Khmer court competed with Đại Việt and with Cham polities, and its kings sought recognition from China. A king who took power in a contested succession needed victories, ritual authority, and monuments, and these needs shaped both his campaigns and his building. At the same time, the sources are uneven: Khmer inscriptions glorify patrons, Vietnamese chronicles favor the Lý court, and Cham inscriptions survive in smaller numbers.

Students should therefore approach each claim by asking who made the source, when, and for whom. This habit is especially useful for Angkor Wat, whose meaning has been interpreted in different ways by twelfth-century patrons, sixteenth-century Buddhist donors, colonial-era scholars, and modern Cambodians.

Section 37.1

Sūryavarman II and the Khmer World of the Early Twelfth Century

Division I — Accession, Authority, and Evidence

Sūryavarman II ruled Angkor from 1113 until about 1150, after a period in which succession had often been contested among regional elite networks. He is remembered for Angkor Wat, but his reign also included Song diplomacy and the most ambitious Khmer campaigns against Đại Việt and Champa recorded for the period. This chapter treats the king and the monument as connected but separable histories.

The early twelfth century followed a period of shifting royal lines. Jayavarman VI, who ruled from 1080 to 1107, and Dharaṇindravarman I, who followed him, are associated with an elite network often called the Mahīdharapura line, a label that is partly a modern convenience. Their power seems to have rested on regional bases outside the old capital as much as on Angkor itself, which means that a king in 1113 inherited a court whose loyalties had to be won.

The reign also marks a change of emphasis. Earlier kings had built important Śaiva foundations, while Sūryavarman II’s court turned decisively to Viṣṇu. That shift did not end Śaiva or Buddhist worship, but it suggests a ruler who used religious patronage to shape a distinctive royal identity at the same time as he pursued regional power.

Section 37.2

Reconstructing the Reign: Inscriptions, Foreign Records, and Monumental Evidence

Evidence comes from Khmer inscriptions, Angkor Wat’s reliefs, Chinese records, Vietnamese chronicles, Cham inscriptions, and later reconstructions. Each served its own purpose: Vietnamese chronicles stress Lý defense, Chinese records note embassies and titles, and Cham inscriptions illuminate Cham politics. Contemporary Khmer inscriptional evidence for parts of the reign is limited, so historians must compare sources rather than reconstruct the reign from the monument alone.

The inscriptional record is uneven. Royal and temple inscriptions in Sanskrit and Old Khmer record donations, lineages, appointments, and praise, and they were written to glorify patrons. Surviving texts say much about the king’s birth, his religious specialists, and the temples of his elite relatives, but comparatively little about administration, warfare, or the reign’s end. This is why the Vietnamese chronicles and Chinese records, which were written for different audiences, have such value.

Angkor Wat itself is evidence of a different kind. Its architecture and reliefs show ideals, not events, and the monument cannot be used as a transparent record of the reign. Historians therefore compare each source type, ask who created it and why, and treat agreement between independent sources as stronger than any single claim. Gaps remain, and the account in this chapter is necessarily provisional.

Dating requires translation between systems. Khmer inscriptions use the Śaka era, which begins in 78 CE, so each date has to be converted, and Chinese records follow imperial reign periods. Vietnamese chronicles such as the Đại Việt sử ký toàn thư were compiled in later centuries from earlier records, so they combine early material with later editing. Cham inscriptions, in Sanskrit and Old Cham, give the Cham elite’s own account but survive in smaller numbers. Each of these conventions affects how confidently a date or event can be placed.

Section 37.3

The Mahīdharapura Connection and the Accession of 1113

Sūryavarman II took the throne in 1113 after rivalry among claimants, and later texts connect him to the Mahīdharapura elite lineage. Genealogies in royal texts served legitimation, so documented relationships must be separated from uncertain reconstruction.

According to Cœdès’s synthesis, drawing on later inscriptions, Sūryavarman II took the throne in 1113 by force, defeating a rival claimant. Scholars treat the details with caution, since the surviving accounts were written to legitimize the victor, and the identity of the rival and the circumstances are reconstructed in different ways. What is clear is that succession was contested and that the new king needed to build support after it.

His family connections were part of that work. Inscriptions associate relatives with important foundations, and at Phanom Rung on the Khorat Plateau UNESCO identifies twelfth-century construction linked to a relative of the king. Such evidence suggests that provincial elite families helped to sustain the court, but it does not allow a complete genealogy. The surviving record therefore suggests regional elite support without establishing every relationship claimed by later genealogies.

Vocabulary: Dynastic legitimacy
Dynastic legitimacy

The claims, genealogical, religious, and military, by which a ruler justified his right to the throne. Royal inscriptions assert these claims and are not neutral genealogies.

Section 37.4

Royal Authority, Court Patronage, and the Consolidation of Power

The king combined military success, religious patronage, appointments, alliances, and royal imagery. Chapter 34 treats Khmer institutions generally; the point here is that he operated within an inherited, competitive system, not above it. He was a ruler as well as a temple builder.

Several instruments of power worked together. Military success, especially in the campaigns of Division II, showed the king’s ability to protect and enrich his followers. Religious specialists, especially Divākarapaṇḍita, who is documented as performing the king’s consecration, provided ritual legitimation. Appointments, family alliances, and the grant of land and rank, which Chapter 34 explains in general terms, bound elites to the court.

Royal display completed the picture. Angkor Wat, the procession relief, and diplomatic embassies all projected authority to different audiences: courtiers, rivals, and foreign courts. None of these made the king’s rule secure by itself. The sources do not allow us to measure how effective each instrument was, and the pressures of repeated warfare may have strained the system, a possibility that Chapter 38 takes up.

Appointments and honors recorded in inscriptions, such as titles and land grants, show a court rewarding service, but the surviving texts mostly record the rewarded, so the extent of royal control remains uncertain. A king’s reputation as a conqueror also made legitimacy fragile: successes strengthened it, and failures such as those against Đại Việt could weaken it. The monuments and reliefs of the reign should therefore be read as efforts to sustain authority, not as proof that it was secure.

Section 37.5

The Regional Political Order: Angkor, Champa, Đại Việt, and China

Division II — Diplomacy, Warfare, and Regional Ambition

Angkor faced Đại Việt under the Lý dynasty to the northeast, Cham polities along the coast, and Song China as a distant diplomatic and commercial partner. These were shifting courts with overlapping interests, not modern national territories, and Champa in particular was a set of regional centers rather than one unified state.

Four centers of power shaped the setting. Angkor, with its agricultural base around the Tonle Sap, looked outward toward the Khorat Plateau and the Mekong. Đại Việt, under the Lý dynasty, was centered on the Red River delta at Thăng Long. Champa was a set of coastal polities, notably Vijaya in what is now Bình Định and Panduranga farther south, linked by sea trade. Song China mattered chiefly as a trading partner and a source of recognition.

These courts were entangled long before 1113. Lý campaigns against Champa in the eleventh century, notably in 1069, gave Đại Việt territory in northern Champa, so Cham rulers had reason to fear their northern neighbor and sometimes to look toward Angkor. Khmer rulers saw the Cham coast as an outlet for trade and as a rival zone. The result was a shifting triangle in which alliances changed with circumstances and authority faded across overlapping frontiers, not fixed borders. Chapter 45 gives the longer history of Champa and Đại Việt.

Geography set the terms of competition. Angkor’s heartland lay north of the Tonle Sap, separated from the middle Mekong and the Cham coast by uplands and from Đại Việt by the Annamite ranges. Đại Việt’s southern frontier lay in the north-central coast region, and Champa’s settled coast was a narrow strip between mountains and sea. Any army moving between these centers faced long supply lines and difficult terrain, which helps explain why large campaigns could win battles without producing lasting control.

Section 37.6

Diplomacy with Song China and the Wider Region

Chinese records report Khmer embassies in 1116 and 1120, and Song sources also record honorific titles conferred on the king. Such exchanges brought recognition, prestige, and trade opportunities, but they should not automatically be read as political subordination, since each court framed ceremonial exchange to suit its own audience.

Chinese records report Khmer embassies in 1116 and 1120, under the Northern Song, and also report honorific titles conferred on the king, which Cœdès places in 1128, after the Song court had moved south. For the Song court such missions fitted a ceremony of recognition, and the Chinese vocabulary for them reflects the Chinese court’s own view. A modern reader should not assume that either side understood the exchange as the king’s subordination.

For Angkor the embassies had practical value: access to Chinese markets and goods, prestige, and recognition at a time when the king needed it. The records are valuable because they date events independently of Khmer inscriptions, but they are brief and reflect Chinese interests, and the Khmer side of the missions is poorly documented. Their brevity shows how uneven the evidence is, not that the missions were unimportant.

Khmer embassies of 1116 and 1120 occurred under the Northern Song. After the Song court’s move south in 1127, maritime commerce became increasingly important to the dynasty. This later development provides context for continuing diplomatic and commercial relations, not an explanation of why the earlier missions took place. The records do not reveal Khmer aims in detail.

Section 37.7

Campaigns against Đại Việt: The Wars of 1128–1138

Following George Cœdès’s synthesis, Khmer attacks on Đại Việt are dated 1128 and 1132 (with Cham participation), with a further campaign dated 1138 in his account and 1137 in Vietnamese accounts. Lý forces repelled them, and the Khmer gained no lasting territory. Distance, terrain, and supply limited operations, and Vietnamese chronicles, written from the defenders' side, are our main narrative source, so Khmer motives are largely inferred.

The sources are asymmetrical. Vietnamese chronicles, compiled by later historians drawing on Lý records, report incursions on the southern frontier and the Lý court's responses. No comparable Khmer narrative of the campaigns survives, and Angkor Wat's reliefs show an army in procession but not these battles. The wars are therefore known chiefly as the defenders remembered them, and Khmer aims, whether plunder, captives, frontier control, or prestige, must be inferred.

Geography explains much of the outcome. Armies had to cross the Mekong lowlands and the Annamite uplands or move along the coast, with long supply lines through forested, thinly settled country. A force able to raid could not necessarily hold territory far from Angkor's agricultural base. The repeated attempts nonetheless show that the king considered the northeastern frontier worth the cost. Hostility was not permanent or inevitable: Chapter 45 shows that Khmer, Cham, and Vietnamese courts also traded, negotiated, and changed sides.

The narrative that follows should be read as a reconstruction. Vietnamese chronicles report Khmer attacks on frontier regions, with the Nghệ An region named in at least some campaigns, and report that Lý forces repelled them. Vietnamese accounts identify Nghệ An as the target of the attack of 1128 and of the combined Khmer–Cham attack of 1132. Cœdès dates a further Khmer campaign against Đại Việt to 1138, while Vietnamese historical accounts record an attack on Nghệ An in 1137; the discrepancy is retained here rather than resolved without further evidence. The chronicles do not say what the Khmer court intended to gain, and the lack of Khmer narratives means that explanations such as seeking plunder, prestige, or strategic security cannot be tested directly.

The pattern is clearer than the details. Large Khmer forces, in one case supported by Cham allies, crossed difficult country and met a state with well-established defenses, and the campaigns ended without lasting territorial change. For a ruler who needed military credit, such failures were costly, but they did not end his reign, which suggests that his political base was strong enough to absorb them.

Section 37.8

Champa: Alliance, Rivalry, and Shifting Diplomatic Relationships

Cham rulers cooperated with Angkor when it served them, as in 1132, and resisted when Khmer power threatened them. Because Champa comprised regional centers such as Vijaya in the north and Panduranga in the south, a single 'Cham policy' toward Angkor did not exist. Chapter 45 treats these relationships at greater length.

Cham–Khmer relations did not follow a single pattern. The courts of Vijaya and Panduranga were distinct polities with their own rulers and interests, and each calculated its relationship with Angkor separately. Cham participation in the Khmer campaign against Đại Việt in 1132 shows that cooperation was possible when both faced the same northern rival.

By 1145 the relationship had turned to invasion, and the sources do not give a full explanation. Historians infer a mix of factors: Khmer interest in coastal trade and in a stronger position against Đại Việt, Cham reluctance to remain a junior partner, and the court’s interest in military prestige. These are inferences, not documented motives. Cham inscriptions best show how Cham elites understood their own choices, and they show that Champa was an active participant and not simply a field for Khmer or Vietnamese ambition.

Cham courts were also in contact with Song China and Đại Việt, and Cham inscriptions, such as those from Mỹ Sơn, show elite patronage of Śaiva temples. These facts show that Champa had its own religious and diplomatic life, so Cham decisions about Angkor cannot be reduced to the effects of Khmer pressure alone.

Section 37.9

The Conquest of Vijaya in 1145

In 1145 Khmer forces invaded Champa and captured Vijaya. This was a military occupation, and the evidence does not show integration into Khmer administration. Cham inscriptions record the disruption from the Cham viewpoint, which complicates Khmer claims of dominance.

The invasion of 1145 targeted Vijaya, the principal Cham political center of the north. Cœdès's synthesis, based on Cham inscriptions and Vietnamese and Chinese notices, reports that Khmer forces took the city. No evidence shows Khmer officials, taxation, or garrisons systematically incorporated into Khmer provincial government, which is why historians hesitate to call the episode a conquest in the administrative sense.

Cham inscriptions matter because they preserve the viewpoint of the occupied and show that Cham elites remained politically active and treated the occupation as an interruption. Occupation could still be destructive, but the sources do not allow reliable estimates of casualties or displacement, and none should be assumed.

Geography again limits what we know. The route of the Khmer army is not documented. Overland movement through the uplands and coastal approaches are both conceivable, but distances were long, and the sources do not say how supply and reinforcement were managed. Vijaya, near the coast in what is now Bình Định, was a port as well as a political center, which would have made its capture valuable for trade, though the evidence for trade consequences is slight.

Historians therefore distinguish three things: a military success, which the sources support; a political arrangement afterward, about which they say little; and an administrative integration, which they do not show. Treating the 1145 capture as the incorporation of Champa into the Khmer realm would claim more than the evidence allows.

Multiple Perspectives: The Campaigns of 1128–1149

Section 37.10

Cham Resistance, Jaya Harivarman I, and the Limits of Khmer Expansion

Jaya Harivarman I led Cham resistance, defeated Khmer-backed forces, and recovered Vijaya in 1149. Sūryavarman II died about 1150, and Cœdès also reports a Khmer expedition against Đại Việt in 1150; the date and circumstances of his death are uncertain. His record was ambitious but contested: success was often temporary.

Cham resistance can be followed in stages in Cœdès’s synthesis. Jaya Harivarman I established himself in Panduranga in 1147, defeated Khmer-backed forces in 1148, and entered Vijaya and was crowned there in 1149. His success rested on Cham regional networks that the Khmer occupation had not displaced, and the occupation left little durable institutional trace.

The end of the reign is poorly documented. Cœdès also reports a Khmer expedition against Đại Việt in 1150, which is not inherently incompatible with a death about 1150, though the date and circumstances of the king’s death remain uncertain. His posthumous name, Paramaviṣṇuloka, is known. His military record combined repeated large mobilizations and one notable occupation with no lasting territorial gains. Historians infer cautiously that these strains may have contributed to the disorder after his death; Chapter 38 takes up that aftermath.

Assessment of the reign’s military record depends on what is measured. As a record of mobilization and reach, it was impressive: forces moved hundreds of kilometers and held a major Cham center for several years. As a record of lasting results, it was weak: no Khmer province in Đại Việt, and Vijaya lost by 1149. According to Cœdès, Sūryavarman II was followed by Dharaṇindravarman II, and the period after 1150 saw further conflict with Champa, which Chapter 38 takes up.

Section 37.11

The Foundation of Angkor Wat: Date, Location, and Royal Ambition

Division III — Building Angkor Wat

Angkor Wat was built during Sūryavarman II’s reign, near the royal capital. The precise building sequence is uncertain, and the monument may not have been finished in every detail in his lifetime. Its scale reflects royal ambition and command of resources, not a documented budget.

Angkor Wat was built within Sūryavarman II’s reign, but no surviving text gives a start date, completion date, or building sequence. Its original name is uncertain: later inscriptions use forms of Biṣṇulok, but that does not establish the name used when the temple was built. Inscriptions document Divākarapaṇḍita as a royal religious adviser who served several kings and who is recorded as performing Sūryavarman II’s consecration. His career shows the importance of court religious specialists, but it does not by itself show a long-standing specifically Vaiṣṇava priesthood.

Evidence of unfinished work, discussed below, shows that carving continued after the main structure rose, and some surfaces were finished long after the king’s time. The monument therefore records a process, not a single completed moment. Its position beside the royal capital also means that it should be read with the rest of the landscape, which Chapter 35 treats.

Archaeological research around the monument suggests that the area was occupied before and after the building phase, so the temple was built in a landscape with earlier use and continued afterward, a point Chapter 35 develops. This is another reason not to treat Angkor Wat as an isolated creation of one reign.

Section 37.12

The Sacred Enclosure: Moat, Causeway, Walls, and Western Approach

APSARA National Authority gives about 1,500 × 1,300 metres for the outer complex including its moat, with the enclosure wall defining about 82 hectares. Visitors cross the moat by a western causeway, pass the outer wall, and proceed through galleries toward the sanctuary. Observed layout is distinct from inferred symbolism.

Most Khmer state temples face east, so the western orientation of Angkor Wat is a notable feature, and its meaning is debated in §37.23. A visitor crossed the moat on a western causeway, passed through an entrance pavilion, and then followed a long raised causeway across a wide forecourt toward the main structure. Together the moat and outer wall define the sacred enclosure, and the long approach slows and frames the visitor’s first view of the towers.

Architecture can be observed, while its symbolism is inferred. We can see the enclosure, moat, and axis directly; whether the moat represents a cosmic ocean, as many scholars suggest, is an interpretation drawn from comparison with Indian cosmology and other Khmer temples, not a statement in a surviving text about this building.

Experience of the space was shaped by its sequence: the water of the moat, the long approach, and the sudden rise of the towers. These effects are observable, though how a twelfth-century visitor interpreted them is not documented. Some scholars have proposed that the plan encodes calendrical or cosmological measurements, but those proposals are controversial and depend on assumptions about the units used.

Section 37.13

The Temple-Mountain: Terraces, Galleries, Towers, and Sacred Ascent

The central structure rises in three levels to a quincunx of five towers, built mainly of sandstone. Access becomes more restricted at each level, which shaped how worshippers and officiants experienced the space. Cosmological readings, such as the towers as Mount Meru, are interpretations of the architecture rather than recorded intentions.

The main structure rises in three levels. The outer level has the first gallery, whose walls carry the bas-reliefs, and a cruciform arrangement of cloisters later associated with Buddha images. The second level is more restricted, and the third carries a central tower and four corner towers in the five-tower (quincunx) pattern, with the central tower rising roughly 65 metres. Steep stairs and narrowing spaces progressively limited who could approach the sanctuary.

The builders used sandstone for the main structure and laterite for foundations and outer elements, a combination common in Khmer building. Corbelled vaults rather than true arches covered galleries, which explains the dark, narrow interiors. Many scholars read the towers as Mount Meru, home of the gods; this fits the cosmological imagery of the reliefs, but it remains a scholarly interpretation, not an ancient statement of intent.

The vertical sequence also carries engineering. Corbelled vaults roof the galleries and the towers rise in stages, so builders had to control the weight and balance of large masses without mortar. The central peak’s height of roughly 65 metres, as APSARA gives it, was achieved by stacking and fitting these courses, which explains the dense, narrow plan. Visitors ascending would have found the space increasingly restricted, and the upper level was not a public meeting space.

Vocabulary: Temple-mountain
Temple-mountain

A Khmer temple form with stepped levels and towers, often interpreted as a symbolic Mount Meru. The cosmological reading is a modern interpretation and not every such temple served the same purpose.

Section 37.14

Construction, Skilled Labor, and Monumental Engineering

Building required sandstone sourcing, transport, precise masonry, planning, and sculptural work by skilled carvers. No reliable figures survive for workforce size, cost, or duration, and the evidence does not show that all labor was compulsory. Chapter 39 treats ordinary life more broadly.

Sandstone is widely attributed to quarries in the Phnom Kulen uplands, with transport probably by canals and waterways to the site, though the exact routes and methods are debated. Blocks were dressed and fitted with great precision without mortar, and much carving appears to have been done after blocks were laid, since unfinished panels survive. That sequence required planned coordination among quarrymen, transporters, masons, architects, and carvers.

No reliable evidence gives workforce totals, cost, or building duration, and figures that appear in popular accounts should not be repeated as fact. Nor does the evidence show that all labor was compulsory. Skilled labor, supply, and royal resources were clearly required, but how they were organized must be inferred from the monument and from comparison with other Angkorian projects, which Chapter 39 discusses.

Hauling and lifting methods are reconstructed from comparison and from tool marks, not from surviving construction records. Ramps, wooden scaffolds, and animal power are plausible hypotheses, but none is documented for Angkor Wat itself, so they should be presented as reconstructions.

Everyday Life and Social History

Quarrymen, transporters, masons, and carvers worked in specialized roles. Few records name them, so historians infer their work from unfinished sections, tool marks, and style differences.

Section 37.15

Angkor Wat in the Wider Twelfth-Century Building Landscape

Other temples are associated with this period, including Beng Mealea, Banteay Samré, and selected Khorat Plateau sites. Some can be tied to the king by inscription; others are assigned to his era chiefly by architectural style, which is weaker evidence.

Other monuments help place Angkor Wat. Beng Mealea, east of Angkor, is a large sandstone temple of similar character, and Banteay Samré, Thommanon, and Chau Say Tevoda share the style usually associated with this period. Several Khorat Plateau sites, including Phanom Rung and Phimai, were built or expanded around this time, with dates resting on inscriptions combined with style.

Style alone is weaker evidence than an inscription naming the king. A temple assigned to his era by style may have been built a decade or two earlier or later, and scholars differ on individual cases. The pattern still suggests a period of extensive and geographically wide building.

Phanom Rung is principally a Śaiva sanctuary and Phimai a Buddhist one, with important construction in this period. This shows that Sūryavarman II’s era supported several traditions, so Vaiṣṇava Angkor Wat should not be read as the king’s only religious program or as evidence that other traditions were suppressed.

Section 37.16

Angkor Wat as a Vaiṣṇava Sanctuary

Division IV — Viṣṇu, Kingship, and the Visual Program

Angkor Wat is principally dedicated to Viṣṇu, a notable shift from the Śaiva emphasis of many earlier royal foundations. Chapter 36 explains Vaiṣṇavism generally; here the question is how Viṣṇu’s cosmic kingship suited a ruler claiming divine association.

Angkor Wat is principally dedicated to Viṣṇu. Many earlier royal foundations emphasized Śiva, although Śaiva worship continued, so the choice reflects royal and priestly preference, not the replacement of one religion by another. The king’s posthumous name, Paramaviṣṇuloka, expresses the idea of a king joining the supreme Viṣṇu’s world, which links royal identity to the deity.

Viṣṇu’s role as upholder of cosmic order, protector against disorder, and divine warrior suited royal ideology, and the reliefs return repeatedly to Viṣṇu and his avatars, Rāma and Kṛṣṇa. This does not mean ordinary Khmer inhabitants shared a single Vaiṣṇava creed; the program expresses the religious vision of the court and its priesthood.

Section 37.17

Reading the Bas-Relief Galleries: Narrative, Arrangement, and Historical Evidence

The galleries carry long carved narrative sequences read in a fixed direction. They are layered: major portions date to or near Sūryavarman II’s time, but APSARA notes sixteenth-century sculptural additions in the northeastern corner, so students must separate the twelfth-century program from later work.

The galleries form a rectangular ring around the first level, and the panels are read in a prescribed direction, usually described as counterclockwise, which is why the funerary debate cites them. Reliefs were carved on sandstone walls in shallow planes, with figures arranged in registers and with short inscriptions identifying some scenes. Because they were carved in place, unfinished sections reveal the working sequence.

The reliefs are historical evidence in three ways: they show how the court wished to present kingship, they preserve details of dress, arms, and ritual, and they show stages of later patronage. They are not a neutral record. The northeastern corner carries sixteenth-century work, as APSARA notes, and students should not attribute every panel to the twelfth century.

Arrangement matters to interpretation. On the south, the procession lies in the west wing and the heavens and hells in the east wing. The Churning of the Ocean of Milk is on the east, and the Battle of Kurukṣetra and the battle of Laṅkā are in the south and north parts of the west gallery. Other panels in the northern and eastern wings show episodes involving Viṣṇu, Kṛṣṇa, and divine battles, and the northeastern corner includes sixteenth-century work.

Because the galleries were meant to be walked, the sequence of scenes shaped what a visitor saw. Scholars disagree on how far the whole circuit forms a single planned argument, and the later additions show that the program was not entirely fixed when the king died.

Vocabulary: Bas-relief
Bas-relief

Sculpture carved in shallow relief on a wall surface. At Angkor Wat the reliefs were carved in place and are not all of the same date.

Section 37.18

The Royal Procession of Sūryavarman II: Kingship Represented in Stone

The historical procession, on the western part of the southern gallery, shows the king, officers, troops, and insignia in hierarchy. It presents an ideal of power, not an unmediated record of army organization. An inscription naming the king by his posthumous name raises questions about when the relief was completed.

The relief runs along the western part of the southern gallery. The king sits on a raised seat with attendants bearing parasols and fans, while officers and soldiers, some mounted on elephants, move in file. Inscriptions label some contingents, including one called Syāṁ Kuk (K.298.28). The precise ethnic or political identification of that term is disputed, so it should not simply be translated as a Siamese or Thai army, but the label suggests that the court chose to display varied contingents. A royal image of this kind communicates hierarchy and authority; it does not photograph actual military organization.

Art historians note that scale expresses rank: the king is the largest and most elevated figure, with many parasols and attendants, and officers and soldiers are shown in ordered files. The inscription naming the king by his posthumous name shows either that the relief was completed after his death or that the labels were added later. This does not make it a record of a particular day’s procession, and it should be read as an official image of the court.

Section 37.19

The Rāmāyaṇa, Mahābhārata, and the Moral World of Epic Warfare

Reliefs of the Battle of Kurukṣetra and the battle of Laṅkā frame warfare through epic models of righteous kingship and divine agency. Comparing them shows differing narrative purposes, one a dynastic civil war and the other a divine champion’s victory over a demon king.

The Battle of Kurukṣetra on the western gallery shows the great Mahābhārata war between two related houses. Opposed lines of warriors, chariots, and elephants press together, and figures such as Bhīṣma on his bed of arrows are commonly identified. The theme is a righteous war whose cost is great, and it presents warfare as moral and tragic, not simply victorious.

The battle of Laṅkā, in the same gallery, shows the Rāmāyaṇa’s climax, with Rāma and the monkey host, including Hanuman, against the multi-headed Rāvaṇa. This is a divine champion defeating a demon king, so it is closer to a vision of ideal kingship defeating disorder. Comparing the two panels shows how the court drew on different models of war, one a dynastic tragedy and the other a righteous conquest, without implying that either describes the king’s own campaigns.

The Mahābhārata and Rāmāyaṇa were not new to the Khmer world. Inscription K.359 (Veal Kantel), dated palaeographically to the seventh century, records in verse 4 the donation of the Mahābhārata, the Rāmāyaṇa, and a Purāṇa, with provision for their daily recitation, so the reliefs drew on long-established knowledge among the court and priests. In the Kurukṣetra panel, the two armies advance in dense opposed ranks, and Bhīṣma on his bed of arrows is commonly identified. In the Laṅkā panel, the multi-headed Rāvaṇa confronts the monkey host, and Rāma is commonly described as standing on Hanuman’s shoulders, though published guides differ on the supporting monkey, and at least one names Sugrīva.

These panels are examples of narrative arranged for display, not records of Khmer warfare. They show weapons, armor, and formations that scholars compare with other evidence, but the details are stylized, and it would be an error to treat the epic armies as portraits of Sūryavarman II’s forces. Their value for historians lies in what they show about the ideas of kingship and war the court wished to present.

Section 37.20

The Churning of the Ocean of Milk: Cosmic Order and Immortality

The eastern-gallery relief shows gods and asuras pulling the serpent Vāsuki around Mount Mandara to produce amṛta, with Viṣṇu at the center. Its religious meaning concerns cosmic order and immortality; links to kingship are plausible but not a single established political reading.

Viṣṇu stands at the center of the eastern-gallery panel, Mount Mandara serves as the churning rod, and the serpent Vāsuki is the rope, pulled by gods on one side and asuras on the other. A turtle supporting the mountain is usually read as Viṣṇu's Kūrma avatar, and apsaras fill the upper zone. The reading through Viṣṇu fits the monument's dedication, but the myth was widely shared in Angkorian religion, so any political reading remains interpretation.

The panel’s composition is balanced, with a roughly equal pull on each side and Viṣṇu at the center. The myth is told in several versions across Indian texts, so the carvers made choices about which elements to emphasize. Some scholars link the scene to royal ideology, with the king as sustainer of cosmic order through cooperation, while others read it as a religious narrative of immortality. The evidence supports the religious reading firmly and the political reading as a plausible inference.

Popular guides often emphasize the size of the panel and the number of figures, but the formal arrangement tells more. The serpent forms a long horizontal line that structures the whole scene, the figures on each side are placed in rows, and Viṣṇu and the churning rod create a vertical axis at the center. This design emphasizes balance and cooperation between opposed groups under divine direction, which is one reason scholars connect the scene to ideas of order.

Section 37.21

Heavens, Hells, Judgment, and the Consequences of Action

The southern gallery depicts heavens and hells, with Yama presiding over judgment. These scenes represent religious ideas about karma and reward, which should not be assumed to describe the beliefs of every Angkorian inhabitant.

The southern gallery’s eastern portion shows rewards and punishments after death. Yama, judge of the dead, appears riding a buffalo, and registers divide heavens from hells, with inscriptions labelling punishments for particular wrongs. The imagery expresses ideas about karma, moral conduct, and consequences in the afterlife that were widely shared in the Indian religious world.

These images teach a moral order, and they likely were meant to affect those who saw them, but the evidence for how ordinary people interpreted them is slight. They show what the court and priests chose to display, not what every inhabitant believed.

The panels are arranged in registers. In the upper registers, figures of high status are shown in processions and palanquins in the heavens, and below them the hells show wrongdoers being beaten, bound, and tormented, with inscriptions labelling punishments for particular wrongs. Yama, the judge of the dead, appears with many arms on a buffalo, directing the souls’ fate, so a viewer’s eye moves from heaven down to hell and back to judgment.

Scholars note that the heavens show a social world resembling the court, which links moral reward to hierarchy. The imagery draws on ideas about karma shared across Indian religious traditions, but how these ideas were understood by different Khmer social groups is not documented, and the panels remain evidence of what the court and priests chose to present.

Section 37.22

Devatas, Decorative Sculpture, Workshops, and Artistic Meaning

Numerous female figures and extensive ornament decorate the walls. They show skilled workshop practice, but not every figure can be read identically, and treating them all as portraits of historical dancers is not supported by the evidence.

Female figures called devatas stand in niches and on walls throughout the monument, with varied headdresses, hairstyles, jewelry, and poses. Their variety shows distinct workshop hands and conventions, and recording these differences helps scholars distinguish carving traditions and later repairs. Ornamental bands, floral scrolls, and pediments show the same attention to craft.

Interpreting them is difficult. Some may represent divine attendants, and the common claim that they depict historical court dancers is not supported by a clear inscription. Their identity and meaning, like the status of the carvers who made them, are matters of inference. What is secure is that the monument reflects a large and highly skilled sculptural enterprise.

Differences in style among panels and figures suggest that several workshops or hands worked on the monument, and unfinished sections reveal the order of work. Sixteenth-century additions differ visibly in style from the twelfth-century carving. Surviving evidence does not tell us the status, pay, or conditions of the sculptors, and this is an important gap in the social history of the monument.

Section 37.23

Temple, Tomb, or Divine Residence? The Debate over Angkor Wat

Division V — Meaning and Later Religious Life

Angkor Wat faces west, is dedicated to Viṣṇu, and its reliefs are viewed counterclockwise, features read by some scholars as funerary.

Supporters of a funerary reading note the western association with death and the counterclockwise circulation of the reliefs, which resembles the direction of funerary ritual. Critics reply that no burial of the king has been securely identified, that a west-facing Viṣṇu temple need not be a tomb, and that Khmer royal foundations often joined worship of a deity with the king's identification with that deity, so a temple could commemorate a ruler without being his tomb. Jinah Kim's work on later Buddhist reuse argues that the temple-or-tomb binary is too rigid.

The posthumous name Paramaviṣṇuloka indicates that the king was understood after death as joining the world of Viṣṇu, which supports a commemorative reading. Yet no securely identified original cult image or burial survives to show what the central sanctuary housed, and the later placement of Buddha images complicates any inference. Every proposed reading must rest on indirect evidence: orientation, the direction of the reliefs, the posthumous name, and comparison with other Khmer temples. No single detail proves burial.

The funerary reading has a distinguished history. Cœdès, building on arguments associated with Jean Przyluski, proposed that Angkor Wat was a funerary monument in which the king, identified with Viṣṇu after death, was commemorated. Later scholarship has questioned the exclusive choice between temple and tomb. Khmer temples commonly joined the worship of a deity with the memory or identification of a ruler (Chapters 34 and 36), so a monument could be a state temple during the king’s life and commemorate him afterward without being a tomb in the narrow sense.

The debate is therefore one of interpretation, not of missing facts alone. Evidence that could advance it includes foundation inscriptions, a securely identified original central image, or reliable archaeological context for the sanctuary, none of which is yet decisive. Students should be able to state each position, identify the evidence behind it, and explain why the available evidence does not settle the question.

Historical Debate: Temple, Tomb, or Divine Residence?

The question: Was Angkor Wat a Viṣṇu temple, a funerary monument, or both?

Funerary reading: Associated with George Cœdès and Jean Przyluski, it points to the west-facing entrance, counterclockwise reliefs, and posthumous name.
Combined reading: Later scholarship, including Jinah Kim’s work on Buddhist reuse, challenges the assumption that temple and tomb are exclusive categories.

Evidence and disagreement: Neither orientation nor posthumous name conclusively proves burial practice. Why it matters: It shapes how the monument’s purpose is understood. Student task: Identify what evidence could settle the question.

Section 37.24

From Vaiṣṇava Royal Monument to Buddhist Sacred Site

After the twelfth century the monument was reused and modified, with Buddhist images and changing ritual practice. The transformation was gradual and not the act of a single king, and continued occupation and devotion are documented.

Evidence for Buddhist reuse includes Buddha images placed in the cruciform galleries and the central sanctuary, together with later inscriptions recording Buddhist donations. Scholars debate exactly when reuse began, and it was probably gradual, involving different groups of patrons and worshippers rather than a single decision. Chapter 38 treats the Buddhist royal program of Jayavarman VII, and Chapter 53 treats the wider Theravāda transformation; here the point is that Angkor Wat itself acquired Buddhist imagery over time.

Continued worship matters because it shows that the monument was not a ruin awaiting rediscovery. Monks, donors, and pilgrims maintained and modified it, and physical changes to the structure show the work of successive generations.

Some of the changes are visible in the building. Buddha images were placed in the cruciform cloisters, and a large eight-armed figure commonly identified as Viṣṇu in origin stands in the western entrance area and is venerated locally under the name Ta Reach. This shows how an older image could be absorbed into later devotion without a single break. Buddhist reuse was therefore a process of adaptation, in which worshippers kept the sanctity of the place while changing how it was understood.

Section 37.25

Angkor Wat in the Sixteenth to Eighteenth Centuries: Restoration, Pilgrimage, and New Meanings

Cambodian royal and nonroyal patrons added sculpture in the sixteenth century, and Khmer inscriptions record Buddhist acts of merit at the site. Foreign pilgrims, including Japanese visitors, also came. Later generations actively remade Angkor Wat.

Between the twelfth and sixteenth centuries the evidence points to continuous use, not abandonment. Buddhist imagery was installed, parts of the site were maintained, and a 2019 archaeological study documents continued occupation of the Angkor Wat landscape. The sixteenth-century northeastern relief additions belong to this later royal and nonroyal patronage.

Inscription IMA 2 (K.303) of 1577 and the separate IMA 3 of 1579 belong to this sixteenth-century phase, and together with the northeastern relief additions they show royal interest in the monument’s upkeep and completion. Foreign visitors also came: a Japanese visitor’s inscription of the 1630s identifies the site with the Jetavana monastery of Buddhist India, showing that the temple’s sacredness was understood in Buddhist terms beyond Cambodia.

These later uses do not erase the twelfth-century program. They show that a monument’s meaning is made by those who use it, and that Angkor Wat’s history includes patrons and worshippers who left far less trace than kings.

The sixteenth-century interest in Angkor Wat took place when the royal court was moving to other centers (Chapter 89), so restoration at Angkor was not a return of the old capital. It was an act of merit and legitimation, linking a king and his family to a famous sacred place. Women of the royal family, such as the queen mother named in IMA 2, appear as important patrons, a reminder that royal religious patronage was not limited to kings.

Angkor Wat retained religious significance and continued to attract Buddhist patrons and worshippers across later centuries, as its inscriptions, images, archaeological record, and accounts of pilgrimage demonstrate. For historians this continuity is evidence of a different kind from the monumental program: it records the choices of monks, donors, and local communities who left fewer written sources than kings did.

Section 37.26

From Living Sanctuary to World Heritage Monument

Division VI — Memory, Heritage, and Interpretation

The nineteenth-century European 'discovery' narrative is misleading: Cambodians had retained knowledge of Angkor Wat and continued to worship there. Colonial scholarship, conservation, and modern archaeology reshaped its image, and modern heritage management is only touched on here.

Cambodians never lost knowledge of Angkor Wat. Monks, pilgrims, and local communities continued to use it, and earlier European visitors, including sixteenth-century Portuguese, described it before Henri Mouhot’s account in 1860 popularized it in Europe. The ‘discovery’ narrative therefore describes the growth of European awareness, not the discovery of an abandoned monument.

French colonial scholarship, through the École française d’Extrême-Orient from 1898, and the conservation work from the early twentieth century, contributed to the archaeological and epigraphic foundations of modern scholarship, but also promoted interpretations that treated Angkor as a lost civilization separate from living Cambodians. Angkor Wat has appeared on Cambodian national flags since the nineteenth century, and was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1992 as part of the Angkor site, with APSARA established in 1995 to manage it. Chapter 61 treats Angkor’s wider later history.

Conservation brought its own interpretations. French conservators in the early and mid twentieth century used techniques such as anastylosis, reassembling collapsed stonework from original blocks, and their research shaped how the monument was studied. At the same time, decades of conflict, looting, and neglect affected the site, and international conservation expanded after 1992. Modern research, including remote sensing and archaeology, continues to revise understanding of the surrounding landscape (Chapter 35).

Section 37.27

Sūryavarman II and Angkor Wat: Power, Religion, War, and Historical Memory

Sūryavarman II pursued an ambitious but contested program of power. Angkor Wat expressed devotion and authority on a monumental scale, yet its long later history shows that its meaning was neither limited to its founder’s intentions nor fixed at construction. Students should distinguish the historical reign, the idealized kingship in the reliefs, and the monument’s later histories.

Three histories should be kept apart. The first is the reign: an ambitious king who took the throne in 1113, used diplomacy, ritual, and war, and gained and lost Vijaya within a few years without lasting territorial gains. The second is the idealized kingship in the reliefs and sanctuary, which expresses religious ideas and royal ambition and not a camera’s record. The third is the monument’s later life as a Buddhist sacred site, remade by patrons for centuries.

Endurance should not be mistaken for continuity of a political system or religious identity. The Khmer state, its religion, and the way people used the monument all changed, while the building remained. This is the chapter’s central lesson: a monument’s meaning is neither limited to its founder’s intentions nor fixed at its construction.

Chapter Summary

Sūryavarman II took the throne in 1113 and consolidated authority through patronage, diplomacy, and war. He sent embassies to Song China, attacked Đại Việt unsuccessfully, and captured Vijaya in 1145, but Jaya Harivarman I recovered it in 1149. Angkor Wat, a Vaiṣṇava temple-mountain with a rich relief program, expressed royal and cosmic order. Debate about its temple or funerary character is unresolved. Later Buddhist use, sixteenth-century additions, pilgrimage, and modern heritage practice show that its meaning kept changing.

The reign’s record was mixed. Embassies to China brought recognition, but campaigns against Đại Việt failed to win lasting gains. The occupation of Vijaya in 1145 was short-lived, because Cham resistance under Jaya Harivarman I, from his establishment in Panduranga in 1147 through victories in 1148, led to his entry into Vijaya in 1149.

The evidence for these events is asymmetrical. Vietnamese chronicles describe the defense, Chinese records note embassies, and Cham inscriptions give the Cham view, while Khmer sources on the campaigns are limited. The monument adds a different kind of evidence, expressing royal ideals through architecture and reliefs that are partly later in date.

The debate over whether Angkor Wat was a temple, a tomb, or both remains open, and the later record of Buddhist devotion, including IMA 2 of 1577 and the separate IMA 3 of 1579, shows that the monument kept being used and remade.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows that kings, monuments, and memory are separate histories. Sūryavarman II’s reign was ambitious and contested, Angkor Wat idealized royal power, and its later users remade it. This prepares for Chapter 38, which turns to Jayavarman VII and the Buddhist Khmer empire.

The chapter also shows how historians work. The reign is reconstructed by comparing sources written for different audiences, and the monument is read through architecture, images, and later inscriptions. Each source type has strengths and limits, and the most reliable conclusions come from agreement across them.

Finally, the chapter shows why a monument’s endurance should be distinguished from continuity. Angkor Wat survived because later generations valued and reused it, and its meaning changed as the Khmer court, religion, and landscape changed. That lesson applies throughout this textbook, from the later chapters on Angkor to the modern chapters on heritage and memory.
