The Origins of Champa

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Along the narrow, mountain-backed coastline of what is today central and southern Vietnam, a very different Southeast Asian world took shape from the one examined in the two preceding chapters. Where Funan and Chenla developed around river deltas, floodplains, and inland lake systems, the communities Chinese sources called Linyi, and whose descendants called their own world Campa, built their political and religious life around short, fast-flowing rivers, narrow coastal plains, and direct access to the open sea.

This chapter shifts the textbook's geographic focus east for the first time since Chapter 17, from the Mekong world examined across Chapters 20 and 21 to the central and southern Vietnamese coast. Readers should not treat this shift as a continuation of the same political history under a new name. Champa developed along its own trajectory, shaped by its own geography, its own language family, and its own pattern of contact with neighboring powers, even as it remained in periodic contact, exchange, and conflict with the Mekong world to its west and the Han and post-Han Chinese frontier to its north.

Students arriving at this chapter already carry two methodological lessons from Chapters 20 and 21 that apply here with equal force. The first is that a name recorded in an outside text, in this case the Chinese term Linyi, is an invitation to investigate rather than a secure description of a bounded, uniform kingdom. The second is that even a substantial local inscriptional record, which this chapter possesses in the Cham and Sanskrit epigraphy associated with sites such as Mỹ Sơn, does not resolve every question about political unity; it often reveals a landscape of regional centers whose relationships to one another remained variable rather than fixed. This chapter's central historical problem is therefore a familiar one, applied to new evidence: was "Champa" ever a single, continuously governed kingdom, or is it better understood as a shifting arrangement of coastal and river-valley centers that Chinese and, later, Cham and Sanskrit sources described using a name that implied more unity than the underlying political reality possessed?

This chapter also introduces a genuinely new element into the textbook's evidentiary toolkit: the Cham language itself, an Austronesian language whose speakers were connected, through the long history of Austronesian seafaring and settlement examined in Chapter 14, to communities across island Southeast Asia and the wider Pacific, rather than to the Austroasiatic- and Sino-Tibetan-speaking populations more prominent on the mainland examined in Chapters 20 and 21. The earliest surviving inscription widely regarded as written in an Austronesian language comes from this coastal world, conventionally dated to around the fourth century CE though its precise date remains debated, a fact that gives this chapter a distinctive place in the history of Southeast Asian writing and language.

Like Funan and Chenla, Champa's early political and religious culture drew extensively on Sanskrit vocabulary, Śaiva religious traditions, and Indic conventions of kingship. As in those earlier chapters, this material should be read through the localization framework introduced in Chapter 19: Cham rulers and religious specialists selected, adapted, and combined South Asian religious and political vocabulary with existing local institutions, language, and belief, producing a genuinely Cham synthesis rather than an imported South Asian court transplanted onto the Vietnamese coast.

Finally, this chapter deliberately stops short of the centuries of sustained contact, exchange, warfare, and territorial competition between Cham polities and Vietnamese states that followed. That longer, more contested history belongs to Chapter 45, "Champa and Đại Việt," examined later in this textbook once Đại Việt's own early history has been introduced in Chapters 23, 42, and 43. This chapter's task is narrower and more foundational: to explain how Cham-speaking coastal communities emerged from indigenous Iron Age foundations, how a distinctive Cham religious and political order took shape at centers such as Mỹ Sơn, and how historians read Chinese, Cham, and Sanskrit evidence together, and against the region's archaeology, to reconstruct this process responsibly.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish "Linyi," a Chinese textual label, and "Campa," a name that appears in Cham and Sanskrit inscriptions from the later part of this chapter's period, from the archaeological and epigraphic record of the central and southern Vietnamese coast.
  2. Explain the relationship between the Sa Huỳnh archaeological culture and the emergence of Cham-speaking coastal communities, and why this relationship supports an indigenous-development interpretation of Cham state formation.
  3. Describe the physical geography of the central and southern Vietnamese coast and explain how short rivers, narrow coastal plains, and mountain corridors shaped a distinctive regional pattern of settlement and political organization.
  4. Evaluate Chinese textual accounts of Linyi, including the traditional founding narrative associated with a figure named Ou Lian, for what they can and cannot establish about early Cham political history.
  5. Identify Cham as an Austronesian language, and explain the significance of the Đông Yên Châu inscription for the history of Southeast Asian and world writing systems.
  6. Analyze the reign associated with Bhadravarman I and the foundation of the religious complex at Mỹ Sơn without treating this evidence as proof of a single, permanently centralized Cham kingdom.
  7. Compare the archaeological and epigraphic evidence from Mỹ Sơn and Trà Kiệu, and evaluate hypotheses concerning their relationship to one another and to other regional centers.
  8. Evaluate the historiographical debate over whether Champa was ever a single unified kingdom or a shifting network of regional principalities.
  9. Apply the localization framework introduced in Chapter 19 to Cham Śaiva religious practice and Sanskrit royal inscriptions.
  10. Assess the significance of Champa's maritime orientation for its economic and cultural connections across the South China Sea and Indian Ocean trading worlds.
  11. Describe Champa's relations with its northern neighbor, the Han and post-Han Chinese-administered region of Jiaozhou, and its western neighbors, the polities examined in Chapters 20 and 21.
  12. Contextualize this chapter's foundational account of early Champa as the necessary background for Chapter 45's later treatment of Champa and Đại Việt.

  3. What can the Đông Yên Châu inscription tell historians about the early use of a written Austronesian language, and what can it not tell them?

Historical Context

This chapter completes Part V, "The Earliest Documented States," having examined Funan in Chapter 20 and Chenla in Chapter 21. Both of those chapters focused on the Mekong world, and both taught a common methodological lesson: a name recorded in an outside or later text, whether Funan, Zhenla, or the further subdivided Land Chenla and Water Chenla, should be treated as a problem for historians to investigate rather than a secure map of indigenous political organization. This chapter applies that same lesson to the central and southern Vietnamese coast, a region historically distinct from the Mekong world in geography, language, and political trajectory, even though the two regions maintained periodic contact throughout the centuries examined here.

The geographic focus of this chapter is the narrow coastal strip of present-day central and southern Vietnam, bounded on the west by the Annamite mountain range and on the east by the South China Sea, and organized around a series of short, fast-flowing rivers, including the Thu Bồn river system near modern Đà Nẵng and Hội An, that drain the mountains directly into the sea across a comparatively narrow coastal plain. This geography differs substantially from the broad river deltas and inland lake systems examined in Chapters 20 and 21, and it shaped a regional pattern of relatively compact, dispersed river-mouth and coastal centers rather than the large contiguous floodplain societies of the Mekong world.

The population historians associate with early Champa spoke Cham, a language belonging to the Austronesian language family examined in Chapter 14, tracing ultimate ancestry to populations that had spread from Taiwan through island Southeast Asia over the preceding centuries and millennia. This linguistic affiliation connects the Cham coast to the maritime and island Southeast Asian world in ways that distinguish it sharply from the Austroasiatic- and Sino-Tibetan-speaking populations more prominent in the mainland societies examined in the two preceding chapters, even though Cham communities also interacted extensively with Mon-Khmer-speaking neighbors to the west.

It is essential to state clearly, before the chapter's detailed content begins, what this chapter does not claim. It does not claim that "Champa" was, at any point in the centuries examined here, a single, continuously governed kingdom with fixed borders and an unbroken succession of rulers. It does not claim that Cham religious and political culture was created by South Asian traders, priests, or colonists, an interpretation this chapter rejects on the same evidentiary grounds developed in Chapters 19 and 20. And it does not claim that the Chinese label "Linyi" straightforwardly and reliably names a single polity whose internal political structure Chinese sources can be trusted to describe accurately. Each of these claims has appeared in older historical writing, and this chapter explains why more recent archaeological, epigraphic, and historiographical scholarship treats them with substantial caution.

Section 22.1

Shifting East: A New Coastal World

Chapters 20 and 21 examined the Mekong world's early documented states through a recurring method: distinguish the outside or later textual label from the archaeological and epigraphic record, and resist collapsing a complex, multi-centered political landscape into a single unified kingdom. This chapter carries that method eastward, to a coastal world with its own distinct geography, language, and history of outside contact.

Students should not treat Champa as a variant of Funan or Chenla, nor as a western outpost of some larger Mekong-centered civilization. Cham-speaking communities developed their own political and religious institutions from their own regional Iron Age foundations, examined in Section 22.3, and their language, economy, and geographic orientation connected them as much to the maritime world of island Southeast Asia, examined in Chapter 14, as to their Mon-Khmer-speaking neighbors to the west. At the same time, this chapter does not treat Champa as historically isolated from the Mekong world or from China; periodic contact, exchange, and conflict with both connect this chapter to the two that preceded it and to the Vietnamese history examined later in Chapter 23 and Volume III.

Section 22.2

"Linyi" and "Champa" as Textual Labels

As with Funan and Chenla, students must distinguish carefully between the names outside or later sources use for this region's political history and the historical realities those names imperfectly describe. Chinese dynastic histories from the Han period onward use the name Linyi, a Chinese transcription, to describe a polity or set of polities along the central Vietnamese coast. This name reflects Chinese administrative and diplomatic categories and should not be assumed to correspond precisely to how the region's own inhabitants understood their political world during its earliest centuries.

A name resembling "Campa," ancestor of the modern English "Champa," appears in Cham-language and Sanskrit inscriptions from around the middle of the seventh century CE onward, considerably later than the Chinese use of "Linyi." Even once this name appears in local inscriptions, however, historians should not assume it necessarily designated a single, bounded, continuously governed state in the modern sense; it may have functioned, at least initially, as a name for a ruling lineage, a sacred landscape, or a network of religious and political allegiance rather than as a strict territorial designation. This chapter therefore uses "Champa" as a conventional label for the broader historical subject, consistent with standard usage in the field, while treating the question of how much political unity that label actually implies as the central problem to be investigated, developed most fully in Section 22.10.

Historical Vocabulary
Linyi / Champa

Definition: "Linyi" is a Chinese transcription, recorded in dynastic histories from the Han period onward, for a polity or set of polities along the central Vietnamese coast. "Champa," from a name transcribed as Campa, appears instead in Cham-language and Sanskrit inscriptions produced within the region itself, but only from around the mid-seventh century CE onward.

Historical context: For roughly the first four or five centuries of this chapter's period, the only textual name available to historians is the outside Chinese term "Linyi." A term resembling "Champa" enters the local inscriptional record only later, and even then may have described a ruling lineage or sacred landscape as much as a bounded territory.

Why it matters: Because neither name is contemporary with the entire span of this chapter, historians cannot assume that either one, at any point, secured a single, stable, self-recognized political identity across the whole of the central and southern Vietnamese coast.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not treat "Linyi" and "Champa" as two different names for one identical, unchanging kingdom, nor assume that the later appearance of "Champa" in local inscriptions resolves the question of political unity that Section 22.10 examines directly.

Section 22.3

Sa Huỳnh: The Coast before Champa

Following the same method Chapter 20 applied to the lower Mekong, this chapter insists that Cham political and religious complexity did not begin with Chinese contact or with the arrival of Sanskrit and Śaiva religious traditions. Archaeological work along the central and southern Vietnamese coast has identified a distinctive Iron Age archaeological culture, conventionally called Sa Huỳnh after its type site, dated broadly from around 1000 BCE into the early centuries CE. Sa Huỳnh sites are known especially for distinctive burial practices, including large ceramic burial jars, and for ornaments and grave goods, including glass and stone beads and animal-shaped earrings, that demonstrate participation in long-distance exchange networks reaching across island Southeast Asia and beyond, connecting to the broader Iron Age exchange patterns examined in Chapter 17.

This archaeological culture is generally regarded by specialists as ancestral, in a broad cultural and demographic sense, to the Cham-speaking populations who later built the political and religious institutions examined later in this chapter. As with Funan's relationship to earlier lower-Mekong settlement, historians should describe this relationship as a long, uneven process of development and transformation rather than assuming a clean, direct line from a specific Sa Huỳnh community to a specific later Cham polity. What the Sa Huỳnh evidence firmly establishes is that the coastal societies later associated with Champa possessed indigenous social organization, craft specialization, and long-distance exchange connections well before any Chinese textual description of the region and well before extensive contact with Sanskritic religious and political traditions.

Historical Vocabulary
Sa Huỳnh Culture

Definition: Sa Huỳnh is the name, taken from a coastal site in present-day central Vietnam, given by archaeologists to an Iron Age archaeological culture spanning roughly 1000 BCE to the early centuries CE, found along the central and southern Vietnamese coast and identified through characteristic burial jars, ceramics, and ornaments.

Historical context: Sa Huỳnh sites demonstrate settled communities engaged in agriculture, craft production, and long-distance maritime and overland exchange, predating any written description of the region by outside observers.

Why it matters: The Sa Huỳnh culture provides an important indigenous archaeological background for understanding the later emergence of Cham-speaking societies, while the precise nature of cultural and demographic continuity remains under investigation, supporting an interpretation of Cham state formation as a local process rather than a creation of outside contact.

Potential misunderstanding: "Sa Huỳnh" is a modern archaeological classification, not a name the people who produced this material culture used for themselves, and it should not be treated as equivalent to a specific named ethnic or political community.

Section 22.4

A Maritime and Riverine Geography

The geography of the central and southern Vietnamese coast differs substantially from the broad river deltas and inland lake systems examined in Chapters 20 and 21, and this difference shaped a distinctive pattern of settlement and political organization. The Annamite mountain range runs close to the coastline for much of its length, leaving only a narrow strip of lowland between the mountains and the South China Sea. Short, fast-flowing rivers, most prominently the Thu Bồn river system near modern Đà Nẵng and Hội An, drain this mountainous interior directly into the sea, creating a series of relatively compact river-mouth and coastal plains rather than one continuous, unified lowland.

This geography produced a historical pattern of dispersed regional centers, each organized around its own river system and coastal plain, connected to one another chiefly by sea rather than by a single continuous overland or riverine network. Later scholarship conventionally associates several such regional centers with different phases and locations of Cham political and religious activity, including a northern zone associated with the Thu Bồn river system and the sites of Mỹ Sơn and Trà Kiệu, and other zones further south along the coast whose more detailed political history belongs to later chapters of this textbook. This chapter follows the method established in Chapters 20 and 21: it organizes political geography around river systems, coastal plains, and named centers rather than a single bounded territorial map.

Section 22.5

Chinese Accounts of Linyi

Chinese dynastic histories from the Han period onward preserve information about a polity or set of polities they call Linyi, located south of the Chinese-administered commandery of Rinan. As with the Chinese accounts of Funan examined in Chapter 20, this information survives chiefly through later compilation, and the earliest events it describes are known only through textual traditions recorded well after the events themselves occurred.

The most commonly cited founding account describes a local uprising, traditionally dated to around 192 CE, led by a figure whose name Chinese sources transcribe as Ou Lian, against Han administrative authority in Rinan, resulting in the establishment of a locally governed polity later called Linyi. Students should apply the same source-critical caution to this narrative that Chapter 20 applied to the Kaundinya founding tradition associated with Funan: the account survives through later Chinese textual compilation, its precise date and details cannot be independently verified through contemporary local evidence, and it should be read as evidence of a Chinese textual tradition explaining the origins of a neighboring polity rather than as a secure, contemporary historical record.

Subsequent Chinese sources describe recurring patterns of conflict, raiding, and diplomatic contact between Linyi and Chinese-administered territory to the north across the following centuries, discussed further in Section 22.13. These accounts, like the Chinese accounts of Funan, reflect Chinese administrative and military interests and cannot, by themselves, establish the internal political structure of the societies they describe. They can, however, be usefully cross-referenced against the archaeological and epigraphic evidence examined later in this chapter.

Section 22.6

Cham as an Austronesian Language

Cham belongs to the Austronesian language family examined in Chapter 14, tracing ultimate ancestry to populations that spread from Taiwan through the Philippines and island Southeast Asia over the preceding centuries and millennia. This linguistic affiliation is historically significant: it connects the Cham coast to a vast, maritime-oriented linguistic and cultural world stretching from Madagascar to the Pacific, distinct from the Austroasiatic, Sino-Tibetan, and Kra-Dai language families more prominent among Cham's mainland neighbors, even though extensive contact and exchange with Mon-Khmer-speaking communities to the west shaped Cham society considerably over time.

The Đông Yên Châu inscription, conventionally dated to around the fourth century CE, though the precise date remains debated among specialists, is widely regarded as the earliest surviving inscription written in an Austronesian language. Composed in Old Cham using a script derived from Indic writing systems, the inscription's content is chiefly a religious formula invoking protection and imprecation associated with a local deity, rather than a royal or dynastic record. Its significance lies less in its specific content than in what it demonstrates about the early adoption and adaptation of Indic script to a local, non-Indic vernacular language, a striking and concrete instance of the localization process examined throughout this textbook since Chapter 19.

Inscription Study

The Đông Yên Châu Inscription

Findspot: Đông Yên Châu, near Trà Kiệu, in present-day central Vietnam · Language: Old Cham, an early written form of the Austronesian language ancestral to modern Cham · Script: an Indic-derived script adapted to represent Old Cham · Date: conventionally placed around the fourth century CE, though the precise date remains debated.

Context: Unlike the Sanskrit royal inscriptions examined later in this chapter, the Đông Yên Châu inscription is not primarily a dynastic or political record. Its content is generally understood as a religious formula, invoking protection for the properties of a local deity and imprecation against those who might damage or interfere with them.

Analysis: The inscription's chief historical significance lies in its early and confident use of a vernacular, non-Indic language, Old Cham, written in an adapted Indic-derived script, at a period when much surviving Southeast Asian epigraphy elsewhere used Sanskrit. This demonstrates that Cham communities were not merely passive recipients of an imported writing system; they adapted that system rapidly and confidently to their own spoken language for their own local religious purposes.

Limitations: The inscription's brevity and religious rather than administrative content mean it cannot, by itself, establish details of political organization, rulers, or territorial extent during this period. Its precise date also remains a matter of scholarly discussion rather than settled fact.

Questions for students:

  1. Why is the Đông Yên Châu inscription historically significant even though its content is religious rather than political?
  2. What does the early adaptation of an Indic-derived script to write a local Austronesian language suggest about the relationship between imported and local elements in early Cham culture?
  3. Why can this inscription not, by itself, establish details of early Cham political organization?
  4. How does this inscription's evidence connect to the localization framework introduced in Chapter 19?
Section 22.7

Bhadravarman I and the Foundation of Mỹ Sơn

Sanskrit inscriptions from the temple complex of Mỹ Sơn, examined in detail in Section 22.8, associate a ruler conventionally called Bhadravarman I, traditionally placed in the late fourth or early fifth century CE, with the foundation of a religious cult centered on a linga, a principal cult object of Śaiva worship, dedicated to a deity whose name, Bhadreśvara, combines the ruler's own name with that of Śiva. This combination is a distinctive and recurring feature of early Southeast Asian Sanskrit royal inscriptions, in which a ruler's patronage of a major deity becomes linguistically fused with that ruler's own identity and legacy.

As with the rulers examined in Chapters 20 and 21, historians should resist treating Bhadravarman I as the founder of a permanently unified Cham kingdom simply because his inscriptions are prominent and well preserved. The inscriptional evidence securely establishes that Bhadravarman I sponsored a major religious foundation at Mỹ Sơn and that this foundation carried his name and religious patronage forward for centuries; it does not, by itself, establish the full territorial extent of his authority, nor does it guarantee that every other regional center along the Cham coast during this period acknowledged his precedence.

Section 22.8

Mỹ Sơn as a Sacred and Political Landscape

Mỹ Sơn, located in a secluded mountain valley near the Thu Bồn river, developed over many centuries into one of the most important religious complexes associated with Cham civilization, containing numerous brick and stone temple structures built and rebuilt across a long span of time, along with a substantial corpus of Sanskrit and Old Cham inscriptions recording royal patronage, religious dedications, and genealogical claims.

This chapter treats Mỹ Sơn much as Chapter 20 treated Óc Eo and Chapter 21 treated Sambor Prei Kuk: as the central archaeological and epigraphic case study through which students can examine, in material detail, what a major Cham religious and political center actually looked like, while resisting the temptation to convert that evidence automatically into proof of a single, permanently unified kingdom. The site's long history of continuous religious use and rebuilding, spanning many reigns and many centuries, demonstrates sustained royal investment in a shared sacred landscape, functioning simultaneously as a center of religious authority and as a symbol of dynastic continuity and legitimacy for successive rulers who associated themselves with its patronage.

Site Study

Mỹ Sơn: A Sacred Landscape across Many Reigns

Archaeological and epigraphic documentation of Mỹ Sơn includes: numerous brick and stone temple structures, built, damaged, and rebuilt across several centuries; Sanskrit and Old Cham inscriptions recording royal patronage, genealogical claims, and religious dedications, associated with numerous different rulers over time rather than a single reign; and evidence of continuous religious use spanning from the period associated with Bhadravarman I through many subsequent centuries examined in later chapters of this textbook.

Student analysis questions:

  1. What does the long span of construction and rebuilding at Mỹ Sơn suggest about the site's importance across many successive reigns rather than a single ruler's lifetime?
  2. Why is Mỹ Sơn's importance as a religious and dynastic symbol not, by itself, sufficient evidence of a single, permanently unified Cham territorial state?
  3. How might comparing Mỹ Sơn's inscriptions across different centuries help historians trace changes in Cham political organization over time?
Section 22.9

Trà Kiệu and the Question of a Capital

Archaeological work at Trà Kiệu, on the coastal plain nearer the mouth of the Thu Bồn river and not far from Mỹ Sơn, has identified evidence of a substantial settlement, including remains suggestive of fortification, elite residence, and craft production, leading many specialists to propose Trà Kiệu as a likely political and administrative center associated with the same regional network as Mỹ Sơn's religious complex.

Following the method applied to Angkor Borei and Óc Eo in Chapter 20, and to Sambor Prei Kuk in Chapter 21, this chapter treats the relationship between Mỹ Sơn and Trà Kiệu as a reasonable working hypothesis rather than a settled fact: Mỹ Sơn as a sacred, dynastic center of religious patronage, and Trà Kiệu as a nearby settlement functioning as an administrative or residential center, together forming components of a single regional political and religious system. This hypothesis is supported by geographic proximity and by the general pattern, observed elsewhere in early Southeast Asia, of religious and administrative functions being distributed across nearby but distinct sites. It has not been proven in the sense of establishing a single, centrally coordinated administrative hierarchy linking the two sites under one uninterrupted line of authority throughout the many centuries this chapter and later chapters examine.

Section 22.10

Was Champa Ever One Kingdom?

This section addresses directly the question this chapter has been building toward since Section 22.2. Chinese textual usage of "Linyi," and even the later Cham and Sanskrit inscriptional use of a name resembling "Campa," can encourage readers to imagine a single, consolidated, territorially uniform kingdom stretching evenly along the length of the central and southern Vietnamese coast. The archaeological and epigraphic evidence examined in this chapter, and the more detailed evidence examined in later chapters covering subsequent centuries, more securely demonstrates a landscape of multiple regional centers, of which the Thu Bồn river system associated with Mỹ Sơn and Trà Kiệu is only the most thoroughly documented for this chapter's period, connected by maritime routes and by shared religious, linguistic, and dynastic traditions, but not necessarily by continuous, uniform political administration.

This chapter's position, consistent with the method developed across Chapters 20 and 21, is that "Champa" is best understood, for the centuries examined here, as a name that came to describe a shared Cham-speaking religious, linguistic, and dynastic world distributed across several regional centers along the coast, rather than as a securely reconstructed single territorial state. Some periods and some rulers, such as those associated with sustained construction and inscriptional activity at Mỹ Sơn, may have achieved substantial authority extending well beyond their immediate locality; other periods may have seen greater independence among regional centers. The precise balance between integration and regional autonomy across this chapter's centuries remains, as with Chenla in Chapter 21, a genuinely open historical question rather than one this textbook can responsibly resolve in either direction.

Historiographical Debate

Was Champa One Kingdom?

The question: Did the political developments Chinese sources describe under the name Linyi, and later Cham and Sanskrit inscriptions describe under a name resembling Campa, correspond to a single, continuously unified kingdom along the central and southern Vietnamese coast, or does the evidence instead show a shifting arrangement of regional centers whose degree of integration varied across time and space?

Older unitary-kingdom interpretation: Earlier historical writing, drawing heavily on the sequence and vocabulary of Chinese dynastic histories, often described Champa as a single kingdom, sometimes assigning it a continuous king list stretching from the second century CE founding narrative through many subsequent centuries, and treating shifts in the location of prominent inscriptions or temple construction as evidence of a single kingdom's capital relocating over time.
Regional-centers reassessment: More recent archaeological and epigraphic scholarship, building on the same kind of methodological reassessment associated with Michael Vickery's work on Chenla, examined in Chapter 21, emphasizes that Cham inscriptions attest to multiple regional centers, including but not limited to the Thu Bồn river system examined in this chapter, whose relationships to one another involved varying degrees of alliance, religious and dynastic connection, and independent local authority, rather than uniform, continuous administration from a single capital.

Evidence: The older interpretation relied heavily on the sequence and categories found in Chinese dynastic histories and on treating Sanskrit royal inscriptions as evidence of a single continuous dynasty. The regional-centers reassessment gives greater weight to the geographic distribution of inscriptions and archaeological sites across the length of the coast, which does not sort neatly into a single, continuously governed territorial state.

Why it matters: This debate shapes how historians describe the following several centuries of Cham history, examined in later chapters, and determines whether later conflicts and negotiations between Cham polities and Đại Việt, examined in Chapter 45, should be understood as relations between two comparably organized states or as a more asymmetric relationship between a centralizing Vietnamese state and a persistently multi-centered Cham coastal world.

Student task: Using the Evidence Window above and the discussion of Mỹ Sơn and Trà Kiệu in Sections 22.8 and 22.9, explain why the evidence supports treating early Champa as a network of regional centers with varying degrees of integration, while avoiding the overcorrection of assuming those centers were entirely independent of one another.

Section 22.11

Localization: Śaiva Religion and Sanskrit Kingship

Cham royal inscriptions draw extensively on Sanskrit vocabulary, Śaiva religious traditions centered on the worship of Śiva, and South Asian conventions of royal genealogy and legitimation, most visibly in the Bhadreśvara cult examined in Section 22.7. As in Chapters 20 and 21, this chapter applies the localization framework introduced in Chapter 19 rather than reverting to an older "Indianization" model that would treat Cham religious and political culture as simply transplanted from South Asia.

The distinctive fusion of a ruler's own name with that of Śiva, as in Bhadreśvara, exemplifies this localization process concretely: it is not a straightforward South Asian religious practice imported unchanged, but a specifically Southeast Asian elaboration in which royal identity and religious devotion become linguistically and theologically intertwined in a way that served local dynastic purposes. Cham temple architecture, sculptural style, and ritual practice likewise developed distinctive local forms that, while drawing recognizably on South Asian religious models, cannot be reduced to a simple copy of any single South Asian source. As with the Funan-era and Chenla-era evidence examined in Chapters 20 and 21, this religious and political evidence should be understood as primarily reflecting the practices and self-presentation of a ruling and religious elite, whose inscriptions survive, rather than as a complete record of the broader Cham population's religious life.

Section 22.12

Maritime Orientation and Regional Exchange

Champa's geography, examined in Section 22.4, oriented its economic and cultural life toward the sea to a degree not matched by the more agrarian, riverine, and lacustrine societies examined in Chapters 20 and 21. Cham ports and coastal settlements participated actively in South China Sea and Indian Ocean maritime exchange networks, connecting the central Vietnamese coast to island Southeast Asia, South Asia, and China, in patterns continuous with the trans-Asian exchange networks examined in Chapter 18.

This maritime orientation should not be read as meaning that Cham society depended on trade alone; as with the caution applied to Funan in Chapter 20, historians should recognize that agriculture along the coast's narrow river valleys and plains, together with craft production, fishing, and forest-product gathering from the nearby Annamite highlands, provided essential economic foundations alongside long-distance exchange. Cham communities likely served, moreover, not merely as passive recipients of foreign goods passing through their ports, but as active producers, traders, and intermediaries within these wider networks, following the same corrective this textbook applied to Óc Eo's craft production in Chapter 20.

Section 22.13

Neighbors to the North: Jiaozhou and the Han-Era Frontier

To the immediate north of the Cham coastal world lay Jiaozhou, the Chinese-administered region encompassing the Red River delta and neighboring commanderies, including Rinan, established under Han imperial expansion and examined in more detail in Chapter 23's treatment of the Red River delta under Chinese imperial rule. Chinese textual sources describe recurring episodes of conflict, raiding, and diplomatic exchange between Linyi and this northern frontier across the centuries examined in this chapter, reflecting a persistent, if fluctuating, relationship between Cham coastal communities and Chinese-administered territory.

This relationship should not be read as evidence that Linyi was simply a periphery of Chinese power, nor that Chinese sources provide a neutral account of these interactions; Chinese dynastic histories, as with their descriptions of Funan and Zhenla examined in Chapters 20 and 21, frame these episodes according to Chinese administrative and military interests, typically emphasizing raids, punitive expeditions, and the restoration of Chinese authority. Cham perspectives on these same episodes, where recoverable at all, must be sought through archaeological evidence and through the comparative reading of later Cham traditions, rather than assumed from Chinese accounts alone.

Section 22.14

Neighbors to the West: Funan and Chenla

To the west, across the Annamite mountains and the Central Highlands, lay the polities examined in Chapters 20 and 21. Evidence for direct political relations between the Cham coast and Funan or Chenla during the centuries examined in this chapter is more limited than the evidence for relations with Chinese-administered territory to the north, but archaeological and art-historical evidence indicates shared participation in overlapping South Asian religious and artistic traditions, and later centuries, examined in subsequent chapters, document more extensive and more clearly documented contact, exchange, and periodic conflict between Cham polities and the Khmer world.

Students should resist projecting the more extensively documented later relationship between Champa and Angkor, examined in later volumes of this textbook, backward onto the earlier centuries examined in this chapter, for which the evidence of direct political relations between the Cham coast and the Mekong world remains considerably thinner than the evidence for relations with China.

Multiple Perspectives

The Early Cham Coast: Perspectives on a Multi-Centered World

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across textual, epigraphic, and archaeological evidence. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor.

From the perspective of a ruler commissioning a Sanskrit inscription at Mỹ Sơn: Associating my name and patronage with a great deity such as Bhadreśvara secures my religious merit and dynastic legitimacy for future generations, whether or not every coastal center from the Thu Bồn valley to more distant shores acknowledges my precedence in practice.
From the perspective of a Chinese frontier official in Jiaozhou: A troublesome polity to the south, which our records call Linyi, periodically raids our territory or sends tribute, and our accounts describe it as a single counterpart for the sake of manageable administrative and military record-keeping, whatever its actual internal political organization.
From the perspective of a coastal trader or fisher along the Cham coast: My livelihood depends on the sea, on the goods that pass through our harbor, and on the agricultural produce of our river valley, connections that matter more to my daily life than any distant ruler's claim to authority over the whole coast.
From the perspective of a modern epigraphist examining Cham and Sanskrit inscriptions across several centuries and sites: The inscriptional record resists easy synthesis into a single national narrative; it instead documents specific, dated, locally situated acts of religious patronage and dynastic claim-making across multiple centers, from which only a carefully qualified, comparative account of regional political organization can responsibly be built.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Sa Huỳnh to Early Champa

What Changed

  • Coastal communities moved from the Sa Huỳnh archaeological culture's indigenous social and exchange patterns toward the more visibly documented political and religious institutions examined in this chapter
  • Sanskrit epigraphy and Śaiva religious patronage, alongside a written form of the local Austronesian language, became prominent features of elite culture from around the fourth and fifth centuries CE onward
  • A name resembling "Campa" entered the local inscriptional record by around the mid-seventh century, considerably later than the Chinese use of "Linyi"
  • Relations with Chinese-administered territory to the north shifted between conflict, raiding, and diplomatic exchange across the centuries examined in this chapter

What Stayed the Same

  • The region's maritime and riverine geography, organized around short rivers, narrow coastal plains, and direct sea access, continued to shape settlement and exchange patterns throughout
  • Political authority remained organized around multiple regional centers connected chiefly by sea and by shared religious and dynastic tradition, rather than a single uniformly administered capital
  • Participation in long-distance maritime exchange networks connecting the coast to island Southeast Asia, South Asia, and China continued without interruption
  • Selective adoption and local transformation of Sanskritic religious and political vocabulary, rather than wholesale replacement of local practice, remained the operative pattern

Why the pattern matters: Describing this period as a continuous transformation of an indigenous coastal world, rather than the arrival of a fully formed foreign civilization, allows historians to explain both the genuine religious and political developments documented in Chinese and local sources and the deep continuities connecting this period back to the Sa Huỳnh archaeological culture.

Cause and Effect

Why a Cham Political and Religious Order Emerged

Long-term causes

Centuries of indigenous settlement, craft specialization, and long-distance exchange along the central and southern Vietnamese coast, evidenced by the Sa Huỳnh archaeological culture, created the demographic density and economic foundation on which later political and religious institutions could build.

Short-term causes

Expanding South China Sea and Indian Ocean exchange networks during the early centuries CE drew Cham coastal communities into more intensive commercial and religious contact, while rulers such as Bhadravarman I mobilized Sanskrit religious vocabulary and Śaiva patronage to establish durable centers of dynastic legitimacy such as Mỹ Sơn.

Effect

By the fourth and fifth centuries CE, the central Vietnamese coast supported religious and political centers substantial enough to leave a rich and continuous inscriptional record, while Chinese observers to the north, and later Cham and Sanskrit inscriptions themselves, applied simplifying labels, Linyi and later Campa, to a persistently multi-centered coastal world.

This chapter's response

Consistent use of the working distinctions among the Chinese textual label, the local inscriptional and archaeological record, and the historical populations of the Cham coast, alongside sustained attention to what Chinese, Cham, and Sanskrit evidence can and cannot establish about political unity.

Historical complexity: No single cause, whether maritime trade, religious patronage, indigenous social development, or Chinese diplomatic classification, adequately explains the emergence of Cham political and religious order; the evidence instead points to a genuinely multi-causal process unfolding unevenly across several centuries and several regional centers.

Everyday Life and Social History

Farmers, Fishers, and Craftspeople of the Cham Coast

The Sanskrit and Old Cham inscriptions examined in this chapter, produced chiefly by and for a ruling and religious elite, tell historians relatively little directly about the daily lives of the majority of the coast's population: farmers cultivating rice and other crops in the region's narrow river valleys and coastal plains, fishers working the coastal waters and river mouths, and craftspeople producing the ceramics, ornaments, and goods whose long developmental roots extend back to the Sa Huỳnh culture examined in Section 22.3.

Archaeological evidence, including continued ceramic production, agricultural infrastructure, and craft workshops identified at sites such as Trà Kiệu, offers historians an important complementary source for reconstructing this largely undocumented majority's economic life, even where inscriptions remain silent about them directly. As in Chapter 21's treatment of temple dependents in the Chenla-period Mekong world, historians examining any Cham inscriptional references to temple personnel, land grants, or labor obligations should exercise similar caution regarding status terminology, considering each term's specific documented context rather than assuming a uniform modern equivalent.

The coast's maritime orientation also likely shaped daily life and social organization in ways distinct from the more agrarian societies examined in Chapters 20 and 21: seasonal fishing, boatbuilding, and participation in coastal and long-distance trade voyages would have structured significant portions of the year for many coastal households, in patterns that later centuries of Cham history, examined in subsequent chapters, continued to display.

Section 22.15

Reprise: Everyday Life on the Cham Coast

The preceding Everyday Life feature has already introduced the coast's largely undocumented majority population. This brief section draws together why that population's economic activity, agriculture in narrow river valleys, fishing, craft production, and maritime trade, matters for understanding the chapter's central argument about political organization. A multi-centered political landscape connected chiefly by sea, rather than one large contiguous agrarian state, is consistent with an economy in which many coastal and river-mouth communities could sustain themselves and participate in long-distance exchange without necessarily depending on a single dominant political center for their livelihood. This economic pattern helps explain, alongside the geographic and epigraphic evidence examined earlier in this chapter, why early Champa took the multi-centered political form this chapter has described.

Section 22.16

Toward Chapter 23: North to the Red River Delta

This chapter closes without narrating the centuries of sustained contact, exchange, warfare, and territorial competition between Cham polities and Vietnamese states that followed the period examined here. That longer history belongs to Chapter 45, "Champa and Đại Việt," which this textbook reaches only after Đại Việt's own early history has been introduced. This chapter has sought instead to establish the necessary foundation for that later discussion: an accurate, appropriately uncertain picture of early Champa as a Cham-speaking, Austronesian-language coastal world, developing from indigenous Sa Huỳnh foundations, selectively adopting and transforming Sanskrit and Śaiva religious and political vocabulary, and organized around multiple regional centers connected chiefly by sea rather than a single uniformly administered kingdom.

Chapter 23 now turns north, to the Red River delta, examining its incorporation into successive Chinese imperial systems, local resistance, migration, administration, agriculture, cultural exchange, and the development of distinctive regional political traditions that would eventually produce the independent Vietnamese states examined in Volume III. Readers should carry forward from this chapter the same methodological lesson now applied successfully to Funan, Chenla, and Champa alike: a name recorded in an outside or later text is an invitation to investigate, not a map to assume.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined the emergence of Cham-speaking political and religious institutions along the central and southern Vietnamese coast, applying the methodological lessons developed in Chapters 20 and 21 to a new geographic and linguistic setting. It began by distinguishing the Chinese textual label "Linyi" and the later local term resembling "Campa" from the archaeological and epigraphic record these names imperfectly describe, insisting that neither name be assumed to designate a single, continuously governed kingdom from the outset.

The chapter traced Cham society's indigenous foundations in the Sa Huỳnh archaeological culture, demonstrating that social organization, craft specialization, and long-distance exchange predated any Chinese textual description of the region by many centuries. It examined the central and southern Vietnamese coast's distinctive maritime and riverine geography, organized around short rivers and narrow coastal plains rather than the broad deltas and lake systems examined in the two preceding chapters, and explained how this geography produced a pattern of dispersed regional centers connected chiefly by sea.

Chinese textual accounts of Linyi, including the traditional founding narrative associated with Ou Lian around 192 CE, were examined with the same source-critical caution Chapter 20 applied to the Kaundinya founding tradition. The chapter introduced Cham as an Austronesian language and examined the Đông Yên Châu inscription, conventionally dated to around the fourth century CE, as widely regarded as the earliest surviving inscription in an Austronesian language, though its precise date remains debated, demonstrating the early and confident local adaptation of an Indic-derived script to a vernacular language.

Bhadravarman I and the foundation of the Bhadreśvara linga cult at Mỹ Sơn served as this chapter's central case study in Cham kingship and religious patronage, examined alongside Trà Kiệu as a possible associated administrative or residential center. The chapter treated the relationship between these two sites, and the broader question of whether Champa was ever a single unified kingdom, as genuinely open historical problems, following the same interpretive posture Chapter 21 applied to Chenla: rejecting both an overconfident unitary-kingdom narrative and an equally overconfident assumption of continuous fragmentation.

The localization framework introduced in Chapter 19 was applied to Cham Śaiva religious practice and Sanskrit royal inscriptions, with the Bhadreśvara cult serving as a concrete example of how Cham rulers fused South Asian religious vocabulary with local dynastic purposes rather than simply importing an unchanged South Asian institution. The chapter also examined Champa's maritime orientation and its relations with neighboring powers, Jiaozhou to the north and Funan and Chenla to the west, cautioning against reading later, more extensively documented relationships, particularly with Đại Việt and Angkor, backward onto this chapter's earlier centuries.

Throughout, the chapter kept the coast's largely undocumented majority population, farmers, fishers, and craftspeople, in view, using archaeological evidence to complement an inscriptional record produced chiefly by and for a ruling and religious elite. The chapter's recurring argument, reinforced through its Evidence Window and Historiographical Debate features, is that early Champa is most usefully understood as a Cham-speaking religious, linguistic, and dynastic world distributed across multiple regional centers, rather than as a securely reconstructed single territorial kingdom, a conclusion that prepares students for the more extensively documented later centuries of Cham history examined in Chapter 45 and beyond.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has carried the methodological lessons of Chapters 20 and 21 into a new geographic and linguistic setting, and the result confirms how broadly applicable those lessons are. Whether the outside label is Funan, Zhenla, or Linyi, and whether the later local term is Chenla or Campa, the same discipline applies: treat the name as a problem to investigate, read local inscriptional and archaeological evidence on its own terms, and resist collapsing a genuinely multi-centered political world into a single kingdom simply because a convenient label exists.

This chapter has also introduced a genuinely new element into the textbook's story: an Austronesian-speaking coastal society whose maritime orientation, distinctive geography, and early vernacular epigraphy in the Đông Yên Châu inscription connect it to the wider Southeast Asian and Pacific world of island and coastal societies examined in Chapter 14, even as its religious and political culture engaged deeply with the same Sanskritic and Śaiva traditions examined across Funan and Chenla.

Students should carry forward from this chapter a strengthened confidence in applying the localization framework across genuinely different regional settings: the Bhadreśvara cult at Mỹ Sơn, like the temple foundations of Sambor Prei Kuk examined in Chapter 21, demonstrates that Southeast Asian societies transformed the religious and political vocabulary they encountered into genuinely local institutions serving local dynastic purposes.

Understanding early Champa in this careful, evidence-conscious way prepares students for the more extensively documented later centuries of Cham history examined in Chapter 45, and it completes Part V's foundational survey of the earliest documented states of Southeast Asia, a survey that has consistently taught the same lesson across three very different regional cases: a name recorded by outsiders, or adopted only later by a society itself, is an invitation to investigate, never a map to assume.
