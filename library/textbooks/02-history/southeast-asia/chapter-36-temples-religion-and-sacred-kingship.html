Temples, Religion, and Sacred Kingship

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

An Angkorian temple was never only a building. It was a sanctuary for a sacred image, a household of priests and dependents, a holder of land and goods, a school of ritual learning, and a claim about how the living, the dead, and the gods were bound together. Religion at Angkor was therefore not merely an ideology that justified kings; it was one of the main ways in which Khmer society organized memory, property, labor, and obligation.

In 879 CE (Śaka 801), according to the date in its Sanskrit verses, a group of six brick sanctuary towers was dedicated at Preah Ko, in the settlement now called Roluos, south of the later Angkor core. The foundation's principal text, K.713, is a bilingual stele. Its Sanskrit material is dated Śaka 801 (879 CE), while later Old Khmer material on the same stele is dated Śaka 815 (893 CE), so the stele records more than one moment in the foundation's life. As scholars summarize its contents, the texts record the installation of sacred images and then turn to a long catalogue of gifts: vessels, textiles, livestock, performers, and dependent persons assigned to serve the gods. The towers are associated with the god Śiva and with the commemoration of royal ancestors of the ruler Indravarman I. A visitor can still stand before the brick towers. What the visitor cannot see without the inscriptions is the institution that once stood around them: the officiants who bathed and clothed the images, the people who grew or prepared offerings, and the legal language that was meant to keep the foundation intact for generations.

This chapter studies that wider world. It examines the religious traditions practiced in the Khmer lands between roughly the ninth and thirteenth centuries, namely Śaivism, Vaiṣṇavism, Buddhism, and the ancestral and place-centered traditions that ran alongside them. It asks how temples were designed and used, how kings, queens, officials, and nonroyal families sponsored them, how endowments turned a single act of piety into a lasting institution, and how religious change occurred across centuries without a simple replacement of one faith by another. Later developments, especially the growth of Theravāda Buddhism in the late twelfth through fourteenth centuries, are treated as part of this story, but the full transformation of Angkor belongs to Chapter 61.

The evidence is uneven in revealing ways. The surviving record consists chiefly of stone: temples, sculpture, and inscriptions. Stone survives because it was the medium of lasting dedication, and it was the medium of the wealthy, the learned, and the royal. Houses, village shrines, wooden halls, textiles, and the ordinary offerings of farming families rarely survive. Inscriptions in Sanskrit verse celebrate foundations and their founders; inscriptions in Old Khmer prose record land, personnel, and obligations. Both kinds of text tell historians far more about donors than about worshippers. A responsible account of Angkorian religion must therefore keep asking whose religion the evidence represents and whose is missing.

The central argument of this chapter is that religion was a fundamental dimension of Angkorian life rather than a decorative layer on political power. Rulers did use religious foundations to commemorate ancestors and display legitimacy, and Chapters 33 and 34 have examined how sacred authority and political order were intertwined. But temples also gave Brahmin teachers a means of transmitting ritual knowledge, gave donors outside the royal family a way to create lasting institutions, and gave surrounding communities labor obligations, festivals, and relationships with sacred places. The chapter therefore treats temples as religious, economic, educational, and social institutions at the same time, while recognizing that not every temple had the same scale, resources, or purpose.

Several cautions run through the chapter. Angkorian religion was not a single homogeneous system, and it was not an uninterrupted contest between rival state religions. The meaning of the term devarāja remains contested, and sacred kingship must not be reduced to the claim that every living ruler was worshipped as a god. A royal posthumous name does not automatically prove that a particular sanctuary functioned as a tomb. And religious change was not a tidy replacement of Hinduism by Mahāyāna Buddhism and then by Theravāda Buddhism: the evidence points to overlapping practices, changing patronage, reuse of sacred spaces, and institutional adaptation.

The chapter also continues the textbook's insistence on Southeast Asian agency. Khmer communities did not passively receive an Indian religion. As Chapter 19 explained, they selected and transformed ideas that circulated across the Indian Ocean and mainland world. Sanskrit was used as a prestige language alongside Old Khmer; Indian deities were given Khmer founders' names and installed in locally designed sanctuaries; and long-established sacred mountains, waters, and ancestors were woven into the temple landscape. The chapter's division of labor is deliberate: Chapter 37 will treat the religious program and construction of Angkor Wat, Chapter 38 the Buddhist programs of Jayavarman VII, and Chapter 39 the broader social life of the Angkorian world. Here the task is to explain how Angkorian religious institutions worked and how they changed.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why royal monuments provide only a partial history of Angkorian religious life, and distinguish court-sponsored religion from the practices of wider society.
  2. Identify the principal sources for Angkorian religious history, including Sanskrit and Old Khmer inscriptions, iconography, architecture, and archaeology, and assess their strengths and limitations.
  3. Describe the main features of Śaivism, Vaiṣṇavism, and Buddhism at Angkor and the roles of their images, priests, and temples.
  4. Distinguish religious coexistence, shared patronage, competition, and doctrinal or ritual blending, and evaluate the limits of the term "syncretism."
  5. Analyze the architecture of the Khmer temple, including the prasat, enclosure, gateway, and temple-mountain, as an organization of sacred space.
  6. Compare early Angkorian temples such as Preah Ko, Bakong, Prasat Kravan, Pre Rup, and Banteay Srei without treating them as stages in an inevitable progression.
  7. Interpret royal ancestor commemoration and posthumous names while avoiding the assumption that every temple was a tomb.
  8. Explain how endowments of land, goods, and dependent personnel turned temples into continuing institutions.
  9. Evaluate how inscription K.842 reveals the connections among theology, property, hospitality, teaching, and supervision at Banteay Srei.
  10. Trace the transition toward Theravāda Buddhism from the late twelfth through fourteenth centuries as a process of institutional and material continuity as well as change.
  11. Assess the participation of farmers, women, craftspeople, and temple dependents in religious life, recognizing the limits of the elite inscriptional record.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did religious beliefs, sacred spaces, ritual specialists, and temple institutions shape Angkorian society, and how did Khmer communities adapt these traditions across centuries?
  2. What can inscriptions, architecture, and sculpture tell historians about religious belief, and what can they not tell us about everyday practice?
  3. Why is "syncretism" an incomplete description of religious life at Angkor, and what other relationships among traditions did the evidence reveal?
  4. In what sense was an Angkorian temple an institution rather than only a building?
  5. How did commemoration of ancestors, sacred images, and royal patronage interact without making every ruler a worshipped god or every temple a tomb?
  6. Who donated to temples, why did they do so, and how should historians weigh devotion against political and social motives?
  7. How did temple endowments organize land, labor, and obligation, and who bore the burdens of those arrangements?
  8. How should historians describe the movement toward Theravāda Buddhism without presenting it as the simple replacement of earlier traditions?

Historical Context

Chapter 33 traced the political transformations from the pre-Angkorian polities of the Mekong world to the Angkorian kingdom, including the traditions surrounding Jayavarman II and the early foundation narratives. Chapter 34 examined the court, provinces, elite networks, and scholarly models of sacred political authority such as the mandala and the devarāja. Chapter 35 described Angkor as an urban and hydraulic landscape of reservoirs, canals, and rice fields. This chapter takes those foundations as given. It asks what the people who built, served, and endowed Angkorian sanctuaries believed and did there, and how religious institutions were sustained.

The religious background to the chapter is long. Inscriptions and sculpture from the pre-Angkorian centuries, treated in Chapters 20 and 21, show that Śaiva, Vaiṣṇava, and Buddhist communities were already active in the lower Mekong and the plains around Tonle Sap before the ninth century, and that Sanskrit and Old Khmer were both used for religious dedication. Khmer elites participated in religious networks that stretched across the Bay of Bengal and the mainland, as Chapter 19 explained. The ninth century did not create religious life in the Khmer lands; it created a distinctive scale and style of temple foundation, concentrated at successive royal and elite centers such as Roluos, Angkor, Koh Ker, and Banteay Srei.

Two analytical cautions frame what follows. First, modern labels such as "Hinduism" or "state religion" may mislead if they suggest a single church or creed. Angkorian inscriptions name particular gods, ritual lineages, and foundations; they do not describe a unified organization of belief. This textbook uses terms such as Śaiva, Vaiṣṇava, and Brahmanical because they correspond more closely to the evidence, while recognizing that scholars differ on how sharply these categories divided real people. Second, the period covered here, mainly the ninth through thirteenth centuries, saw changes that reached beyond religion: new capitals, changing patrons, shifting trade, and political upheavals. Religious history cannot be isolated from these developments, but neither can it be reduced to them.

Division I · Section 36.1

Religion at Angkor: More Than Royal Temples

The great Angkorian monuments dominate popular images of Khmer religion, and with good reason: they are monumental, carefully made, and often precisely dated by their inscriptions. But they preserve a selective record. Stone and brick endure, while the wooden houses, village shrines, halls, and perishable offerings of ordinary communities do not. Inscriptions were commissioned by kings, officials, teachers, and wealthy families, and they concentrate on foundations, genealogies, and endowments. The religious life that survives in the record is therefore disproportionately the religion of those who could afford to build in durable materials and to commission texts in Sanskrit and Old Khmer.

For this reason, the chapter distinguishes court-sponsored religion from the beliefs and practices of wider society. Court-sponsored religion includes the foundation of sanctuaries by rulers and elites, the consecration of images by Brahmin specialists, the maintenance of endowments, and the use of Sanskrit verse to praise donors. It is documented in detail. The religion of farmers, fishing families, artisans, and household communities included veneration of ancestors, relationships with local spirits and sacred places, and participation in festivals and labor obligations. It is documented thinly and mostly indirectly. The two overlapped heavily: ordinary people supplied labor and produce to temples, and sacred places that mattered to villagers sometimes became sites of elite foundations. But overlap does not mean identity, and historians should not assume that a temple's official theology described what everyone believed.

It is also important to understand what an Angkorian "temple" was. Khmer sanctuaries were not congregational halls in which the laity assembled to hear teaching. The sanctuary chamber was small and housed a sacred image, usually a liṅga, an image of a deity, or an image associated with a commemorated person; rites were performed by specialists, and different categories of people probably had different degrees of access to different parts of the complex, although the evidence for the details is limited. A temple was a sacred residence for the deity and a center for services of care, offering, and teaching, and the surrounding land, water, and people were part of the institution.

Division I · Section 36.2

Religious Inheritance: From Pre-Angkorian Traditions to Angkor

Religion at Angkor grew from an older foundation. Inscriptions and sanctuaries from the sixth through eighth centuries, including the large brick temple complex at Sambor Prei Kuk, associated with the seventh-century ruler Īśānavarman I, show devotion to Śiva, Viṣṇu, and the Buddha in the lands later governed from Angkor. Sculpture from the pre-Angkorian period includes images of Harihara, a deity combining Śiva (Hara) and Viṣṇu (Hari) in a single figure, and Buddhist images attest the presence of Buddhist communities. Old Khmer was already used for inscriptions: one of the earliest dated Old Khmer texts, from Angkor Borei, is dated to 611 CE, and Sanskrit verse appears beside Old Khmer prose in the same period.

What Khmer communities did with these inherited traditions matters as much as what they inherited. The practice of naming an installed deity after its founder is a good example. A liṅga installed by a ruler or elite might carry a name formed from the founder's name plus the suffix -īśvara, "lord," so that Indravarman's Bakong liṅga was called Indreśvara. This tied a pan-Asian religious idea, the worship of Śiva through the liṅga, to a distinctively local assertion about founder, lineage, and place. Similarly, Khmer sanctuaries used brick, sandstone, and laterite construction techniques, tower plans, and decorative programs that were not simply copied from South Asian temples. Scholars generally interpret such combinations as the localization discussed in Chapter 19: selective adoption and transformation by Southeast Asian elites and communities.

Continuity should not be exaggerated. The ninth-century foundations, with their scale, concentration of resources, and tight relationship with the rulers of the new Angkorian kingdom, differ from many pre-Angkorian foundations in the surviving record. But the religious vocabulary, the specialists, and the models of dedication were largely inherited. Angkorian religious history is best understood as a transformation of existing practice under new political and economic conditions.

Division I · Section 36.3

Sources for Angkorian Religious History

Historians reconstruct Angkorian religion from several kinds of evidence, each with its own strengths. Inscriptions are the most important. Sanskrit inscriptions are usually in verse and often open with invocations of deities and praise of the founder, sometimes including genealogies. Old Khmer inscriptions are generally in prose and record the practical consequences of a foundation: land, goods, personnel, boundaries, and obligations. Inscriptions are commonly carved on the doorposts of sanctuaries or on stone stelae. Dated inscriptions typically use the Śaka era, and the standard catalogue numbers, such as K.713 or K.842, derive from the corpus compiled by George Cœdès, now supplemented by modern digital editions such as those of the DHARMA project.

Architecture and iconography provide a second body of evidence. The plan of a sanctuary, the position of its doors, and the sculpture on its lintels, pediments, and walls reveal how its builders imagined sacred space. Identification of images depends on attributes (a trident, a disc and conch, a lotus), on accompanying inscriptions, and on comparison with other monuments. Archaeology adds information on construction techniques, ritual deposits, settlement around temples, and later reuse, although excavation of religious sites has been uneven and the remains of perishable structures are limited.

Later and outside accounts supplement the record. The Chinese envoy Zhou Daguan wrote about the Khmer capital after his 1296–1297 stay and described several categories of religious specialists, whose Chinese labels scholars usually, though not uncontestedly, map onto Buddhist monks, Brahmin-associated specialists, and a third group often interpreted as Śaiva ascetics. His account is valuable because it comes from an observer unconnected to the court's own self-presentation, but it reflects a single visit near the end of the period and the interests and assumptions of a Chinese official. Modern Cambodian religious practice offers comparative clues, but using present-day practice to reconstruct the ninth century requires great caution. As a whole, these sources reveal official beliefs and elite foundations far more readily than everyday religious practice, and the chapter's conclusions are accordingly strongest for temples and their patrons, and more tentative for ordinary religious life.

Division I · Section 36.4

Religious Pluralism and Historical Change

A central interpretive question is how to describe the relationships among religious traditions in the Angkorian world. Older accounts often described a succession of "state religions," with Śaivism dominant under some rulers, Vaiṣṇavism under others, and Buddhism later displacing both. More recent scholarship, including Julia Estève's study of Angkorian religion through Khmer inscriptions and their social contexts, has emphasized multiple interacting traditions rather than a single dominant creed. Under this approach, a ruler's preferred deity does not define the whole religious landscape, and the presence of several traditions within one court or one family does not prove that they were merged.

Four relationships need to be kept distinct. Coexistence means that different traditions were practiced in the same society. Shared patronage means that the same donors supported institutions of more than one tradition. Competition means that traditions or their patrons contended for resources, status, or royal favor. Blending means that doctrine or ritual from one tradition was actually fused with another. The evidence supports all four in different cases, but it does not support assuming that one implies another. A ruler who supported a Buddhist minister did not necessarily hold Buddhist beliefs, and a deity such as Harihara combining Śiva and Viṣṇu represents a specific doctrinal and iconographic solution rather than a general merging of all religions. Section 36.8 and its Historical Debate examine this problem directly.

The chapter's chronology concentrates on the ninth through thirteenth centuries, when the evidence for temple foundations and endowments is richest. Later developments, especially the growth of Theravāda institutions and the reuse of earlier sanctuaries, are treated in Section 36.24 as necessary context. Modern categories such as "Hinduism" are used sparingly and only where scholarly convention requires them, since the inscriptions identify specific gods, teachers, and traditions rather than a single named religion.

Division II · Section 36.5

Śaivism: Śiva, the Liṅga, and Sacred Authority

Śiva is the deity most frequently named in the principal royal and elite foundations of the ninth and tenth centuries. Preah Ko, the Bakong, Phnom Bakheng, Banteay Srei, and later the Baphuon were Śaiva sanctuaries in the sense that their principal sacred object was a form of Śiva, most often a liṅga. The inscriptions reveal that this devotion rested on a body of ritual knowledge. The Sdok Kak Thom inscription, examined in Chapter 33, names Śaiva scriptures that scholars connect with the Śaiva ritual traditions of South Asia, and priests of Śaiva lineages appear as royal chaplains and teachers across the period.

Historical Vocabulary
Liṅga

Definition: An aniconic (non-figural) emblem of Śiva, usually a cylindrical stone set upright on a base, often with a channel (the somasūtra) through which water poured over the stone could drain away.

Historical context: At Angkor, a liṅga was normally the central sacred object of a Śaiva sanctuary. It was consecrated in a ritual, given a name (often combining the founder's name with -īśvara), and bathed and honored in regular worship. Kings, queens, and elite families installed liṅgas, and the name chosen linked the god to the founder.

Why it matters: The liṅga was the focal point of many temples whose inscriptions record the endowments that supported them. Understanding it explains why so many Angkorian sanctuaries contain no figural statue of the principal deity.

Potential misunderstanding: Modern viewers sometimes read the liṅga primarily through later or external interpretations. In Angkorian evidence, it is best understood through its ritual role and the inscriptions that accompany it. The liṅga of a king did not make the king himself a god; scholars debate what the naming conventions imply.

Śaiva worship included the consecration of the liṅga, its ritual bathing, and offerings, performed by Brahmin officiants. The relationship between Śaiva devotion and kingship is a major theme of Khmer history, but it must be described carefully. Rulers installed liṅgas bearing their own names or those of ancestors and presented themselves as devotees under Śiva's protection. Scholars differ on whether such naming expressed an identification of the king with Śiva, a special sacred relationship between founder and god, or a way of rooting a dynastic cult in a widely recognized deity. Chapter 34 has examined the competing interpretations of the devarāja. For present purposes, the safest statement is that the evidence shows Śaiva ritual authority closely connected to kingship, not that every Khmer ruler was worshipped as Śiva in his lifetime.

Śaiva worship rested on specialized learning. Scholars identify the scriptures named in the Sdok Kak Thom inscription (Chapter 33) with the Śaiva Mantramārga, a tradition in which ritual power was transmitted through initiation and guided by authoritative scriptures, and Alexis Sanderson's studies of the Śaiva religion among the Khmers remain a basic reference for this inference. The implication is that serious Śaiva ritual was not a matter of general devotion alone. Officiants had to be trained and initiated, and a consecrated liṅga was a ritual object whose installation required the competence of a qualified teacher. Inscriptions confirm the social side of this arrangement by recording teachers, pupils, and families of priests, even where they do not describe the liturgy itself.

Banteay Srei offers unusually concrete evidence of Śaiva devotion in practice. According to its foundation stele K.842, the central liṅga was named Śrī Tribhuvanamaheśvara, "Great Lord of the Threefold World," an appellation of Śiva. A learned Sanskrit hymn of eleven verses to Śiva, carved in a doorway of the temple and edited and translated by Dominic Goodall, shows that Śaiva devotion was expressed in sophisticated poetry as well as in ritual. A short Sanskrit verse (K.574) records that the founder Yajñavarāha installed images of Umā and Maheśvara for the increase of his parents' merit, so that a single complex combined an aniconic liṅga with figural images of Śiva and his consort and linked both to family commemoration. These examples show Śaiva religion as devotion, scholarship, ritual, and family piety at once.

Śaivism also shaped sacred geography. Vat Phou's mountain, known in inscriptions as the Liṅgaparvata, was central to the Khmer Śaiva landscape according to Sanderson's analysis, and its long record of use illustrates how Śaiva institutions attached themselves to places that were already sacred. The devarāja debates examined in Chapter 34 concern how this religious world related to kingship; the evidence discussed here shows that Śaiva sanctuaries were founded by many people, in many places, for many reasons, and not only by rulers seeking a cult of the king.

Division II · Section 36.6

Vaiṣṇavism: Viṣṇu, Avatāras, and Royal Patronage

Viṣṇu was honored in the Khmer lands long before Angkor Wat. Pre-Angkorian sculpture includes images of Viṣṇu and of Harihara, and Viṣṇu continued as a major deity in the Angkorian centuries. The brick sanctuary of Prasat Kravan, dedicated in 921, is a notable Vaiṣṇava foundation: its interior walls carry brick reliefs of Viṣṇu in his four-armed form and of the goddess Lakṣmī, a rare survival of interior sacred imagery. Inscription K.269 identifies a group of high-ranking officials as the commissioners of the temple in 921, not the reigning king, a reminder that important sacred foundations were not exclusively royal.

Vaiṣṇava devotion at Angkor was expressed through images, ritual, and narrative. Viṣṇu's avatāras, the forms through which the god intervenes in the world, and narratives from the epics and Purāṇas were represented in sculpture across the period; scenes from the Rāmāyaṇa and Kṛṣṇa legends are well attested in Khmer art, including at Banteay Srei. These narratives were sources of religious teaching and also of royal imagery: Sanskrit verses frequently compared kings to Viṣṇu for their power to protect and sustain the world. The comparison is a rhetorical and theological idiom of praise and does not by itself show that a king was worshipped as Viṣṇu in life. Posthumous names such as the one given to Suryavarman II, Paramaviṣṇuloka, suggest the continuing association between Viṣṇu and royal memory, a theme taken up in Chapter 37, which treats Angkor Wat's Vaiṣṇava program. This section therefore establishes only the earlier basis on which that program rested.

Vaiṣṇava worship is documented in far less ritual detail than Śaiva worship. Historians depend chiefly on images, on inscriptions that invoke Viṣṇu or praise donors in Vaiṣṇava terms, and on posthumous names. The posthumous name Viṣṇuloka, given to Jayavarman III, who died no later than 877, shows Viṣṇu already present in royal memory before the great Angkorian temples. The name of the Roluos capital, Hariharālaya, incorporates Harihara, the combined Śiva-Viṣṇu deity, indicating that the idea of a single sacred figure uniting the two gods carried prestige in the ninth century. These are better described as a Vaiṣṇava presence within a shared religious culture than as a rival cult.

Banteay Srei is a Śaiva foundation, yet its pediments and lintels carry scenes from epic and Purāṇic narratives of several kinds, among them Rāvaṇa shaking Mount Kailāsa, the mountain of Śiva, and Kṛṣṇa's killing of Kaṃsa, a Vaiṣṇava episode. The point is not that Banteay Srei was Vaiṣṇava. It is that a Śaiva sanctuary could be decorated with stories associated with other gods, and that sculptors and patrons evidently found them compatible with the sanctuary's purpose. Stories were a shared literary and visual resource, whereas rites of consecration and daily worship remained tied to particular traditions and specialists, a distinction that matters for the debate in Section 36.8.

Division II · Section 36.7

Buddhism before the Great Buddhist Building Programs

Buddhism did not arrive at Angkor with Jayavarman VII in the late twelfth century. Buddhist communities and images are attested in the pre-Angkorian lands, and Buddhist foundations and patrons appear throughout the Angkorian period. Yaśovarman I's late-ninth-century hermitage foundations, known from a series of inscriptions, are described as including Buddhist as well as Śaiva and Vaiṣṇava establishments. In the mid-tenth century, the minister Kavīndrārimathana, a Buddhist serving under Rājendravarman II, is associated with Buddhist foundations at Bat Chum, dated 960. These examples show Buddhist patronage by elite officials within a court whose major royal temples were Brahmanical.

The Buddhism of these centuries included the veneration of the Buddha and of bodhisattvas such as Avalokiteśvara and expressed itself in Sanskrit inscriptions and in images, often associated by scholars with Mahāyāna traditions, sometimes with tantric elements. The posthumous name of Suryavarman I, Nirvāṇapada, is often cited as an indication of Buddhist inclinations, though a posthumous name alone is an uncertain guide to a ruler's personal religion. The eleventh century also saw continuing Buddhist inscriptions and images. Because Buddhist institutions were often smaller or less documented than royal Śaiva temples, historians must avoid concluding from the monumental record that Buddhism was weak. Section 36.24 and Chapter 38 trace how Buddhist patronage expanded in the late twelfth century; the point here is the long earlier history that made that expansion possible.

The evidence for Buddhist institutions is different in kind from the evidence for Śaiva temples. Buddhist communities of this period often appear in Sanskrit inscriptions composed by learned officials and in smaller foundations, rather than in large monumental complexes. Kavīndrārimathana's foundation at Bat Chum, dated 960, consists of brick towers and inscriptions that present a Buddhist minister as a founder and patron. The Yaśovarman I hermitage inscriptions show that a Buddhist establishment was recognized alongside Brahmanical ones in the ninth-century program of āśrama foundations, with rules to govern it. Hermitages of this kind were communities of religious practitioners, and their regulations are generally described as addressing matters such as hospitality and the protection of the foundation.

The content of this Buddhism is known mainly from images and verse. Devotion to bodhisattvas such as Avalokiteśvara, the pursuit of merit, and Mahāyāna ideas are visible in the images and Sanskrit inscriptions; Buddhist officials served rulers whose major temples were Śaiva, and Buddhist inscriptions used the same learned court idiom as Brahmanical ones. In the eleventh century, Suryavarman I (r. c. 1002–1050) is remembered with the posthumous name Nirvāṇapada, and Buddhist inscriptions and images continue. Because Buddhist foundations were frequently smaller, and because the monumental record favors royal Brahmanical temples, historians must treat the apparent prominence of Śaivism as partly a product of survival and patronage, not as proof that Buddhism was marginal.

Division II · Section 36.8

Coexistence, Competition, and the Interaction of Religious Traditions

Evidence for interaction among traditions is abundant but varied. Shared patronage appears when the same court or family supported institutions of different traditions, as in the hermitage foundations of Yaśovarman I or the presence of a Buddhist minister at a Brahmanical court. Shared sacred vocabulary appears when inscriptions invoke several deities, or when a king is praised through comparisons with Viṣṇu and Śiva in a single verse. Shared sacred space appears when later builders reuse or modify older sanctuaries. Specific doctrinal and iconographic fusion appears in figures such as Harihara. Competition is attested in episodes of image modification or removal in later centuries, discussed in Chapters 38 and 61.

But these facts do not all point to a single conclusion. An individual donor who supported both a Śaiva foundation and a Buddhist one might have done so because both met social or familial obligations, because different institutions served different needs, or because the donor valued both; none of these possibilities proves that the doctrines were identical. The difference between traditions was real in ritual, textual authority, and institutional organization, even when they shared patrons and a Sanskrit literary culture. The Historical Debate below asks students to weigh these possibilities.

The practical task is to ask, for each piece of evidence, which relationship it demonstrates. Shared patronage is clearest at court: the guru Yajñavarāha, a Śaiva Brahmin who founded Banteay Srei, served Jayavarman V, while Kavīndrārimathana, a Buddhist, served Rājendravarman II. Both men were prominent learned elites and both founded temples, but their foundations remained institutionally separate. Shared visual resources are shown by Banteay Srei's mixed narrative sculpture. Specific doctrinal and iconographic fusion appears in Harihara, which has its own history and does not license the conclusion that all deities were treated as one. Competition is better documented for later centuries, when images were modified, than for the ninth and tenth, though competition for royal favor and resources among elite foundations is plausible and sometimes visible.

Careful reading of inscriptions helps. An opening invocation of a god tells historians about the donor's tradition. A list of recipients of gifts tells them about institutions. A hermitage rule tells them how a community was meant to live. None of these, taken alone, shows a shared belief. Dominic Goodall's work on Khmer inscriptions has examined the label "syncretism" in connection with particular texts, and the broader lesson is methodological: general labels must be tested against specific evidence. The Historical Debate below invites students to do so.

Historical Debate

Religious Pluralism or Syncretism at Angkor?

The question: Did Angkorian religion consist of several distinct traditions living alongside one another, or of a blended religion in which Śaiva, Vaiṣṇava, and Buddhist elements were merged?

Interpretation A — Syncretism: Some scholars have emphasized evidence of fusion: Harihara images combining Śiva and Viṣṇu, royal inscriptions invoking several traditions, and the adoption of Buddhist and Brahmanical idioms by the same court. On this view, Khmer religious culture was characterized by blending that produced a distinctive local synthesis.
Interpretation B — Pluralism: Others, including recent work on Angkorian religion through Khmer inscriptions and their social contexts, argue that the evidence shows several traditions, each with its own specialists, texts, and institutions, interacting in particular circumstances. They caution that a shared court or shared donors does not demonstrate a shared doctrine, and that "syncretism" can obscure real differences in ritual and belief.
Interpretation C — Case-by-case analysis: A third approach holds that both descriptions are partly right, depending on the case: some images and texts show genuine blending, while many institutions maintained clear boundaries. The task is to identify, for each piece of evidence, which relationship (coexistence, shared patronage, shared space, competition, or blending) it actually demonstrates.

Evidence: Both sides draw on the same corpus of inscriptions, sculpture, and architecture, including Harihara images, the hermitage foundations of Yaśovarman I, Buddhist inscriptions in a Brahmanical court, and the later reuse of sanctuaries.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and terminology more than basic facts: how much weight to give a shared idiom, whether shared patronage reflects shared belief, and whether "syncretism" describes a social or a doctrinal phenomenon.

Why it matters: If Angkorian religion was a single blended system, religious change after the twelfth century may appear as a rupture with a unified tradition. If it was plural, change appears as shifts in the relative prominence and patronage of distinct institutions.

Student task: Choose one piece of evidence discussed in this chapter and classify it as coexistence, shared patronage, shared space, competition, or blending. Explain what additional evidence could confirm or challenge your classification.

Division II · Section 36.9

Local Spirits, Ancestors, Sacred Places, and Religious Continuity

Alongside the named traditions, Khmer communities honored mountains, rivers, springs, land, and ancestors. These relationships are harder to document, but they shaped the placement and meaning of many temples. Vat Phou, on the slope of a mountain whose summit has a natural form that has been associated with a liṅga, was a sacred place long before and after its Angkorian temple buildings. At Kbal Spean, on Phnom Kulen, liṅgas and images of Viṣṇu are carved into a riverbed and its banks, so that the flowing water itself became a sacred medium; the carvings are generally assigned to the Angkorian period. Mountains such as Phnom Kulen, associated in tradition with early royal consecration, remained important sacred landscapes.

Ancestors were central. Preah Ko commemorated royal ancestors through images in sanctuary towers, and inscriptions across the period record gifts made to secure merit for parents and forebears. Old Khmer inscriptions apply the term kamratāṅ, roughly "lord," to deities and to respected persons and images, an indication that the boundary between honored human beings and sacred beings was not rigid. In modern Cambodia, village-level territorial and ancestral spirits, commonly called neak ta, are widely honored; historians use such practice as a comparison, not as direct evidence for the Angkorian period, because belief and ritual change over a millennium.

It would be misleading to treat these practices as "primitive survivals" opposed to the temple religions. The sources suggest interaction: Brahmanical and Buddhist institutions were established at places already sacred, local deities and place names were incorporated into foundation language, and communities that served temples continued to maintain their own ritual relations with land and water. The significance for this chapter is that religious continuity at Angkor depended as much on place and kinship as on doctrine, and that the great temples sat within a wider sacred landscape that they did not wholly control.

Division III · Section 36.10

The Khmer Temple: Sanctuary, Enclosure, and Sacred Organization

The basic unit of Khmer religious architecture is the prasat, a tower sanctuary. A prasat typically has a small central chamber (the cella) for the principal image, a corbelled vault rising above it, and exterior doors, often with some of them false, that mark the sacred orientation of the building. Most Angkorian temples face east. The sanctuary may stand alone, as in simple foundations, or in groups, such as the six brick towers of Preah Ko or the central and subsidiary towers of larger complexes.

Historical Vocabulary
Prasat

Definition: A Khmer tower sanctuary, from Sanskrit prāsāda, a "palace" or elevated shrine; the standard form of Angkorian sacred building.

Historical context: A prasat houses the central sacred image in a small interior chamber, with a tiered roof of corbelled masonry. Larger temples arrange multiple prasats and subsidiary structures within enclosures.

Why it matters: The prasat is the architectural expression of the sanctuary as the god's dwelling. Its small interior reflects that it was a place for the image and its officiants, not a hall for large gatherings.

Potential misunderstanding: The tower is not a "tomb" by definition. Some prasats commemorate deceased persons, but others honor deities without any funerary role.

Larger temples organized sacred space in concentric layers. The central sanctuary lay within enclosure walls, often of laterite, pierced by gateways (gopuras) that controlled passage. Courtyards held subsidiary shrines, long halls, and small "library" buildings, whose precise uses remain debated. Causeways, terraces, and stairs led visitors through successive thresholds. This arrangement made architecture an organization of relationships: between the sacred image and the priests who tended it, between priests and donors, and between the temple and the wider community outside its walls. Historians infer these functions from layout, inscriptions, and comparison, and the architecture itself records physical arrangements rather than specific ritual practice.

Specific plans illustrate the variety of arrangements. Preah Ko arranges six brick sanctuary towers in two rows on a common low platform, facing east, so that the sanctuaries read as a related group, an arrangement that fits their dedication to commemorated ancestors in male and female pairs. The Bakong places its principal tiered sanctuary within a series of enclosures and moats, with smaller brick towers around the base. Banteay Srei is organized in concentric enclosures entered through gateways, and its foundation stele was found in one of the gopuras, in the outer enclosures, in 1936. The location is a reminder that inscriptions were displayed at points of passage as well as inside sanctuaries; doorposts and stelae at gateways made the foundation's terms visible to those who entered.

Within the enclosures, the "libraries" and long halls are less securely understood. Their names are modern conventions, and scholars debate whether libraries held manuscripts, ritual objects, or sacred fire, and what activities long halls housed. Likewise, the restriction of access is mostly inferred from design: a small, dark inner chamber approached through successive thresholds suggests that entry to the sanctuary was limited to officiants, while courtyards and outer spaces would have allowed wider participation. The architecture records physical arrangements; the social rules governing them have to be reconstructed from inscriptions, comparison with other temple traditions, and probability.

Division III · Section 36.11

Temple Mountains, Meru, and Sacred Geography

Several of the most important early Angkorian temples rise in stepped tiers. The Bakong, dedicated in 881 at Roluos, is a five-tiered sandstone pyramid surrounded by smaller brick towers, with a sanctuary at its summit. Phnom Bakheng, built under Yaśovarman I, sits on a natural hill and carries a pyramid with a large number of subsidiary shrines. Pre Rup (961) and, on a larger scale, the central structure at Koh Ker are tiered temples of the tenth century. Scholars commonly call these "temple-mountains" and connect them with Mount Meru, the cosmic mountain at the center of the universe in Indian cosmology and the home of the gods.

The interpretation is reasonable but requires care. The tiered form and the elevated sanctuary suggest a symbolic ascent, and Sanskrit inscriptions use mountain imagery for royal foundations. However, the same temples also served as dedications to Śiva and, in the case of Preah Ko and similar monuments, as commemoration of ancestors. Cosmology, ancestry, and royal prestige were therefore interwoven. The older view that a "state temple" at the capital's center was the single focal point of a ruler's cult has been qualified by recognition that rulers and elites founded multiple sanctuaries, and that temples had varied roles. The temple-mountain should be understood as one important type of sacred architecture, not the only form of Angkorian religious building.

The specific examples show variation. The Bakong's five tiers rise in diminishing steps toward a summit sanctuary, and stone elephants stand at the corners of the lower tiers. Phnom Bakheng uses a natural hill as its core, enhancing an existing elevation instead of building a free-standing pyramid, and thereby joins sacred geography to constructed form. The Baphuon, built in the eleventh century, continued the tradition of a tiered Śaiva sanctuary on a large scale. The identification of such temples with Meru is a modern scholarly interpretation supported by inscriptional mountain imagery and by form, but few texts state explicitly that a given tiered temple represents Meru. Historians therefore treat it as a well-supported hypothesis, not a settled fact.

Alternative and complementary readings matter. Tiered temples were also dedications to Śiva and, in some cases, to ancestors, and temple-mountains and flat-plan temples coexisted. Banteay Srei and Prasat Kravan, neither a temple-mountain, are among the most important sanctuaries of their time. A map or typology of Angkorian temples should therefore avoid ranking the temple-mountain as the true Angkorian form and treating other plans as lesser variants.

Division III · Section 36.12

The Development of Angkorian Temple Architecture

Angkorian builders worked in several materials. Brick, used at Preah Ko and for the subsidiary towers of the Bakong, allowed fine surface finishing with carved stucco, parts of which survive at Preah Ko. Sandstone, quarried and transported to sites, permitted precise carving and became the characteristic material of later monumental temples, including the Bakong's tiers and the fine reliefs of Banteay Srei. Laterite, a locally available stone, was widely used for foundations, enclosure walls, and concealed cores. Builders did not use true arches; roofs and doorways relied on corbelling, in which courses of masonry project progressively until they meet. Construction was labor-intensive and depended on organized supplies of materials and workers, a matter treated in Chapter 35 in relation to water and landscape and in Section 36.20 in relation to temple dependents.

Temple plans varied. Preah Ko is a row-based group of brick sanctuaries. The Bakong combines a stepped sandstone temple with brick towers. Prasat Kravan is a compact group of brick towers with interior reliefs. Pre Rup combines brick, laterite, and sandstone in a tiered plan. Banteay Srei is small in scale but extraordinarily detailed, built largely of pink sandstone with carved pediments and lintels. In the eleventh century, Ta Keo, an unfinished sandstone temple-mountain, and the Baphuon, a Śaiva temple-mountain built in the mid-eleventh century, show continued experimentation. These monuments should be compared as responses to different patrons, resources, and purposes, not arranged as a ladder leading to Angkor Wat.

Techniques reflect organization and skill. Sandstone blocks were generally fitted without mortar and carved after assembly, which meant that sculptors worked on the standing monument; this is visible at Banteay Srei, where fine relief carving covers the surfaces. Laterite was quarried near the sites, while the sandstone sources in the Kulen hills are generally identified as the origin of much Angkorian sandstone, a link that connects temple building to the sacred mountain landscape and to the transport systems discussed in Chapter 35. Brick structures such as Prasat Kravan were finished with interior reliefs carved in the brick itself, a rare survival of sacred imagery in an interior setting.

The record also contains unfinished and altered monuments. Ta Keo, an eleventh-century sandstone temple-mountain, was left without its full decorative carving. The reasons are uncertain, and historians should not assume a single cause such as the death of a patron or a political crisis. Such cases show that building a major temple required sustained resources and labor, and that foundations might be incomplete, modified, or reused. They also warn against treating the surviving monuments as a complete or evenly preserved sample of what was built.

Division III · Section 36.13

Sacred Images, Sculpture, and Religious Iconography

Sacred images were the objects around which temples were organized. In Śaiva sanctuaries these were liṅgas and, in some cases, images of Śiva or of Śiva with the goddess Umā. In Vaiṣṇava sanctuaries, images of Viṣṇu, often with four arms holding attributes such as the disc and conch, were installed. Harihara combined the two. Buddhist images included the Buddha and bodhisattvas, especially Avalokiteśvara. Guardian figures flanked doors and entrances, and narrative sculpture on lintels and pediments depicted episodes from epic and Purāṇic stories.

Art historians identify deities by attributes, by accompanying inscriptions, and by comparison with other works. Inscriptions at sanctuary doorposts frequently record that an image was installed on a certain date and give its name, linking a specific object to a founder and a time. But identification is not always straightforward. Images were restored, relocated, recarved, or defaced in later centuries; Buddhist images might be altered when patronage changed, and sanctuaries of one tradition might later house another's images. A statue or a sanctuary found in a given condition today may therefore represent a later phase of use. Historians accordingly distinguish the original dedication from later modification, and they treat the surviving record as the outcome of centuries of care, reuse, and damage.

Inscriptions often explain what an image was meant to do. The verse K.574 states that Yajñavarāha installed images of Umā and Maheśvara for the increase of his parents' merit, which ties an image to a family purpose; the same complex records that his sister Jāhnavī installed a liṅga of Īśvara in the southern tower, as a short inscription on the southern doorjamb states. Such texts show that images were donated by individuals for stated reasons, with names and relationships recorded. They also show that sanctuaries within a single complex could belong to different members of a founding family.

Identification of a deity from an image alone can be uncertain, which is why historians prefer to combine attributes, inscriptions, and findspot. Context may have changed: images may have been moved, reused, or damaged, and later alterations can obscure original identity. When a modern museum label gives a deity's name, it often rests on an interpretation that may deserve a note of uncertainty. Treating sacred images as sources therefore requires attention to their later histories as well as to their first dedication.

Division III · Section 36.14

Sacred Space in Practice: Orientation, Procession, Water, and Ritual Movement

Sacred architecture shapes movement. Approaching an Angkorian temple typically involved crossing a causeway, entering through a gateway, passing through courtyards, and ascending steps toward a sanctuary whose interior was small and restricted. The orientation toward the east, the raising of the sanctuary above the surrounding ground, and the sequence of thresholds expressed a graded sacredness. Circumambulation, walking around a sacred center, is a widely attested South and Southeast Asian practice that the layout of courtyards and terraces would have supported, though the Angkorian evidence for specific processional routes is mainly architectural and comparative.

Water was an integral part of the experience. Moats around temple enclosures and ponds within them have been interpreted both as practical features and as symbolic oceans surrounding the cosmic mountain. Ritual bathing of the liṅga required water, and the channel of the pedestal carried used water away. The engineering of reservoirs and canals belongs to Chapter 35; the point here is that water had ritual meaning as well as agricultural function, and that the same water system supported both fields and sanctuaries. Historians should keep distinct what a structure physically contained (a pond, a channel, a causeway) from what they infer about the ritual that took place there; the former is observable, the latter is reconstructed from texts, comparison, and plausibility.

Evidence for how worshippers actually moved is limited. Architecture suggests a ritual choreography of approach, ascent, and circumambulation, and inscriptions suggest that guests and visitors were received at foundations, as the hospitality provisions at Banteay Srei indicate. Beyond that, the sources say little about processions, festivals, or the participation of ordinary people. A responsible account says what the spaces allowed, what texts record, and where inference begins. The experience of a temple would have differed for officiant, donor, dependent worker, and visiting traveler, and the surviving evidence mostly reflects the first two.

Division IV · Section 36.15

Consecration, Offerings, Festivals, and Temple Ritual

The founding of a sanctuary was marked by consecration: the installation of the image in a rite performed by Brahmin officiants. Inscriptions record the dates of such events, often with the Śaka year, month, and day, and name the image installed. These records are directly documented evidence that consecration occurred and that it was remembered as the beginning of the institution. The detailed content of the ritual is less well documented. Historians reconstruct it by comparison with the Śaiva and Vaiṣṇava ritual traditions of South Asia and with the scriptures named in Khmer inscriptions, an approach that is informative but inferential, because Khmer practice may have differed.

Daily worship involved bathing, clothing, and honoring the image with offerings. Inscriptions list items given for such use, including food, lamps, perfumes, textiles, and vessels. The purposes of ritual, as expressed in inscriptions, included sustaining the god's favor, securing merit for donors and their families, and protecting the realm. Periodic festivals are suggested by inscriptional references to recurring ceremonies and by Zhou Daguan's later description of festive occasions, but detailed festival calendars for the ninth through twelfth centuries are not securely documented. As elsewhere, the chapter distinguishes what inscriptions affirm (consecrations, gifts, obligations) from what is reconstructed (specific liturgies and festival practice).

The founding of Banteay Srei illustrates what inscriptions can establish. K.842 carries a foundation date of Śaka 889 (967 CE) and later royal provisions dated Śaka 890 (968 CE), which means that the institution's setup was recorded in more than one stage: first the foundation and its deity, then the king's provisions for supporting and protecting it. Directly documented are the dates, the named deity, and the stated obligations. Not documented in detail are the liturgy performed at consecration, the participants, and the festivals that followed. Where scholars describe these, they rely on Śaiva ritual texts and on comparison, and the descriptions should be understood as reconstruction.

A useful distinction is among three levels of evidence. Documented practice includes consecration with dates, the installation of images, and the assignment of offerings. Reasonable inference includes the daily rites of bathing, clothing, and offering, which are implied by endowments and by comparison with other temples. Speculative reconstruction includes detailed festival calendars and liturgical sequences for which the Angkorian evidence is thin. Students should be able to identify which level a given claim rests on.

Division IV · Section 36.16

Brahmins, Religious Teachers, and the Authority of Ritual Knowledge

Ritual knowledge required specialists. Brahmins performed consecrations, daily worship, and ceremonies for kings and elite families, and served as royal chaplains (purohita). Teachers (ācārya, guru) transmitted knowledge of scriptures, Sanskrit, and rites to pupils, and family lineages of priests held and passed on offices. The Sdok Kak Thom inscription, discussed in Chapter 33, shows one such family's long association with royal ritual. Yajñavarāha, founder of Banteay Srei, was a Brahmin teacher, and his foundation (Section 36.22) shows how a ritual specialist could also become a patron and institution-builder.

Historical Vocabulary
Ācārya

Definition: A teacher and ritual authority, in Sanskrit usage a master who instructs pupils in sacred knowledge and may preside at rites.

Historical context: In Angkorian inscriptions, ācāryas and gurus appear as teachers of kings and elites, as officiants at consecrations, and as heads or supervisors of foundations. Their authority rested on learning and lineage.

Why it matters: The ācārya shows that religious authority at Angkor was not only royal; specialist knowledge conferred standing that rulers needed and often rewarded.

Potential misunderstanding: The term does not describe a uniform office. Responsibilities and status varied with tradition and institution.

The authority of ritual knowledge was tied to kingship but not reducible to it. Rulers depended on Brahmin specialists for consecration and for the performance of rites believed to sustain the realm, and rulers rewarded such specialists with land, titles, and positions. At the same time, specialists maintained their own lineages and institutions and could found temples of their own. Chapter 34 has examined these relationships as problems of political organization; here the emphasis is on the religious responsibilities of the specialists and on how learning, ritual competence, and family continuity gave them durable social standing.

The transmission of ritual knowledge can be traced through foundations. The scriptural teaching provided for in K.842 shows that Banteay Srei was designed not only to house a liṅga but to sustain learning. The Sanskrit hymn carved in the temple's doorway, and the verse inscriptions of the foundation, show that its circle commanded advanced Sanskrit composition. Yajñavarāha is described in the sources as the guru of Jayavarman V, so his authority linked a temple, a teaching lineage, and the court. Educational institutions of this kind helped reproduce the class of specialists on which both temples and the state depended.

The sources reveal less about how priests were trained day to day, how many pupils a foundation taught, or how advancement was determined. Historians can say that Śaiva ritual traditions required initiation and learning, that families and teachers transmitted them, and that foundations such as Banteay Srei provided for teaching. They cannot easily describe curricula, which must be reconstructed from the scriptures named in inscriptions and from the practice of related traditions in South Asia. Rewards for ritual authority included land, titles, and the capacity to found temples, but they also carried obligations to the foundation and to the ruler.

Division IV · Section 36.17

Royal Ancestors, Posthumous Names, and Sacred Commemoration

Commemoration of the dead was a major purpose of religious foundation. Preah Ko is the leading case study. Its inscriptions record the installation of images in the six towers, and scholars generally associate the front row of towers with male ancestors and the rear row with female counterparts, with the central figures in each commemoration given forms linked to Śiva. The towers thus combine a Śaiva dedication with a dynastic memorial: ancestors are honored through images that take the names and forms of deities. The commemoration of family members in this way expressed lineage, honored parents, and presented Indravarman I as heir to a line.

Posthumous names are another form of commemoration. Rulers were given names after death that reflected a destination or association, such as Parameśvara for Jayavarman II, Nirvāṇapada for Suryavarman I, and Paramaviṣṇuloka for Suryavarman II. These names are valuable evidence of how rulers were remembered and of the religious idiom of their dynasties. But they should be used carefully. A posthumous name does not by itself show a ruler's personal religious practice, and it does not prove that a particular building functioned as a tomb. Some temples, such as Pre Rup, have been interpreted as funerary monuments, but this is an inference, and many sanctuaries commemorated deities, ancestors, or both without any attested burial. Historians therefore treat "temple" and "tomb" as distinct categories and argue for the second only when evidence supports it.

Preah Ko's case shows both the strengths and the limits of the evidence. The usual interpretation of its towers (the three front towers commemorating male ancestors, including Jayavarman II, and the rear towers their female counterparts) rests on the interpretation of the stele K.713 together with the architecture. The stele's two dated portions, in Sanskrit (Śaka 801, 879 CE) and in Old Khmer (Śaka 815, 893 CE), indicate that the record of the foundation was added to over time, though the stele itself does not by itself reveal what ritual commemoration involved in later years. The case demonstrates how ancestors could be honored in the form of deities while the temple remained a sanctuary for Śiva, and it warns against reading a funerary function into every commemorative foundation.

Division IV · Section 36.18

Kings, Queens, and Elites as Religious Patrons

Why did people found temples? Inscriptions give several answers at once: devotion to a deity, honor to parents and ancestors, the earning of merit (puṇya), the establishment of lasting fame, and the demonstration of status. Rulers had added reasons, since a major foundation displayed wealth, linked a ruler to ancestors, and drew resources and specialists into a visible center. Historians should not reduce all religious giving to political calculation, because inscriptions also express genuine devotion and concern for the afterlife; nor should they accept formulaic piety as a complete account of motive. Both belong in the explanation.

Patronage extended beyond kings. Prasat Kravan was founded by officials rather than the king; Banteay Srei by a Brahmin counselor and teacher; Bat Chum by a Buddhist minister. Royal women and women of elite families appear in inscriptions as donors and as figures of religious standing, and late-twelfth-century inscriptions record queens such as Jayarājadevī in roles of learning and religious support, a subject for Chapter 38. The existence of nonroyal foundations means that temple building was a field of elite competition and cooperation, not a monopoly of the throne.

Banteay Srei again provides the clearest picture of a family's patronage. K.842 attributes the foundation to Yajñavarāha, who acted with his younger brother Viṣṇukumāra, as scholarly accounts of the stele describe; K.574 records that he installed images for his parents' merit; and a separate short inscription records that his sister Jāhnavī installed a liṅga in the southern tower. The complex thus records a family's religious program, with different members donating different sanctuaries and images. It also shows that women could be donors in their own right, naming their gifts in stone, although the evidence for women's roles remains thinner than for men's.

The Śaka 890 provisions in K.842 further show that patronage was reciprocal. According to scholarly summaries of the text, K.842 arranges for the merit of the foundation to be shared among the king, the founder, and the religious officials responsible for protecting it. This arrangement gave royal and religious actors a stake in the foundation's survival and shows that patronage was an exchange of benefits as well as an act of piety.

Multiple Perspectives

A Temple Foundation Seen from Five Positions

The king: A foundation commemorates ancestors, honors a deity, and displays the dynasty's capacity to command resources and specialists. It strengthens ties to lineage and to the Brahmin families who perform royal rites.
The queen or royal woman: Foundations and gifts can secure merit for herself and her family, express personal devotion, and, in some cases, establish her standing as a learned or pious figure, though the evidence about women's roles is uneven.
The Brahmin teacher or officiant: A foundation offers a continuing place for ritual, teaching, and the transmission of knowledge, and a secure income for the lineage that serves it. It also ties him to rulers whose favor affects his institution.
The nonroyal donor: A gift to a temple expresses devotion and secures merit, and may also raise the standing of the donor's family among peers and officials.
The dependent community: Assignment to a foundation could bring protection, steady obligations, and a place in an institution, but it also meant service duties and limited freedom; the sources, written by donors, seldom record how dependents themselves saw it.

Why the comparison matters: These groups did not have equal power or equal voice in the record. The inscription speaks for the donor; the dependent community appears mainly as a list of names and tasks.

Division V · Section 36.19

Temple Endowments: Land, Livestock, Goods, and Income

A temple lasted only if it was supported. Inscriptions record endowments of land, including rice fields and other productive property, of livestock, of valuable vessels and textiles, of food and other regular supplies, and of dependent persons. The gift was designed to sustain worship over time: income from land and labor was meant to supply daily offerings, support officiants, and maintain buildings. Donors recorded endowments in stone for several reasons: to establish proof of the gift, to define the property and obligations belonging to the foundation, to secure royal recognition, and to protect the endowment. Many inscriptions include warnings or curses against those who would harm the foundation, and some record royal confirmation or exemptions, indicating that endowments needed legal and sacred protection.

Inscriptional lists must be read as claims, not as audited inventories. The Preah Ko stele K.713 is bilingual, combining Sanskrit material dated Śaka 801 (879 CE) with later Old Khmer material dated Śaka 815 (893 CE). It gives an elaborate list of offerings, including precious vessels, textiles, performers, dependents, and livestock, and the amounts and praise it contains express the donor's wish to display generosity as much as to record accounts. Historians use such lists to understand the kinds of resources dedicated and the scale a donor wished to claim, but they cannot assume that every figure has been independently verified.

Endowment inscriptions had a typical structure. Sanskrit verses commonly invoke the deity, praise the donor, and state the religious benefit sought; Old Khmer prose then specifies the practical terms, including property, personnel, and obligations. The division of labor between the languages follows the division of purposes: Sanskrit expresses merit and claims of protection, while Khmer records what could be enforced and measured. Reading only one portion gives an incomplete picture, which is why the bilingual structure of K.713 and K.842 matters.

Protection took several forms. Texts record sacred sanctions against those who would harm a foundation, assign responsibility for supervision, and, as in K.842, share merit with those charged with protecting the gift. Royal confirmation added legal authority, since a gift confirmed by the king could not easily be challenged by officials. Formulas of perpetuity express the intention that a foundation should last far beyond the donor, although the surviving record cannot show how often that intention was realized.

Scale varied greatly. Preah Ko, a royal foundation, was supplied with an extensive catalogue of offerings; Banteay Srei, a nonroyal foundation, was smaller but carefully defined. The comparison shows that "temple endowment" covered a range of arrangements, and that the size and complexity of an endowment depended on the patron's resources and on the institution's purposes.

Cause and Effect

How Religious Endowments Created Enduring Institutions and Obligations

Long-term causes

Inherited traditions of foundation and gift; the importance of merit, ancestry, and ritual in elite life; an economy able to support non-agricultural specialists; and a legal and epigraphic culture of recording gifts.

Short-term causes

The wish of a ruler, official, or teacher to commemorate ancestors, honor a deity, and establish a name; the availability of land, labor, and valuable goods; and the support of royal or local authority.

Immediate trigger

Consecration of an image, followed by a formal gift of resources and personnel recorded in an inscription.

Immediate effects

Creation of a body of property and personnel dedicated to a sanctuary; assignment of ritual duties; protection by recorded rules and sacred sanctions.

Long-term consequences

Temples became continuing institutions with their own staff, land, and obligations, interacting with other temples, local communities, and the state; foundations could outlast the donor and be reused later.

Historical complexity

Not all endowments were equal in scale or durability. Some foundations were large and richly supplied; others were modest, and some may have been neglected or absorbed by later authorities.
Division V · Section 36.20

Temple Personnel, Dependents, Artisans, and Labor

Inscriptions name a range of people attached to temples: priests and officiants, musicians and dancers, guards, cultivators, cooks, cleaners, and craftspeople. Some tasks required specialized training, such as ritual officiation or performance; others involved ordinary work in fields, kitchens, and workshops. Old Khmer inscriptions often use the term khñuṃ for dependent persons, commonly translated "slave" or "servant." The translation is debated because the word seems to cover a spectrum of dependency, including persons bound by hereditary service, by debt, or by gift, and not all of the conditions implied the same degree of coercion.

Historians therefore avoid assuming that everyone assigned to a temple was either enslaved or freely employed. Evidence suggests differences in status, in the kind of service owed, and in the degree of freedom. Some personnel held specialized and respected roles, and some positions were hereditary. Others were evidently dependent persons assigned by donors along with land. Because the inscriptions were written from the donor's perspective, they record what was given and expected, not how dependents experienced their obligations. Chapter 39 examines slavery and labor in the Angkorian world more broadly.

Personnel can be grouped by function, with the caution that the categories overlap. Ritual specialists performed worship and consecration and, in some foundations, taught. Performers, including musicians and dancers, took part in ceremonies. Service households prepared food and kept the grounds and buildings. Cultivators worked endowed land and supplied produce. Artisans made vessels, textiles, and building elements. Inscriptions list such categories, and often name individuals and families, indicating that the personnel were identifiable members of the foundation's household and that service could be inherited.

The translation of dependency terms is a recognized problem. In at least one major bilingual inscription, the Sanskrit text uses a word translated "slave" while the Khmer text calls the same people "servants of the god," and Eileen Lustig has read the Khmer phrase as suggesting a higher standing than the Sanskrit label conveys. The example shows that the two languages of an inscription could describe the same group in different terms, and that the wording chosen reflected audience and purpose. Historians therefore treat khñuṃ as a category of dependency that varied in degree, rather than as a single legal condition.

The evidence also suggests that dependent status could carry specific rights and burdens: assignment together with land, hereditary obligations, and protection through the foundation's rules. What it does not tell us is how often persons left or were moved between foundations, how they were treated in practice, or how they interpreted their position. These absences are not merely gaps; they reflect whose voices the inscriptions were designed to preserve.

Everyday Life and Social History

Religious Workers and Temple-Dependent Households

For a household assigned to a temple, religion was largely a matter of work. Cultivators grew rice and other produce on endowed land; cooks prepared food for offerings and for officiants and guests; cleaners and guards tended the grounds and buildings; musicians and dancers performed for the deity. Some families probably served across generations, and the temple's endowment provided a framework of obligation and, potentially, protection. Craftspeople supplied vessels, textiles, and building work for foundations that needed them.

Historians reconstruct these lives from lists of names, gifts, and duties, together with architectural and archaeological evidence of workshops and settlements. The record rarely says how dependents themselves understood the arrangement, whether they could leave, or how they took part in festivals. The picture is therefore partial: it shows a large, organized workforce linked to religious institutions, but leaves much of its daily experience unrecorded.

Division V · Section 36.21

Managing Temple Institutions: Administration, Rights, and Ritual Obligations

An endowed foundation needed management. It had to identify its property and boundaries, assign duties, ensure that offerings were made, and protect its rights against claims by neighbors, officials, or other foundations. Inscriptions are the main evidence for these arrangements. They record the allocation of land and people, specify the services owed to the deity, name those responsible for supervision, and sometimes establish relationships with other temples, such as supplies or personnel provided by one foundation to another. Royal officials could confirm donations or grant exemptions, tying temple administration into the wider structures examined in Chapter 34.

The limits of the evidence matter. Inscriptions are formal records of founding or confirming acts, not daily account books. They do not show how rice was measured each season, how disputes were settled in practice, or how successfully obligations were enforced. Historians can therefore reconstruct the intended structure of an institution more securely than its routine operation. What the texts do show is that religious foundations were legally and administratively sophisticated organizations, with inscriptions serving as durable instruments of record and protection.

Management depended on written and oral authority working together. A royal order, in Old Khmer a śāsana, could instruct officials to record or confirm a donation, and the text was then carved so that its terms were visible and durable. A later inscription at Banteay Srei, a doorjamb text dated Śaka 933 (1011 CE), records an act under Suryavarman I connected with land and the god Tribhuvanamaheśvara, and names the official, titled mratāñ khloñ, instructed to have it engraved; students should consult the DHARMA edition for its precise terms. Its existence shows that the foundation remained a matter of administrative attention more than four decades after its consecration.

Several mechanisms protected foundations. Boundaries could be recorded, supervisors named, and sanctions invoked. Links to other foundations, such as the one set out in K.842, created networks of mutual obligation that could reinforce a foundation's position but also constrained its independence. Merit sharing, as in K.842, gave protectors an incentive to defend the foundation. Together these arrangements made a temple a legally defined institution with enforceable claims rather than merely a pious gift.

The limits remain. Inscriptions state obligations, but do not report how often they were fulfilled, how disputes were resolved, or how the foundation's income was used in a particular season. Historians can describe the designed structure of an institution with more confidence than its day-to-day functioning, and should make this distinction explicit.

Division V · Section 36.22

Banteay Srei and the Religious Foundation as an Institution

Banteay Srei, in the northeastern part of the Angkor region, was founded in the late 960s by Yajñavarāha, a Brahmin teacher and counselor associated with the reigns of Rājendravarman II and Jayavarman V. The site is generally identified with a settlement named Īśvarapura. The temple is dedicated to Śiva, and its small, finely carved sandstone buildings are famous for their sculpted pediments and lintels, which include scenes from epic and Purāṇic narratives. Banteay Srei is significant for historians because it was not a royal state temple. It was the foundation of a learned, well-connected nonroyal elite, and its main inscription survives in a form that reveals the institution behind the building.

The foundation stele, K.842, is bilingual. It combines Sanskrit verse, which invokes Śiva and praises the founder and the king, with Old Khmer prose that sets out practical provisions. The text is not a single-date record: the foundation material is dated Śaka 889 (967 CE), while the royal provisions are dated Śaka 890 (968 CE). The DHARMA edition confirms provisions concerning a relationship with another Śaiva foundation, hospitality, scriptural teaching, Śaiva supervision, and protection of the foundation. Taken together, these provisions show an institution designed for continuity: it was to be supplied, to receive guests, to teach, and to be overseen. The temple building was the visible center of a larger arrangement of obligations, a point students can see by reading the inscription alongside the architecture.

Reading the foundation as an institution means following its relationships. The foundation's deity, Śrī Tribhuvanamaheśvara, defined its religious purpose. Its donors were Yajñavarāha and his family, with royal support. Its resources were assigned by the foundation texts. Its obligations connected it to another Śaiva foundation, requiring coordination between institutions. Its hospitality provisions made it a place that received guests, which also tied it to the surrounding region and to travelers. Its teaching provision made it a center of scriptural learning. Its supervision arrangements named responsibility for oversight. And royal protection gave it legal and sacred security.

These elements reinforced one another. Teaching reproduced the specialists who served the foundation; hospitality brought visitors and prestige; protection secured resources; supervision connected the foundation to the court. A building alone, however beautiful, would not have performed these functions. The case also demonstrates how a nonroyal foundation could be woven into the political order. K.842 praises Jayavarman V and shares merit with royal and religious figures, tying a family temple to the kingdom's ritual life, and the later Śaka 933 act confirms that the connection continued.

Students should keep the limits in view. The stele is a foundation text, and the later doorjamb act shows continuity but not the foundation's actual wealth, the number of its pupils, or the lives of its dependents. The most responsible conclusion is that K.842 reveals the intended architecture of a religious institution and that the temple's carvings and plan show the care that went into its sacred center. What made it a continuing institution was the web of obligations around the building.

Division VI · Section 36.23

Beyond the Royal Court: Communities and Religious Participation

Temples depended on the surrounding population. Farmers worked endowed fields or supplied produce; craftspeople made goods for sanctuaries; performers took part in worship; and local donors, some of them of modest means, gave smaller gifts that are occasionally recorded in Old Khmer inscriptions. Women appear as donors, as members of elite families, and among temple personnel. Festivals, offerings, and communal labor connected temples to local life, even where the evidence does not describe the ceremonies in detail.

The limits of the record must be stated plainly. Most surviving inscriptions record elite foundations. Ordinary households honored ancestors and local places in ways that left few permanent traces, and we know much less about their religious lives than about those of donors. Historians use archaeology, later accounts, and comparison to suggest the outlines of local worship, but must avoid treating the elite inscriptional record as a complete portrait of Angkorian religion. What can be said is that religion linked the court and local society through labor, offerings, and shared ritual occasions, while also reflecting inequality in who gave, who served, and who was recorded.

The foundation texts show points of contact with communities. Endowed land lay in a landscape of villages and fields, so a temple's income came from the labor of cultivators who might also be members of nearby communities. Hospitality provisions such as those at Banteay Srei imply that travelers and neighbors came to the foundation and were received there. Teaching meant that pupils came from families who could spare them for learning. These ties made temples part of local economic and social networks, though the inscriptions mostly describe them from the foundation's point of view.

Local religion beyond the temple is more elusive. Ancestors were honored in households, sacred places such as springs, hills, and rivers attracted devotion, and festival occasions brought communities together. The temple's role in these practices varied. In some cases it appropriated a place already sacred; in others, communities' own practices coexisted with it. Modern Cambodian village religion provides suggestive comparisons, but the thousand-year gap requires caution, and historians generally treat the comparison as a prompt for questions and not as evidence.

Women, farmers, and craftspeople appear in the sources as donors, as dependents, and as workers, but rarely as speakers. A balanced account recognizes that temples offered social roles and sometimes protection, imposed obligations, and reproduced inequalities of status, and that these experiences were not uniform across households or regions.

Division VI · Section 36.24

Religious Change: Mahāyāna Buddhism, Theravāda Buddhism, and the Reuse of Sacred Spaces

From the late twelfth century, Buddhist patronage expanded sharply under Jayavarman VII, whose policies Chapter 38 examines. Mahāyāna Buddhist monuments and images became prominent in the capital and provinces. Later in the thirteenth century, some of these Buddhist images and sanctuaries were modified, a development often associated with a Śaiva-oriented reign, showing that religious change was not a straight line. Chapter 61 treats the wider transformation of Angkor.

Historical Vocabulary
Theravāda and Vihāra

Definition: Theravāda is the Buddhist tradition that relies on the Pāli canon and a monastic community (saṅgha) and that became dominant across much of mainland Southeast Asia. A vihāra is a monastic residence or, in later usage, a shrine hall associated with monks.

Historical context: In the Khmer lands, Theravāda grew in prominence from roughly the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, alongside earlier Buddhist and Brahmanical traditions. It involved monastic institutions, use of Pāli, and new building forms. Chapter 53 examines its wider spread across the mainland.

Why it matters: The shift to Theravāda affected architecture, patronage, and the organization of religious communities, not just doctrine.

Potential misunderstanding: Theravāda did not simply replace earlier traditions overnight. The evidence shows overlap, reuse of older sanctuaries, and continuing royal patronage.

Theravāda growth involved changing architectural and institutional forms: monastic compounds, assembly and shrine halls, and communities of monks supported by lay giving. Recent archaeological and architectural scholarship, including Mariko Shimoda's architectural study and the 2026 research of Stephen Berquist and Andrew Harris, supports interpreting this change as involving considerable continuity. Researchers identify early monastic construction and the reuse of older sacred sites, while evidence indicates that royal patronage continued into the fourteenth century. Older Hindu and Buddhist sanctuaries were not simply abandoned; they were reused and reinterpreted, and Angkor Wat itself, discussed in Chapter 37, became a Buddhist site in later centuries. Zhou Daguan's 1296–1297 account distinguishes Buddhist monks, a group usually identified with Brahmin specialists, and a third group commonly interpreted (not uncontestedly) as Śaiva ascetics, evidence that several religious groups were visible at the end of the thirteenth century. This section does not claim a decline of Angkorian religion; it describes adaptation of institutions and spaces.

Architectural evidence for Theravāda comes from monastic remains, not only from texts. Mariko Shimoda's 2022 architectural study examines early Theravāda monasteries in Angkor Thom and proposes a typology of their surviving remains, giving historians a way to recognize monastic building within the older city. Research by Stephen Berquist and Andrew Harris (2026) argues that religious transformation could proceed through familiar architectural and institutional forms, so that new religious practice did not require a complete break with earlier building traditions. Together, these studies support an interpretation of change as adaptation.

Reuse is central. Older Hindu and Buddhist sanctuaries were maintained, reinterpreted, and sometimes given new images. Patronage did not end with the twelfth century; royal support for religious institutions is attested into the fourteenth century. The material record thus shows older foundations continuing to serve as sacred places while new monastic communities grew up around and within them. This resolves an apparent paradox: religion could change substantially in doctrine and practice while the sacred landscape remained largely continuous.

The boundaries of this chapter matter here. The political and economic transformation of the Angkorian order, including changes in trade, environment, and capital, belongs to Chapter 61, and Jayavarman VII's specific policies to Chapter 38. This section has described only the religious and institutional side of change, which was gradual, uneven, and not reducible to either a collapse or a simple conversion.

Division VI · Section 36.25

Religion and Sacred Kingship in Angkorian Society: Continuity, Change, and Historical Interpretation

Taken together, the evidence shows religion at Angkor operating at several levels. It shaped belief and ritual; it gave form to architecture and sacred landscape; it commemorated ancestors and expressed kingship; it created endowed institutions that held property and organized labor; and it connected court and community. These dimensions cannot be collapsed into a single function. Religion was not only royal ideology, since teachers, officials, and nonroyal families founded temples and since communities maintained their own sacred relationships. Nor was it independent of politics, since rulers sponsored foundations, relied on ritual specialists, and used religious language to express authority.

Interpretation remains active. Scholars continue to debate the meaning of devarāja, the extent of syncretism, the function of temple-mountains, and the interpretation of dependents' status. The chapter's cautious approach is to separate what inscriptions and monuments directly document from what is inferred, and to avoid treating Angkorian religion as one homogeneous system.

The chapter's findings can be summarized in terms of institutions. Temples served as sacred residences, centers of learning, landholders, employers, and places of hospitality. Their durability depended on endowments, personnel, legal protection, and royal or elite support, and the same features explain why they could be reused and adapted when religious preferences shifted. Because temples were rooted in communities and landscapes as well as in courts, religious change was typically a matter of reassignment and reinterpretation more than of erasure.

The interpretive lesson is to hold several claims together. Religion expressed royal legitimacy, yet it also expressed family devotion, scholarly lineage, community obligation, and local sacred geography. Angkorian society included Śaiva, Vaiṣṇava, Buddhist, and local traditions that interacted in different ways at different times. And the evidence privileges donors and specialists, so conclusions about ordinary participation must be tentative. These points prepare the ground for the monumental histories of Chapters 37 and 38 and the social history of Chapter 39.

Court patrons and specialists experienced shifts in patronage most directly, while local communities may have experienced greater continuity in their relationships to land and ancestors. Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that religion at Angkor was a fundamental dimension of social life rather than only a justification for royal authority. Temples were sanctuaries, institutions of property and labor, centers of ritual learning, and sites of commemoration, and they connected rulers, specialists, elites, and communities.

The chapter began by showing that royal monuments preserve a selective record. Court-sponsored religion is richly documented in stone and inscription, while the religion of ordinary communities is documented thinly and indirectly. Sources include Sanskrit and Old Khmer inscriptions, iconography, architecture, archaeology, and later accounts, each with limits.

Angkorian religion drew on pre-Angkorian traditions of Śaiva, Vaiṣṇava, and Buddhist practice, which Khmer communities localized through naming conventions, building forms, and use of both Sanskrit and Old Khmer. The chapter stressed pluralism: coexistence, shared patronage, competition, and blending are different relationships, and the Historical Debate asked students to distinguish them.

Śaivism, with the liṅga at the center of many foundations, was prominent in royal and elite temples, closely connected with kingship but not demonstrating that every ruler was worshipped as a god. Vaiṣṇavism, represented by Prasat Kravan and extensive narrative sculpture, and Buddhism, attested long before Jayavarman VII, were also significant. Local spirits, ancestors, mountains, and waters formed part of the sacred landscape.

Khmer temple architecture organized sacred space through prasats, enclosures, gateways, and, in some cases, tiered temple-mountains associated with Mount Meru. Preah Ko, the Bakong, Prasat Kravan, Pre Rup, and Banteay Srei show diversity of materials, plans, and patrons, and should be compared rather than treated as steps toward Angkor Wat. Sacred images, orientation, and water gave ritual meaning to the temple setting.

Ritual and patronage connected specialists and donors. Brahmins and ācāryas consecrated images and transmitted knowledge; ancestors were commemorated, as at Preah Ko; posthumous names reflect memory but do not prove tombs. Kings, queens, officials, and nonroyal families founded temples for devotion, merit, and status, without reducing all motives to political calculation.

Temple economies made foundations lasting. Endowments of land, goods, and dependent persons supported worship; personnel ranged from priests to cultivators with varied and poorly documented statuses; and inscriptions protected and defined rights and obligations. Banteay Srei and inscription K.842 showed how theology, property, hospitality, teaching, and supervision were joined in one institution.

The chapter treated religious change from the late twelfth century as a process of overlapping practice, changing patronage, reuse of sacred spaces, and institutional adaptation, with Theravāda growth involving architectural and institutional change as well as new ideas. This avoids a simple replacement narrative.

Throughout, the chapter sought to separate directly documented evidence from inference and to remember that the surviving record favors elites. Its conclusion is that temples were enduring centers of social organization as well as worship, and that Angkorian religious life must be understood through their institutions, communities, and change over time. Chapters 37, 38, 39, and 61 build on these foundations.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Śaivism
The tradition of devotion to Śiva, expressed at Angkor chiefly through the liṅga and the ritual services of Brahmin specialists.
Vaiṣṇavism
The tradition of devotion to Viṣṇu and his avatāras, represented at Angkor in sanctuaries such as Prasat Kravan and in narrative sculpture.
Mahāyāna
A Buddhist tradition emphasizing bodhisattvas such as Avalokiteśvara, attested at Angkor in inscriptions and images before and during the great twelfth-century building programs.
Theravāda
The Buddhist tradition based on the Pāli canon and a monastic community, which grew in prominence in the Khmer lands from about the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries.
Liṅga
An aniconic emblem of Śiva, usually an upright stone on a base, serving as the central sacred object of many Śaiva sanctuaries.
Avatāra
A form through which Viṣṇu is said to appear in the world, narrated in epic and Purāṇic literature and represented in sculpture.
Bodhisattva
In Mahāyāna Buddhism, a being devoted to enlightenment for the benefit of others, such as Avalokiteśvara.
Harihara
A deity combining Hara (Śiva) and Hari (Viṣṇu), represented in Khmer sculpture and linked to a specific iconographic solution rather than general religious merging.
Prasat
A Khmer tower sanctuary housing a sacred image in a small interior chamber beneath a corbelled roof.
Gopura
A gateway structure in a temple enclosure wall that controls passage between spaces of differing sacredness.
Cella
The small inner chamber of a sanctuary where the principal image stood.
Temple-mountain
A tiered Khmer temple whose stepped form and elevated sanctuary are interpreted as evoking Mount Meru; one type among several temple forms.
Mount Meru
The cosmic mountain at the center of the universe in Indian cosmology and the abode of gods, used in interpreting temple symbolism.
Brahmin
A member of the priestly and learned class who performed consecrations and rites and served as chaplains and teachers.
Ācārya
A teacher and ritual authority who instructed pupils and presided over rites, with varying responsibilities across institutions.
Endowment
A gift of land, goods, and personnel to a religious foundation intended to sustain worship and maintenance over time.
Foundation
A religious institution established by a founder, comprising a sanctuary, its property, its personnel, and its obligations.
Āśrama
A hermitage or religious community, several of which were founded by Yaśovarman I with rules for their governance.
Khñuṃ
An Old Khmer term for dependent persons, commonly translated "slave" or "servant," covering a spectrum of dependency whose exact conditions are debated.
Śaka era
The calendar era used to date Angkorian inscriptions; adding 78 to a Śaka year gives the approximate Common Era year.
Posthumous name
A name given to a ruler after death that reflects memory or religious association; it does not by itself prove personal practice or a funerary function for a temple.
Syncretism
The blending of elements from different religious traditions; at Angkor it applies to some images and texts but is an incomplete description of the religious landscape.
Pluralism
The presence of several distinct religious traditions interacting in one society without necessarily merging.
Vihāra
A monastic residence or shrine hall associated with Buddhist monks.
Devarāja
A term in Khmer inscriptions whose meaning is contested; it should not be equated automatically with worship of every living ruler as a god.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has located religion between the institutional history of Chapter 34, the physical landscape of Chapter 35, and the royal and monumental histories of Chapters 37 and 38. It shows that Angkorian religion cannot be reduced to royal ideology: it was a way in which communities established sacred relationships, rulers commemorated dynasties, donors founded lasting institutions, and specialists organized ritual knowledge, property, and labor.

The chapter also reveals how historians reason from partial evidence. The best-documented religion is that of donors and specialists, while the religion of farmers, dependents, and local communities survives mostly by implication. Careful distinctions, such as between coexistence and blending, between a temple and a tomb, and between an inscription's claim and an audited fact, are essential to avoid overstating what the sources show.

The institutions and spaces examined here shaped what followed. Endowed foundations, reused sanctuaries, and networks of teachers and donors provided material and social continuity as patronage shifted toward Buddhist traditions and eventually Theravāda monastic communities. Chapter 37 turns to Angkor Wat, Chapter 38 to Jayavarman VII, Chapter 39 to everyday life, and Chapter 61 to the wider transformation of Angkor. Throughout, Khmer society appears as an active creator of its religious world, not a passive recipient of outside traditions.
