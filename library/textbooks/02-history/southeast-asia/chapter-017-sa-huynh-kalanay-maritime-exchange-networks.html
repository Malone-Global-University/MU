Sa Huynh, Kalanay, and the Maritime Exchange Networks

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Long before Southeast Asia's earliest documented states, coastal and island communities were already moving pottery styles, ornaments, raw materials, technologies, ritual practices, and perhaps craftspeople across the seas, but shared objects do not necessarily imply a single culture, ethnicity, migration, or centrally organized trading system.

Chapter 16 closed with an image of extraordinary reach: bronze drums cast somewhere in the Dong Son world of northern Vietnam turning up, centuries later, in burial sites hundreds and even thousands of kilometers away, evidence of exchange networks that moved heavy, technically sophisticated objects across enormous distances long before any Southeast Asian state left a written record of its own existence. That image raises an obvious question, one that occupied archaeologists working across coastal Vietnam, the Philippines, Borneo, and eastern Indonesia through much of the twentieth century and that continues to occupy them today: what kind of maritime world could move objects, styles, and materials across such distances, and how do historians reconstruct that world when it left no chronicles, no inscriptions, and no named merchants or rulers?

This chapter opens Volume II and Part IV, "Networks Before Empires," precisely because the evidence it examines belongs to a period before the best-documented historical states of the region, examined beginning in Part V, had yet emerged, or at least before those states left the kind of textual record that allows historians to identify individual ports, dynasties, or trading houses by name. The evidence instead comes chiefly from archaeology: cemeteries along the central and southern Vietnamese coast filled with lidded burial jars; a cave on the Philippine island of Masbate that gave its name to a distinctive pottery tradition; ornaments made from nephrite sourced to eastern Taiwan and found, thousands of kilometers away, in graves in the Philippines, Borneo, Vietnam, Cambodia, and peninsular Thailand; and glass and stone beads whose raw materials, in some cases, originated far outside maritime Southeast Asia altogether.

For much of the twentieth century, archaeologists who noticed similarities among decorated pottery from central Vietnam and pottery from the Philippines grouped this material together under a single label, the "Sa Huynh-Kalanay Interaction Sphere," naming it for two of the most thoroughly studied sites, Sa Huynh in central Vietnam and Kalanay Cave in the Philippines. That label captured something real: communities across a wide arc of the South China Sea and neighboring waters were, in some sense, in contact with one another during the final centuries BCE and the opening centuries CE. But the label has also proven, on closer examination, to be considerably less tidy than it first appeared. More recent research comparing pottery in detail has found that the classic Sa Huynh material is not always the closest match for what turns up in the Philippines, that other Vietnamese sites resemble Philippine material more closely than Sa Huynh itself does, and that the broad category concealed a great deal of local diversity within it.

This chapter treats that historiographical complication not as a problem to be smoothed over but as one of its central lessons. Archaeologists work, of necessity, from what survives: pottery, ornaments, metal objects, and the burials in which people were laid to rest, rather than from written testimony describing who traded with whom, on what terms, and why. Interpreting that material evidence requires distinguishing several different possibilities that can all produce the same superficial pattern in the archaeological record. A pot found far from where its clay was quarried might have been carried there by a trader, carried there by the person who made it, copied locally by someone who had only seen one example, or produced independently using a widely shared technique that owed nothing to direct contact at all. Similarity in decoration, in other words, is evidence that something connected two places; it is not, by itself, proof of what that connection actually was.

The chapter's larger argument follows directly from this evidentiary caution. Coastal and island Southeast Asia in the Iron Age were not isolated backwaters awaiting the arrival of Indian and Chinese traders to be drawn into wider history, a story this textbook has already rejected in Chapter 1 and will continue to reject throughout. They were instead already the setting for extensive, long-distance maritime exchange, conducted by island and coastal communities themselves, using boats, coastal knowledge, kinship networks, and craft skills that these societies had developed over many centuries. When the trade linking South Asia, China, and the wider Indian Ocean world intensified in the centuries examined in Chapter 18, it did not create Southeast Asian connectivity out of nothing. It expanded through, and became entangled with, a maritime world that coastal and island Southeast Asians had already built.

Readers should also keep firmly in view a point this textbook has emphasized since Chapters 13 through 15: shared material culture does not equal shared ethnicity, shared language, or shared political organization. The communities that buried their dead in lidded jars along the Vietnamese coast, the communities that left decorated pottery in a cave on Masbate, and the communities in Borneo and eastern Indonesia that adopted, adapted, or locally reinterpreted nephrite ornaments and imported beads were not one people moving as a single wave, nor were they governed by any common political authority. They were separate, regionally distinct communities whose members chose, for reasons historians can only partially reconstruct, to adopt certain objects, styles, and materials from their neighbors while modifying, rejecting, or reinterpreting others.

Understanding this network on its own terms, before turning in Chapter 18 to its increasing entanglement with Indian Ocean and East Asian trade, therefore requires the same evidentiary discipline that has structured this textbook's treatment of prehistory throughout Volume I: careful attention to what archaeological evidence can and cannot establish, resistance to the temptation to read a single grand narrative into a scatter of similar pots and ornaments, and an insistence that the coastal and island peoples of this period were active participants in constructing their own maritime world rather than passive recipients waiting for history to arrive by ship from elsewhere.

Historical Context

This chapter builds directly on the foundation laid across Volume I. Chapters 12 through 16 traced the uneven adoption of agriculture, the spread of Austroasiatic-, Austronesian-, Tai-Kadai-, Hmong-Mien-, and Tibeto-Burman-speaking populations, and the development of bronze and iron metallurgy at sites such as Ban Chiang and within the Dong Son tradition of northern Vietnam. Chapter 14 in particular established that Austronesian-speaking populations, moving from Taiwan through the northern Philippines and into island Southeast Asia over many centuries, carried with them particular seafaring technologies, including outrigger and plank-built watercraft, along with agricultural practices and language, that they transmitted widely and combined with existing regional knowledge. This textbook does not treat the Austronesian dispersal as the origin of maritime capability in island Southeast Asia; earlier populations in the Philippines and elsewhere in the archipelago were almost certainly already capable of significant seafaring and may well have been embedded in their own maritime networks well before Austronesian speakers arrived. What the Austronesian dispersal contributed was a substantial expansion, intensification, and further transmission of particular technologies and long-distance connections, building upon, rather than single-handedly creating, an already meaningful regional capacity for sea travel.

By the final centuries BCE, communities across coastal Vietnam, the Philippine archipelago, Borneo, the Thai-Malay Peninsula, and eastern Indonesia possessed iron tools and weapons, sophisticated pottery traditions, and access to ornamental materials, including nephrite, carnelian, agate, and glass, some of which originated far beyond their immediate surroundings. This chronological setting corresponds to what archaeologists working in the region often call the Metal Age or Iron Age, a period label describing a technological stage rather than a single shared culture or political order. As with the terminology examined in Chapter 15, students should understand these period labels as analytical conveniences rather than categories the communities themselves would have recognized.

The geographic setting is equally important. The seas connecting central and southern Vietnam, the Philippine archipelago, Borneo, the Thai-Malay Peninsula, and eastern Indonesia, chiefly the South China Sea together with the Gulf of Thailand, the Sulu Sea, and the waters of the Philippine and Indonesian archipelagos, are not open ocean in the sense of the mid-Pacific. They are studded with islands, fringed with coastlines, and shaped by monsoon winds that, as Chapter 4 examined in more systematic detail, reverse direction seasonally and make round-trip voyaging by sail and paddle practical across substantial distances. This chapter treats these seas, consistent with Chapter 3's introduction of Maritime Southeast Asia, as connective corridors rather than barriers separating otherwise isolated communities.

Historians and archaeologists studying this period must also reckon with the layered history of their own discipline. Much of the foundational fieldwork at sites such as Sa Huynh and Kalanay Cave was conducted decades ago, often under colonial or early postcolonial conditions, using methods and comparative frameworks that later generations of archaeologists have substantially revised. This chapter presents both the classic archaeological findings and the more recent reassessments that have complicated them, since understanding how interpretations have changed is itself part of understanding the history.

Section 17.1

From Objects on the Move to Networks before Empires

Chapter 16 left students with a puzzle rather than a resolved narrative. Bronze drums associated with the Dong Son tradition of the Red River region, along with related bronze and iron objects, have been recovered from sites scattered across a wide arc of mainland and island Southeast Asia, at distances that make it unlikely, though not strictly impossible, that a single workshop supplied every community that came to possess one; as this chapter's own nephrite case study will show, a single raw-material source can in fact feed an enormous and geographically dispersed network, so wide distribution alone does not rule out a single point of origin for raw material even when it makes a single point of manufacture doubtful. Some of these objects likely moved as prestige gifts exchanged between elites; others may have moved through more diffuse chains of trade, passing through several hands before reaching their final resting place in a grave. Whatever the precise mechanism, the underlying fact is significant: heavy, technically demanding, and symbolically important objects were capable of traveling very long distances across Iron Age Southeast Asia, well before any of the region's best-documented states, examined beginning in Part V of this textbook, had left written records of their own.

This chapter asks a more focused version of the same question. Rather than tracing a single artifact type across the whole region, it examines a specific, intensively studied cluster of evidence, decorated pottery, jar burials, and ornamental materials, that connects coastal Vietnam, the Philippine archipelago, Borneo, the Thai-Malay Peninsula, and eastern Indonesia during the final centuries BCE and opening centuries CE. This body of evidence has historically been given a name, the "Sa Huynh-Kalanay Interaction Sphere," that this chapter uses cautiously, since one of its central purposes is to show students both what that label captures and where it breaks down under closer scrutiny.

Three propositions guide the chapter throughout. First, objects can move without armies, without states, and without the kind of centralized commercial institutions, ports, customs houses, contracts, that later chapters will examine in the context of Srivijaya and Melaka. Second, materials can move farther than the people who originally worked them, so that the presence of a foreign raw material in a grave does not automatically mean that a foreign person, rather than a foreign object or a well-traveled idea, arrived at that grave's community. Third, and perhaps most importantly for historical method, shared styles do not establish common ethnicity, language, or political organization; two communities that produced similar-looking pottery might have been in direct, sustained contact, or might instead have arrived independently at similar solutions to similar technical problems, or might have been connected only indirectly through several intermediary communities.

Historical Vocabulary
Interaction Sphere

Interaction sphere is an archaeological term describing a geographic zone within which multiple communities share certain traits of material culture, such as pottery form, decoration, or ornament style, understood by archaeologists to result from some form of contact, exchange, or mutual influence rather than from shared ethnicity, language, or centralized political authority. The term is deliberately broad: it commits an archaeologist only to the claim that connection of some kind existed, not to any particular claim about who moved, what exactly was exchanged, or how the connection was organized socially.

This chapter uses the "Sa Huynh-Kalanay Interaction Sphere," a label developed by twentieth-century archaeologists to describe pottery similarities linking coastal Vietnam and the Philippines, as its central case study in both the usefulness and the limits of this kind of category. As Section 17.7's Historical Debate feature examines in detail, more recent, more finely grained comparison of specific site assemblages has shown that the broad label can obscure real differences among the communities it groups together, and can imply a tighter, more uniform connection than the underlying evidence actually supports. Students should treat "interaction sphere" as a useful starting hypothesis for organizing scattered archaeological evidence, not as a settled description of a single, bounded ancient culture.

Section 17.2

The Seas as Connective Spaces

Before turning to specific sites, students need a clear picture of the geography this chapter treats as a single, interconnected zone: the central and southern coast of Vietnam; the Philippine archipelago; the island of Borneo; the Thai-Malay Peninsula, bridging the Gulf of Thailand and the Andaman Sea; and the islands of eastern Indonesia. These lands are separated by the South China Sea, the Sulu and Celebes Seas, the Gulf of Thailand, and the waters of the Philippine and Indonesian archipelagos, seas that, as Chapter 3 established, should be understood as corridors of communication rather than simply as boundaries dividing one community from another.

Coastal and island communities living along these waters possessed, by the period this chapter examines, watercraft, navigational knowledge, and an understanding of seasonal monsoon wind patterns, examined more systematically in Chapter 4, that made round-trip voyaging across substantial distances a practical undertaking rather than a hazardous exception. A community's access to the sea, in other words, placed it not at the margin of Southeast Asian history but potentially near its center, since coastal settlements, river mouths, and strategically located islands could all function as points of contact, exchange, and transmission for goods, styles, and ideas moving among many different communities.

This geographic framing matters because older narratives sometimes implicitly treated island and coastal Southeast Asia as a periphery, significant chiefly insofar as it lay along routes connecting more historically prominent regions, such as India and China, examined in Chapter 18. This chapter insists on the opposite emphasis. The maritime world examined here was not a corridor connecting elsewhere to elsewhere; it was itself a historically significant zone of exchange, innovation, and regional interaction, populated by communities whose choices, skills, and social relationships produced the network this chapter reconstructs.

Section 17.3

Reconstructing a Maritime Network from Archaeology

Because no contemporary written source describes this network from the perspective of its own participants, historians and archaeologists must reconstruct it almost entirely from material evidence. That evidence includes pottery form and surface decoration; the technical sequence by which pottery and ornaments were manufactured; the geological and chemical sourcing of raw materials such as nephrite, carnelian, and agate; the typology and distribution of metal artifacts; the arrangement and contents of burials; and the radiocarbon dating of organic material recovered from excavated contexts. Each of these evidentiary categories carries its own strengths and its own limitations, and a responsible interpretation depends on combining several of them rather than relying on any single type of evidence alone.

Pottery decoration is the most visually striking and most frequently discussed form of evidence in the literature on this network, but it is also, on its own, the least conclusive. Two communities might produce visually similar decorated pottery because one learned the technique directly from the other, because both learned it from a shared third source, because potters or their finished wares traveled between the two communities, or because two independent groups of artisans, working with related raw materials and common technical constraints, arrived at broadly similar decorative solutions without any direct contact at all. Archaeologists therefore increasingly supplement surface-level stylistic comparison with study of the chaîne opératoire, the reconstructed sequence of technical choices, such as clay preparation, forming technique, and firing method, by which a vessel was actually made. Two vessels that look alike on the surface but were manufactured through different technical sequences weaken the case for a shared production tradition, since it becomes harder to argue that both descend from the same workshop or teaching lineage; such a finding is most persuasive when combined with additional evidence, such as clay composition, petrographic analysis, or chemical sourcing, since manufacturing sequence alone cannot fully establish where an object was actually made.

Chemical sourcing offers a different, often more conclusive kind of evidence, particularly for stone and mineral ornaments. By analyzing the trace-element composition of a nephrite ornament and comparing it against samples from known geological source areas, researchers can, in favorable cases, identify with considerable confidence where the raw material originated, even when the object itself was found thousands of kilometers away and was manufactured long after the raw material was first quarried. This method underlies much of the strongest evidence presented later in this chapter concerning the nephrite exchange network linking Taiwan to the Philippines, Borneo, Vietnam, Cambodia, and peninsular Thailand.

Mortuary archaeology, the excavation and analysis of burial sites, provides a further crucial line of evidence, since burials often preserve, in a single sealed context, a snapshot of what objects a community considered appropriate to bury with its dead at a particular moment. Burial assemblages can reveal not only what materials a community had access to but also something about social status, ritual practice, and the symbolic value placed on particular objects, questions this chapter returns to throughout its treatment of Sa Huynh and related sites.

Finally, radiocarbon dating of organic remains recovered in association with pottery, metal objects, or burials allows archaeologists to establish, within a margin of statistical uncertainty, when a particular site or burial episode occurred. Because dating methods carry their own margins of error, and because older excavations sometimes lack the careful stratigraphic recording that later archaeologists rely on, chronological placement for many sites discussed in this chapter should be understood as an estimated range rather than a precisely fixed date.

Historical Vocabulary
Provenance (Chemical Sourcing)

Provenance studies, in archaeological usage, refer to methods for determining the geographic origin of the raw material used to make an artifact, most often through chemical or mineralogical analysis that compares an artifact's trace-element composition against samples collected from known geological sources. For stone ornaments such as nephrite, provenance studies can identify, with a useful degree of confidence, which quarry or source area supplied the raw material from which a particular ornament was carved, even when the ornament itself has traveled a very long distance from that source before being deposited in a grave or settlement.

Provenance evidence is especially valuable in this chapter's discussion of nephrite ornaments because it does not depend on subjective visual comparison of style, the kind of comparison that has proven less reliable in the case of Sa Huynh and Kalanay pottery. A chemical match between an ornament recovered in the Philippines and a quarry source in eastern Taiwan establishes, independent of any argument about artistic style, that raw material moved across that distance. It does not, on its own, establish whether the finished ornament traveled as a completed object, whether unworked or partially worked material traveled and was finished locally, or whether a traveling craftsperson carried both material and technique. Students should treat provenance evidence as a strong foundation for reconstructing exchange while remaining cautious about which further inferences it can support.

Section 17.4

Sa Huynh: Coastal Vietnam in the Early Iron Age

Sa Huynh, a coastal locality in central Vietnam, has given its name to one of the most extensively studied Iron Age archaeological traditions in mainland Southeast Asia. Sites associated with this tradition, located along the central and southern Vietnamese coast and extending into adjacent river valleys and inland plains, are known chiefly through cemeteries in which the dead were interred within large lidded ceramic jars, accompanied by iron tools and weapons, pottery vessels, and ornaments made from glass, carnelian, agate, nephrite, and, in some contexts, gold. These burial assemblages date principally to the final centuries BCE and the opening centuries CE, though the boundaries of this chronological range remain subject to ongoing refinement as new sites are excavated and dated.

Communities associated with this tradition combined maritime access with participation in exchange networks that brought them iron, imported ornamental stones, and glass, materials whose ultimate origins, in the case of some glass and stone types, lay well beyond immediate Southeast Asian sources. At the same time, students should resist the temptation to treat "Sa Huynh" as the name of a unified people, kingdom, or ethnic group. It designates an archaeological tradition, identified by shared burial customs and associated material culture, distributed across a coastal and riverine zone that likely encompassed a number of distinct, locally organized communities rather than a single centralized political authority. This distinction mirrors the caution this textbook applied in Chapter 15 to language families and in Chapter 16 to the Dong Son bronze tradition: an archaeologically defined category describes a pattern in the material record, not necessarily a bounded political or ethnic community.

Recent scholarly synthesis has reinforced this caution by emphasizing that Sa Huynh should be understood as one among several related but regionally distinct jar-burial traditions found along the Vietnamese and adjacent coasts, rather than as a single archaeological culture spreading unchanged from a single point of origin. Sites differ from one another in the details of jar form, burial arrangement, and associated grave goods, even while sharing the broad practice of jar interment and participation in overlapping exchange networks. This internal diversity is itself significant evidence against treating the region as culturally uniform even within what outside observers, working retrospectively, have grouped under a single label.

Section 17.5

Jar Burials and the Archaeology of Mortuary Communities

Jar burial, the practice of interring the dead, in whole or in part, within large ceramic jars, often sealed with a lid and sometimes accompanied by a rich array of grave goods, is the single most characteristic mortuary practice associated with the Sa Huynh tradition and related sites elsewhere in the region. Cemeteries associated with this practice have yielded large numbers of jars together with pottery vessels, iron implements such as knives, spearheads, and agricultural tools, bronze objects, and ornaments crafted from glass, carnelian, agate, nephrite, and occasionally gold. The sheer quantity and variety of ornamental material recovered from some cemeteries indicates that certain individuals, or certain communities as a whole, had substantial access to imported prestige goods and devoted considerable resources to funerary display.

Jar burial offers historians a particularly instructive case study in the limits of archaeological classification. On the surface, the practice appears to unify a wide swath of coastal Vietnam under a single funerary custom. Closer examination, however, reveals meaningful variation: jar shape, the manner in which bodies or remains were placed within the jar, the presence or absence of a matching lid, and the specific combination of grave goods differ from site to site and, in some cases, from one burial to the next within a single cemetery. Recent scholarship has argued that some of these regionally distinct jar-burial traditions may have developed at least partly through local, indigenous processes rather than spreading solely through the direct transmission of a single burial custom from one origin point across the entire coast. This argument does not deny that these communities were in contact with one another and with more distant partners; it instead cautions against assuming that a shared general practice, jar burial, implies a single point of origin, a single ethnic group, or a uniform set of underlying beliefs about death and the afterlife.

The presence of imported materials within these burials, meanwhile, provides some of the strongest direct evidence for this chapter's central claim that coastal Vietnamese communities were active participants in long-distance exchange well before the more thoroughly documented trade examined in Chapter 18. Glass beads, in particular, occur in large quantities at some Sa Huynh-related cemeteries, and analysis of glass composition in comparable Southeast Asian contexts has, in various studies, pointed toward South Asian and, in some cases, more distant origins for at least some of the raw glass or finished beads in circulation. Establishing the precise origin of any individual bead requires specialized compositional analysis, and results vary by site and by bead type; students should therefore treat claims about the ultimate origin of specific glass or stone ornaments as claims resting on particular scientific studies rather than as settled and uniform findings applicable to every bead recovered from every site.

Section 17.6

Kalanay and the Philippine Metal Age

Kalanay Cave, located on the island of Masbate in the central Philippines, gave its name to a distinctive tradition of decorated earthenware associated with the Philippine Metal Age, a broad period characterized by the increasing use of iron alongside continued use of stone and shell tools, expanding mortuary elaboration, and growing evidence of long-distance exchange. Comparative archaeological chronology places the wider Kalanay-related ceramic repertoire across a considerably longer span than this chapter's central case study, extending from roughly the middle of the first millennium BCE into the first millennium CE. This chapter's discussion, and its comparison with Sa Huynh, focuses specifically on the portion of that longer sequence most relevant to the intensified maritime network of the final centuries BCE and opening centuries CE; readers should not assume that the entire Philippine Metal Age or the full Kalanay ceramic sequence is confined to that narrower window. Pottery associated with this tradition is typically carinated, meaning it has a distinct angular shoulder or ridge in its profile, and bears incised, impressed, and sometimes stamped decorative motifs applied before firing.

When archaeologists working in the Philippines in the mid-twentieth century compared Kalanay pottery with decorated pottery recovered from Vietnam, they noted enough resemblance in vessel form and decorative technique to propose that the two traditions were historically connected, part of a single, broadly shared ceramic and cultural pattern extending across the intervening seas. This comparison became the foundation for the "Sa Huynh-Kalanay Interaction Sphere" model discussed in the section that follows. As with Sa Huynh, however, students should understand "Kalanay" as the name of an archaeologically defined ceramic tradition centered on a particular type site rather than as the name of a single people, kingdom, or ethnic group. Related decorated pottery has since been identified at numerous other sites across the central and southern Philippines, again displaying enough local variation to caution against treating the entire tradition as culturally uniform.

Section 17.7

Pots, Styles, Technologies, or People? The Historical Debate

Similar pots may reveal contact, imitation, shared technological knowledge, traveling craftspeople, exchanged vessels, or some combination of these; resemblance alone does not tell historians which mechanism produced it. This methodological caution, introduced in Section 17.3, becomes especially important when evaluating the interpretive category that has dominated discussion of Sa Huynh and Kalanay pottery for decades.

Historical Debate

Does a Sa Huynh–Kalanay Interaction Sphere Really Exist?

The question: Does the archaeological evidence support treating pottery similarities between coastal Vietnam and the Philippines as reflecting a single, bounded "Sa Huynh-Kalanay Interaction Sphere," or does that older classification obscure a more complicated pattern of overlapping, locally varied networks?

Interpretation A — the classic interaction-sphere model: Mid-twentieth-century and later archaeologists, comparing decorated pottery from Sa Huynh-related sites in Vietnam with material from Kalanay Cave and related Philippine sites, argued that shared vessel forms, carinated profiles, and incised and impressed decorative motifs reflected sustained, direct, long-distance contact across the South China Sea and neighboring waters, conventionally dated to roughly 500 BCE to 100 or 200 CE. This model treated "Sa Huynh-Kalanay" as a coherent, named archaeological horizon linking Vietnam, the Philippines, and by extension other sites showing similar material, including parts of Borneo, the Thai-Malay Peninsula, and eastern Indonesia.
Interpretation B — a more fragmented and locally varied network: More recent reappraisal, based on detailed comparison of specific ceramic assemblages, has argued that the classification was built largely on general morphological and decorative resemblance, a method that can obscure considerable diversity among the assemblages being compared. Detailed comparative work has found that classic Sa Huynh pottery is not always the closest match for Kalanay-related Philippine material, and that some Philippine assemblages instead resemble pottery from other Vietnamese sites, such as Hoa Diem, that are not conventionally classified under the Sa Huynh label at all. The Hoa Diem Kalanay-type jar burials, moreover, appear to date mainly to the second and third centuries CE, likely after the decline of the classic Sa Huynh tradition itself, which means the old equation is problematic chronologically as well as stylistically: the closest ceramic parallel to Kalanay may not even belong to the same period as Sa Huynh. This interpretation does not deny long-distance contact across the South China Sea during this period; it argues instead for a more fragmented picture of multiple, overlapping local and regional networks rather than a single, cleanly bounded interaction sphere named for two type sites.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same underlying body of pottery form, decoration, and, increasingly, technological analysis of manufacturing sequence, together with the geographic distribution of related material across Vietnam, the Philippines, Borneo, the Thai-Malay Peninsula, and eastern Indonesia. They differ chiefly in how finely they distinguish among individual site assemblages and in how much weight they place on specific, detailed comparison versus broader typological grouping.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns classification and interpretation more than it concerns the underlying fact of long-distance contact. Scholars broadly agree that communities across this maritime zone were in some form of contact during the final centuries BCE and early centuries CE; they disagree about whether "Sa Huynh-Kalanay" is a historically accurate label for that contact or an oversimplified umbrella term that groups genuinely distinct local traditions together.

Why it matters: The label a historian uses shapes the story that gets told. Treating "Sa Huynh-Kalanay" as a single bounded culture risks implying a level of uniformity, and perhaps even a single point of origin or a single migrating population, that the more fragmented evidence does not support. Treating the region instead as a set of overlapping, locally distinct communities connected by multiple, possibly indirect, exchange relationships better reflects both the archaeological record and this textbook's broader commitment to recognizing regional diversity even within connected historical zones.

Student task: Using the evidence summarized above, explain what is gained and what is lost by continuing to use the term "Sa Huynh-Kalanay Interaction Sphere" as a teaching label, even after more detailed research has complicated its accuracy. Consider whether a term can remain useful as a historiographical reference point even after the interpretation it originally represented has been substantially revised.

This debate leads directly to a broader interpretive question that this chapter treats as a governing methodological theme: when pottery, ornaments, or other objects reveal connections between distant communities, what actually moved? Finished vessels might have been carried by traders, by travelers, or as part of marriage exchanges between communities. Alternatively, potters themselves, or other craft specialists, might have relocated, whether temporarily or permanently, carrying their skills and stylistic preferences with them. In still other cases, a community might have acquired only an idea, seeing a foreign vessel once, through trade or travel, and then reproducing an approximation of its form and decoration using entirely local materials and local hands. Marriage networks, which frequently moved individuals, and with them their skills and preferences, between communities, offer a further plausible mechanism, though direct archaeological evidence for marriage practice in this period is extremely limited.

Archaeologists increasingly attempt to distinguish among these possibilities by looking beyond surface decoration to the full manufacturing sequence, or chaîne opératoire, of a vessel: how the clay was prepared, how the vessel was formed, and how it was fired. Two vessels that share decorative motifs but were produced through different technical sequences weaken the case that both were made by the same hands or the same workshop tradition, and, especially when combined with clay composition or contextual evidence, may support an interpretation of local imitation rather than direct importation of the vessel itself or its maker. Two vessels sharing both decoration and manufacturing technique, by contrast, point more strongly toward either direct importation or the movement of the craftsperson responsible for making them, though even this stronger pattern is best confirmed alongside other lines of evidence. This distinction, though technically demanding to establish in practice, offers historians a more secure basis for interpretation than surface appearance alone, even if manufacturing technique cannot, on its own, fully settle questions of geographic origin.

Section 17.8

Nephrite, Lingling-o, and Portable Prestige

If pottery evidence for this period requires careful and often inconclusive interpretation, the evidence concerning nephrite ornaments offers one of the clearest and most striking demonstrations of long-distance maritime exchange available anywhere in the archaeological record of early Southeast Asia. Chemical sourcing studies, comparing the trace-element composition of nephrite artifacts against known geological sources, have identified the Fengtian source area in eastern Taiwan as the origin of raw material used to produce ornaments recovered from sites across the Philippines, Borneo, Vietnam, Cambodia, and peninsular Thailand. This distribution spans a maritime zone of approximately three thousand kilometers, an extraordinary distance for the movement of a single, geologically distinctive raw material during the period in question, roughly the final centuries BCE through the early centuries CE, with the network's most extensive documented reach concentrated within that broad chronological window.

Two ornament types are especially prominent within this network: the three-pointed ear ornament conventionally known by the Philippine term lingling-o, and pendants carved in the form of a bicephalous, or double-headed, animal, generally interpreted as representing paired animal heads facing in opposite directions from a shared body; archaeologists have not securely identified the species these heads represent, and this chapter avoids speculating about it. The two forms, taken together, define the broad reach of the Fengtian-nephrite network, but their individual distributions are not identical: documented finds of the three-pointed lingling-o span a wider area, including parts of Borneo and eastern Cambodia, while documented finds of the double-headed animal pendant occupy a somewhat narrower range, concentrated in Taiwan and its offshore islands, the Philippines, central and southern Vietnam, and central Thailand. Their recurring presence at multiple sites within these ranges shows that these ornament styles circulated widely and were valued across a number of distinct communities, even as local workshops introduced their own stylistic variations; their precise symbolic or social meanings, however, remain unknown.

What makes this network especially significant for historical interpretation is evidence suggesting that it involved more than the simple one-way export of finished ornaments from a single manufacturing center. Analysis of manufacturing debris, partially worked material, and finished ornaments at multiple sites within the network indicates that nephrite, in some cases, moved as raw or partially prepared material rather than exclusively as finished objects, with at least some carving and finishing work carried out at locations well removed from the original Taiwanese source. This pattern suggests a distributed production system, in which craft knowledge and, quite possibly, craftspeople themselves moved alongside raw material, rather than a simple exporter-and-importer relationship between a single production center and passive recipient communities elsewhere.

Historical Vocabulary
Lingling-o

Lingling-o is a term, drawn from Philippine usage, applied by archaeologists to a distinctive style of ear ornament characterized by a ring form interrupted by three projecting points. Ornaments of this general type, carved from nephrite and other materials, have been recovered from sites across the Philippines, Borneo, Vietnam, Cambodia, and peninsular Thailand, making the form one of the most widely distributed single ornament types associated with the exchange network examined in this chapter.

Students should understand lingling-o as a descriptive archaeological category referring to a recognizable ornament shape, produced in variant local styles at multiple locations, rather than as evidence of a single workshop, a single ethnic group, or a single centrally organized trading operation. The wide distribution of the general form, alongside meaningful local variation in its specific execution, exemplifies this chapter's broader argument that shared symbolic objects could circulate across an enormous maritime zone while still being adapted, reproduced, and given local meaning by the many distinct communities that used them.

Evidence Window

Sourcing Fengtian Nephrite: What Chemical Analysis Can and Cannot Tell Historians

Evidence type: Archaeometric (chemical and mineralogical) sourcing analysis of nephrite artifacts, compared against geological reference samples · Scope: Ornaments recovered from sites in the Philippines, Borneo, Vietnam, Cambodia, and peninsular Thailand · Comparative source area: The Fengtian nephrite deposit of eastern Taiwan

What the method establishes: By comparing the trace-element and mineralogical signature of nephrite artifacts against samples collected from known geological deposits, researchers can identify, with a strong degree of confidence in many cases, which source area supplied the raw material for a given ornament. Multiple independent studies applying this method to ornaments recovered across the network's geographic range have consistently identified eastern Taiwan's Fengtian deposit as the source for a substantial proportion of the nephrite examined, establishing beyond reasonable dispute that raw material moved across a maritime distance of roughly three thousand kilometers during the period under study.

What the method cannot, by itself, establish: Chemical sourcing identifies where the raw material originated; it does not, on its own, reveal whether a given ornament traveled as a finished object, whether raw or partially worked material traveled and was finished elsewhere, whether the individuals who carved a particular ornament had themselves traveled from Taiwan, or what social relationships, trade, tribute, marriage exchange, gift-giving between elites, accompanied the material's movement. Answering those further questions requires combining sourcing data with evidence of manufacturing debris, stylistic variation, and burial context at each site.

Analysis: This evidence is valuable to students because it demonstrates, through a method independent of the more subjective stylistic comparisons discussed elsewhere in this chapter, that an extensive, long-distance maritime exchange network genuinely existed during this period. At the same time, it illustrates the general principle that even strong scientific evidence answers a narrower question than it might initially appear to answer, and that historians must combine multiple lines of evidence to reconstruct the fuller social and economic picture.

Section 17.9

Beads, Metals, and Other Exchange Goods

Nephrite ornaments were far from the only materials circulating within this network. Burial assemblages at Sa Huynh-related sites and elsewhere across the region examined in this chapter regularly include glass beads, together with beads and ornaments made from carnelian and agate, materials whose distinctive coloration and translucency made them attractive for personal adornment and, evidently, for funerary display. Some of these materials, particularly certain glass and stone types, likely originated well beyond the immediate South China Sea zone, pointing toward exchange connections that, even at this early period, extended some distance beyond the network's core geographic range examined in this chapter.

Metal objects, including iron tools and weapons and, in some contexts, bronze items, likewise appear as significant components of burial assemblages, reflecting both practical use and, in many cases, symbolic or status-related value. The presence of substantial quantities of imported ornamental material in some burials, set alongside more modest assemblages in others, suggests meaningful social differentiation within these communities, though reconstructing the precise nature of that differentiation, whether based on inherited status, individual achievement, occupational specialization, or some combination of factors, remains difficult given the limits of the available evidence.

This chapter deliberately stops short of explaining in detail where every imported material ultimately originated or how it reached Southeast Asian shores from more distant sources, since that fuller account belongs to Chapter 18's examination of expanding Indian Ocean, South Asian, and Chinese trade. What matters for this chapter is establishing that Southeast Asian coastal and island communities were already active participants in a wider exchange system, receiving, using, and often locally reworking materials of varied origin, before the intensified international trade examined in the following chapter reshaped the scale and character of that system still further.

Section 17.10

Borneo and Eastern Indonesia within the Network

A chapter organized only around coastal Vietnam and the Philippines would risk reproducing, in miniature, the very error this textbook has criticized throughout: the tendency to let two well-studied locations stand in for an entire region. Borneo and eastern Indonesia, including parts of Maluku, are integral rather than peripheral nodes within the maritime network examined in this chapter, and their archaeological record deserves attention in its own right.

Sites in Borneo and eastern Indonesia have yielded material, including nephrite ornaments consistent with the Taiwan-sourced network examined in Section 17.8, decorated pottery bearing some resemblance to Vietnamese and Philippine material, and locally distinctive assemblages that do not map neatly onto either the Sa Huynh or Kalanay classification. This pattern is best understood not as evidence that these islands passively received a single, ready-made cultural package transmitted from elsewhere, but as evidence of active local participation, selection, and adaptation.

Archaeologists studying exchange networks of this kind have found it useful to distinguish among three categories of material at any given site: objects that were genuinely imported from elsewhere; objects manufactured locally in imitation of a foreign style, using local materials and local techniques; and objects manufactured locally using imported raw material or an imported technique, while following a locally distinctive form. This three-part distinction offers students a valuable analytical tool, since it moves discussion beyond a simple binary of "local" versus "foreign" and toward a more historically accurate picture of communities selectively combining imported and local elements according to their own preferences and needs.

Applying this distinction to the material recovered from Borneo and eastern Indonesia suggests that communities in these islands were neither isolated from the wider network nor simply absorbed into a uniform regional culture defined by developments in Vietnam or the Philippines. They participated in exchange on their own terms, adopting certain imported materials and techniques while maintaining, modifying, or independently developing their own stylistic and technical traditions.

Section 17.11

Maritime Networks without Merchant Empires

A network capable of moving nephrite across three thousand kilometers of sea, of transmitting shared pottery motifs across the South China Sea, and of distributing glass and stone ornaments of varied and sometimes distant origin might seem to require the kind of institutional apparatus, coinage, written contracts, centralized ports, and territorial states, that later chapters will examine in the context of Srivijaya, Melaka, and other historically documented trading polities. The evidence examined in this chapter, however, belongs to a period before any such institutions are documented in this maritime zone, which raises an important question: what social mechanisms could have sustained exchange on this scale without them?

Historians and archaeologists have proposed several plausible mechanisms, none mutually exclusive and none susceptible to definitive proof given the available evidence. Kinship and marriage networks may have linked communities across considerable distances, creating durable social relationships along which goods, materials, and craft knowledge could travel as part of ordinary social exchange rather than formal commerce. Reciprocal gift exchange between community leaders or other prominent individuals, a practice documented ethnographically in many parts of the Pacific and island Southeast Asia in more recent centuries, offers another plausible model, in which valuable or symbolically significant objects moved as part of relationships of obligation and prestige rather than as commodities exchanged for a fixed price. Itinerant craft specialists, individuals or small groups who traveled seasonally or periodically to practice their skills in different communities, could have carried both finished objects and technical knowledge across considerable distances. Seasonal voyaging, timed to favorable monsoon winds and undertaken by small groups of skilled sailors, could have connected communities at intervals without requiring permanent settlement, standing fleets, or centralized political direction. Finally, chains of local intermediaries, in which goods passed from one community to a neighboring one, and then onward again, could have moved materials across very long total distances without requiring that any single group or individual travel the entire route.

These mechanisms should be presented to students as plausible, evidence-consistent models rather than as established historical facts, since the archaeological record, by its nature, reveals the results of exchange far more clearly than it reveals the social processes that produced it. The central historical point these models collectively support, however, is a robust one: extensive, long-distance maritime networks are possible without centralized political control, without written contracts, and without the kind of territorial state apparatus that later chapters of this textbook will examine in far greater detail. Networks can be extensive without being centralized.

Section 17.12

Local Communities, Not Passive Recipients

The evidence examined throughout this chapter, from the internal diversity of Sa Huynh-related jar burials to the locally varied execution of the lingling-o ornament form to the mixed local, imported, and hybrid assemblages recovered from Borneo and eastern Indonesia, converges on a single interpretive conclusion that this textbook has already articulated in Chapter 1 and will continue to develop throughout the volumes that follow: Southeast Asian communities selected, adapted, and transformed the materials, styles, and technologies that reached them through exchange, rather than passively absorbing an externally imposed cultural package.

A coastal Vietnamese, Philippine, Bornean, or eastern Indonesian community encountering an imported object had several possible responses available to it. A community might adopt an imported object largely unchanged, valuing it precisely because of its foreign origin and the prestige that origin conferred. A community might instead copy the form of an admired foreign object using entirely local materials and techniques, producing a recognizable but locally distinctive version. A community might combine imported raw material with a locally preferred form, producing an object that was, in a meaningful sense, neither purely foreign nor purely local. Or a community might reject certain imported elements altogether while adopting others, exercising a kind of selective curation over which outside influences it chose to incorporate into its own material and symbolic world.

This pattern of active selection carries an important consequence for how students should understand the relationship between connectivity and diversity in this period. Exchange across this maritime network did not homogenize the communities it connected. If anything, the evidence suggests that connectivity and regional diversity increased simultaneously, since access to a wider range of imported materials, styles, and techniques gave local communities more raw material, in every sense of that phrase, from which to construct their own distinctive local traditions. This is precisely the same dynamic that Chapter 19, examining the later historiographical debate over "Indianization," will describe as localization: the selective adoption and transformation of outside elements to serve local needs and local meanings, rather than the simple, unmediated transmission of a foreign culture onto a passive recipient population.

Section 17.13

Toward Chapter 18: An Already-Connected Maritime World

This chapter has reconstructed a maritime exchange network linking coastal Vietnam, the Philippine archipelago, Borneo, the Thai-Malay Peninsula, and eastern Indonesia during the final centuries BCE and the opening centuries CE, using archaeological evidence, pottery, jar burials, and, most decisively, chemically sourced nephrite ornaments, in the near-total absence of any contemporary written record produced by the network's own participants. That network was neither centrally organized nor culturally uniform. It was instead a set of overlapping, locally varied relationships sustained by seafaring skill, coastal knowledge, kinship and social exchange, and, in all likelihood, itinerant craft specialists moving among communities that retained their own distinctive local identities even as they participated in wider circuits of exchange.

Chapter 18 turns next to the expanding trade connecting Southeast Asia with South Asia, China, and, through intermediary chains of exchange, the wider Indian Ocean and Mediterranean-connected world. That expanding trade is a central subject of this textbook and will receive sustained attention throughout Parts IV through VI. But it did not create Southeast Asian maritime connectivity out of nothing, and it did not introduce international exchange to communities that had previously known only isolation. External trade expanded through a region that already possessed maritime knowledge, exchange practices, coastal intermediaries, and inter-island networks; it did not create Southeast Asian connectivity from nothing. Chapter 18 will accordingly place particular emphasis on Southeast Asian merchants, ports, and intermediaries, following the same interpretive commitment this chapter has modeled: treating Southeast Asian societies as active builders of their own commercial world rather than as passive terrain awaiting more historically significant neighbors.

Multiple Perspectives

Reading the Same Pot Differently: Perspectives within Archaeological Practice

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives representing distinct methodological approaches within archaeology, not quotations from any single named scholar.

The stylistic archaeologist: Compares vessel form and surface decoration across sites, treating shared motifs and shapes as the most visible and most readily comparable evidence for contact between distant communities, while acknowledging that surface similarity alone cannot distinguish direct importation from independent local imitation.
The technological archaeologist: Reconstructs the chaîne opératoire, or manufacturing sequence, behind a vessel or ornament, arguing that shared technique, especially when combined with compositional and contextual evidence, provides stronger support for direct transmission than shared appearance alone, since a community could imitate a foreign design without ever learning the technical process by which the original was made.
The provenance specialist: Relies on chemical and mineralogical sourcing of raw materials such as nephrite, treating the geological origin of a material as the most objectively verifiable form of evidence for long-distance movement, while acknowledging that sourcing alone cannot reveal the social relationships through which a material traveled.
The mortuary archaeologist: Focuses on the arrangement of burials and the combination of grave goods within them, arguing that funerary context reveals not only exchange but also social status, ritual meaning, and community identity, and cautioning against interpreting imported objects purely as evidence of trade routes while ignoring their local symbolic significance.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Austronesian Dispersal to Iron Age Maritime Networks

What Changed

  • Seafaring capacity, already present in island Southeast Asia before Austronesian speakers arrived but expanded substantially during the Austronesian dispersal examined in Chapter 14, was applied by the Iron Age to sustain regular, patterned, long-distance exchange rather than primarily one-way settlement voyaging
  • The range and variety of materials in circulation expanded to include nephrite, glass, and stone ornaments alongside earlier bronze and, increasingly, iron metalwork
  • Burial practices, including elaborate jar interment, developed to incorporate substantial quantities of imported prestige goods, indicating growing social differentiation in some communities
  • Distinct regional pottery and ornament traditions developed alongside, rather than in place of, increasing interregional contact

What Stayed the Same

  • Communities across this maritime zone remained politically decentralized, without evidence of the territorial states examined in Part V
  • Local diversity in material culture persisted even as shared regional styles and materials circulated widely
  • The seas connecting coastal Vietnam, the Philippines, Borneo, the Thai-Malay Peninsula, and eastern Indonesia continued to function as corridors of contact rather than barriers

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both continuity and change prevents students from assuming either that Iron Age maritime exchange emerged suddenly from nothing or that it produced a single, unified regional culture; it instead built on long-established seafaring capacity to create denser, more materially varied, but still locally diverse networks of contact.

Cause and Effect

Why Maritime Exchange Intensified during the Iron Age

Long-term causes

Long-standing regional seafaring traditions, already present among island Southeast Asian populations before Austronesian speakers arrived and substantially expanded through the Austronesian dispersal examined in Chapter 14, had distributed watercraft technology, navigational knowledge, and coastal populations across the region, creating the underlying capacity for sustained maritime contact.

Short-term causes

The spread of iron technology and the growing social value placed on ornamental materials such as nephrite, glass, and stone beads created increased demand for materials that were unevenly distributed geographically, encouraging exchange between communities with access to different resources.

Effect

A dense, though not centrally organized, maritime exchange network developed across the South China Sea and neighboring waters, moving pottery styles, ornamental materials, and craft knowledge among coastal Vietnam, the Philippines, Borneo, the Thai-Malay Peninsula, and eastern Indonesia, most intensively during the final centuries BCE and early centuries CE.

This chapter's response

Careful use of multiple, independent evidentiary methods, stylistic comparison, technological reconstruction, chemical sourcing, and mortuary analysis, to reconstruct this network responsibly, while resisting overly tidy classifications such as the older "interaction sphere" model where recent evidence complicates them.

Historical complexity: No single cause, whether technological, demographic, or social, fully explains the intensification of exchange during this period; historians instead describe a convergence of long-term seafaring capacity with shorter-term technological and social developments that together made denser exchange both possible and desirable.

Everyday Life and Social History

Ordinary Communities within an Extraordinary Network

Behind the pottery sherds, nephrite ornaments, and jar burials examined in this chapter stood ordinary coastal and island communities engaged in farming, fishing, potting, metalworking, and the raising of families, for whom participation in long-distance exchange was almost certainly interwoven with everyday subsistence and social life rather than separated from it. A household that fished the coastal waters of central Vietnam, tended nearby fields, and also produced or acquired decorated pottery and ornamental beads was not living two separate lives, one local and one connected to distant exchange; these activities were part of a single, integrated way of life.

The burial evidence examined throughout this chapter offers one of the few windows historians have into the social organization of these communities, since the quantity and quality of grave goods accompanying different individuals suggest that access to imported prestige materials was not evenly distributed. Some individuals were buried with substantial quantities of beads, ornaments, and metal objects, while others received more modest treatment, a pattern that likely reflects differences in social status, wealth, or communal role, though the precise basis for that differentiation, whether inherited, achieved through individual accomplishment, or tied to specialized craft or ritual roles, cannot be established with certainty from burial evidence alone. Because written sources from within these communities do not survive, historians must rely on this kind of indirect archaeological evidence, supplemented by careful comparison with better-documented societies elsewhere, to reconstruct the texture of everyday social life during this period.

Chapter Summary

This chapter opened Volume II and Part IV, "Networks Before Empires," by examining archaeological evidence for an extensive maritime exchange network linking coastal Vietnam, the Philippine archipelago, Borneo, the Thai-Malay Peninsula, and eastern Indonesia during the final centuries BCE and the opening centuries CE, a period before the region's best-documented historical states, examined beginning in Part V, had left substantial written records of their own.

The chapter began with the Sa Huynh burial tradition of coastal and riverine central and southern Vietnam, characterized chiefly by lidded jar burials accompanied by iron tools, pottery, and ornaments made from glass, carnelian, agate, and nephrite. It emphasized that Sa Huynh should be understood as an archaeologically defined tradition encompassing meaningful local variation rather than as the name of a single unified people or political authority, and that recent scholarship has increasingly recognized the possibility of locally distinct, indigenously developed jar-burial customs existing alongside broader regional connections.

The chapter then turned to Kalanay Cave on the Philippine island of Masbate and the distinctive carinated, decoratively incised pottery tradition associated with it, situating this material within the broader Philippine Metal Age. It explained how mid-twentieth-century archaeologists, noting resemblances between Kalanay and Sa Huynh pottery, proposed a "Sa Huynh-Kalanay Interaction Sphere" linking Vietnam and the Philippines, and it examined, through this chapter's central Historical Debate feature, how more recent, detailed comparative analysis has complicated this classification, showing that some Philippine material resembles other Vietnamese sites, such as Hoa Diem, more closely than it resembles classic Sa Huynh pottery.

This debate anchored the chapter's central methodological lesson: similarity in pottery style can result from direct importation, the movement of craftspeople, local imitation of an admired foreign object, or independent invention, and historians must draw on additional evidence, particularly the technical manufacturing sequence, or chaîne opératoire, behind an object, to distinguish among these possibilities responsibly.

The chapter presented chemically sourced nephrite as some of the strongest available evidence for this network's existence and scale. Ornaments including the three-pointed lingling-o ear ornament and double-headed animal pendants, both made from nephrite ultimately sourced to the Fengtian deposit of eastern Taiwan, have been recovered from sites across the Philippines, Borneo, Vietnam, Cambodia, and peninsular Thailand, though the two ornament types do not share an identical distribution, together spanning a maritime distance of roughly three thousand kilometers. Evidence of manufacturing debris at multiple locations within this range suggested a distributed production system rather than a simple one-way export relationship, implying that craft knowledge, and quite possibly craftspeople, moved alongside raw material.

The chapter extended its geographic scope to Borneo and eastern Indonesia, using a three-part analytical distinction, imported objects, local imitations of foreign styles, and locally made objects using imported material or technique, to explain how these islands participated actively in the network rather than passively receiving a uniform cultural package from elsewhere.

The chapter then addressed the social mechanisms that might have sustained such an extensive network without centralized political authority, written contracts, or coinage, proposing kinship and marriage networks, reciprocal gift exchange, itinerant craft specialists, seasonal voyaging, and chains of local intermediaries as plausible, evidence-consistent models. It emphasized that networks can be extensive without being centralized, a conclusion with significant implications for how historians understand economic and social organization before the emergence of territorial states.

Throughout, the chapter reinforced this textbook's governing interpretive commitment, first articulated in Chapter 1: that Southeast Asian communities were active agents who selected, adapted, and transformed imported materials, styles, and techniques according to their own needs and preferences, rather than passive recipients of an externally imposed culture. Connectivity and regional diversity, the chapter argued, increased simultaneously rather than one dissolving into the other.

The chapter closed by establishing the essential premise for Chapter 18: that the intensifying Indian Ocean and East Asian trade examined in the following chapter expanded through, rather than created, an already-connected Southeast Asian maritime world, one built by coastal and island communities themselves over many preceding centuries.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Interaction sphere
An archaeological term describing a region within which multiple communities shared certain material culture traits, understood to result from contact and exchange rather than necessarily from shared ethnicity or political unity; the "Sa Huynh-Kalanay Interaction Sphere" is this chapter's central example and central caution.
Provenance (chemical sourcing)
The determination of the geological or geographic origin of an artifact's raw material, typically through chemical or mineralogical analysis compared against known source samples.
Chaîne opératoire
The reconstructed technical sequence, from raw material preparation through forming and finishing, by which an artifact such as a pottery vessel or ornament was manufactured; used by archaeologists, together with compositional and contextual evidence, to help assess local production versus importation rather than to establish it by itself.
Jar burial
A mortuary practice in which the dead are interred within large ceramic jars, often sealed with a lid and accompanied by grave goods; the characteristic mortuary practice of the Sa Huynh tradition and related sites.
Lingling-o
A three-pointed ring-form ear ornament, carved from nephrite and other materials, found in variant local styles across the Philippines, Borneo, Vietnam, Cambodia, and peninsular Thailand.
Double-headed animal pendant
An ornament type depicting paired animal heads facing in opposite directions from a shared body, found alongside lingling-o ornaments within the nephrite exchange network examined in this chapter.
Metal Age
A period label used, particularly in Philippine archaeology, to describe a broad era of expanding iron and continued bronze use, spanning roughly the middle of the first millennium BCE into the first millennium CE; a technological stage rather than a single culture, and considerably longer than the narrower window this chapter examines for the Sa Huynh-Kalanay network comparison.
Sa Huynh
A coastal locality and associated archaeological tradition in central and southern Vietnam, known chiefly through jar burials, iron tools, pottery, and imported ornaments.
Kalanay
A cave site on Masbate in the central Philippines that gave its name to a distinctive decorated earthenware tradition associated with the Philippine Metal Age.
Nephrite
A hard, often green or greenish stone used across this network for ornaments such as lingling-o and double-headed animal pendants; a major source area has been chemically identified in eastern Taiwan.
Carinated vessel
A pottery vessel with a distinct angular shoulder or ridge in its profile, a characteristic form associated with the Kalanay tradition.
Local imitation
The reproduction, using local materials and techniques, of an admired foreign object's form or decoration; technological analysis, combined with compositional and contextual evidence, can help assess whether an object was locally produced rather than imported.
Entrepôt
A commercial hub whose importance derives from its role in exchange rather than from an extensive agricultural hinterland; a concept anticipated by, though not fully realized within, the networks examined in this chapter.
Mortuary archaeology
The study of burial sites and practices as evidence for social organization, ritual belief, exchange, and status differentiation.
Radiocarbon dating
A scientific method for estimating the age of organic remains, used to establish chronological ranges for archaeological sites discussed in this chapter, subject to margins of statistical uncertainty.
Reciprocal exchange
A pattern of exchange based on social obligation and prestige rather than fixed-price commerce, proposed as one possible mechanism sustaining this network.
Prestige good
An object valued chiefly for the social status, symbolic meaning, or display value it conferred rather than for its practical utility alone.
Intermediary
A community or individual through whom goods passed on their way between two more distant points, allowing materials to travel long total distances through a chain of shorter exchanges.
Coastal corridor
A stretch of coastline or adjacent sea functioning as a route of communication and exchange rather than as a boundary separating communities.
Seasonal voyaging
Long-distance sea travel timed to favorable monsoon wind patterns, allowing round-trip journeys across substantial maritime distances.
Archaeological culture
A grouping of sites sharing similar material culture, used by archaeologists as an analytical convenience that does not necessarily correspond to a single ethnic group, language community, or political authority.
Localization
The process by which a community selects and transforms outside materials, styles, or ideas to fit local needs, a concept this chapter anticipates and Chapter 19 examines in more depth.
Distributed production
A manufacturing pattern in which raw material, craft knowledge, or craftspeople move among multiple locations, so that finished objects are produced at more than one site rather than exported exclusively from a single center.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it establishes, at the outset of Volume II, that Southeast Asia's connection to a wider commercial and cultural world was not something that happened to the region from outside, arriving with the first Indian or Chinese ship to reach its shores. It was something coastal and island Southeast Asians had already built for themselves, using their own boats, their own craft skills, their own social relationships, and their own understanding of the seas that connected, rather than divided, their communities.

The larger process this chapter reveals, the movement of objects, materials, and technical knowledge across a network sustained by kinship, gift exchange, itinerant specialists, and seasonal voyaging rather than by states or written contracts, will recur throughout this textbook in new forms. The entrepôts of Srivijaya and Melaka, examined in Volumes II, IV, and V, and the diaspora and merchant networks examined in Volume V and beyond, all rest on maritime and commercial capacities whose roots extend back to the Iron Age world reconstructed in this chapter.

The continuities that survived beyond this chapter's chronological scope are equally significant. The pattern of active local adaptation, in which communities selected, modified, and reinterpreted imported materials and styles rather than simply absorbing them, anticipates the more fully documented processes of religious and political localization examined in Chapter 19 and throughout this textbook's treatment of the classical kingdoms in Volumes II and III. The methodological caution this chapter has modeled, distinguishing evidence for contact from evidence for shared identity, and distinguishing what a particular evidentiary method can establish from what it cannot, will remain essential throughout a textbook that relies heavily on archaeology for its earliest chapters and continues to weigh evidence carefully even once written sources become available.

Understanding this chapter well equips students to read Chapter 18 with the right expectations: not as the story of how Southeast Asia was discovered and connected by outside powers, but as the story of how an already-connected Southeast Asian maritime world became further entangled with expanding Indian Ocean and East Asian trade, a distinction this textbook considers essential to writing this region's history honestly.
