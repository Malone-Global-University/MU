Srivijaya and the Maritime Buddhist World

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Srivijaya's international importance rested less on the uniform administration of a continuous block of territory than on its ability to coordinate ports, river mouths, local rulers, shipping communities, diplomatic missions, monasteries, and pilgrimage routes, so that commerce, political prestige, and Buddhist patronage reinforced one another across several seas.

In 671 CE a Chinese Buddhist monk named Yijing left the southern Chinese port of Guangzhou intending to reach India. By his own later account, he did not sail directly there. He first went to a place Chinese writers called Foshi, a name generally identified with Srivijaya, and he remained about six months, studying Sanskrit grammar before continuing west. Yijing had a destination in the great Buddhist centers of eastern India, but his route, his preparation, and his later recommendation to other monks all pass through a port-centered polity on the southeastern coast of Sumatra. His journey makes a point that this chapter develops at length: by the late seventh century, Srivijaya was a place that people from other societies planned around, not merely sailed past.

Chapter 28 asked how Srivijaya formed: how a political network centered on the Musi river and Palembang emerged in the seventh century, and what its earliest inscriptions reveal about rulers, oaths, and relations with surrounding Malay communities. This chapter asks a different question. How did that network become a major commercial, diplomatic, and religious presence linking maritime Southeast Asia with China and South Asia, and what kind of political power did such a network actually represent? The change of question brings a change of scale. The source base also expands from a handful of Old Malay inscriptions to Chinese court records, Buddhist travel writing, Sanskrit inscriptions from the Malay Peninsula and eastern India, Arabic and Persian geographical writing, shipwrecks, and archaeological finds from several countries.

The chapter's argument has two parts. The first is historical. Srivijaya's mature importance depended on strategically valuable nodes and relationships: ports and river mouths, local rulers who owed it various degrees of allegiance, merchants and shipping specialists, embassies to the Chinese court, and monastic communities that attracted foreign scholars and pilgrims. Trade, diplomacy, and Buddhist patronage were not separate sectors. A ruler who sheltered ships also sheltered monks, who often traveled on the same vessels; a ruler who founded a monastery enhanced his reputation among the same merchants and envoys who determined his commercial standing. The second part of the argument is methodological. Chinese accounts, Buddhist travel records, Southeast Asian inscriptions, South Asian inscriptions, and archaeology each illuminate a different aspect of Srivijaya, and none of them provides a reliable imperial map. Foreign sources, in particular, tell us far more about routes, diplomacy, and reputation than about internal administration.

That methodological caution matters because older scholarship often reconstructed Srivijaya as a vast, centrally governed maritime empire controlling Sumatra, the Malay Peninsula, and the straits for centuries. More recent work has challenged the assumption of uniform centralization and continuous territorial control, while still recognizing that Srivijaya was an exceptionally important political and commercial presence. This textbook follows the second line of interpretation. The chapter therefore repeatedly distinguishes wide influence from uniform administration, and it treats the old question "how large was the Srivijayan empire?" as less productive than the question "what relationships did Srivijaya organize, and how durable were they?"

The chapter also contributes to the textbook's larger commitment to treating Southeast Asian societies as historical actors. It is easy to describe Srivijaya as a convenient waystation on the sea road between India and China. The evidence points elsewhere. Srivijayan rulers sent embassies, patronized Buddhist foundations abroad, hosted foreign scholars, and positioned their polity within a Buddhist geography that stretched from southern China to eastern India. Foreign monks and envoys came to Srivijaya because of what it offered, not simply because it lay on the way. The idea of a passive crossroads cannot account for that pattern.

The chapter proceeds in stages. Sections 29.1 through 29.5 establish the maritime geography, monsoon rhythms, and commercial mechanics that made a port-centered polity powerful. Sections 29.6 and 29.7 examine relations with Tang China and the difficult vocabulary of "tribute." Sections 29.8 through 29.12 turn to Yijing, Buddhist learning, pilgrimage, and patronage. Sections 29.13 through 29.16 follow Srivijayan connections to the Malay Peninsula, Central Java, and South Asia, including the Nalanda copperplate. The final sections confront the problem of control and the major historiographical debate over whether Srivijaya was an empire, a thalassocracy, a mandala, or a network, before handing the story to Chapter 30 and the Sailendras of Central Java.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Srivijaya's position among rivers, straits, and monsoon sea routes created commercial opportunities without automatically guaranteeing supremacy.
  2. Describe the mechanisms, such as provisioning, anchorage, brokerage, information, and political protection, that made a maritime entrepôt powerful.
  3. Analyze relations between Srivijaya and Tang China, and distinguish Chinese court vocabulary such as "tribute" from the actual political relationships it describes.
  4. Evaluate Yijing's account of Srivijaya as evidence, identifying both what it reveals and the perspective that shapes it.
  5. Assess the evidence for Srivijaya as a center of Buddhist learning without anachronistically describing it as a modern university.
  6. Trace pilgrimage and monastic mobility among China, Southeast Asia, and India, and explain why commercial and religious networks overlapped without being identical.
  7. Interpret the Ligor inscription and the Nalanda copperplate as evidence for Srivijayan connections to the Malay Peninsula and eastern India, noting their limits.
  8. Distinguish what the evidence establishes about connections among Sumatra, the Malay Peninsula, and the Sailendras from what remains debated.
  9. Compare scholarly models of Srivijaya as empire, thalassocracy, mandala, and maritime network, and distinguish wide influence from uniform administration.
  10. Connect Srivijaya's mature networks to the wider Bay of Bengal and South China Sea worlds without reverting to a passive model of "Indianization."
  11. Contextualize Srivijaya's later eighth- and ninth-century connections with Java as a bridge to the Sailendra narrative of Chapter 30.

Historical Context

Students arrive at Chapter 29 with several background developments already in hand. Chapter 18 traced the expansion of exchange linking Southeast Asia with South Asia, China, and the wider Indian Ocean, and Chapter 19 examined how Southeast Asian communities selectively adopted and transformed Sanskrit, Buddhist, Hindu, and courtly forms rather than receiving them passively. Chapter 20 introduced the Mekong delta polity that Chinese sources called Funan, and Chapter 26 examined early peninsular centers such as Kedah, Langkasuka, and Tambralinga, whose transpeninsular routes carried goods and people between the Bay of Bengal and the South China Sea. These earlier networks form the commercial and religious background to Srivijaya.

Chapter 28 then examined Srivijaya's formation in the seventh century. Its principal evidence consists of a small group of Old Malay inscriptions from southern Sumatra and Bangka, which record rulers, oaths, expeditions, and Buddhist acts of merit, together with archaeological work around Palembang and the Musi basin. From that material historians infer a ruling center that combined control of river access with relationships among surrounding Malay communities. Details of that formation phase belong to Chapter 28 and are not repeated here. What matters for this chapter is that by the time Yijing arrived in 671, Srivijaya was already known abroad under a name that Chinese writers rendered as Foshi or Shilifoshi.

Two contexts outside Southeast Asia shaped Srivijaya's opportunities. In China, the Tang dynasty (618–907) presided over an expanding economy and a cosmopolitan court that took a strong interest in southern products and foreign religious specialists, and a maritime trade superintendency was established at Guangzhou in the early Tang period. In South Asia, major Buddhist monastic institutions in eastern India, above all Nalanda, attracted students from across Asia, while Indian Ocean merchants of many origins sailed across the Bay of Bengal. Srivijaya lay between these two worlds, but it also linked them to a third: the producing regions of Sumatra, the Malay Peninsula, Borneo, Java, and eastern Indonesia, whose forest, mineral, and aromatic products supplied long-distance trade.

Students should avoid two opposite errors. The first is to treat Srivijaya as a ready-made empire whose size and administrative reach can be read from Chinese descriptions or from the scattered locations of its inscriptions. The second is to treat it as only a trading post that happened to attract passing monks. The evidence supports a more complicated picture. Srivijaya combined real political authority with a loose, relational geography of influence, and its Buddhist and commercial roles reinforced each other. This chapter attempts to describe that combination while remaining honest about how much remains uncertain.

Section 29.1

From Political Formation to Maritime Power

The evidence for Srivijaya changes character between the seventh century and the eighth and ninth. Chapter 28 relied heavily on a few Old Malay inscriptions that speak in the voice of the ruling center, describing oaths, expeditions, and acts of merit. From the late seventh century onward, much of the most informative evidence comes from outside: Chinese travelers and court histories, later Arabic and Persian geographical writing, and inscriptions from the Malay Peninsula and eastern India that mention rulers and foundations connected to the Srivijayan world. This shift in evidence is itself historically revealing. It marks the moment at which Srivijaya enters the awareness of other societies, and it means that historians now see Srivijaya largely through the eyes of people who were passing through, trading with, or studying in it.

The shift is also a warning. Foreign observers describe what mattered to them: harbors, diplomatic protocol, monastic discipline, products for sale. They do not describe how a ruler in Palembang collected revenue, appointed officials, or enforced obligations on distant communities. As a result, any narrative of Srivijaya's maturity must constantly separate what is documented, such as embassies, travelers' stays, and foundations, from what is inferred, such as the extent of political control. This chapter adopts that discipline throughout. It treats Srivijaya's mature power as a pattern of relationships whose outlines can be traced, rather than as a territory whose borders can be drawn.

What distinguishes the mature phase is the interlocking of three roles. Srivijaya was a commercial hub whose value lay in services as much as in goods. It was a diplomatic actor that communicated with the Chinese court and, through inscriptions and patronage, with institutions in South Asia. And it was a Buddhist center whose monastic communities attracted foreign students. Each role depended on the others, and none can be understood in isolation. The next four sections establish the geographic and commercial foundations of the first role before the chapter turns to the other two.

Historical Vocabulary
Foshi, Sanfoqi, Zabaj, and Suvarnadvipa

Definition. These are four externally supplied names that scholars generally associate with Srivijaya or the region it dominated. Foshi (and the fuller Shilifoshi, a rendering of Sanskrit Śrīvijaya) is the Chinese form used in Tang sources, including Yijing. Sanfoqi is the name used in Song-dynasty sources. Zabaj (also written Zabag or Zabaj) appears in Arabic and Persian geographical writing of the ninth and tenth centuries. Suvarnadvipa, "Island of Gold," is a Sanskrit name that appears in Indian traditions and in the Nalanda copperplate's title for Balaputradeva.

Historical context. Each name reflects the vantage point and vocabulary of the people who used it. Chinese writers rendered the local name phonetically. Arab and Persian writers adapted a name they heard in ports. Indian writers used a long-established label for the lands to the east that stressed gold and wealth.

Why it matters. The names let historians connect scattered evidence, but each equation is an inference. The identification of Foshi with Srivijaya is strongly supported; the identification of Zabaj and of Suvarnadvipa with exactly the same political entity is widely accepted but should not be treated as exact.

Potential misunderstanding. Students should not assume that the same name always denoted the same place or the same extent of authority across centuries. A name used abroad could describe a reputation, a port, or a region rather than a defined state.

Section 29.2

The Maritime Geography of Srivijaya's Wider World

Palembang sits some distance inland on the Musi river, within a lowland landscape of tidal rivers, swamps, and estuaries. The Musi connects the interior to a coast that faces the Bangka Strait, a passage separating Sumatra from the island of Bangka. From there, vessels could proceed north toward the Strait of Malacca, the principal passage between the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea, or south and east through routes leading to the Sunda Strait and the Java Sea. On the opposite side of the Strait of Malacca lie the Malay Peninsula and its transpeninsular crossings, examined in Chapter 26. To the east, the South China Sea leads toward Vietnam's coast and southern China. To the west, the Bay of Bengal and Indian Ocean lead to Sri Lanka, southern and eastern India, and beyond.

This geography creates routes and choices, not automatic supremacy. Many ports in the region had access to the same waters, and Chapter 26 showed that the peninsula offered overland alternatives to the full sea passage. A ship could pass through the Strait of Malacca, cross the peninsula by portage and river, or take other routes through the archipelago. Srivijaya's advantage, to the extent it had one, depended on how well its rulers made their harbors attractive, safe, and well supplied, and on how effectively they cultivated relationships with the communities and rulers who controlled other points along the way. Geography presented a menu of possibilities, and political and commercial skill determined who benefited.

The wider world of Srivijaya therefore extended well beyond Palembang. It included the river mouths and harbors of Sumatra's east coast, including the Jambi region associated with Malayu; peninsular ports on both sides of the Malay Peninsula; the strait zones where pilots and local seafarers were indispensable; and links across open water to Java, the South China Sea coast, and the Bay of Bengal. No single map can show all of these as a unified territory, and the next feature asks students to examine how such a space is best represented.

Section 29.3

Monsoons, Sailing Seasons, and the Rhythm of Maritime Exchange

As Chapter 4 explained, the monsoon system reverses prevailing winds seasonally across the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea. Broadly, northeasterly winds in the northern winter favored passages from China toward the south and west, and southwesterly winds in the northern summer favored return voyages. Ships using sail technology of the period depended on these patterns, which meant that long journeys were organized around seasonal windows rather than departure on demand. Yijing's own journeys, which his account tied to the arrival of favorable winds and to the availability of vessels, illustrate how a traveler's itinerary depended on schedules set by nature and by shipping.

The consequences for ports along the route were substantial. A ship that left China with the winter wind might reach the southern waters of the Strait of Malacca region well before the winds permitted the next stage of its journey. Crews and passengers then waited, sometimes for months. During this time they needed food, water, shelter, repairs, storage for cargo, and opportunities to trade. They also needed reliable information about conditions ahead and about other vessels. Ports located where ships naturally paused or transferred cargo therefore gained a role beyond simple transit. They became places of seasonal concentration, where merchants, sailors, officials, and travelers lingered long enough to generate demand for services and to form relationships.

Historians should be careful not to overstate this mechanism. The monsoons imposed constraints, but they did not dictate that any particular port would prosper. Several ports in the region, including ones on the peninsula and the Sumatran coast, offered comparable opportunities. Which ones thrived depended on political stability, the security of anchorage, the availability of provisions, and the reputations that rulers built. The monsoon explains why a port might become a meeting place; it does not explain why Palembang and its network, rather than another, became so prominent in the late seventh and eighth centuries. That explanation requires the commercial, political, and religious factors examined in the following sections.

Section 29.4

What Made a Maritime Entrepôt Powerful?

An entrepôt is a port whose wealth derives chiefly from its role in exchange rather than from an extensive agricultural hinterland, as defined in Chapter 1. The power of such a port rested on a bundle of services. The first was provisioning: supplying food, fresh water, timber for repairs, and other necessities for crews that might spend long periods in port. The second was safe anchorage and warehousing, which allowed cargo to be stored and reassembled. The third was brokerage and information. Brokers matched buyers and sellers, translated between languages and customs, and shared knowledge about prices, voyages, and political conditions. The fourth was political protection: a ruler who could guarantee security within the harbor and its approaches, and who could enforce agreements, made the port attractive to merchants who might otherwise fear seizure or extortion.

A fifth source of power was redistribution. A port ruler who received goods from traveling merchants and from producing communities could convert them into gifts, rewards, and diplomatic presents, strengthening alliances with dependent rulers and local leaders. A sixth was access to suppliers. Srivijaya did not need to produce everything it traded. It needed relationships with the communities of the interior and the coasts who gathered forest products, mined metals, or processed aromatics, and with the shipping specialists who carried goods to market. Control in this sense meant brokering access rather than owning production.

Taken together, these elements explain how a polity with a comparatively modest agricultural base in a tidal river lowland could exercise influence out of proportion to its apparent size. They also explain why the interpretive framework of the following sections emphasizes relationships over territory. A ruler whose power depended on being a reliable, well-connected node had strong incentives to cultivate dependents and partners, to host foreign visitors, and to maintain a reputation that attracted ships. A ruler who tried instead to impose uniform administration over every river mouth and harbor would have faced practical obstacles and, in many cases, little incentive. The evidence, though limited, is consistent with the first pattern more than the second.

Section 29.5

Commodities, Hinterlands, and Long-Distance Exchange

The goods that moved through the Srivijayan world came from many sources. From Sumatra and the surrounding region came forest and aromatic products: camphor (associated especially with the northwestern Sumatran coast), benzoin, aloeswood, and other resins, along with gold, tin, and other metals from various parts of the region. Other products came from farther afield, including sandalwood from the eastern archipelago and, through networks that reached eastern Indonesia, cloves and nutmeg, whose circulation is attested more strongly in later periods. Birds' feathers, tortoiseshell, rhinoceros horn, and ivory are among the other regional goods mentioned in foreign sources. Enslaved people were also part of regional exchange in this period, though evidence for their numbers and treatment in the Srivijayan world specifically is thin, and students should not assume uniform patterns.

Imports and goods in transit included Chinese ceramics, silk, and metalwork; textiles and beads from South Asia; and glass and other goods associated with Indian Ocean merchants. The Belitung shipwreck, discovered off the island of Belitung and generally placed in the second quarter of the ninth century on the basis of an inscribed bowl corresponding to 826 CE (a date after which the voyage must have taken place) and other evidence, preserves an extraordinary cargo of Chinese ceramics, especially bowls from kilns in Hunan and other regions, together with other goods. The vessel's sewn-plank construction is characteristic of Indian Ocean shipbuilding traditions. It demonstrates the scale and reach of long-distance trade passing through these waters, though it does not tell us who owned the cargo, where the ship was bound, or what ports it had visited, and students should not read it as proof of Srivijayan commercial control.

Simple labels such as "spice monopoly" obscure these realities. Srivijaya was not a single-commodity producer, and no known evidence establishes that it monopolized any commodity across the whole region. The more defensible description is that it participated in, and organized elements of, a diverse trade whose composition changed over time and whose producers included many communities beyond its direct reach. Hinterland relationships, those between a port ruler and the communities supplying the port, were as central to commercial success as the long-distance links to China and India. The archaeological and documentary record for these hinterland relationships remains much weaker than the record for the long-distance routes, and that imbalance should be kept in mind.

Section 29.6

Srivijaya and Tang China

Chinese dynastic records preserve evidence that rulers of Srivijaya sent embassies to the Tang court. These records, compiled by Chinese officials and historians, list missions arriving at the capital in the late seventh and eighth centuries, with the name of the polity rendered as Shilifoshi. Missions typically included gifts, and the court responded with its own presents and sometimes with titles or formal recognition. Records of such exchanges survive in uneven form, and counts differ among compilations. What is clear is that Srivijayan rulers knew how to engage Chinese diplomatic protocol and saw advantage in doing so.

The incentives were commercial and political. Diplomatic exchange gave a ruler a recognized channel to a vast market and a source of prestige, since the court's acknowledgment could enhance standing among local rivals and visiting merchants. For the Tang court, receiving envoys from the south reinforced a worldview in which distant rulers acknowledged the emperor's virtue, and it facilitated access to southern products. Missions also linked directly to commerce, since envoys and their entourages could carry goods, and the court's maritime trade superintendency at Guangzhou regulated much of the port's foreign commerce. The records therefore reflect an overlap of diplomacy, ceremony, and trade rather than a purely political relationship.

The pattern of surviving records also deserves attention. Chinese compilations show clusters of Srivijayan missions in the late seventh and eighth centuries, relatively few in the ninth century, and a renewed abundance under the Song dynasty from the later tenth century, when the name Sanfoqi appears. These fluctuations may reflect real changes in Srivijaya's diplomatic activity, changes in conditions at Guangzhou, such as the disorder of the late ninth century, changes in Chinese record-keeping, or the redirection of commerce through other ports and routes. Historians therefore treat the pattern as suggestive but not as a precise measure of Srivijaya's fortunes.

Section 29.7

Tribute, Trade, and Diplomatic Language

Chinese sources describe foreign missions in a vocabulary that presented the emperor as the center of a moral and political order. Foreign envoys "came to court," brought "tribute," and received "bestowals." Modern readers may take this language at face value and conclude that the sending polity was a vassal. That inference is unsafe. The vocabulary expressed the Chinese court's ideal order and its preferred way of recording foreign contact. It was not a neutral description of political reality, and it was applied to polities whose relationship to China ranged from close and subordinate to distant and largely independent.

For Srivijaya, there is no evidence that Chinese officials governed, taxed, or garrisoned any part of its territory. The missions are better understood as diplomacy combined with trade, in which a ruler accepted the court's ceremonial framing because doing so opened access to benefits. A Srivijayan embassy could carry the ruler's own message and objectives, and what Chinese compilers recorded as submission may have been, from the sender's perspective, an exchange between rulers that conferred prestige on both. We lack Srivijayan accounts of these missions, so the sender's understanding must be inferred.

Historical Vocabulary
Chaogong (the "tribute" system)

Definition. Chaogong is the Chinese term usually translated as "tribute," referring to the presentation of gifts by foreign envoys at the imperial court and the court's reciprocal gifts and recognition.

Historical context. The term belonged to the court's own conceptual vocabulary and was applied across many centuries to many different relationships, from states under real Chinese pressure to distant trading partners.

Why it matters. Historians read tribute missions as evidence for diplomacy, ritual, and commerce. They do not treat them as a reliable gauge of political subordination.

Potential misunderstanding. The English word "tribute" suggests payment under compulsion, as in a modern hierarchy of overlord and vassal. Applied automatically to Srivijaya, it makes a polity that sought trade and prestige look like a subject state.

Section 29.8

Yijing Arrives in Srivijaya

Yijing (635–713 CE) was a Chinese Buddhist monk who devoted much of his life to seeking the monastic rules and scriptures of India. According to his own writings, he left Guangzhou in 671 on a voyage that took him first to Foshi. He reports that he stayed there for about six months, studying Sanskrit grammar, and then continued by way of other ports on the Sumatran and peninsular coasts toward eastern India, where he spent years studying, including at Nalanda. On his return, by about 686–687, he was again in the Srivijayan and Malayu sphere, where he remained for several years working on compositions and translations. He made a trip to Guangzhou in 689, returned to Srivijaya with assistants, and went back to China finally around 694–695; his years of work there included sending texts and reports to China.

Two of Yijing's works are especially valuable: a record of Buddhist practices he observed in India and the southern seas, and a collection of biographies of monks who traveled westward in search of the Buddhist law. Their purpose was practical and devotional. Yijing wished to instruct Chinese monastics in proper monastic discipline and to commemorate the travelers who had risked their lives. This purpose shapes what he reports. He describes monastic routine, the study of grammar and texts, the availability of teachers, and the conditions of sea travel. He does not describe Srivijayan taxation, officialdom, or territorial extent, and when he mentions the ruler, it is chiefly as a patron who supported him and facilitated his onward travel.

Students should treat Yijing as an exceptionally valuable but partial witness. He was a foreign visitor with a specific religious agenda, relying on his own observations and on what he was told. His claims about numbers of monks and the quality of study are impressions, not statistics, and his interest in orthodoxy colors his judgments. At the same time, his account has an immediacy that no other source for the period matches, and it supplies the core evidence for the next several sections.

Primary Source Window

Yijing on Preparing in Srivijaya before Proceeding to India, Late Seventh Century CE

Creator: Yijing, Chinese Buddhist monk and traveler · Date: composed in the 690s CE on the basis of experiences beginning in 671 · Type: Travel and monastic-practice record written for a Chinese Buddhist audience

Context: Yijing wrote while resident in Srivijaya and in the years immediately after, seeking to inform Chinese monks about conditions in India and the southern seas. He had traveled in the region, studied in Srivijaya and in India, and wished to guide others who might follow.

Paraphrase / Synthesis (not a verbatim excerpt):

Yijing reports that Foshi was home to a large community of Buddhist monks, which he puts at more than a thousand, whose study and discipline resembled those of India. He advises Chinese monks who intend to travel to India that they would do well to spend a year or two in Foshi first, studying the proper monastic rules and Sanskrit, before continuing. He also describes receiving the support of the ruler for his own onward journey.

Analysis: The passage shows that, by the late seventh century, Srivijaya had monastic communities capable of teaching Sanskrit and monastic discipline to foreign visitors, and that a well-informed traveler regarded it as a sensible preparatory stop. It also shows that royal support helped travelers move onward, linking religious mobility to political patronage.

Limitations: The source cannot tell us how monasteries were organized, who funded them, how many monks were resident at any time, or how far royal support extended. The figure of more than a thousand monks is an impression from a single visitor. Yijing also cannot tell us about Srivijayan administration or territorial extent.

Perspective: A Chinese monk concerned with orthodoxy, writing for Chinese Buddhist readers. Perspectives not represented include those of Srivijayan monks, ordinary lay supporters, merchants, and the rulers themselves.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the source say about Foshi and about the advice Yijing gives to other monks?
  2. Who created the source and under what circumstances?
  3. What purposes might have shaped Yijing's description, and how might they affect his emphasis on orthodoxy and on the ruler's support?
  4. What can historians learn from this source about Srivijaya's religious and intellectual role?
  5. What can historians not establish from this source alone about Srivijayan institutions or territorial control?
Section 29.9

Srivijaya as a Center of Buddhist Learning

Yijing's recommendation that Chinese monks study in Srivijaya before going to India implies an intellectual infrastructure: teachers competent in Sanskrit grammar, communities that observed monastic discipline, and a supply of texts. He names a learned teacher in Foshi, Sakyakirti, and describes monks who studied the same subjects as their counterparts in India. He also gives an assessment of monastic affiliation in the southern seas, reporting that most communities followed the monastic law of the Mulasarvastivada school, with some Mahayana presence in Malayu. The identification of schools reflects Yijing's own expertise, and the picture is one visitor's classification of a varied scene.

Sanskrit was the key to this learning. Grammar was the gateway to Buddhist scriptures and commentaries in their Indian form, and students who mastered it could study directly in India. Srivijaya's value as a preparatory center lay in letting visitors acquire that skill and practice monastic discipline in a setting where Sanskrit teaching and Buddhist community life were available, and where the political environment was stable enough to support them. The Old Malay inscriptions of Chapter 28 already include Sanskrit-derived vocabulary and Buddhist concepts, which is consistent with local familiarity with Sanskrit learning.

It would be misleading to call Srivijaya a "university" in the modern sense. The sources do not describe degree programs, fixed curricula, or a single institution comparable to Nalanda. What they describe is a cluster of monastic communities and teachers, supported by royal and lay patronage, that were attractive to visitors. Archaeological traces of Buddhist images, votive objects, and structures in Sumatra support the presence of Buddhist practice, though the chronology of individual sites, including large complexes such as those near Jambi, is often later than the period of Yijing and requires cautious dating. The fair conclusion is that Srivijaya had substantial Buddhist learning, enough to command foreign respect, without implying a formal, centralized academic institution.

Section 29.10

Pilgrimage between China, Southeast Asia, and India

Yijing was one of many pilgrims who crossed the seas in search of Buddhist learning and sacred sites. His biographies of monks who traveled westward describe journeys by both overland and maritime routes, and they show that the sea passage grew in importance in the period. Srivijaya appears in these accounts as a recurring stage. Chinese monks bound for India stopped there to prepare, wait for winds, or find passage, and some stayed for extended periods. Movement in the other direction also occurred: later biographical traditions describe Indian and other South Asian Buddhist teachers who traveled eastward by sea, with Srivijaya sometimes mentioned as part of the passage, though those later traditions require cautious handling.

Pilgrimage thus created a religious geography that included Srivijaya as part of the map of Buddhist Asia. Southern China, the Sumatran and peninsular ports, and eastern India were connected by a sequence of stops where monks could find shelter, instruction, and onward passage. For Srivijaya's rulers and monastic communities, being part of this geography conferred prestige and a stream of visitors who brought texts, news, and sometimes patronage.

The evidence is, however, uneven. Chinese pilgrim accounts are the richest source, which means that the story is told largely from the standpoint of travelers from China. The experiences of Indian and Southeast Asian monks are far less documented, and the volume of eastbound traffic cannot be estimated. Students should therefore avoid describing the pilgrimage network as a symmetrical flow, and should recognize that its visible shape reflects the survival of Chinese records.

Section 29.11

Merchants and Monks on the Same Sea Routes

A central insight of this chapter is that religious mobility depended on the same infrastructure as commercial mobility. Yijing's account of his own voyage shows him traveling on merchant shipping, relying on seasonal winds, and depending on the goodwill and resources of a port ruler. According to the usual reading of his account, he left Guangzhou on a merchant ship, and he later relied on royal assistance for further passage. Monks needed vessels, provisions, pilots, and safe harbors, and these were supplied by the same commercial networks that carried goods. Merchants, in turn, benefited from religious institutions that provided hospitality, translation skills, and links to distant communities.

The networks overlapped without being identical. Not every merchant was Buddhist, and many seafarers belonged to other religious traditions or to none that left records. Not every monk traveled under merchant protection. Monastic and commercial communities pursued different goals and followed different internal rules. Yet they intersected at ports, where a monastery might house visiting travelers, receive gifts from merchants, and gain from the prosperity of the harbor. The relationship was one of mutual convenience, not of identity.

This overlap helps explain why Srivijaya's commercial and religious importance grew together. A ruler who attracted ships also attracted monks, and a ruler who attracted monks gained a reputation that appealed to patrons and traders alike. The next feature turns from rulers and travelers to the ordinary people whose labor and daily lives sustained this network.

Everyday Life and Social History

Who Sustained a Maritime Buddhist World?

The sources for Srivijaya mostly describe rulers, envoys, and elite travelers. Yet the system they describe could not have functioned without large numbers of people whose names we do not know. Boat builders and sailors constructed and crewed the vessels. Pilots and local seafaring communities knew the waters of the straits and river mouths, and later evidence suggests that communities of sea-oriented people, often called Orang Laut in later sources, played important roles in the maritime world, though direct documentation for the seventh and eighth centuries is limited. Farmers and fisherfolk of the river lowlands supplied food. Gatherers in the forests and uplands collected resins, aromatics, and other goods that entered trade. Porters, laborers, and artisans handled cargo and made goods for local use and exchange.

Monastic life also depended on lay support. Monks relied on donations of food, labor, and materials, and monasteries needed communities that would sustain them. Yijing's account of the routine of monastic life suggests daily patterns of study, ritual, and observance, but it is silent about the households that supported them. Women, enslaved and dependent laborers, and non-elite families are largely invisible in the surviving texts. Historians must infer their presence from the scale of activity described and from comparison with better-documented societies, and should remain explicit about what is inference. The wealth of a trading port was unevenly distributed, and there is no evidence that prosperity for rulers and merchants meant prosperity for the people who did the work.

Section 29.12

Buddhist Patronage and Political Prestige

Rulers who supported monasteries, scholars, and travelers gained benefits of several kinds. In the Buddhist understanding of the period, supporting the monastic community generated religious merit, which could be shared with family, subjects, and ancestors. Seventh-century inscriptions already associated with Srivijaya's formation connect rulership with Buddhist acts of merit, and later foundations continue that pattern. Patronage also created ties with monastic communities abroad, whose members could spread a ruler's reputation along the pilgrimage routes. And it signaled a ruler's wealth and legitimacy to rivals, subordinates, and visiting merchants.

Patronage could extend beyond a ruler's own domain. The Ligor inscription records Buddhist foundations on the peninsula, and the Nalanda copperplate records a monastery established in eastern India by a Southeast Asian ruler. Such foundations expressed religious devotion, but they also projected the ruler's name and standing into other regions' political and religious landscapes. In this way patronage worked as a form of diplomacy, creating durable, visible links with other courts and institutions.

Patronage was not simply instrumental. Historians cannot reconstruct the inner motives of rulers, and it would be a mistake to assume that piety was merely cover for politics or that politics was irrelevant to piety. The evidence shows that devotion, prestige, and commerce reinforced one another, and that is the more accurate way to describe the pattern.

Cause and Effect

Why Commercial and Buddhist Importance Reinforced One Another

Long-term causes

Centuries of maritime exchange across the Bay of Bengal and South China Sea created ports, brokers, and ties to South Asian and Chinese Buddhist communities. Earlier Southeast Asian engagement with Buddhist ideas, discussed in Chapters 18 and 19, prepared rulers and local elites to welcome Buddhist institutions.

Short-term causes

Srivijaya's consolidation of a river-mouth center and relationships with surrounding communities in the seventh century, examined in Chapter 28, gave it a stable base. Rising Chinese interest in southern trade and in Buddhist travel increased the demand for reliable stopping points.

Immediate context and transformation

Travelers such as Yijing used Srivijaya as a place to wait, study, and prepare. Rulers supported them and founded religious establishments, while merchants and envoys moved through the same ports. A cycle emerged in which hospitality to monks enhanced reputation, and reputation attracted ships and visitors.

Effects

Srivijaya acquired an international profile as both a port and a Buddhist center. Its rulers gained diplomatic connections, foreign records of their activities, and, through patronage, a presence in distant religious landscapes.

Historical complexity: Religion was not merely an ornament of trade, and trade was not merely a means to religious ends. The two reinforced each other through shared infrastructure and shared patrons. The mechanism, however, is inferred from scattered evidence and should not be mistaken for a documented policy. Other ports also offered both commerce and religious institutions, so these factors do not by themselves explain why Srivijaya in particular became prominent.

Section 29.13

The Malay Peninsula: Ligor/Chaiya and the Problem of Reach

One of the most important inscriptions for Srivijaya's wider connections was found in southern Thailand on the Malay Peninsula. Its provenance is traditionally associated with Nakhon Si Thammarat (Ligor), although a Chaiya provenance has also been proposed. It is a stone stele inscribed on two faces in Sanskrit. Face A bears a date (Saka 697) corresponding to 775 CE and records that a king of Srivijaya had built Buddhist sanctuaries. Face B, which praises a ruler of the Sailendra dynasty, carries no date. Older scholarship placed it close in time to Face A, but later palaeographic work has argued that it may be substantially later, perhaps into the early ninth century, so the two faces should not be treated as a single securely dated statement of 775. The inscription is the principal contemporary evidence that a Srivijayan king was patronizing Buddhist foundations on the peninsula in the later eighth century.

The inscription's significance is clear, but its interpretation is not. Scholars debate how the two faces relate to each other. Some have read them as evidence that a Sailendra king also ruled Srivijaya, implying a single dynasty connecting Sumatra, the peninsula, and Java. Others argue that the faces refer to different rulers and different moments and that the connection between the Sailendras and Srivijaya is not established by this text alone. The stele's original provenance is also uncertain, and the political status of the area at the time, whether directly governed, loosely allied, or independent but friendly, cannot be determined from the inscription.

The Ligor inscription therefore illustrates the "problem of reach." It documents Srivijayan patronage on the peninsula, which suggests ties of some strength. It does not document a system of provincial administration, tax collection, or military garrison. Students should treat it as evidence for Srivijayan influence and religious activity on the peninsula, not as a map of conquest, and should recognize that the peninsula had its own centers, such as Kedah and Tambralinga, with their own histories, as discussed in Chapter 26.

Section 29.14

Srivijaya, the Sailendras, and Central Java: Connections without Collapsing Them Together

The name Sailendra appears in inscriptions from Java and, as the Ligor stele shows, on the Malay Peninsula. In Java, inscriptions associated with Sailendra patronage record Buddhist foundations in the later eighth century, and the dynasty is connected with Central Javanese monumental building discussed in Chapters 30 and 31. Evidence from Sumatra, the peninsula, and Java thus seems to link these regions' histories, and many scholars have sought to explain the links through a single dynastic or political relationship.

Two broad interpretations have shaped the discussion. One, associated especially with the scholar George Coedès, proposed that the Sailendras were rulers of Srivijaya who also held power in Java, moving between the two. Another line of argument, developed by Indonesian and Dutch scholars, held that the Sailendras were a Javanese dynasty whose origins and political center lay in Java, and that their relationship with Srivijaya was one of contact, rivalry, or marriage alliance rather than identity. More recent reassessments have emphasized how limited the evidence is and how much of the older reconstruction depended on combining texts that may not refer to the same events. The disagreement remains unresolved.

This chapter therefore takes a restrained position: evidence connects Sumatra, the peninsula, and Java, and the connections included shared Buddhist patronage and possibly dynastic ties, but the precise political relationships are debated. The actual history of the Sailendra dynasty, Central Javanese political competition, and the agricultural and political foundations of the Javanese courts belong to Chapter 30, and Chapter 31 will examine Borobudur. Chapter 29 therefore does not narrate Javanese succession and uses Java only to illustrate the breadth of Srivijaya's connections.

Section 29.15

Balaputradeva and Nalanda

The Nalanda copperplate is a charter issued in the name of the Pala king Devapaladeva, who ruled in eastern India in the early to mid-ninth century (Pala chronology is disputed, and published reconstructions of his reign differ by a decade or more). Editors also read the charter's regnal year differently: 35 in a recent critical edition, 39 in older editions. It records a royal grant of villages to support a Buddhist monastery at Nalanda that had been founded by Balaputradeva, described as king of Suvarnadvipa. The text also describes the donor's lineage, tracing his ancestry to a ruler of Yavabhumi, a name usually associated with Java, and mentions his father and a connection to the Sailendra family. Copperplate grants of this kind were legal and religious documents intended to secure a donation for the long term, and this one is exceptionally important as contemporary evidence from South Asia for a maritime Southeast Asian ruler's patronage of an Indian Buddhist institution.

Wording matters. The inscription names Balaputradeva as ruler of Suvarnadvipa; it does not simply say "Srivijaya" in the form that older textbook summaries sometimes imply. Equating Suvarnadvipa with Srivijaya is plausible and widely accepted, since the name was used for the Sumatran-centered realm. But the text also gives its subject a Javanese ancestry, which is part of the Sailendra problem discussed above. For this reason, historians use the copperplate cautiously, as evidence for the reach of a maritime Southeast Asian ruler's religious connections and for the prestige of Nalanda, while recognizing that it does not by itself settle questions about dynastic identity or political control.

The grant also says something about South Asia. Eastern Indian rulers and monastic institutions accepted and recorded patronage from a distant Southeast Asian king, and the Pala court issued a formal charter to confirm it. This is evidence of a two-way relationship, with benefits for both sides: the monastery received revenue, and the donor received merit and standing in a leading center of Buddhist learning.

Section 29.16

South Asia beyond Nalanda: Religious and Commercial Connections

Nalanda is the most famous point of connection, but evidence points to a wider Bay of Bengal world. A Tamil inscription found at Takuapa on the Malay Peninsula, probably from the ninth century, records a tank called Śrī Avanināṟaṇam and associates it with the Maṇikkirāmam merchant guild and other groups. The tank's name may derive from Avaninārāyaṇa, a biruda of the Pallava king Nandivarman III, but the damaged inscription does not securely establish direct Pallava political authority at Takuapa. It does indicate a South Indian commercial presence on the peninsula. Buddhist votive tablets and images found in Sumatra and on the peninsula point to the circulation of Buddhist objects and ritual practices. Later, the Chola copperplate grant of about 1005 CE refers to a Buddhist monastery at Nagapattinam on the southeastern Indian coast associated with a Srivijayan king, showing that this tradition of patronage in South Asia continued into the eleventh century.

Later Tibetan tradition adds a further dimension. Tibetan sources place the Buddhist teacher Atisa in Suvarnadvipa for about twelve years, commonly dated c. 1011–1023, studying with a master known as Dharmakirti of Suvarnadvipa, usually identified with Serlingpa. These accounts are much later than the events they describe, their chronology is debated, and they belong to a religious tradition with its own interests. The location of the teacher's center within the Srivijayan world also remains debated: Palembang should not be assumed, and some modern work has proposed Kedah. The accounts are best treated as evidence that the Srivijayan world retained a reputation for Buddhist learning, not as precise historical documentation.

These strands of evidence should not be read through the old model of passive "Indianization," as if Indian culture simply spread to Southeast Asia. Chapter 19 explained why that model has been revised. Southeast Asian rulers selected and sponsored Buddhist institutions, sent patronage abroad, and hosted visitors, and South Asian courts and monastic centers responded to Southeast Asian initiative. Monks, merchants, texts, ritual practices, prestige goods, and political relationships crossed the same ocean in both directions, even if the surviving evidence is uneven.

Multiple Perspectives

One Maritime World, Five Vantage Points

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns that historians infer from the evidence. They are not quotations from any named historical actor, and no surviving source records most of these perspectives directly.

A ruler of Palembang: Hosting ships and monks, sending embassies, and founding religious establishments strengthened his reputation and tied dependent rulers and merchants to his harbor. His power depended on remaining an attractive, reliable node, and on maintaining relationships rather than administering every locality.
A Chinese Buddhist pilgrim: Srivijaya was a place to prepare for the journey to India, to study Sanskrit and monastic discipline, and to find passage. Its interest lay in the opportunities it offered the traveler, and this perspective dominates the surviving record.
A merchant or shipmaster: Ports were places to wait for winds, provision, find buyers, and secure protection. Political stability, fair dealing, and good information mattered more than religious affiliation, though religious communities could provide hospitality and contacts.
A local ruler on the Malay Peninsula: A Srivijayan king's patronage of Buddhist foundations in his region could be both an honor and a sign of influence. Such a ruler might welcome the benefits of association while seeking to preserve his own standing, and the inscription does not tell us how he understood the relationship.
A monk at an Indian Buddhist institution: A distant ruler's donation provided revenue and prestige, and a monastery founded by a Southeast Asian king gave the institution a link to a faraway world. The sources record the donation, not the monk's own reflections.

Why this matters: These perspectives did not carry equal power or leave equal evidence. The ruler, pilgrim, and Indian grant are best documented; the experiences of local rulers, laborers, and seafarers are largely inferred.

Section 29.17

Did Srivijaya Control the Straits?

Chapter 28 introduced the question of whether Srivijaya "controlled" the Strait of Malacca. The older picture imagined fleets permanently stopping every ship and forcing it to call at Srivijayan ports. The evidence does not support such a total system. Foreign sources describe an important trading realm, but none describes a standing blockade. More plausible mechanisms for influence include relationships with local rulers and seafaring communities who knew and policed the waters, the capacity to offer security and services at attractive ports, diplomatic and religious prestige that drew ships voluntarily, and, at times, the capacity to apply pressure on rivals or on communities that bypassed its harbors. Later Malay and other traditions, which are much less contemporary, preserve ideas of rulers whose authority rested on ties to sea-oriented communities, but these must be used carefully for the earlier period.

Claims of a total commercial "monopoly" should be treated with skepticism. The peninsula's overland routes offered alternatives. Ports elsewhere in Sumatra, on the peninsula, in Java, and on the South China Sea coast competed for traffic. The Belitung wreck itself shows that long-distance shipping passed through the broader region with a cargo that does not obviously reflect Srivijayan commercial direction. Arabic sources of the ninth century, which describe foreign merchants shifting operations to Kalah on the Malay Peninsula (often identified with Kedah) after the 879 sack of Guangzhou, show that merchants could shift their activity to other ports of the region when circumstances changed.

The better description is of influence over nodes, not complete control of a waterway. Srivijaya shaped the conditions under which many ships operated, and its position gave it leverage, but that leverage was contingent on relationships that had to be maintained and could be challenged. This understanding anticipates the debate of the next section.

Historical Vocabulary
Thalassocracy

Definition. A thalassocracy is a state whose power derives chiefly from control of, or predominance on, the sea, as opposed to control of a large land territory.

Historical context. Scholars have applied the word to Srivijaya since the early twentieth century, often in conjunction with, or as a modification of, the idea of a Srivijayan empire.

Why it matters. The term highlights the maritime basis of Srivijaya's power, but it can imply a navy-based domination of the sea that the evidence does not demonstrate.

Potential misunderstanding. Students should not assume that "thalassocracy" means a standing fleet policing a defined stretch of sea. For Srivijaya, it is better read as shorthand for a power organized around ports, rivers, and sea-oriented relationships.

Section 29.18

Empire, Thalassocracy, Mandala, or Maritime Network?

The question of how to describe Srivijaya's political nature has been one of the most persistent in Southeast Asian historiography, and the following feature sets out the main interpretations. Students should read it alongside the evidence discussed in the preceding sections, particularly the nature of the foreign sources and the limits of the inscriptions.

Historical Debate

Was Srivijaya an Empire?

The question: Was Srivijaya a centralized territorial empire dominating Sumatra, the Malay Peninsula, and the straits, or was it something else: a thalassocracy, a mandala-type polity of overlapping spheres of authority, or a looser network of ports and relationships?

Interpretation A — the older imperial model: Early twentieth-century reconstructions, especially Coedès's, combined Chinese, Arabic, Sanskrit, and Old Malay sources to describe a large, durable maritime empire, centered on Palembang, controlling the straits and extending its authority over wide areas of Sumatra and the peninsula, with dynastic links to Java.
Interpretation B — thalassocracy and mandala models: Later scholars such as O. W. Wolters emphasized the commercial and ritual basis of Srivijayan power and applied the mandala concept, introduced in Chapter 1, to describe authority radiating from a center and overlapping with other centers. Others stressed the port-centered, sea-oriented character of the polity, and the importance of its relationships with local rulers and seafaring communities.
Interpretation C — reassessment of centralization: Hermann Kulke, in an influential 1993 reassessment, questioned the older model of a continuously centralized empire, emphasizing the evidence for a royal center (the kadatuan, a term appearing in Old Malay inscriptions) and for ties with dependent rulers, while doubting assumptions of uniform administration. Archaeologists such as Pierre-Yves Manguin have drawn attention to the Musi basin and to the need to ground interpretation in excavation rather than in textual inference alone.

Evidence: The sources include Old Malay inscriptions, Chinese records, Arabic geographical writing, Buddhist travel accounts, the Ligor and Nalanda inscriptions, and archaeological finds from Palembang, Jambi, the peninsula, and shipwrecks. None provides a map of administrative control, and many have been interpreted by combining texts that may not refer to the same moment.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns terminology, scale, and interpretation more than the basic fact that Srivijaya was important. Scholars differ on how much territory it governed, how continuous its authority was, how to read the inscriptions' evidence of loyalty and oaths, and whether "empire" can describe a polity that appears to have depended on relationships more than on administration.

Why it matters: How historians describe Srivijaya affects how they understand state formation in maritime Southeast Asia, how they compare it with agrarian states on the mainland and in Java, and how they interpret later polities, such as Melaka and Johor-Riau, that drew on its legacy.

Student task: Distinguish "wide influence" from "uniform administration," and then decide which of the three interpretations best fits the evidence discussed in this chapter. Identify one type of new evidence, such as additional excavations or newly discovered inscriptions, that could most clarify the problem.

This textbook adopts a practical position. It treats Srivijaya as a politically significant polity centered on Palembang whose strength lay in networks of relationships, and it uses terms such as "network," "mandala," and "thalassocracy" as analytical conveniences rather than as definitive labels. It treats "empire" with caution, because the term suggests uniform territorial administration that the evidence does not demonstrate.

Section 29.19

A Maritime Buddhist World and the Turn toward Central Java

Srivijaya's importance came from joining political, commercial, diplomatic, and Buddhist networks. Its harbors offered services to merchants and travelers. Its rulers sent embassies to China and patronized foundations abroad. Its monastic communities attracted foreign students, and its name circulated from southern China to eastern India and into Arabic and Persian writing. These roles reinforced one another, and it is their combination, not any single one, that explains Srivijaya's international standing. At the same time, the evidence consistently warns against inferring uniform administration from such influence.

The chapter's evidence also points forward. Ligor, the Nalanda copperplate, and the Sailendra connection show that Srivijaya's history cannot be separated from that of Java. From the later eighth century, Central Java emerged as another major center of Buddhist patronage and monumental building, supported by a different agrarian and political foundation. Chapter 30 will examine the Sailendra dynasty, Central Javanese political competition, agricultural foundations, and links to wider maritime networks, and Chapter 31 will turn to Borobudur. Srivijaya continued as an important presence into the tenth century and beyond, and later changes in trade routes and regional competition are the subject of Chapter 47.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Seventh-Century Formation to Mature International Network

What Changed

  • The evidence shifted from mainly local inscriptions to a mix dominated by foreign accounts, diplomatic records, and distant inscriptions.
  • Srivijaya's profile broadened from a Sumatran river-mouth polity to a name known in China, India, and the Islamic world.
  • Buddhist learning, pilgrimage, and patronage became prominent elements of its reputation.
  • Connections with the peninsula, Java, and eastern India became part of its documented relationships.

What Stayed the Same

  • Palembang and the Musi remained a major Srivijayan core through much of the period examined here; in the later history of the polity, Jambi became increasingly important and eventually emerged as a political center, a transformation examined in Chapter 47.
  • Power continued to rest on relationships with ports, rulers, and communities rather than on demonstrable uniform administration.
  • Dependence on monsoon seasonality and on shipping specialists persisted.
  • Ordinary farmers, fishers, gatherers, and sailors continued to be mostly invisible in the sources.

When and how: Change was gradual and uneven, visible chiefly in the documentary record from the late seventh century, and it was experienced most directly by rulers, merchants, and monastic communities. Most inhabitants experienced greater continuity in daily life. Why the pattern matters: it shows that Srivijaya's importance grew from the accumulation of connections, not from a single act of expansion.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced how Srivijaya, formed in the seventh century as a Palembang-centered political network, became a major commercial, diplomatic, and Buddhist presence linking maritime Southeast Asia with China and South Asia. Its central argument is that Srivijaya's mature power rested on coordinating strategically valuable nodes, people, and relationships, not on uniformly administering a continuous territory. Commerce, political prestige, and Buddhist patronage reinforced one another, giving Srivijaya an international importance far beyond Palembang.

The geographic foundation was a position among rivers, straits, and monsoon seas. The Musi river, the Bangka Strait, the Strait of Malacca, the peninsula's crossings, and the connecting seas created routes and choices but did not guarantee supremacy. Monsoon seasonality forced ships to pause, which created demand for provisioning, anchorage, brokerage, information, and protection. A port that supplied these services reliably, and that cultivated relationships with suppliers and local rulers, could prosper without producing the goods it traded.

The goods themselves came from many places: forest and aromatic products, metals, and other regional commodities, together with Chinese ceramics and silk and South Asian textiles and beads. The Belitung shipwreck illustrates the scale of long-distance shipping in regional waters. Simple labels such as "spice monopoly" obscure a diverse trade, and no evidence establishes that Srivijaya monopolized any commodity across the region.

Relations with Tang China are documented in Chinese records of embassies from Shilifoshi. These records reflect a mixture of diplomacy, ceremony, and trade. The Chinese vocabulary of "tribute" expressed the court's own worldview and should not be read as proof of political subordination. Fluctuations in the surviving record reflect a combination of changes in Srivijayan activity, conditions at Guangzhou, and patterns of record-keeping.

Yijing's account of his stay in 671 and his later residence supplies the core evidence for Srivijaya as a center of Buddhist learning. He found a large monastic community, teachers of Sanskrit grammar, and observance of monastic discipline, and he recommended that other Chinese monks prepare there before traveling to India. His account is valuable but partial, written by a Chinese monk with a specific purpose, and it does not describe administration or territory. Srivijaya should be seen as a cluster of monastic communities supported by patronage, not as a modern university.

Pilgrims and merchants shared ships, winds, ports, and rulers, and their networks overlapped without being identical. Rulers who supported monasteries gained merit, international standing, and ties with other courts, and patronage worked as a form of diplomacy. The pilgrimage network is documented mainly from the Chinese side, so its visible shape reflects the survival of Chinese records.

The Ligor inscription of 775 documents Srivijayan Buddhist patronage on the Malay Peninsula, while its second face, which is undated and may be substantially later, introduces a Sailendra ruler. Scholars disagree about how the faces relate and about whether the Sailendras ruled Srivijaya, were a Javanese dynasty allied or competing with it, or something else. The inscription establishes influence and religious activity, not a system of provincial administration.

The Nalanda copperplate of the Pala king Devapaladeva records a grant to a monastery founded at Nalanda by Balaputradeva, king of Suvarnadvipa, and offers exceptional evidence for the reach of maritime Southeast Asian patronage into eastern India. Its wording must be handled precisely: it names Suvarnadvipa, not simply Srivijaya, and it describes a lineage that links to Java. Other evidence, including the Takuapa Tamil inscription, the Chola grant of about 1005, and later Tibetan traditions, places Srivijaya within a wider Bay of Bengal world of religious and commercial exchange.

On the question of control, the evidence does not support a standing blockade of the straits or a total commercial monopoly. Srivijaya influenced conditions through relationships with ports, local rulers, seafaring communities, and prestige. The historical debate over whether Srivijaya was an empire, a thalassocracy, a mandala, or a network turns on how to describe such influence. This textbook treats "empire" with caution and emphasizes the distinction between wide influence and uniform administration.

Finally, the chapter has shown that Srivijaya's history was bound up with that of Java and that from the later eighth century Central Java emerged as another major center of Buddhist patronage and monumental building, supported by a different agrarian and political foundation. That story belongs to Chapters 30 and 31. Srivijaya's later transformations belong to Chapter 47. What this chapter provides is the understanding that Srivijaya was neither a passive crossroads nor a territorial empire in the older sense, but an active Southeast Asian polity that shaped, and was shaped by, a maritime Buddhist world.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 29 matters because it shows how a Southeast Asian polity could become internationally significant without resembling the territorial states that modern readers expect. Srivijaya's story invites students to ask what power meant in a world of monsoon seas, river mouths, and portable religious prestige. Its rulers, merchants, monks, and sailors built a network that mattered in China, India, and the Islamic world, and the evidence for it comes from a remarkable range of sources.

The chapter also reveals a larger process. Trade, diplomacy, and religion did not operate in separate spheres in the early maritime world. The same ports, ships, and rulers supported all three, and the resulting overlap shaped how Southeast Asian polities presented themselves and how others saw them. This pattern anticipates later developments in the textbook, including the Islamic commercial networks of Chapters 62 through 66, in which merchants, scholars, and rulers again moved along shared maritime routes.

The methodological lessons are equally important. The chapter demonstrates how historians work when no single source provides an internal account: by comparing Chinese, Arabic, Sanskrit, Old Malay, and archaeological evidence, noting each source's purpose and limits, and resisting the temptation to turn influence into a map of control. The debate over empire, thalassocracy, mandala, and network will recur whenever the textbook confronts polities whose authority rested on relationships rather than administration.

Finally, the chapter points forward. Connections with Java and the Sailendras lead to Chapters 30 and 31, where the focus shifts to the agrarian foundations and monumental Buddhism of Central Java. Srivijaya's later transformations will be examined in Chapter 47. What survives across these chapters is the recognition that Southeast Asian societies were historical actors that selected, transformed, and transmitted ideas and goods across the seas, and that their histories are best understood on their own terms.
