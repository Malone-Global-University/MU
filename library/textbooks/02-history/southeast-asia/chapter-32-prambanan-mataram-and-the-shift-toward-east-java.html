Prambanan, Mataram, and the Shift toward East Java

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Prambanan is both a monument and a document of political ambition: a vast Śaiva sanctuary on a volcanic plain, dedicated within a generation of Borobudur's completion, and built by a court whose later rulers would govern from the Brantas valley, hundreds of kilometres to the east. Between the temple and the move lies the history of an agricultural kingdom, its officials, its villages, and the people who worked its fields and quarried its stone.

On the plain east of the Opak River, within sight of Mount Merapi's slopes, stands a cluster of towers that rise from nested courtyards of volcanic stone. The tallest, the central sanctuary of the god Śiva, reaches roughly forty-seven metres, and it is flanked by shrines to Viṣṇu and Brahmā and faced by smaller buildings associated with the gods' animal mounts. Around this inner core spread rings of far smaller shrines, in numbers that make the whole complex one of the largest temple compounds ever built in Southeast Asia. Today it is commonly called Prambanan, after the nearby village, or Loro Jonggrang in later folklore. The name its builders used appears to have been Śivagṛha, "House of Śiva," a term found in an inscription dated 856 CE.

That inscription ties the temple to a moment of political consolidation, but it does not tell the whole story, and the chapter must say plainly why. The metrical Old Javanese text was issued under a king known as Dyah Lokapāla, associated with the title Rakai Kayuwangi, and it refers to the withdrawal of his predecessor Rakai Pikatan, under whom construction probably began. The dedication of 856, the foundation of the work, and later expansions are separate phases that popular accounts often compress into the achievement of a single ruler. Historians also disagree about how to read the inscription's account of a conflict with an enemy who held a stone stronghold. A lord named Kumbhayoni, attested in other inscriptions, has been proposed as that enemy, and an older reading found a Śailendra prince, Bālaputra, in the text; both identifications are modern interpretations, and the second has been challenged on epigraphic grounds.

This chapter follows the kingdom behind the monument. It examines what historians can and cannot say about Pikatan's rise, how Śaiva and Buddhist patronage overlapped around Prambanan, how a Central Javanese court raised revenue, administered land, and drew on the labour of villages, and how royal authority was reorganized under Balitung and his successors. It then turns to the most debated question of the period: why, around 929 CE, a king named Mpu Siṇḍok issued charters in East Java and ruled from courts in the Brantas basin. The answer has often been sought in a single dramatic cause, usually a volcanic eruption or a defeat. The evidence does not support any single account, and the chapter treats environmental, political, and economic explanations as hypotheses of differing strength.

The evidence is uneven. Inscriptions on stone and copper, written in Sanskrit and Old Javanese, preserve royal orders, land grants, tax privileges, and lists of officials, but they were produced by courts and religious establishments, not by cultivators. Archaeology contributes the temples themselves, their stone, and the wider landscape of settlements and fields, although excavation of ordinary habitation sites remains far less extensive than excavation of monuments. Later Javanese chronicles are separated from these events by centuries and cannot be treated as contemporary records. Throughout, the chapter distinguishes what inscriptions state, what archaeology shows, and what modern scholars have inferred by combining them.

Chapter 32 continues the story begun in Chapter 30 on the Śailendras and Central Java and in Chapter 31 on Borobudur. It does not retell Śailendra origins or Borobudur's iconography. It also stops with the earliest East Javanese courts under Siṇḍok, leaving the later dynasties of the Brantas region and the long history that leads to Singhasari and Majapahit to Chapters 48 and 49 and the chapters that follow. Its central claim is that the move east involved real changes in political geography and royal authority alongside striking continuities in institutions, religious patronage, economic relationships, and the lives of village communities.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why historians must reconstruct ninth- and tenth-century Mataram from inscriptions, archaeology, and retrospective succession lists rather than from continuous narrative sources.
  2. Distinguish the patronage, construction, and dedication phases of Prambanan and assess why the temple cannot be attributed to a single ruler.
  3. Analyze the Śivagṛha inscription of 856 as evidence for kingship, temple dedication, and political conflict, including its limitations and its contested readings.
  4. Describe the architectural organization of Prambanan and the religious significance of its temples of Śiva, Viṣṇu, and Brahmā.
  5. Assess the evidence for coexistence of Buddhist and Śaiva patronage in the Prambanan landscape and the problems with narratives of religious replacement.
  6. Explain how Mataram's titles, officials, land grants, and tax privileges organized relationships among the court, temples, and villages.
  7. Reconstruct everyday life in Central Javanese villages where evidence permits, including agriculture, labour, gender, and the burdens of royal and religious projects.
  8. Trace the political sequence from Pikatan through Kayuwangi, Balitung, Dakṣa, Tulodong, and Wawa to Mpu Siṇḍok.
  9. Evaluate environmental, political, and economic explanations for the shift toward East Java and explain why none should be treated as demonstrated.
  10. Compare Central Javanese and early East Javanese courts in terms of institutions, religion, and economic connections, identifying continuities and changes.

Historical Context

By the early ninth century, the volcanic plains of Central Java, watered by rivers descending from Merapi, Merbabu, and Sumbing, had become a densely organized agricultural zone. Chapter 30 examined the Śailendras and the political competition among Central Javanese rulers, and Chapter 31 examined Borobudur on the Kedu plain as the great Buddhist monument produced within that environment. Earlier inscriptions, including the Canggal inscription associated with the ruler Sañjaya, show Śaiva royal patronage in Central Java well before Prambanan, and Buddhist foundations such as Kalasan, dated 778, show that both traditions were supported by court elites. Chapter 19 introduced the view, now widely held, that Javanese rulers selected and transformed Sanskritic religious and political forms, and that is the framework used here.

Central Java in this period was linked to wider worlds without being merely their periphery. Maritime exchange connected Javanese courts with the powers examined in Chapters 28 and 29, and Buddhist and Śaiva ideas travelled along Indian Ocean routes. Yet the court's own base was agrarian: irrigated rice fields on fertile volcanic soil supported a population large enough to supply the labour and revenue behind Borobudur and Prambanan. The environment also posed risks. Chapter 5 discusses volcanoes, earthquakes, and river systems, and this chapter treats them as real hazards that rulers and villagers managed, not as scenery.

Students should also note a terminological trap. The ninth- and tenth-century kingdom is often called Mataram, but inscriptions more commonly name the realm or its court Medang, and the later Islamic sultanate of Mataram, examined in Chapter 83, is a different state. This chapter uses "Mataram" as the conventional scholarly label for the Central Javanese kingdom and explains the term further in the Historical Vocabulary feature in Section 32.2. The chapter also avoids the older reconstruction that treated Central Java as divided between a Hindu Sañjaya dynasty and a Buddhist Śailendra dynasty, with each rise and fall explained by religious rivalry. Chapter 30 explains why that framework has been substantially revised.

Section 32.1

Central Java in the Mid-Ninth Century

The heartland of the kingdom lay on two neighbouring lowland zones. To the northwest, the Kedu plain between the Progo and Elo rivers had supported Borobudur and a dense scatter of smaller foundations. To the southeast, the Prambanan plain, bounded on the west by the Opak River and on the south by a limestone ridge, lay on the southern side of Mount Merapi. Both were built up by volcanic outwash, and both combined fertile alluvium with abundant river water. Neither was closed off. Valley routes and low passes connected them with one another, with the northern coast, and, by longer routes, with the Brantas basin in the east. Chapter 3 introduced Java as an island of unusually productive volcanic lowlands within the maritime world, and these two plains are the clearest historical expression of that productivity.

Political authority across this landscape was not a matter of a single fixed capital. Inscriptions refer to royal seats and palaces, among them places called Medang, Mamrati, and Poh Pitu, and scholars debate where these lay and whether they were successive or simultaneous. The practical meaning is that a king's court could be re-sited within the kingdom's heartland without implying a political rupture. This is one reason that the eventual move to East Java, examined in Sections 32.19 to 32.21, has to be judged against a background in which courts were already mobile, and in which royal authority rested on ties to landed lords and communities more than on possession of a particular city.

By about 850 the Prambanan plain already carried a thick layer of earlier religious and political construction. Kalasan, dedicated in 778, and Sari lay close by, the large Buddhist complex of Sewu stood to the north, and the plateau of Ratu Boko above the plain preserved the remains of a walled and terraced complex that earlier inscriptions connect with a Buddhist establishment. Prambanan was therefore added to a landscape in which earlier rulers and elites had already invested heavily. It was not placed on empty ground, and it did not mark a clean break between a Buddhist and a Śaiva age. Chapters 30 and 31 explain the earlier Buddhist patronage; this chapter takes the landscape as its starting point and asks how a Śaiva sanctuary of unprecedented scale came to be added to it.

Section 32.2

Reconstructing Mataram: Inscriptions, Archaeology, and Historical Uncertainty

The political history of the period rests chiefly on epigraphy, the study of inscriptions. These are cut in stone or engraved on copper plates in Old Javanese and, in some cases, Sanskrit, and they are dated in the Śaka era, which counts from 78 CE. Dates are sometimes given in words, sometimes by a chronogram, a phrase whose words stand for numbers, and the conversion to the Common Era depends on calendrical calculation that scholars have refined over a century. Most inscriptions are charters recording a royal order: the creation of a tax-privileged territory, the settlement of a dispute, or the endowment of a shrine. They name officials, villages, and witnesses, and they typically end with blessings and curses. Their great value is that they are contemporary. Their limitation is that they were produced by or for courts and religious establishments, so they show how the court wished its relations with temples and villages to be understood, not how those relations felt to cultivators.

Succession is harder to establish than it first appears. The Mantyasih inscription of 907 and the Wanua Tengah III charter of 908 list earlier kings, and scholars use them to reconstruct the line from Sañjaya through Pikatan and Lokapāla to Balitung. But both were issued under Balitung and present the royal past in a form that served his claim to legitimacy. They omit rulers who did not fit, including the Śailendra-associated figures discussed in Chapter 30, and the two lists do not agree in every detail. Dates for individual reigns are therefore modern reconstructions: the accession dates found in Wanua Tengah III are real data, but the end of one reign and the length of another often have to be inferred. Different modern reconstructions differ by several years for the early tenth century, and this chapter gives ranges and identifies where scholars disagree.

Archaeology supplies the other main line of evidence. Temples are visible, datable by style and sometimes by associated inscriptions, and they preserve sculpture and construction techniques. Fields, houses, workshops, and markets are far less visible because most ordinary buildings were of wood, bamboo, and thatch. Environmental evidence, including volcanic deposits and sediment records, can show that eruptions or floods occurred, but dating them precisely enough to connect them with a particular reign is difficult. Later Javanese chronicles and folklore, such as the story of the maiden Loro Jonggrang, were written or fixed centuries afterwards and cannot be treated as evidence for ninth-century events. For these reasons the chapter separates three kinds of statement throughout: what contemporary inscriptions say, what archaeology and environmental science show, and what modern scholars have inferred by combining them.

Historical Vocabulary
Medang and Mataram

Definition. Medang (Old Javanese Mdaṅ) is the name most often given in inscriptions to the realm or its royal seat. Mataram is the conventional modern label for the Central Javanese kingdom of roughly the eighth to early tenth centuries, a word that also occurs in inscriptions in a regional sense.

Why it matters. Books use different labels for the same political tradition, and students should recognize them as equivalents. The later Islamic sultanate of Mataram (Chapter 83) is a separate state.

Potential misunderstanding. Neither label implies a single fixed capital or a bounded territory. As Chapter 1 explained for mandala-like polities, authority was exercised unevenly over a region through lords and communities, and the court was movable.

Section 32.3

Rakai Pikatan and the Reconfiguration of Royal Authority

Rakai Pikatan, known in some inscriptions as Dyah Saladu, is one of the kings listed in the retrospective inscriptions, and he reigned in the middle decades of the ninth century. Rakai marks him as lord of the locality of Pikatan, the base from which he rose. Several inscriptions associate him with the Plaosan complex and with a royal pair read as himself and Śrī Kahulunnan, usually identified as Pramodhawardhani, the daughter of the Śailendra king Samaratungga named in the Karangtengah (Kayumwungan) inscription of 824. The marriage that this implies is plausible and widely accepted, but it is a reconstruction built from separate documents, and no single text says in so many words that Pikatan, a Śaiva lord, married a Buddhist Śailendra heiress. Even so, the picture of a union that joined two powerful families fits the evidence for elite alliance better than the older picture of a Hindu faction overthrowing a Buddhist dynasty.

That older picture deserves a direct answer, since it long organized the textbook version of this period. It treated Pikatan's rise as the victory of a Śaiva Sañjaya line over a Buddhist Śailendra line, explained Prambanan as a monument of that victory, and read the Buddhist foundations of Plaosan and Sewu as relics of the displaced rival. The difficulty is that the evidence does not show two dynasties in sectarian conflict. It shows an interrelated elite, with intermarriage, overlapping patronage, and rival claimants for particular offices and thrones. Buddhist building continued while Prambanan rose. Some scholars see a genuine shift of emphasis in royal religious patronage toward Śaivism at this time, and the scale of Prambanan supports that. But a shift of emphasis within a shared elite is a different historical claim from the replacement of one religion or one dynasty by another.

A further point concerns how Pikatan's reign ended. The Śivagṛha inscription, examined in the next section, refers to a king called Jātiningrat, a name generally understood as one adopted by Pikatan on leaving the throne, who relinquished kingship to Lokapāla. Wanua Tengah III gives the accession of Lokapāla as 855. If this reading is right, Pikatan withdrew into a religious role and the succession passed to a successor in a planned transfer, not by violent overthrow, even though conflict with other claimants followed. The relationship between Pikatan and Lokapāla is not stated outright in any inscription; the usual view that Lokapāla was Pikatan's son is a hypothesis, though a reasonable one given the transmission of the throne and the way the 856 text describes it.

Section 32.4

The Śivagṛha Inscription of 856

The Śivagṛha inscription is cut on an andesite stele found at Prambanan and now in the National Museum in Jakarta, where it is catalogued as D.28. It is dated by a chronogram to Śaka 778, which corresponds to 856 CE and, by the usual calendrical conversion, to November of that year. It was issued under Dyah Lokapāla, who is generally identified with the king called Rakai Kayuwangi in the later lists. Scholars describe it as a metrical Old Javanese text, composed in verse, which uses Sanskrit-derived religious and royal vocabulary but is not a bilingual Sanskrit and Old Javanese document. This is significant because verse composition of this kind is rare for the period, and it implies a court with poets as well as officials. Some scholars have used the poem's date to argue that the Old Javanese Kakawin Rāmāyaṇa belongs to the same milieu, though that dating is itself debated.

The inscription is damaged, and its contents have been read in different ways. Scholars generally agree that it concerns the dedication of a Śaiva sanctuary, which gives it its name, "House of Śiva," and that it refers to the succession of Lokapāla after the withdrawal of a king called Jātiningrat. It also describes a conflict with an enemy who had taken refuge in a stronghold of stone, which has often been connected with Ratu Boko. Some scholars have also read a passage as referring to engineering works that diverted a stream beside the temple site, though this reading depends on difficult text. Where the sources stop, interpretation takes over. The text does not say how large the temple was in 856, whether the whole compound existed, or who worked on it. It also does not give a name to the enemy in terms that all scholars accept.

The identification of that enemy is the most contested question about the inscription, and two separate claims have been merged in popular accounts. The first is epigraphic. The scholar J. G. de Casparis read the word "Bālaputra" in damaged characters of the text, and used this to argue that the stronghold belonged to a Śailendra prince who was defeated at this time. Later epigraphers have criticized that reading, arguing that the characters on the stone do not support it, so the presence of Bālaputra in the Śivagṛha inscription is not secure. The second claim is historical. A lord called Rakai Walaing pu Kumbhayoni is independently attested in other inscriptions, including the Pereng inscription, dated Śaka 784 (863 CE), which lists villages belonging to him, and Sanskrit inscriptions on the Ratu Boko plateau recording the installation of lingas. Some scholars have proposed that he was the opponent described in the Śivagṛha text. That proposal is an interpretation by modern scholars, not a statement in the inscription itself, and it faces a difficulty: if Kumbhayoni was the defeated rival, the Pereng inscription shows him still holding villages seven years later. Either he survived and was accommodated, or the two should not be identified. The chapter treats the matter as unresolved and returns to it in the Historical Debate (Section 32.20).

The text records that the king called Jātiningrat gave up the throne and that Dyah Lokapāla succeeded him. It praises the establishment of a Śaiva sanctuary, describes a war against an enemy who held a stronghold built of stone, and, in some readings, refers to works that changed the course of a stream near the temple.

Analysis: The source presents succession, victory, temple dedication, and landscape engineering as parts of one royal achievement, which suggests how the court understood kingship: as the capacity to protect, to endow the sacred, and to reshape the land. If the reading about the stream is correct, building the sanctuary involved altering the plain and its water, not only raising stone.

Limitations: Damage to the stone and difficulty of the verse produce several competing readings. The text does not establish the size of the compound, the number of workers, or the order of building. It does not securely identify the enemy: the reading of Bālaputra is contested on epigraphic grounds, and the connection with Kumbhayoni, who is attested in other inscriptions, is a modern proposal. The identification of the Śivagṛha with Prambanan's central temple is widely accepted but is an inference from findspot and content.

Perspective: The text speaks for the victorious court. The defeated enemy, the labourers, and the villagers whose fields and water were affected are not heard.

Section 32.5

The Construction of Prambanan

Prambanan is built of andesite, a volcanic stone, shaped into blocks that were fitted together without mortar, with the carved decoration worked on the assembled walls. The plan is concentric. The innermost courtyard holds eight principal buildings: the three major temples, the three smaller temples facing them, and two flanking shrines. Beyond lies a second enclosure with rows of small shrines, and beyond that a larger outer boundary. UNESCO counts about 240 temples and shrines in the complex, most of them small and many surviving only as foundations. The central sanctuary of Śiva, about forty-seven metres tall, is visible across the plain, and its tall, narrow profile with stepped roof stages gives the complex a vertical character that contrasts with the broad, stepped mass of Borobudur.

The sequence of building is not securely documented. Many scholars think that work began under Pikatan, that the Śivagṛha was dedicated in 856 under Lokapāla, and that building and decoration continued under later kings, perhaps including Balitung and Dakṣa. No single inscription states this order, so it is a reconstruction from style, inscriptions, and the logic of such a project. A complex of this scale required surveying and planning, the preparation of the site, quarrying and shaping of stone, the training and organization of skilled carvers, and years of coordinated work. It is more accurate to speak of building phases under successive patrons than of the work of a single ruler, and the 856 dedication should not be treated as the completion of the whole.

The monument also had a long life after the ninth century. Earthquakes have damaged it repeatedly, most recently in 2006, and after centuries of neglect many blocks lay scattered. Restoration since the early twentieth century has rebuilt parts of the complex from recovered stones, and several of the central buildings were reconstructed on the basis of what survived. Visitors thus see a monument that is part ancient and part modern reconstruction, and archaeologists distinguish original stone from restored parts. The folk name Loro Jonggrang, "slender maiden," belongs to a later legend linked to the Durgā image in the north cella of the Śiva temple and to the Ratu Boko plateau. The legend is a record of how later Javanese communities remembered the ruins, not of why the temple was built.

Section 32.6

The Temples of Śiva, Viṣṇu, and Brahmā

The central and tallest temple is dedicated to Śiva. Its principal chamber held an image of Śiva as Mahādeva, and three additional chambers contain images identified as Agastya, Gaṇeśa, and Durgā in her form as slayer of the buffalo demon. The presence of Agastya, a sage prominent in Javanese Śaiva tradition, and of Durgā shows that the sanctuary drew on a wider Śaiva pantheon than the three great gods alone. To the north stands the temple of Viṣṇu and to the south the temple of Brahmā, both smaller than Śiva's. Facing them are three small temples associated with the animal mounts, or vāhana, of the gods: the bull Nandi for Śiva, the eagle Garuḍa for Viṣṇu, and the goose Haṃsa for Brahmā. Only the Nandi shrine retains its image in good condition.

The arrangement presents a trio of major gods, but the architecture gives Śiva unmistakable primacy through height, central position, and the chambers of attendant deities. Whether the plan expresses a formal doctrine of divine functions or a hierarchy within Śaiva worship is a question of interpretation. The temples are decorated with guardian figures, celestial couples, lions in niches, and wish-fulfilling trees flanked by animals and mythical beings. These belong to a widely shared South and Southeast Asian vocabulary, but they are carved in a recognizably Javanese style, with proportions and details that differ from Indian prototypes. Historians use Prambanan as evidence for the selection and transformation of Sanskritic forms discussed in Chapter 19, not for their simple import.

Each building was also a ritual space. Interiors were small and dark, entered through a single doorway, and intended for the installation of an image and for the performance of rites by priests, not for congregational assembly. The surrounding courtyards were the areas in which the larger community could gather, process around the sanctuaries, and witness ceremonies. Reading the complex as a sacred landscape, with graded zones of increasing sanctity toward the centre, helps explain why a royal patron would invest so heavily in it: it was a setting for dedicated acts of merit and for the display of royal piety before an assembled public.

Section 32.7

Śaiva Kingship, Priests, and Sacred Authority

Śaivism, the worship of Śiva as the supreme deity, was supported by Central Javanese rulers through the building of shrines, the endowment of ritual, and the appointment of religious specialists. Inscriptions mention priests and ritual experts who officiated at temple dedications and at the consecration of tax-privileged territories, and the presence of such specialists in witness lists shows that religious authority was part of the machinery of government. Sanskrit and Old Javanese religious texts survive, some of them only in later manuscripts, and they describe doctrine and ritual, but it is difficult to determine which texts circulated in the ninth century and who read them. The earlier Dinoyo inscription of 760, from East Java, shows that Agastya was already a focus of Śaiva devotion, and his image at Prambanan fits a long tradition.

Scholars debate how directly kings were identified with deities. Some inscriptions describe rulers in language that compares them to gods, and Wanua Tengah III describes Balitung as an incarnation of Rudra, a form of Śiva, while other documents give him titles built on Viṣṇu's epithets. This shows that royal legitimacy was expressed through the language of divine kingship, and that the king's relation to the gods was claimed in several registers. It does not show that every subject understood kingship in these terms, or that the king was worshipped as a god in daily life. Village communities almost certainly kept their own cults of ancestors, spirits, and places, which inscriptions mention only indirectly in curse formulas and ritual provisions. Court Śaivism and village religion overlapped without being identical.

Religious institutions were also economic and social ones. Temples held lands or revenues through sīma grants, employed or supported priests, craftsmen, and servants, and hosted festivals that drew people from surrounding villages. A priesthood supported by royal endowment had an interest in the continuation of the king's rule, and a king depended on priests to perform the rites that made his rule legitimate. For this reason, the history of Śaiva patronage cannot be separated from the history of land, revenue, and political alliance, which the following sections examine.

Section 32.8

The Rāmāyaṇa, Sculpture, and Court Culture

Among Prambanan's best-known features are narrative reliefs carved along the inner balustrades of the temples. The Rāmāyaṇa story begins on the Śiva temple and continues on the Brahmā temple, and it is arranged to be followed in clockwise circumambulation, the ritual movement known as pradakṣiṇa, in which the worshipper keeps the sanctuary on the right. The Viṣṇu temple carries a separate series of reliefs depicting the life of Kṛṣṇa. The reliefs thus tie narrative to ritual movement: to follow the story, the visitor performed an act of worship. The scenes include the abduction of Sītā, the alliance with the monkey Hanuman and his allies, and the war in Laṅkā, and they are rendered with a clarity that allows a viewer who knew the story to recognize each episode.

Old Javanese literature includes a Kakawin Rāmāyaṇa, a long poem adapted from Sanskrit models, that is traditionally associated with the Central Javanese period, and some scholars have argued for a date in the mid-ninth century and a connection with the court of the Śivagṛha. The relation between the poem and the reliefs is debated. The reliefs sometimes parallel the poem's episodes, but differences in selection and emphasis leave it unclear whether the sculptors followed the poem, a related textual or oral tradition, or both. It is also tempting to read the story as a political allegory about righteous kingship and the defeat of a rival, and the coincidence with the conflict described in the 856 text has encouraged such readings. No source states that the builders intended particular parallels, so these interpretations should be treated as suggestions, not as documented meanings.

The reliefs also illustrate court culture. They require skilled designers who could plan a continuous narrative over many panels, carvers who worked in a common style, and an audience educated to recognize the stories. The presence of a verse inscription and of a major poem in Old Javanese points to a courtly literary culture, supported by patronage, in which Sanskrit-derived forms were adapted to the Javanese language. This is an example, discussed more generally in Chapter 19, of localization: the stories and the forms came from South Asia, but their execution, language, and setting were Javanese.

Section 32.9

Buddhist and Śaiva Traditions in the Prambanan Landscape

Less than a kilometre north of Prambanan lies Sewu, a large Buddhist complex with a central sanctuary surrounded by hundreds of smaller shrines, and a short distance to the east stands Plaosan, a Buddhist complex whose inscriptions mention royal patrons. Kalasan and Sari were earlier Buddhist foundations on the same plain. The Ratu Boko plateau held a complex whose history extends from Buddhist use in the late eighth century to the Śaiva lingas and fortification connected with the events of the 850s and 860s. Borobudur, examined in Chapter 31, remained a major sacred site on the Kedu plain. The Prambanan plain was therefore a concentrated zone of Buddhist and Śaiva monuments, many built within a few generations of each other and visible to one another across a short distance.

This density makes any narrative of the replacement of Buddhism by Śaivism difficult to sustain. The same elite families appear to have supported both traditions, and Buddhist building continued when Prambanan rose. At the same time, Prambanan's scale and the circumstances of its dedication suggest a deliberate royal emphasis on Śaiva patronage at this moment, and rivalry for prestige among patrons is possible. The most defensible reading is coexistence with shifting royal emphasis, in which different courtly factions and kings gave greater weight to one tradition or another without suppressing the other. Later East Javanese charters show a king, Siṇḍok, with ties to both Śaiva foundations and Buddhist literature (Section 32.21), which supports this view and indicates continuity beyond the move.

The coexistence was also practical. The Śaiva and Buddhist temples drew on the same craft traditions, similar building techniques, and probably the same pool of skilled workers, and the villages that supplied them lived side by side. For ordinary villagers, the question that mattered was often which institution held the rights to a village's revenue and labour, a question more tied to land and obligation than to doctrine. That fact brings the discussion back to the economic base of monumental construction.

Section 32.10

The Economics of Monumental Construction

Building Prambanan required quarrying and moving large quantities of stone, cutting and fitting blocks, carving reliefs and images, and feeding, housing, and directing the people who did this. No ninth-century document gives labour counts, wages, or stone volumes, so any numerical estimate would be speculation. Volcanic landscapes supply andesite in river beds and outcrops, and Central Javanese temples were built of local volcanic stone, but the specific sources for Prambanan's blocks and the routes by which they were carried are questions for specialized study. Inscriptions do show the mechanisms available to the court: officials could assign villages obligations to supply labour or goods, kings could free communities from taxes in return for service to a shrine, and revenue from tax-privileged lands (Section 32.13) could be directed to temples for maintenance.

Skilled workers formed a distinct category. Stone carvers and masons, metalworkers who made tools and ritual implements, carpenters who built scaffolding and the wooden roofs of auxiliary buildings, and bronze workers who cast images were specialists whose skills could not be quickly acquired. Some were probably attached to the court or to religious foundations, while others worked for hire or under obligation. Large projects also provided continuing employment for such workers across generations, and the similarity of carving style at Prambanan, Plaosan, and other sites suggests shared workshop traditions. The monument is a record of an organized and durable skilled workforce, as well as of royal will.

Monumental building was a form of redistribution and display. Feeding workers, endowing priests, and holding dedication ceremonies required agricultural surplus, and the king's ability to deliver all of it demonstrated his authority. The costs, however, fell unevenly. Households who supplied labour or produce during construction seasons lost time from their own fields, and a project that altered watercourses could affect irrigation. There is no direct evidence of resentment at Prambanan, but the inscriptions of the period record resistance to royal power in other contexts, and historians infer that large projects strained resources even when they enhanced prestige. The sources cannot measure the strain, and students should avoid both romanticizing monumental building as unqualified achievement and assuming that it was merely oppressive.

Section 32.11

Rakai Kayuwangi Dyah Lokapāla and Royal Consolidation

Lokapāla's accession is dated by Wanua Tengah III to 855, and his reign is usually reckoned to have lasted roughly three decades, to about 885, though the later end is less firmly fixed than the beginning. He is known by his birth name Lokapāla and by the title Rakai Kayuwangi, which marks him as lord of the district of Kayuwangi before he held the throne. This pairing illustrates a general feature of Mataram's politics: a king came to the throne from a position as a territorial lord, and he carried the name of his base into the royal title. During his reign he issued numerous charters on stone and metal, and the Śivagṛha inscription of 856 stands at the beginning of this series as the monument of his accession.

The evidence suggests that consolidation was neither automatic nor uncontested. The war recorded in the Śivagṛha text shows that the early years included armed conflict with a rival who held a fortified position. Another inscription, associated with the site of Wuatan Tija and read by some scholars as recording an attempted abduction of one of the king's wives, has been taken as a sign of continuing resistance to the king's power, although the interpretation of such an episode depends on a fragmentary text. The Pereng inscription of 863 shows a regional lord, Kumbhayoni, holding villages in the Prambanan area, which implies that the king's authority coexisted with powerful local landholders who were not simply dispossessed. Kingship in this period was a negotiated relationship with such lords, and consolidation meant managing, rewarding, and sometimes defeating them.

Beyond conflict, the evidence points to continuity in court institutions. Charters from Lokapāla's reign show the same pattern of officials, tax privileges, and temple endowments seen in earlier decades, which suggests that the administrative apparatus remained largely stable across changes of reign. After him the sequence becomes less clear. Wanua Tengah III is usually read as naming additional short-reigned rulers between Lokapāla and Balitung, among them Dyah Tagwas, who do not appear in the Mantyasih list, and the reign of Rakai Watuhumalang is known mainly from retrospective sources. The thinness of the contemporary record for the late ninth century is itself a historical fact, and it cautions against assuming either rapid turmoil or smooth stability. What can be said is that when Balitung came to the throne in 898 he did so within a system whose forms were firmly established.

Section 32.12

The Political Institutions of Mataram

At the top of the political order stood the king, styled with titles such as śrī mahārāja and with a regnal name that was usually Sanskritic, in addition to his birth name and his rakai title. Beneath him was an aristocracy marked by titles. Lords of localities bore the title rakai, a word combined with a place name, so that "Rakai Kayuwangi" means the lord of Kayuwangi. Some of these lords rose to the highest offices, and kings themselves bore a rakai title before accession, which indicates that royal power was built from landed bases. A smaller group of high officials bore the title rakryan and sat in the court's inner circle. The leading ministerial office was rakryan mahāmantri i hino, usually understood as a chief minister, and this office was often held by the king's designated successor or closest associate. In Balitung's reign, for example, Dakṣa held this position and was recognized in a way that suggests near parity with the king, and he later became king himself.

Historical Vocabulary
Rakai and Rakryan

Definition. Rakai is a title of a lord associated with a named locality. Rakryan is a title borne by high court officials and members of the royal council.

Historical context. Both titles appear in Central Javanese inscriptions and continue, with changes, in East Javanese charters.

Why it matters. They show that royal power rested on a hierarchy of landed lords and court ministers, not on a purely bureaucratic structure, and that offices could lead to kingship.

Potential misunderstanding. Modern readers may translate these as "duke" or "minister," but the exact duties, and the relation between title and office, varied and are not fully understood.

Below the court, administration worked through territorial and village structures. Inscriptions mention settlements, called wanua, headed by local leaders, and groups of elders who took part in legal and ceremonial acts. Larger districts under officials are also mentioned, and the exact hierarchy among them is difficult to reconstruct. Royal orders were transmitted through officials who travelled to the locality, assembled the village leaders and neighbouring communities, and recorded the transaction in the presence of witnesses. A distinctive feature of the charters is the careful listing of those present, often in a fixed order of rank, which shows that legitimacy depended on the participation of neighbouring communities and officials and that the order of precedence was itself a political statement. Judicial matters were also handled through officials, and some charters resolve disputes about debts or boundaries.

The system can be described as a layered arrangement in which the king's authority was mediated by landed lords, court ministers, and local elders, and in which local negotiation shaped how orders were carried out. It had strengths: it allowed a court to rule a populous region without a large standing bureaucracy, and it tied elite families to the throne through office and marriage. It also had weaknesses. Offices could become bases for rivals, as the succession struggles at the end of Balitung's reign and after it suggest, and a king who lost the support of key lords had few independent means of enforcement. This structural feature helps explain both the periodic contests for the throne and the capacity of the system to relocate, since a court's strength lay in its relationships with lords and communities more than in a fixed physical centre.

Section 32.13

Land Grants, Sīma, Taxation, and Royal Revenue

Inscriptions suggest that royal revenue came from several sources: a share of agricultural produce, labour obligations, and levies on crafts, markets, and services. Charters frequently list categories of tax and obligation, and they mention officials responsible for collecting revenue and for supervising markets. A central mechanism of governance was the grant of sīma status, which exempted a defined territory from specified royal taxes and obligations and redirected its revenues to a designated purpose, most often the maintenance of a temple or shrine. A sīma was not a gift of land in the modern sense of ownership but a transfer of rights over the revenue, labour, and sometimes jurisdiction of a locality. Kings used sīma to endow religious establishments, to reward officials or lords, and to secure the loyalty of communities.

Historical Vocabulary
Sīma

Definition. A tax-privileged territory or foundation created by royal charter, whose revenues and obligations were redirected from the crown to a specified beneficiary, often a religious establishment.

Historical context. Sīma charters form a large portion of the surviving Javanese inscriptions of the ninth and tenth centuries.

Why it matters. They show how courts, temples, and villages were bound in fiscal and ritual relationships, and they preserve the names of officials, villages, and tax categories.

Potential misunderstanding. The term does not simply mean "tax-free land." Obligations and benefits were redirected, and villagers still owed duties, now to a different recipient.

Establishing a sīma typically involved a ceremony in which royal officials, local elders, and witnesses assembled, the privileges were announced, gifts were distributed to those present, and curses were invoked against anyone who violated the grant. The gifts, often of cloth and small amounts of precious metal, and the public setting helped secure local acceptance, and the ceremony gave the transaction religious as well as legal force. Because the ceremony and the written record created a shared understanding, sīma charters can be read as instruments of political negotiation as well as of fiscal policy. They reveal limits on royal power: the court needed the cooperation of local communities and could not simply order changes unilaterally.

Charters also show the use of grants in response to specific circumstances. The Poh inscription of 905 freed a village from tax in return for caring for a funerary shrine, and the Kubu-Kubu inscription of the same year rewarded two lords with a village for conquering a district at royal instruction. The Rukam inscription of 907 established sīma status for Rukam in favour of the king's grandmother, Rakryān Sañjīwana, in connection with volcanic destruction and obligations to a religious establishment at Limwung (Section 32.14). These examples show that sīma and related grants were flexible tools for reward, service, and relief. They also show the tension built into the system: each grant redirected revenue from the crown, so that generosity to temples and elites reduced the resources available for other purposes, and a ruler had to balance the loyalty gained against the revenue lost.

Section 32.14

Rice Agriculture, Irrigation, and Environmental Management

The economy rested on rice cultivation. Inscriptions distinguish irrigated fields, sawah, from dry fields, and they mention water-control works such as weirs and channels. Volcanic soils, regular rainfall tied to the monsoon systems described in Chapter 4, and abundant river water made intensive cultivation possible in favoured locations, though the evidence for how often fields were cropped in the ninth century is limited. Buffalo were important for ploughing, and fruit trees, legumes, vegetables, and other crops complemented rice. Cultivation was not uniform across the region: irrigated lowlands, dry fields on higher ground, and plots at the forest edge coexisted, and each had its own rhythm of labour and risk. The comparison with the Angkorian hydraulic landscape examined in Chapter 35 is instructive, since Java's rivers and rainfall allowed many communities to manage water at village scale without a single centrally built system, a pattern that shaped how the court related to cultivators.

The same volcanic environment that enriched the soil carried hazards. Eruptions of Merapi deposit ash that can fertilize fields over time but can also bury them, and flows of volcanic debris, called lahars, can change river courses and destroy channels. Earthquakes damage buildings and fields. A contemporary inscription of 907, the Rukam charter, records a royal order establishing sīma status for Rukam in favour of Rakryān Sañjīwana, the king's grandmother, in connection with volcanic destruction and with the upkeep of a religious establishment at Limwung. Scholars differ over whether Rukam itself or another settlement was the place destroyed, and the text does not name the volcano, so it should not be assumed to be Merapi. Even so, the charter is important evidence in two ways. It indicates that a volcanic disaster affected people in the Central Javanese kingdom, and it shows that the court dealt with its consequences through the existing fiscal and religious machinery, not through any recorded relocation. Communities repaired canals and replanted after disturbances, which required labour and organization.

For these reasons, an agrarian state's prosperity was tied to its capacity to maintain water systems and respond to environmental disruption, a point that bears on the debate over the eastward shift (Section 32.20). But two cautions apply. First, the risks of the volcanic plains were long familiar, and nothing indicates that people regarded them as unmanageable. Second, evidence for the scale of any specific eruption in the 920s has not been established, so the 907 inscription cannot simply be extended to explain a move twenty years later. It indicates that a volcanic event occurred and that the court addressed its consequences by administrative means, which supports a careful rather than a catastrophic reading.

Section 32.15

Village Society, Households, Craftspeople, and Religious Communities

Inscriptions provide fragments of village life. The settlement, wanua, was the basic social unit, led by local headmen and elders who appear as witnesses and as parties to legal acts. Charters list specialized occupations and the tax categories attached to them, including metalworkers, potters, weavers, and other artisans, as well as traders and those who provided services. These lists indicate a society with considerable craft specialization, in which villages were not only agricultural units but also producers of goods for exchange and for the court. Status was marked by titles and ranks, and people could be bound by debt or service obligations, though the sources do not allow a precise account of how extensive such dependence was or how it differed among regions.

Women appear in the inscriptions as members of the royal family, as donors, and as participants in ceremonies. Balitung's grandmother Tammer, for example, takes a place just after the king and before the chief minister in the list of attendees at the Poh grant, while another royal grandmother, Rakryān Sañjīwana, is the beneficiary of the Rukam charter of 907. These cases are from the elite, and they show that senior women could hold property and rank, and could be recognized in public acts. They cannot show how women of farming households experienced their work or their legal position. Women's labour in rice cultivation, food processing, weaving, and market exchange is attested in comparative Southeast Asian evidence but is documented in these sources only indirectly.

Everyday Life and Social History

Fields, Work, and the Social Costs of Royal Patronage

A household in a Central Javanese village probably organized its year around the rice cycle: preparing fields and channels, planting, tending water, and harvesting, with additional work in gardens, with livestock, and in craft production. Households met obligations to the court or to a temple, which might include a share of produce, labour for construction or maintenance, and participation in ceremonies. When a sīma was established, villagers witnessed the ceremony, received small gifts, and learned that their obligations now flowed toward a different recipient. Sometimes this meant a change of patron without a change of burden; sometimes it meant relief from royal demands in exchange for service to a shrine.

Royal and religious building brought benefits and costs. Temples could redistribute resources, host festivals, and support specialists, and a village attached to a temple could gain stability and status. But building projects also demanded labour at times when households needed it for their own fields. Because inscriptions record the court's decisions, not household accounts, historians reconstruct these effects by inference. Students should remember that the monuments that dominate the landscape are evidence of what rulers could organize and extract, as well as of artistic achievement, and that behind each building stood many households whose names are not recorded.

Religious life in the villages extended beyond the court's Śaiva and Buddhist foundations. Shrines, sacred springs, and caityas appear in grants, and the care of a funerary monument for an ancestral figure could be the basis for a tax exemption, as in the Poh grant. Villages supported religious specialists and held ceremonies tied to the agricultural year, and these local practices coexisted with the institutions of the court. The evidence does not allow a full reconstruction, but it indicates that religion was woven into economic and social life through endowments, festivals, and local cults, and that religious institutions were embedded in village networks of obligation.

Section 32.16

Commerce, Transportation, and Mataram's Wider Connections

Although the court's base was agricultural, Mataram was not isolated from trade. Inscriptions refer to markets, to a rotating market schedule linked to the five-day Javanese week, and to traders who owed taxes. Local exchange moved rice, cloth, iron goods, pottery, salt, and other products among villages and between inland and coastal areas. Gold and silver circulated in small units, and charters record gifts and payments of precious metal, which indicates a degree of monetization alongside payment in kind. Rivers and overland tracks linked the Kedu and Prambanan plains with the northern coast and with East Java, and officials and traders moved along them. The mobility of officials and priests, and the practice of assembling large groups for sīma ceremonies, also imply well-used routes between localities.

Connections beyond Java are documented by imported ceramics and by the shared religious and artistic vocabulary seen in temples. The maritime networks of Srivijaya, discussed in Chapters 28 and 29, linked Java with Sumatra, the Malay Peninsula, India, and China, and Javanese rulers took part in the exchange of goods and ideas. Epigraphic evidence from Ratu Boko and the Abhayagiri inscription of 792, for example, shows that Buddhist communities in Java had links to wider Buddhist networks, and the cosmopolitan character of Śaiva culture is visible in Sanskrit verse and in the figure of Agastya. The extent to which Mataram's court controlled maritime trade, as against benefiting from agricultural exports and local exchange, is uncertain. The evidence shows a kingdom with an agrarian core and growing commercial ties, whose later eastward movement placed the court nearer to an important river and to ports on the north and east coasts, a point developed in Section 32.19.

Section 32.17

Balitung and the Reorganization of Royal Authority

Balitung, who came to the throne in 898 according to Wanua Tengah III and reigned until about 910, is among the best-documented kings of the period. He was lord of Watukura before accession, and he used a range of regnal names, including Śrī Dharmodaya Mahāśambhu, a title with a Śaiva element, as well as others built on epithets of Viṣṇu. Wanua Tengah III describes him as an incarnation of Rudra. The variety of his titles in different documents suggests an effort to present kingship through multiple religious idioms, though his reasons are unclear. His origins are debated: some scholars propose that he gained the throne through a marriage connection or as a figure from outside the main line, and the care taken in his retrospective lists to establish a descent from earlier kings suggests that legitimacy was a concern.

The Mantyasih inscription of 907 lists earlier kings and grants tax-privileged status to Mantyasih in recognition of its role in maintaining a shrine connected with royal ancestors, while Wanua Tengah III of 908 again presents a line of rulers and records the accession dates of some of them. These are the retrospective documents on which much of the dynastic reconstruction rests, and their purpose should be kept in view: they place Balitung within a prestigious past. Other charters show administration in action. The Kubu-Kubu inscription of 905 rewards two lords for conquering a district, the Poh inscription of 905 gives tax privilege for care of a funerary shrine and names senior family members among the attendees, and the Tiga Ron inscription of 900 includes an extended narrative about the king noticing the poor state of a temple's fountain. Such texts illustrate a court actively reorganizing obligations, rewarding service, and using religious endowment to bind local communities.

Balitung's chief minister was Dakṣa, who held the office of rakryan mahāmantri i hino and, in 905, is described in an unusual way as the king's companion, which suggests a status approaching parity. The same Dakṣa became king soon after Balitung's reign ended. Some scholars, including the historian Boechari, argue that Dakṣa's accession followed a rebellion, while others consider a more peaceful succession possible, since the sources do not describe the end of the reign. Evidence of campaigns in outlying districts, such as the conquest recorded at Kubu-Kubu, and of political connections toward the east suggests that Balitung's court operated over a wider area than earlier rulers, though the nature of control in these areas is debated. This widening is one of the reasons historians doubt that the later eastward move was a sudden rupture: the kingdom's political world already extended eastward before Siṇḍok.

Section 32.18

Dakṣa, Tulodong, Wawa, and the Early Tenth-Century Court

After Balitung, the inscriptions name three kings in quick succession: Dakṣa, Tulodong, and Wawa. Dating is uncertain. Dakṣa is usually placed from about 910 to about 919, though some reconstructions give him a shorter reign; Tulodong from about 919 to between 924 and 928; and Wawa from about 924 or 928 to 929. Their reigns are shorter and less fully documented than Balitung's. Dakṣa, who emphasized his descent from the Sañjaya line in his titles, is sometimes credited with continuing building work at Prambanan, though this is an inference from his status and the plausibility of continuing patronage, not a documented fact. Tulodong is often described as a son-in-law of Dakṣa, and some scholars regard him as a political outsider who was not recorded among the officials of his predecessor.

Wawa's rise is also unclear. Some scholars read the evidence as indicating that Wawa overthrew Tulodong, while others note that the sources do not describe the circumstances. What matters for the chapter's argument is the pattern. Several short reigns followed one another, the surviving charters of the period are fewer, and some come from locations farther east than the older core, which indicates a shifting geography of royal activity. Rapid turnover is consistent with political difficulty, but short reigns alone do not prove crisis, and the limited documentation may reflect the accidents of survival. Students should therefore treat "instability" as a plausible description of the period, not as a demonstrated cause of the eastward move.

Mpu Siṇḍok appears in this period as a senior official. According to the usual reading of the inscriptions, he held the office of rakryan mapatih i halu under Tulodong and that of rakryan mahāmantri i hino under Wawa, an office that, as Balitung's reign shows, could lead to the throne. Some scholars argue from the evidence that Siṇḍok married a daughter of Wawa, which would give him a dynastic route to kingship, though this too is inferential. How Wawa's reign ended, and whether through death, defeat, or abdication, is not securely established. The key point is that the move east was not carried out by an outside conqueror but by a leading figure from within the court's own office-holding elite, which supports arguments for institutional continuity.

Section 32.19

East Java before the Royal Shift

East Java was not a frontier waiting to be settled. The Brantas River, which rises in the highlands and flows through a series of basins to the Madura Strait, supported dense agriculture and offered a route to the coast. The Dinoyo inscription of 760 (Śaka 682), found at Malang, concerns a ruler named Gajayana and the dedication of a foundation connected with Agastya, which shows that organized courts and Śaiva patronage existed in the east well before Siṇḍok. Archaeological and epigraphic traces of settlements, shrines, and trade link the Brantas region to Central Java and to coastal ports. The region should not be imagined as a blank space onto which a Central Javanese court moved, but as a developed zone with its own communities, lords, and religious sites.

The connections with the Mataram political world were also longstanding. Balitung's reign shows military and administrative activity extending eastward, and the careers of some officials suggest movement between regions. The Brantas valley offered strategic advantages: access to wet-rice land, a river corridor to the sea, and proximity to ports that traded with the wider archipelago, including the spice-producing islands to the east. Mount Penanggungan and the neighbouring highlands became sites of religious foundations, and the Cunggrang inscription of 929 concerns a village on that mountain's slopes, which indicates that Siṇḍok's authority and patronage quickly reached into the region. These advantages do not prove why the court moved, but they show that the destination was a developed, connected region. The move is better understood as relocation within a political world that already included both areas.

Section 32.20

Why Did the Centers of Power Move East?

Historians have proposed several explanations, and each has strengths and weaknesses. An environmental explanation points to volcanic activity. Merapi is among the most active volcanoes in Indonesia, and geological research has identified eruptive episodes in its history, leading some scholars to suggest that a major eruption forced the court to relocate. The 907 Rukam inscription indicates that a volcanic disaster affected the kingdom, but it also shows the court responding within the existing system, and it does not identify the volcano. The difficulty is that the dating of specific eruptions is imprecise, that proposals for catastrophic events are not securely tied to the 920s, and that no contemporary inscription records a disaster as the reason for moving. The kingdom had lived with this volcano for centuries. A hazard that damaged particular districts could have contributed to relocation, but it has not been shown to be the cause.

A political explanation points to dynastic instability, rivalry among lords, or pressure from rival powers. The short reigns after Balitung and the conflicts recorded in the 850s are consistent with political difficulty, and Siṇḍok's rise from chief minister to king suggests a realignment within the elite. Some modern writers have also suggested pressure from Sumatra-based Srivijaya, but no contemporary source documents an invasion or decisive defeat of the court around 929, and later traditions about the fall of East Javanese courts concern events beyond this chapter. An economic explanation suggests that the Brantas basin and its ports offered better access to expanding maritime trade, including the spice trade of the eastern archipelago, and that rulers moved toward commercial opportunity. This idea fits the later importance of East Java in trade but is hard to prove for the 920s. Combinations of factors are possible, and the explanations are not mutually exclusive.

A further consideration is what the documents say about the move. The Turyyan inscription of 929 attests Siṇḍok's royal seat at Tamwlang, and later charters show a court at Watugaluh, with the Anjukladang inscription of 937 and others recording its activity. That the court had relocated is established by comparing these records with the earlier Central Javanese evidence, not by any statement in Turyyan itself. The charters present the court as a continuing kingdom and do not explain its location in terms of disaster or defeat. Some scholars take this as evidence that the move was not conquest-driven; others caution that a court's own charters would be unlikely to record a forced retreat. The same evidence cannot settle the question, and methodologically the debate illustrates a common problem: the move is clearly documented, but its cause is not. Where the evidence is silent, historians must distinguish a plausible hypothesis from a demonstrated explanation, and should avoid dramatic single-cause stories that are attractive for narrative reasons.

Historical Debate

Why Did Mataram's Principal Courts Shift toward East Java?

The question: What caused the transfer of the principal royal courts from Central to East Java around 929?

Environmental interpretations: Volcanic eruptions, lahars, or associated disruptions of agriculture and water systems forced relocation, or at least encouraged it.
Political interpretations: Dynastic competition, the rise of Siṇḍok from within the elite, or external pressure shaped the move.
Economic interpretations: The Brantas basin offered better opportunities for agriculture and commerce, attracting the court.

Evidence: Inscriptions document the move and the continuity of titles and institutions. The 907 Rukam charter documents a volcanic disaster, left unnamed, and a royal response. Geological studies provide eruption histories, though with dating uncertainty. Archaeology documents settlement and temple building in both regions.

Points of disagreement: Disagreement concerns causation and chronology, in particular whether specific disasters occurred in the right window and whether political instability was a cause or a consequence. It also concerns how far a court's own charters can be trusted to reveal the reasons for a move.

A related debate: The identification of the enemy in the Śivagṛha text (Section 32.4) is itself disputed, and with it the question whether the 850s conflict reflects dynastic rivalry between Śailendra-associated and Sañjaya-associated families or a contest among regional lords. The answer affects how much political instability can be traced back before the tenth century.

Why it matters: Environmental explanations shift attention to hazard and vulnerability, political explanations to elite competition, and economic explanations to trade. Each implies a different picture of the kingdom's strength.

Student task: Identify what kind of new evidence, such as dated volcanic deposits, further inscriptions, or excavated settlements, would most clearly distinguish among these explanations, and explain why.

Cause and Effect

Multiple Factors behind the Eastward Shift

Long-term causes

Political connections to the east, the economic potential of the Brantas basin, long exposure of the Central Javanese plains to volcanic hazard, and an elite structure in which lords held local bases and courts were movable.

Short-term causes

Possible instability after Balitung, a rapid succession of rulers, and the rise of an official, Siṇḍok, to the throne.

Event or transformation

The court is attested at Tamwlang in East Java in 929 and later at Watugaluh.

Effects

Continuation of offices, titles, and sīma practice in a new region; a political and commercial base closer to the eastern ports.

Historical complexity: No single factor is demonstrated. The move probably reflected a combination of political, environmental, and economic considerations, and the relative weights are unknown.

Section 32.21

Mpu Siṇḍok and the Emergence of East Javanese Courts

The earliest datable charters of Mpu Siṇḍok, including the Turyyan inscription of 929 (Śaka 851), come from East Java. He took the royal name Śrī Īśānavikrama Dharmmotuṅgadewa and ruled, according to the usual reconstruction, until about 947 or 948. More than twenty dated inscriptions of his reign have been found across East Java, which is a substantial documentary base for a reign of less than two decades. His charters locate his court first at Tamwlang and later at Watugaluh. Scholars identify these places in the Brantas region, often in the Jombang area, though some have argued that Tamwlang lay nearer Malang, where the Turyyan inscription was found, so precise identification remains uncertain. The kingdom is still called Medang in the inscriptions, which suggests that contemporaries saw continuity with the earlier realm.

The continuities are substantial. The charters follow the same form as Central Javanese ones, with sīma grants, ceremonies, lists of officials, and curse formulas. The offices of rakryan mahāmantri i hino and rakryan mapatih i halu continue, and the pattern of tax privileges and temple endowments remains. The inscriptions record grants to communities for building and maintaining shrines and water works, and for supporting local religious establishments, which indicates that the court worked through the same fiscal and religious mechanisms in the new setting. Titles such as rakai remain in use. The Śaiva patronage of the court is also evident, including at foundations on Mount Penanggungan, and later Old Javanese literary and religious culture continued to develop in the east.

Religious patronage across traditions requires careful statement. The Sang Hyang Kamahāyānikan, a Buddhist text in Old Javanese and Sanskrit, is traditionally linked to Siṇḍok's period, and one version contains a colophon that mentions him. But the dating of the surviving versions and the relationship among the manuscripts are debated, and the association of a textual recension with a king does not by itself show that he commissioned the text or financed its religious community. The text is better taken as evidence of Buddhist intellectual activity in Java in this period, whose connection to royal sponsorship is uncertain. Siṇḍok's Śaiva foundations and grants are documented in inscriptions, while evidence for direct royal Buddhist patronage under him rests on less secure ground. The overall picture is of a court whose religious environment included both traditions, as before.

Changes were also real. The political centre moved to a different environment, with the Brantas river system, a different relation to volcanic landscapes, and closer ties to eastern coasts. Large stone temple building on the scale of Prambanan did not continue in the same form in East Java during this period, though later East Javanese rulers built extensively in other styles and materials. Why this is so is uncertain, and may reflect changes in resources, patronage, or the survival of evidence. Siṇḍok was succeeded by his daughter, Śrī Īśānatunggawijaya, according to later inscriptions, and the eleventh-century Pucangan inscription of Airlangga traces that king's lineage back to Siṇḍok, a retrospective source that shows how the East Javanese line remembered its origin. The later history of those dynasties, and the states of Singhasari and Majapahit, belong to Chapters 48 and 49 and beyond, and are not narrated here.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Central Javanese Mataram to the Early East Javanese Courts

What Changed

  • The location of the principal courts, from the Kedu and Prambanan plains to the Brantas basin
  • The environmental setting and the relation to coastal ports
  • The scale and form of monumental stone temple building

What Stayed the Same

  • The name Medang, the form of charters, and the use of Old Javanese and Sanskrit
  • Titles and offices such as rakai, rakryan mahāmantri i hino, and rakryan mapatih i halu
  • The sīma system and the rice-based economy
  • A religious environment including both Śaiva and Buddhist communities

Pattern: The change was gradual in preparation and abrupt in documentation. Royal and priestly elites experienced it most directly, and village life likely continued largely along earlier lines, though the evidence for villagers is sparse.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Mataram's transition from Central to East Java was a change in political geography and royal authority accompanied by substantial continuities in institutions, religious patronage, economic relationships, and village society. Prambanan, the most visible monument of the period, and the move east, the most debated event, belong to one connected story of an agrarian kingdom and its governance.

The chapter first established the evidence. Inscriptions provide contemporary documents but come from royal and religious institutions, and retrospective lists issued under Balitung present a curated royal past. Archaeology reveals temples more readily than villages. Later chronicles and folklore, including the story of Loro Jonggrang, are not evidence for the ninth century. These limits shaped every conclusion.

Prambanan was built on a plain already rich in Buddhist and royal foundations. Construction probably began under Pikatan, the Śivagṛha was dedicated under Dyah Lokapāla in 856, and later rulers may have expanded the complex. The metrical Old Javanese inscription of 856 joins succession, temple dedication, landscape engineering, and victory over an enemy in a stone stronghold, but its damaged text leaves the enemy's identity open: the reading of Bālaputra is challenged, and the proposed identification with Kumbhayoni, who is attested in other inscriptions including one of 863, is a modern interpretation.

The temple's architecture gives primacy to Śiva while including shrines to Viṣṇu and Brahmā and images of Agastya, Gaṇeśa, and Durgā. Its reliefs of the Rāmāyaṇa and Kṛṣṇa stories show Javanese artists working with widely shared narratives in a local style. These features illustrate selection and transformation of Sanskritic traditions, not simple importation.

The coexistence of Prambanan with Sewu, Plaosan, Kalasan, and Borobudur undermines narratives of religious replacement and of a Hindu dynasty overcoming a Buddhist one. Marriage alliances, shared elite patronage, and continued Buddhist building suggest coexistence with shifting royal emphasis.

Mataram's government combined a king, a landed aristocracy bearing rakai titles, ministers bearing rakryan titles, and village leaders and elders. The sīma system redirected revenues and obligations to temples and beneficiaries through ceremonies that required local participation. Agricultural production rested on irrigated and dry fields, managed in a volcanic environment that both enriched and endangered the plains. Villagers, craftspeople, and workers bore much of the cost of royal and religious projects, though they left few direct records.

Under Balitung, charters and retrospective inscriptions reveal efforts to organize administration and legitimate rule, and indications of political reach toward the east. The reigns of Dakṣa, Tulodong, and Wawa are thinly documented, and the rise of Mpu Siṇḍok from the office of chief minister to the throne shows that the eastward transfer was carried out within the court's own elite.

East Java was already a developed and connected region, as the Dinoyo inscription of 760 and the resources of the Brantas basin indicate. Proposed explanations for the move, volcanic, political, and economic, are all plausible in part and none is demonstrated. Siṇḍok's charters, beginning in 929, show continuity of titles, offices, sīma practice, and religious patronage, together with real changes of location, environment, and building practice.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows how much historians can learn from a kingdom that left few narratives. A monument, a handful of inscriptions, and the traces of an administrative system allow a careful reconstruction of how a court organized religion, land, and labour, while the same evidence also shows how much remains unknown. The lesson is not only about Java. It illustrates how to hold a confident account of what is documented beside honest uncertainty about what is inferred.

The chapter also shows why dramatic explanations deserve caution. The image of a volcano driving a civilization from its homeland is memorable, but the sources document a move, a continuity of institutions, and a set of possible reasons. By emphasizing continuity of offices, charters, and patronage, the chapter treats the eastward shift as a transformation within a political tradition and not as an end.

Mataram's story also bears on the textbook's larger argument. Javanese rulers, priests, builders, and villagers selected, adapted, and transformed Sanskritic forms in their own landscape, and they sustained Buddhist and Śaiva traditions side by side. The East Javanese courts that Siṇḍok founded drew on this inheritance, and the later states examined in Volume III and beyond, including Singhasari and Majapahit, developed in a region that was already part of a wider Javanese political and commercial world. Understanding that continuity is what allows the next chapters to treat East Java as a historical actor in its own right.
