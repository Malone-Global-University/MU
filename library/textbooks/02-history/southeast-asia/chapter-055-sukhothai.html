Sukhothai

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Sukhothai was a real medieval kingdom, a cluster of connected towns and a network of rulers, monks, farmers, potters, and traders in the lower northern plains of present-day Thailand. It was also, later, a story: a first capital, a golden age, a birthplace of a nation. This chapter studies the kingdom through its sources and then studies the story told about it.

In 1833, according to the traditions preserved in Thai royal scholarship, a Buddhist monk who was also a prince visited the ruins of Sukhothai. He was Mongkut, a son of King Rama II and a future king himself. Among the remains he found a four-sided stone pillar covered in writing and a slab of stone that had apparently served as a seat. He took both to Bangkok. The pillar is now known as Sukhothai Inscription No. 1, or the Ramkhamhaeng inscription. Its text describes a ruler who hangs a bell for those with grievances, who lets traders go about their business without tolls, who invents a script for the Tai language, and who rules a land where there is fish in the water and rice in the fields. It has become one of the most famous documents in Thai history. It is also one of the most disputed.

The pillar introduces the chapter's central problem. The kingdom of Sukhothai, which flourished from the thirteenth century to the early fifteenth, is documented by a handful of stone inscriptions, some archaeological remains of striking beauty, a few foreign references, and chronicles composed long after the events they describe. Each kind of evidence answers some questions and silences others. A royal inscription speaks in the voice of power and says what the ruler wished to be believed. A chronicle written in a later century may preserve genuine memory, or may rearrange the past to suit a new capital. Archaeology can show that a reservoir was dug or a kiln fired, but usually not who ordered it or why. A historian of Sukhothai therefore works like a detective, comparing witnesses who were never in the same room.

Sukhothai did not appear in an empty landscape. The Yom River plain had been settled for a long time before Tai-speaking rulers took power there. Mon-speaking and Khmer-connected communities had built temples, shaped trade routes, and established religious traditions, as earlier chapters on Dvaravati (Chapter 25) and the Khmer world (Chapters 33 through 39) have shown. The Tai-speaking populations whose gradual movements were examined in Chapter 54 took over and transformed an existing political and religious landscape through alliance, marriage, warfare, and adaptation, rather than founding something from nothing. The kingdom they built was a web of principalities (mueang) linked by kinship and obligation, not a centralized territorial state with fixed borders.

In the fourteenth century Sukhothai's rulers also became prominent patrons of Theravada Buddhism, building on the regional transformation described in Chapter 53. King Lithai, who took the royal title Mahādhammarāja, is traditionally credited with a major work on Buddhist cosmology, and an inscription of 1361 records his religious activity. Sukhothai's monasteries drew on Sri Lankan, Mon, and Khmer traditions, and its sculptors developed a distinctive vocabulary of Buddha images. These were achievements of a particular political community at a particular moment, and they connected it to a wide Pali-speaking Buddhist world.

The kingdom's political life was also contested and changeable. Sukhothai's rulers competed and cooperated with Lan Na to the north (Chapter 56), with Mon centers to the west, with Khmer-influenced centers to the south and east, and, increasingly in the later fourteenth century, with the rising kingdom of Ayutthaya (Chapter 57). Its absorption into Ayutthaya's orbit was a long process of pressure, accommodation, intermarriage, and institutional change, not a single conquest. Elites and institutions associated with Sukhothai persisted well after the conventional end date of 1438.

The final reason Sukhothai matters is the way it has been remembered. From the nineteenth century onward, Thai royal scholars, nationalist historians, archaeologists, and educators presented Sukhothai as the first Thai capital and the dawn of a continuous national history. That presentation is a powerful historical tradition and deserves study in its own right. It should not be confused with a description of what thirteenth-century Tai-speaking rulers understood themselves to be doing. This chapter keeps the two apart: the medieval kingdom, examined through its sources, and the later meanings assigned to it.

The chapter proceeds in five divisions. The first examines the evidence, the landscape, and the early political traditions. The second studies Ramkhamhaeng, the inscription that bears his name, and the debate over its authenticity. The third turns to towns, agriculture, commerce, and society. The fourth examines Buddhism, Lithai, literature, and art. The fifth follows the political transformation of the kingdom, its rediscovery, and its historical significance.

Historical Context

By the early thirteenth century the mainland was in political motion. The Khmer empire, whose peak under Jayavarman VII was examined in Chapter 38, had extended its influence, in ways whose exact extent remain debated, across much of the Chao Phraya basin and neighboring valleys, and Khmer-style temples survive in the lower north. In the west, Pagan (Chapters 40 and 41) had built a large Buddhist kingdom in the Irrawaddy basin and would decline in the later thirteenth century. Mon-speaking communities in the Chao Phraya plain and lower Burma maintained Buddhist and commercial traditions, and the Mon port-state of Martaban would become important in the fourteenth century (Chapter 60).

Tai-speaking populations were by this time settled across a wide arc of the northern and central mainland. Chapter 54 examined how they arrived gradually, over centuries, through migration, intermarriage, and assimilation rather than a single invasion, and how Tai-ruled centers such as Chiang Saen and Phayao emerged. Sukhothai belongs to this larger pattern, but should not be treated as its inevitable culmination. Other Tai-ruled polities emerged at the same time, and some, such as Lan Na (Chapter 56), would prove at least as durable.

Religiously, the century saw a gradual strengthening of Theravada Buddhism, with its Pali scriptures, monastic ordination lineages, and links to Sri Lanka (Chapter 53). Rulers who patronized monasteries gained moral prestige as well as practical allies in literacy, ritual, and community organization. The period was also one of intensified overland and maritime exchange. The Yuan dynasty's Mongol campaigns and diplomacy (Chapter 46) reached several mainland states, and Chinese records of the late thirteenth century mention missions from polities to the south.

Students should keep three cautions in mind. First, Sukhothai's early history is poorly documented, and the dates often given for it are reconstructions. Second, "Thai" in the modern national sense did not exist in the thirteenth century; the people of Sukhothai spoke a Tai language and had their own local, religious, and dynastic identities. Third, the later traditions that made Sukhothai a national origin point came from the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and this chapter treats them as part of the history to be explained.

Section 55.1

Reconstructing Sukhothai: Inscriptions, Archaeology, and Historical Memory

Every account of Sukhothai rests on a small and uneven body of evidence. The most important contemporary or near-contemporary sources are stone inscriptions, numbered by modern editors in the order they entered the Siamese royal and later national collections. Inscription No. 1 (the Ramkhamhaeng inscription) and No. 2 (from Wat Si Chum) are the two most discussed, and a series of fourteenth-century inscriptions, among them the texts from Wat Pa Mamuang, record royal donations, religious foundations, and dynastic genealogies. Most surviving Sukhothai-period stones date from the fourteenth century. That matters, because the kingdom's earliest decades, which include its supposed founding, are therefore known mainly from sources written later.

A second body of evidence is archaeological: city walls, moats, reservoirs, brick and laterite temples, Buddha images, kiln sites, and burials. This evidence is physical and datable, though dating is often approximate and depends on stylistic comparison, stratigraphy, and associated ceramics. It reveals patterns of settlement and production that no inscription mentions, but it rarely names rulers or explains political decisions. Third are the chronicles, among them Ayutthayan and northern Tai texts compiled in later centuries, which describe Sukhothai's rulers from the vantage point of rival or successor courts. Some preserve genuine memory, but they also compress, reorder, and sometimes invent. Fourth are foreign records: Chinese dynastic histories mention missions from a polity named Xian, and later Mon, Burmese, and Lan Na traditions refer to Sukhothai-linked figures. Foreign records are valuable for outside chronology, but they use their own names and categories, and linking them to specific Sukhothai rulers is often uncertain.

The modern historiography of Sukhothai has its own history. Early twentieth-century scholars, notably George Coedès, who edited and translated the inscriptions in his Recueil des inscriptions du Siam (1924), and Prince Damrong Rajanubhab, whose work shaped Thai national history, established a narrative of Sukhothai's founding and Ramkhamhaeng's greatness that became standard. From the 1970s, scholars including Michael Vickery re-examined that narrative critically. His 1978 survey of recent Sukhothai historiography showed how difficult it is to convert retrospective, partial, and sometimes conflicting texts into a firm early chronology. Debate over Inscription No. 1, discussed in Section 55.6, intensified after the 1980s.

Two consequences follow. A royal inscription is evidence of what a ruler or his court wished to record and have remembered; it is not a neutral description of reality. Inscription No. 1's claims about prosperity, justice, and conquest are claims. And a retrospective tradition, however venerable, is not automatically history. The commonly cited founding date of about 1238 should be understood as traditional or reconstructed. This chapter flags such dates as it goes.

Section 55.2

The Yom River Basin and the World before Sukhothai

Sukhothai lies in the lower northern part of present-day Thailand, on a broad plain near the Yom River, whose course has shifted over the centuries. The Yom is one of the rivers that feed the Chao Phraya system, and the plain is bounded by hills to the west and north from which seasonal streams descend. The plain is well suited to wet-rice cultivation if water can be stored and directed, a point discussed in Section 55.10. To the north, the Yom valley leads toward Phrae and the upland basins of the Tai-speaking north. To the east, routes lead toward Phitsanulok and the Nan valley, and onward toward the Lao-speaking Mekong world. To the southwest, overland passes lead toward the Mon-influenced coast around Martaban. To the south, the great Chao Phraya plain opens toward the later sites of Suphanburi and Ayutthaya. Sukhothai's location was thus a junction, and its rulers could draw on several directions of exchange.

The region was not empty when Tai-speaking rulers became prominent. Mon-speaking communities, associated in earlier centuries with Dvaravati Buddhism (Chapter 25), left sculpture and settlement evidence across the central plain, and Mon-language influence is detectable in the language of some Sukhothai inscriptions. Khmer-connected centers existed in the lower north. Khmer-style sanctuaries survive at the sites later known as Wat Si Sawai in Sukhothai and Wat Phra Si Rattana Mahathat in Chaliang, an older settlement beside Si Satchanalai. Scholars debate how far Angkorian political authority extended into this region, and whether a Khmer governor ruled Sukhothai at the time the traditional accounts describe. The architectural evidence nonetheless shows that Khmer building traditions, whether imposed or adopted, were present here before Tai-ruled Sukhothai.

The earlier population was probably linguistically and religiously varied. Tai speakers had been settling the region for some time, as Chapter 54 explained, and Mon, Khmer, and Tai communities almost certainly lived side by side, intermarried, traded, and shared religious sites. Sukhothai's rulers took advantage of this mixed environment. The early Tai-ruled center did not displace earlier institutions wholesale; it inherited temples, ritual specialists, and routes, and it reinterpreted them.

For students, the lesson is that "before Sukhothai" does not mean "before civilization." The accounts that emphasize a dramatic Tai seizure of power from a distant Khmer overlord compress a gradual process into a single episode, and archaeology supports the picture of layered continuity rather than sharp rupture.

Environmental conditions also shaped the plain's history. Seasonal rainfall and flooding made water the central variable of agricultural life, and shifting river channels could alter which settlements enjoyed reliable access to it. Disease environments, forest cover in the hills, and the availability of clay, laterite, and fuel also influenced where people settled and what they could build. These conditions created possibilities and constraints, but they did not by themselves determine political outcomes: the same plain could support a small cluster of villages or, under the right combination of leadership, labor, and trade, a walled town of many temples.

Archaeological Study

The Landscape before the Kingdom

Students should consider what each kind of archaeological evidence can and cannot show about the plain before Tai-ruled Sukhothai.

  • Khmer-style temples (such as Wat Si Sawai and Wat Phra Si Rattana Mahathat at Chaliang): show Khmer building traditions and probably Khmer-linked religious patronage. They do not by themselves prove direct Angkorian administration, and some may have been built or modified by later Tai-ruled communities.
  • Older sculpture and religious objects in Mon-associated styles: show Buddhist traditions earlier than Tai rule. Stylistic labels do not prove the ethnic identity of makers or donors.
  • Settlement patterns, moats, and mounds: show earlier occupation and the reuse of sites. Dating is usually approximate and often depends on a few excavated contexts.
  • Language in later inscriptions: Mon features in the language of Inscription No. 2 hint at earlier cultural contact, though linguists continue to debate how to interpret them.

Student questions:

  1. Why might the presence of a Khmer-style temple not prove that Angkor governed the area?
  2. How can Mon features in an inscription's language be evidence of cultural contact without proving that the writer was ethnically Mon?
  3. Why is "layered continuity" a better description of the plain's history than "Tai conquest"?
Section 55.3

The Founding Traditions: Bang Klang Hao, Pha Mueang, and the Wat Si Chum Inscription

The fullest early account of Sukhothai's political beginnings is preserved in Inscription No. 2, found at Wat Si Chum. It is generally dated to the fourteenth century, probably well after the events it narrates. The text is linked to the religious career and memorial self-presentation of the senior monk Mahāthera Si Sattha (Śrīśraddhā); it interweaves religious dedication with remembered political history. The inscription is written in an early Tai language whose Old Mon affinities have been carefully analyzed by Christian Bauer. The precise relationship among the monk, the narrative voice, and the physical act of commissioning the inscription warrants care: none makes the text an eyewitness account of Sukhothai's founding.

According to the traditional account, two rulers cooperated. Bang Klang Hao held a mueang called Bang Yang, and Pha Mueang held another center. They are portrayed as friends and allies who set out against a governor identified as Khom Sabat Khlon Lamphong, associated with Sukhothai and called "Khom," a term often understood to mean Khmer. Bang Klang Hao's forces took Si Satchanalai and then Sukhothai, and the governor withdrew. Pha Mueang is then said to have conferred a royal title, and in the usual reading a sword, on his ally, who becomes Si Inthrathit. The story ends with Si Inthrathit as ruler of Sukhothai.

The account is valuable but cannot be treated as an annal. It was composed generations after the events, in a religious setting, and likely served the dynasty's interest in presenting its founder as legitimately installed. The story's central features, such as an alliance between two rulers, a title conferred by a peer rather than by an overlord, and the displacement of a "Khom" governor, are plausible, but the details of who Khom Sabat Khlon Lamphong was, how Khmer authority was exercised, and when the events occurred are open questions. The conventional date of about 1238 is a modern reconstruction. Some historians, notably Michael Vickery, have argued for a significantly later chronology for the earliest rulers.

The inscription's emphasis on Pha Mueang's role is also telling. The ruler of Sukhothai is presented as one among several peer rulers, with authority depending on recognition by other lords. That picture fits the political world of mueang and alliances better than a story of a single founder creating a kingdom from scratch.

Comparison with other mainland founding stories is instructive. Many early states preserved narratives in which a ruler is installed by an ally, a spirit, or a religious figure, and such stories often serve to explain why one lineage rather than another holds authority. The Sukhothai account belongs to this family. Its value lies less in settling who did what in a particular year than in showing how the dynasty chose to present its origins, and which relationships, with a peer ally, with a displaced governor, and with Buddhist narrators, it considered worth recording for posterity.

Primary Source Window

Wat Si Chum Inscription (Sukhothai Inscription No. 2)

Creator and perspective: a religious inscription associated with Mahāthera Si Sattha (Śrīśraddhā) and his patronage circle · Date: fourteenth century, later than the founding events described · Type: stone inscription combining monastic self-presentation and retrospective political narrative; see Griswold and Prasert (1972).

Context: The inscription was found at Wat Si Chum, one of the monastic sites of the Sukhothai area. It records a remembered account of Bang Klang Hao, Pha Mueang, and the establishment of Si Inthrathit, alongside a religious narrative. Its language has been studied for Mon affinities.

Editorial paraphrase (not a verbatim quotation):

The text describes two allied rulers who move against a governor holding Sukhothai, take the town, and install one of them as its king. The senior ally confers a royal title, and in the usual reading a sword, on his companion, and the new king rules from Sukhothai. The narrative is framed by religious material and by dedications.

Analysis: The inscription preserves a political memory of how Sukhothai's ruling line understood its beginnings: as a negotiated alliance among peer lords against an existing governor.

Limitations: The text was composed after the events, in a religious setting, and does not supply a securely dated chronology. It cannot establish when the events happened, who Khom Sabat Khlon Lamphong was, or how far Khmer authority extended.

Perspective: The perspective is that of a religious narrator writing within the dynasty's circle of patronage. Perspectives of the governor, of Khmer authorities, of local townspeople, and of rival Tai rulers are absent.

Section 55.4

Si Inthrathit, Ban Mueang, and the Consolidation of Royal Authority

Si Inthrathit is the title of the first ruler of the Sukhothai line in the traditional accounts, and Inscription No. 1 names him as the father of Ramkhamhaeng. The traditions do not describe a detailed administration, but they suggest the character of early royal authority. A ruler of Sukhothai was first among several rulers of neighboring mueang. His power depended on his ability to lead in war, protect cultivators and traders, honor Buddhist monks, and keep the loyalty of kin and allies. The Wat Si Chum narrative itself shows a title conferred by a peer and a political order of overlapping obligations.

Sukhothai and Si Satchanalai, later linked by a road, formed a pair. Royal sons and relatives were stationed in related towns, and the arrangement allowed the dynasty to extend influence without building a large bureaucracy. Succession in these early generations appears to have passed laterally between brothers as well as from father to son, which is a common feature of early Tai-ruled polities. Inscription No. 1 reports that Ramkhamhaeng served his older brother Ban Mueang, who ruled after their father, and succeeded him; the text presents this as a harmonious sequence, but succession in such systems often involved competition among royal kin.

At the local level, authority was exercised through the heads of mueang and villages, who owed the ruler loyalty, military service, and probably labor and a share of produce, though sources for early taxation are thin. Communities of cultivators lived in village clusters, and rulers' wealth depended on their control of people and agricultural surplus more than of territory in a map sense. For this reason it is better to speak of a political center whose influence waxed and waned than of a state with fixed borders.

Consolidation also involved religion and ritual. Rulers supported monasteries, honored spirit cults, and used Buddhist and Brahmanical symbols to present themselves as protectors. These practices helped to unify a population of mixed origins and gave rulers allies among literate monks. In this sense the "kingdom" of the early Sukhothai rulers was built as much through patronage and alliance as through conquest.

The limits of the evidence deserve emphasis here. No contemporary inscription describes Si Inthrathit's reign, and what we know of it comes through the memory of his descendants. Historians therefore speak of a dynasty whose founder is a figure of tradition, and they use the surviving texts to reconstruct how later rulers wished to be connected to him. That connection mattered politically, because descent from the founder gave later kings a claim to seniority over rival branches of the family and over neighboring lords.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Earlier Settlements to a Tai-Ruled Center

What changed? The language and dynasty of the ruling elite shifted to Tai speakers; the rulers took royal titles and presented themselves as independent of Khmer-associated authority; Sukhothai became the senior center of a network of related mueang.

What remained continuous? Wet-rice agriculture, village communities, temples and religious specialists, Mon and Khmer cultural influences, and the trade routes of the plain persisted. Buildings were reused and sculptural and architectural traditions continued.

When did change occur? Gradually. The traditional 1238 date compresses a process that probably unfolded over decades and for which firm dates are lacking.

Was change gradual, uneven, abrupt, contested? Mostly gradual and uneven, with episodes of conflict and negotiation.

Who experienced change most directly? Ruling families, local lords, and monastic communities whose patronage shifted. Cultivators likely experienced more continuity in daily work and obligations.

Why does the pattern matter? It guards against reading Sukhothai as a sudden birth of a Tai kingdom and shows that political change often rearranges inherited institutions.

Section 55.5

Ramkhamhaeng and the Expansion of Sukhothai's Political Influence

Ramkhamhaeng, a name usually rendered "Rama the Bold," is traditionally placed in the late thirteenth century, with reign dates often given as roughly 1279 to the late 1290s. These dates are reconstructions. He is known chiefly from Inscription No. 1, which was written, if authentic, in his name or under his patronage, and which presents him as a warrior, a generous ruler, and a patron of Buddhism. Because the text is the main source for his achievements, anything said about him is shaped by its claims.

The inscription's autobiographical section describes his youth. As a young man he is said to have fought beside his father against the ruler of a neighboring mueang, Chot, and to have won a prized elephant. After his father's death he served his elder brother, and upon that brother's death he became ruler. The text then catalogues his generosity: he shares food and valuables, honors his mother, and offers fruit and delicacies to petitioners. It praises the prosperity of the land, with its orchards and rice fields, and describes the monks who dwell in a forest monastery and the great stone seat in a sugar-palm grove where he sits to hear teaching.

The final section of the inscription lists the peoples and places that its ruler claims to have brought under his influence. The list extends in all four directions: to the east toward the Mekong and the Lao centers; to the south along the Chao Phraya plain and onward to the Malay Peninsula and the coast; to the west toward the Mon-influenced coast; and to the north toward the upland Tai-speaking centers. If read as a map of direct administration, it would describe an empire across much of the mainland. Historians have urged more caution. A list of this kind may record tribute relationships, military raids, personal allegiances, or the ruler's aspirations. Evidence from other sources does not confirm direct Sukhothai administration across such a wide area, and the mueang system suggests that most of these places retained their own rulers.

It helps to distinguish three tiers. In the core, Sukhothai and Si Satchanalai were closely tied to the ruling family. In an intermediate zone, rulers of neighboring mueang probably acknowledged Sukhothai's seniority, supplied troops, or sent gifts. In a distant zone, claims may have reflected episodic influence, diplomacy, or religious connection rather than control. Chinese records of missions from Xian in 1282 and later years suggest that polities in the region were in contact with the Yuan court. Whether those missions came from Sukhothai, from other centers, or from a combination remains debated. Ramkhamhaeng's reign is thus best understood as a period of increased prominence and negotiated influence, not as the moment of a vast, administered empire.

Religion and ritual formed part of Ramkhamhaeng's public image. The inscription links the ruler to a stone seat used for teaching and to a forest monastery, and portrays him as a patron who listens to monks. Such passages show that even in a text dominated by warfare and generosity, Buddhist legitimacy was already part of how rulers wished to appear. How much of this reflects the thirteenth century, and how much later elaboration, depends on the unresolved question of the inscription's date, discussed in the next section.

Section 55.6

The Ramkhamhaeng Inscription: Authenticity, Dating, and Interpretation

The inscription's physical history begins in 1833, when the monk-prince Mongkut visited Sukhothai and brought the pillar to Bangkok. Mongkut and later scholars read and interpreted it, and over the following century it became the basis of the standard narrative of early Sukhothai. The text was later edited and translated by a succession of scholars, among them Coedès and, in a detailed English edition and commentary, A. B. Griswold and Prasert na Nagara. In 2003 UNESCO inscribed it in the Memory of the World register. That recognition honors its importance as documentary heritage. It does not certify that every historical statement in the text is correct.

The text's internal chronology is part of the problem. It refers to the creation of a Tai script in 1283 and is conventionally dated to 1292. These dates were calculated from a calendrical reference, in the Saka era, in the text. Scholars have long noted that the inscription's script, spelling, and vocabulary differ from later Sukhothai inscriptions: for example, vowel signs appear in a linear arrangement that differs from the later convention. Some read these differences as evidence of an earlier stage of the script, which would support an early date. Others have read the same differences as signs of an unusual or artificial composition.

The controversy came to a head in the 1980s and 1990s. The art historian Piriya Krairiksh argued, on a combination of stylistic and historical grounds, that the inscription was not a thirteenth-century text but a nineteenth-century composition, and he associated it with the circle of Prince Mongkut. Michael Vickery, who had already criticized the uncritical use of the Sukhothai traditions, raised doubts about the inscription's language, content, and historical context. Other scholars, including David Wyatt and Hans Penth, offered arguments for an early origin or answered the forgery arguments, pointing to linguistic features, comparison with other inscriptions, and difficulties in explaining how a nineteenth-century composer could have produced the text. The scholarly exchange was collected in The Ram Khamhaeng Controversy, edited by James R. Chamberlain (1991).

No single piece of evidence has settled the question, and students should note that the debate is not always a simple choice between authentic and forged. Some scholars hold that the text is authentic and early; others that it is a later composition; and others entertain intermediate possibilities, such as an early text that was recopied or revised. What the debate demonstrates is the importance of method. A text that shaped a national history for more than a century turned out to be less secure than it had seemed, and historians must therefore use it with explicit caution and corroborate its claims with independent evidence.

Historical Debate

How Should Inscription No. 1 Be Dated?

The question: Is Inscription No. 1 a genuine product of the late thirteenth century, a later composition, or something in between? The answer changes how historians use it as evidence for Ramkhamhaeng, early Tai writing, and early Sukhothai kingship.

Interpretation A: an authentic late-thirteenth-century text. Scholars in this tradition, including those who edited the text in the twentieth century and later defenders such as David Wyatt and Hans Penth, argue that its language, script, and content fit an early stage of Tai writing, that comparisons with other inscriptions support an early date, and that forgery theories do not satisfactorily explain how such a text could have been composed in the nineteenth century.
Interpretation B: a later composition. Piriya Krairiksh argued for a nineteenth-century origin, associating the text with Mongkut's circle. Michael Vickery raised doubts about its linguistic and historical consistency and about the traditions built on it. These scholars emphasize that the text is suspiciously aligned with later political and religious ideals and that its discovery circumstances were not independently documented.
Interpretation C: intermediate possibilities. Some scholars have considered that the text may rest on an early original that was later copied, revised, or expanded, so that its authenticity may not be all-or-nothing.

Evidence: The debate draws on script and orthography, vocabulary and grammar, comparison with other inscriptions, art-historical dating, the circumstances of discovery, and the plausibility of the text's historical content. No external evidence independently confirms its specific claims.

Points of disagreement: The disputes concern dating, interpretation of linguistic features, how to weigh the circumstances of discovery, and how to reason about historical plausibility. Terminology and evidence are both contested.

Why it matters: If the text is authentic and early, it is a rare contemporary witness to thirteenth-century Tai kingship and writing. If it is later, much of the standard picture of Ramkhamhaeng must be re-based on other evidence. Either way, UNESCO recognition does not settle the question.

Student task: Summarize the strongest argument for each interpretation in your own words, list one kind of new evidence that could help to decide between them, and explain how you would use the inscription in a history of Sukhothai if the question remains unresolved.

Section 55.7

Royal Government, Justice, and the Ideals of Kingship

Inscription No. 1 offers one of the most celebrated images of kingship in Southeast Asian history. The ruler is accessible to petitioners: a bell hangs at the gate, and any person with a grievance may ring it and be heard. He is generous, he protects property and inheritance, and he does not tax the roads, so that traders can move their goods freely. He is a devout Buddhist who honors monks and listens to their teaching. Later Thai writers described this ideal as paternal rule, with the king as a father to his people, and the title Phokhun, "father lord," associated with early Sukhothai rulers, has encouraged that reading.

This picture should be read as an ideal. The text is a royal self-portrait, and its images of the bell, free trade, and orderly inheritance describe how a good king was supposed to behave. They do not prove that every subject enjoyed equal access to the king, that the bell was rung regularly, or that taxation did not exist. Rulers in the period commonly drew revenue from labor service, shares of agricultural produce, tolls and levies in various forms, and royal trade. The absence of road tolls may reflect a specific policy on certain routes, a boast, or a selective description. Without independent evidence, historians cannot determine how often it operated in practice.

The inscription also reveals the values of the court. A ruler's legitimacy depended on military success, generosity, justice, and Buddhist patronage. Inheritance rules, as reported, favored children as heirs, which suggests that property rights were recognized in law or custom. The emphasis on trade shows that the court regarded commerce as a source of prosperity and prestige, and the presence of foreign and distant goods in archaeological contexts suggests the plausibility of that concern.

Institutions behind these ideals are poorly documented. Officials appear in later inscriptions, and royal kin ruled associated towns, but the details of courts, legal procedure, and fiscal administration are unclear. The safest conclusion is that Sukhothai's rulers articulated a durable ideal of just, accessible kingship, which later generations found attractive and amplified, and that the machinery that produced the ideal's outcomes remains largely invisible.

Comparison helps. Other mainland courts of the period, including Pagan's successors and Lan Na, also presented kings as protectors of religion, petitioners' advocates, and patrons of trade, and Pali Buddhist texts offered a shared model of the just ruler. Sukhothai's ideal was therefore not unique, though its expression in the Tai language and in a famous inscription gave it unusual visibility. Later Thai writers who treated the bell and the toll-free road as defining features of a distinctive national tradition were reading a widely shared ideal through the lens of their own time.

Historical Vocabulary
Phokhun, Dhammarāja, and Royal Authority

Definition. Phokhun is a Tai title usually translated "father lord." It combines a word for father with a word for a lord or chief, and appears in Inscription No. 1 in connection with Ramkhamhaeng. Dhammarāja (Pali; "righteous king" or "king of the dhamma") names a ruler whose legitimacy rests on upholding Buddhist teaching and justice. Lithai took the title Mahādhammarāja, "great righteous king."

Historical context. The two titles reflect different layers of royal self-presentation. Phokhun belongs to the vocabulary of Tai chiefs and lords of mueang, in which a ruler is the senior kinsman and protector of a community. Dhammarāja draws on Pali Buddhist ideas, widely shared across the Theravada world, that link rulers to the moral order.

Why it matters. The shift from Phokhun to Dhammarāja in the sources mirrors a broader movement from personal, kin-based authority toward authority expressed through Buddhist ideals (Section 55.14).

Potential misunderstanding. "Father-lord" has been read as proof of a uniquely benevolent, democratic, or egalitarian Thai kingship. The title shows how rulers wished to be seen. It is not evidence about actual access, justice, or taxation.

Section 55.8

Sukhothai's Relations with Lan Na, Mon Communities, Khmer Centers, and China

Sukhothai's political life unfolded among neighbors. To the north, the Tai-ruled centers that became Lan Na (Chapter 56) were forming around the same time. Later northern chronicles tell of friendly relations among Ramkhamhaeng, Mangrai of Chiang Saen and later Chiang Mai, and Ngam Mueang of Phayao, including stories of an oath of friendship and of Ramkhamhaeng's help in planning a city. These stories carry the weight of later northern and central traditions and cannot be treated as contemporary documentation. They do indicate that later generations remembered the early Tai-ruled centers as allies, and the relations among their rulers probably involved a mix of diplomacy, rivalry, and kinship. In the fourteenth century relations with Lan Na included both cooperation and conflict, over contested border towns along the Yom and its neighbors.

To the west, Mon communities and the port of Martaban formed a gateway to the Bay of Bengal. Later Mon and Burmese chronicles link Sukhothai with the founder of Martaban's kingdom, in stories of connections and even marital ties, but these accounts are difficult to align with other evidence. Mon Buddhist institutions and language influenced Sukhothai's religious life, as the Mon affinities in Inscription No. 2 suggest. Overland routes through the western hills offered access to the coast and to the wider Indian Ocean world.

To the south and east, Khmer-influenced centers, among them Lopburi and others on the central plain, represented older traditions. Angkor itself was in a period of transformation (Chapter 61), and its influence on the western periphery was weakening, though Khmer architecture and ritual continued to affect Tai-ruled courts. Inscription No. 1's list includes places on the southern plain and the Malay Peninsula, including Nakhon Si Thammarat, which was an important center of Buddhist learning with Sri Lankan connections. Suphanburi, on the lower plain, was a power in its own right and would become a key partner of Ayutthaya in the fourteenth century.

China appears in the record as a distant but significant partner. Yuan records note missions from Xian beginning in the early 1280s, and the Yuan court sought to establish tributary relations with many southern states (Chapter 46). A popular tradition holds that Ramkhamhaeng visited China and brought back potters, but the evidence for this tradition is weak, and kiln archaeology points to a later and more complex origin for Sukhothai's ceramic industry (Section 55.11). The broader point is that Sukhothai participated in a network of diplomatic and commercial relationships. The claim that it directly controlled extensive territories throughout mainland Southeast Asia is not supported by independent evidence.

Section 55.9

Sukhothai, Si Satchanalai, and Kamphaeng Phet: The Connected Urban Landscape

UNESCO's World Heritage property inscribed in 1991 groups three towns: Sukhothai, Si Satchanalai, and Kamphaeng Phet. Together they preserve walls, moats, reservoirs, roads, and a dense array of temples. They are often described as a single connected landscape, but the towns had different histories, and their relationships changed between the thirteenth and fifteenth centuries. It is a mistake to assume that they always formed one uniform administrative structure.

Sukhothai itself was a walled town with concentric ramparts and moats, a central religious precinct, and numerous temples inside and outside the walls. At its core stands Wat Mahathat, the largest monastic complex, with a central relic tower and many smaller shrines. Other temples, such as Wat Si Sawai with its Khmer-style towers, Wat Sa Si on an island in a reservoir, and Wat Si Chum with its enormous seated Buddha, lie within or near the walls. Reservoirs inside and outside the town, and a network of channels, supplied water for consumption, ritual, and cultivation. The dating of individual structures varies, and many were built or rebuilt in the fourteenth century and later.

Si Satchanalai, to the north on the Yom, incorporated an older settlement, Chaliang, whose Khmer-style sanctuary suggests a pre-Tai religious history, and a newer walled town with temples such as Wat Chang Lom, a bell-shaped relic tower decorated with elephants. In the narratives of the early dynasty, Si Satchanalai was closely tied to Sukhothai, and members of the royal family ruled it. Later it became an important center of ceramic production. Lithai is said to have ruled there before taking Sukhothai, an episode discussed in Section 55.14.

Kamphaeng Phet, to the southwest on the Ping River, whose name means "diamond wall," seems to have gained prominence in the fourteenth century. Its temples, among them Wat Phra Kaeo and Wat Chang Rob, with its elephant-buttressed tower, and its laterite walls suggest a significant provincial center, and it was a strategic gateway between Sukhothai and the southern plain. Its position on the frontier between Sukhothai and Ayutthaya's sphere gave it military significance in the later fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. The road traditionally associated with the name Phra Ruang linked Sukhothai with neighboring towns, and sections survive. Scholars debate its date and extent.

The towns' later histories also differed. Sukhothai's walls and temples were gradually reduced in importance as political power moved south, while Kamphaeng Phet retained military significance for longer and Si Satchanalai continued as a center of craft production. Some monasteries remained in use after the fifteenth century, which helps explain why the ruins survived in recognizable form. Archaeologists continue to refine the chronology of individual monuments, and students should treat the dates attached to temples in guidebooks as provisional unless supported by inscriptions or excavation.

Section 55.10

Rice Cultivation, Water Management, Villages, and Agricultural Production

Sukhothai's wealth rested on agriculture. The surrounding plains supported wet-rice cultivation, supplemented by orchards, gardens, fishing, and the gathering of forest products. Rainfall in the region is seasonal, concentrated in the monsoon months, and cultivators needed ways to capture water and spread it across fields. Archaeological survey has documented reservoirs within and around the towns, earthen embankments, and channels that diverted streams from the western hills. Local terminology for such reservoirs is traphang. This infrastructure supported settlement, ritual, and agriculture alike, and the reservoir beside a monastery often served both religious and practical purposes.

The dating and administration of these systems remain open questions. Some structures may date to the early Tai-ruled period, others to the fourteenth century or later, and some may be older. Inscription No. 1 describes orchards, rice, and fish in glowing terms, but a royal inscription is not a survey. The two kinds of evidence, the inscription's praise and the archaeological remains, are complementary, not interchangeable. The remains show that substantial water management existed. They do not show that a single king designed it, or that it operated as a unified state system.

Building and maintaining embankments and channels required labor. Rulers probably relied on corvée obligations, on communities' own cooperation, and on religious donors who funded reservoirs as acts of merit. Monasteries held land and dependents and were among the institutions that coordinated work. The seasonal rhythm of rice cultivation, with plowing and transplanting at the onset of rains, harvest at the end, and slack periods in the dry season, shaped when labor could be mobilized for building and war. Villages were probably organized around kinship, shared water, and a local headman or lord, and cultivators owed produce and service to their local rulers.

Upland communities in the hills to the west and north practiced different forms of agriculture, including swidden cultivation, and supplied forest goods. Their relationship to the lowland towns, through trade and tribute, matters for understanding the kingdom's economy, though sources rarely describe them.

Waterworks should not be imagined as one uninterrupted system controlled from a palace. An embankment might protect nearby fields, another might retain water for a monastery, and a third could support a settlement with its own patrons and labor arrangements. Their construction dates, repairs, and changing uses must be established case by case. The same channels could carry floodwater or sediment that eventually altered their function. For this reason, mapping visible reservoirs cannot by itself reveal how much rice Sukhothai produced, whether households possessed secure access to water, or how far royal commands reached into particular villages. These distinctions matter when evaluating claims that monumental irrigation automatically demonstrates a strong centralized state.

Everyday Life and Social History

The Agricultural Communities of Sukhothai

For most inhabitants, daily life revolved around fields, water, and village. A household worked its own plots, planted when the rains began, and shared the labor of repairing embankments and channels with neighbors. Part of the harvest went to local lords, to temples and monasteries, and to household needs. Fishing in ponds, streams, and flooded fields supplemented rice, and gardens and orchards added fruit and betel. Religious life punctuated the agricultural year: households gave food to monks, contributed to merit-making festivals, and honored local spirits alongside Buddhist practice.

We know this life mostly indirectly. Inscriptions record donations and royal ideals, and archaeology shows tools, pottery, and settlement traces, but cultivators themselves left no written voice. Historians must reason from the physical landscape, comparisons with better-documented later villages, and incidental references, while avoiding the temptation to project later Ayutthayan or modern village institutions backward without evidence.

Section 55.11

Ceramics, Crafts, Markets, and Regional Commerce

Sukhothai and Si Satchanalai are famous for their ceramics, and it is tempting to credit a flourishing export industry to the great king of the late thirteenth century. The evidence points to a more gradual development. Kiln archaeology at Si Satchanalai and Sukhothai, together with the study of ceramics recovered from shipwrecks in Southeast Asian waters, indicates that the main phases of stoneware production, including glazed wares with green and gray-green celadon-like finishes and wares decorated in iron-brown under a clear glaze, belong largely to the later fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. Earlier production of simpler wares for local use is likely, but the large-scale export industry developed generations after Ramkhamhaeng.

The tradition that Ramkhamhaeng brought Chinese potters to Sukhothai is not securely documented, and the chronology of kiln development does not fit it well. Technological influence from Chinese ceramic traditions is probable, but the routes and agents are uncertain, and local innovation and borrowing from other regional traditions seem to have mattered as well. Products included bowls, plates, jars, and architectural ceramics for temples, along with small figurines. Some were exported by overland and maritime routes, and cargoes of this ware have been found far from the kilns. Production depended on clay, fuel, and kiln workers, and on markets that included the courts, monasteries, and traders.

Other crafts also flourished: bronze casting for Buddha images, stucco work, stone carving, goldsmithing, weaving, and woodworking. Religious demand was a major driver. Markets in the towns circulated food, cloth, ceramics, metal goods, and forest products such as aromatic woods and resins. Inscription No. 1 speaks of traders moving freely with elephants, horses, and other goods, a picture consistent with overland trade but not independently quantified. Overland caravans connected Sukhothai with the north, the west, and the lower plain, and the western passes allowed contact with Martaban's maritime trade.

Commerce was not automatically a source of broad prosperity. Rulers and monasteries gained from trade directly and indirectly, and profits flowed unevenly. Evidence for the scale and monetization of Sukhothai's economy is limited, and students should resist figures or claims about its volume. What is clear is that the economy combined an agricultural base, specialized craft production that expanded over time, and a regional exchange network with links to both the Bay of Bengal and the South China Sea world.

The interpretation of ceramic evidence illustrates how archaeology can correct tradition. Because shipwreck cargoes and datable kiln deposits can be tied to approximate periods, they provide an independent check on stories that attribute an industry to a famous ruler. They also show that Sukhothai-area wares reached markets across maritime Southeast Asia, which adds a coastal and overseas dimension to a kingdom often described as landlocked. Ceramic production may also have drawn labor and fuel from surrounding villages, linking the craft economy to the agricultural one.

Section 55.12

Households, Women, Labor, Social Hierarchy, and Local Exchange

Sukhothai society was hierarchical. At the top stood the royal family and the lords of mueang. Below them were officials, military leaders, and wealthy donors, followed by the mass of cultivators and craftspeople, and at the bottom, dependents and people held in bondage, whose status could arise from debt, capture, or birth. Inscriptions refer to nobles, commoners, and dependents, but the precise legal categories and their relation to later Ayutthayan institutions are not known. Students should avoid assuming that later systems of rank and labor registration operated unchanged in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries.

Households were the basic units of production and probably combined kin in extended or multigenerational arrangements. Inheritance by children is attested in Inscription No. 1, which suggests that property was held by families and passed on within them. Marriage alliances were important to rulers and lords as tools of diplomacy. Women's roles are visible in glimpses: royal women appear as mothers, patrons, and links in dynastic alliances, and women as donors to monasteries are known from fourteenth-century and later inscriptions. In agricultural households, women almost certainly participated in cultivation, processing, weaving, marketing, and household management, though direct documentation is thin.

Labor obligations ran in several directions. Cultivators and craftspeople owed service and produce to lords and kings. Monasteries received land and dependents through donation. Skilled workers, such as potters, bronze casters, and builders, may have been organized in communities or attached to patrons. Inequality was marked, and the monuments we admire were built by people whose names are not recorded. Local exchange occurred in markets and through direct barter between neighbors, and merchants, including itinerant traders, linked towns to uplands and to distant networks.

Royal inscriptions are poor sources for such lives. They record royal and religious acts, and when they mention ordinary people they do so mainly as recipients of generosity or as donated dependents. Reconstructing everyday society requires combining these glimpses with archaeology and comparative reasoning, and recognizing the limits of what can be said.

Religious institutions provide further glimpses of social structure. Donation records show that land and dependents could be given to monasteries, creating communities whose members owed labor to the temple rather than to a lay lord. Such arrangements gave monasteries economic weight and offered some dependents a degree of protection, though the evidence does not allow confident generalization about their conditions. Debt, warfare, and punishment could also produce bondage. The existence of these categories reminds students that the celebrated monuments and the admired ideal of kingship coexisted with marked inequality and unfree labor.

Section 55.13

Theravada Buddhism, Monastic Communities, and Royal Patronage

Sukhothai's Buddhist institutions were not an isolated local invention. The regional transformation examined in Chapter 53 gave rulers access to a widely shared Pali Buddhist tradition, and Sukhothai participated in the networks through which monks, texts, relics, and ordination lineages circulated. Connections ran to Sri Lanka, regarded as a source of orthodox ordination, often through intermediary centers on the mainland and the peninsula. Nakhon Si Thammarat, which Inscription No. 1 mentions in connection with a monk, was one such intermediary. Mon monasteries, linked to Martaban and to the earlier Mon Buddhist tradition, and the Buddhist legacy of Pagan (Chapter 41) were others. Anne M. Blackburn's study of Buddhist political and religious networks across the Indian Ocean helps place Sukhothai's rulers in this wider "Pali arena."

Within Sukhothai, monasteries took different forms. Inscription No. 1 contrasts a forest monastery, where monks devoted themselves to meditation and study, with monasteries nearer the settlements. Monasteries received donations of land, labor, and goods, housed monks and novices, taught reading and religious texts, and held relics and images. They functioned as centers of literacy, ritual, and community life, and as holders of economic resources. Relics, such as those enshrined in great towers at Wat Mahathat and elsewhere, gave sacred focus to political centers.

Buddhism coexisted with other religious practice. Inscription No. 1 mentions a powerful mountain spirit honored by the ruler, and Brahmanical rites, probably performed by court specialists, continued to shape royal ceremony. Sukhothai religion was therefore plural, with Buddhism increasingly prominent in royal self-presentation but not exclusive. Ordinary people likely combined Buddhist merit-making with ancestor and spirit cults.

For rulers, Buddhist patronage brought legitimacy and allies. Monks were literate and could serve as advisors, scribes, and mediators, while rulers' gifts and protection supported the monastic community. The relationship was reciprocal, and it gave Sukhothai's kings a language of virtue that transcended local lordship.

Royal and private patronage also created institutional dilemmas. A monastery's land might be assigned to sustain food offerings, robes, repairs, or teaching, but its cultivation depended on labor and relations with people who lived nearby. Gifts praised as acts of merit could therefore change obligations and access to resources. Inscriptions identify generous donors more readily than cultivators, tenants, or dependents who supplied the work; the absence of these voices should not be mistaken for universal consent. A respected monastic teacher might lend authority to a ruler while also judging that ruler against expectations of discipline and generosity. Such relationships made Buddhist institutions politically consequential without turning monks into royal officials. They also explain why multiple patrons and monastic communities could coexist in one kingdom, sometimes with different attachments and priorities.

Cause and Effect

Buddhist Patronage and Political Legitimacy

Long-term causes

Earlier Mon, Khmer, and Pagan Buddhist traditions; the growth of Pali scholarship and Sri Lankan-linked ordination lineages across the mainland (Chapter 53); and the need of mixed-population mueang for unifying rituals.

Short-term causes

Competition among Tai-ruled centers for prestige; the weakening of older Khmer-associated authority; and rulers' desire for literate allies and a vocabulary of righteous kingship.

Immediate effects

Donations to monasteries, construction of relic towers and images, invitations to prominent monks, and royal presentation as protector of the religion.

Long-term consequences

A durable model of Buddhist kingship adopted by later courts, strengthened monastic networks across the mainland, and a legacy of religious architecture and texts.

Historical complexity: Patronage did not automatically produce legitimacy. It worked where it matched local expectations and where military and economic strength supported it, and rivals could use the same language.

Section 55.14

Lithai and the Reconfiguration of Buddhist Kingship

Lithai, commonly identified as Mahādhammarāja I, ruled Sukhothai in the middle of the fourteenth century. Reconstructions place his accession around 1347, though the dates are not firm. Traditions and some inscriptions suggest that before becoming king at Sukhothai he held a position at Si Satchanalai and then asserted authority at the capital in the aftermath of a succession dispute. His reign is notable chiefly for its religious character. The title Mahādhammarāja, "great righteous king," announced a ruler whose legitimacy rested on upholding the dhamma, and his patronage of monks, temples, and learning set a pattern.

Lithai's political situation was demanding. Ayutthaya was founded in 1351 on the southern plain and began to expand. Lan Na and other neighbors pursued their own interests. A ruler who could present himself as a righteous king, supported by a respected monastic community, had resources beyond the purely military. Buddhist kingship also carried an ideal of universal rulership, in which the righteous king was a wheel-turning monarch (cakkavatti), and it was well suited to a ruler who sought to hold together a diverse and scattered set of mueang.

The 1361 inscriptions associated with Lithai's religious program supply unusually specific evidence. In their edition of the king's epigraphy, A. B. Griswold and Prasert na Nagara distinguish a Khmer-language inscription (No. 4), a related early Thai inscription (No. 5), a Pali composition honoring the king (No. 6), and another Thai text (No. 7). These are separate records, not one bilingual inscription, and their physical histories and degrees of preservation differ. The related accounts describe gifts to the monastic community, the reception of a senior Buddhist teacher, and the ruler's temporary entry into the monkhood. They place his novice ordination in the palace and full ordination at the Mango Grove in 1361. Such ritual action expressed piety and royal legitimacy, but was not itself evidence that the king abandoned governance permanently.

These texts should be read as royal and religious documents. They tell us what Lithai wished to be seen doing and how his court understood proper kingship. They cannot show how widely his religious program was accepted, or how it affected people outside the court. Their testimony does, however, confirm that Sukhothai's rulers were connected to the broader Buddhist networks described in Section 55.13.

The same inscriptional corpus helps distinguish a reconstructible succession from a smooth dynastic legend. Griswold and Prasert interpreted the evidence as indicating that Lithai was governing at Si Satchanalai before securing the capital in 1347, after an intervening claimant, often rendered Nua Nam Tham, held the throne. The reconstruction rests on readings of inscriptions and their chronology, not on a complete court chronicle. It provides a more historically useful picture than assuming that a reigning king's son always succeeded peacefully. The religious celebrations of 1361 therefore occurred against a background of remembered political competition. Their language of merit, ceremony, and recognized monastic authority could strengthen the public representation of a kingship whose political resources remained negotiated. Even here, an inscription documents the claims and ceremonies of its sponsors rather than measuring the ruler's actual control of every town in his sphere.

Section 55.15

The Traiphumikatha: Cosmology, Ethics, and the Buddhist Political Imagination

The Traiphumikatha, often called Traiphum Phra Ruang, "the Three Worlds of King Ruang," is a Buddhist cosmological treatise traditionally attributed to Lithai. Tradition dates its composition to 1345, and holds that the author compiled it from many Pali scriptures and commentaries and wrote it, according to some versions, for the instruction of his mother. It became one of the most influential works of Thai Buddhist literature, and Frank and Mani Reynolds published an English translation, Three Worlds According to King Ruang (1982).

The text describes the cosmos as three realms: the world of sensual desire, the world of form, and the world of formlessness, together comprising thirty-one planes of existence, from hells at the bottom through the human world and the heavens of the gods to the highest formless states. Beings are reborn into these planes according to their actions, or karma, and the text details the fates that await the good and the wicked, including vivid descriptions of hells. It also teaches that merit, earned through generosity, moral conduct, and meditation, improves rebirth, and it places the figure of the righteous king, who rules by the dhamma and protects his subjects, at the center of a moral universe.

The work belongs to a Buddhist political imagination in which kingship is moral cosmology applied to society. A king who acts justly sustains the order of the world, and a wicked king imperils it. The treatise did not simply describe rulers' ideals. It also gave the ruler's subjects a framework for understanding hierarchy, obligation, and reward.

Historians approach the work's date and authorship carefully. The traditional attribution to Lithai and the date 1345 are part of the text's received history. The surviving manuscripts are later copies, and the text shows signs of having been revised and expanded over time. Some scholars accept an essentially fourteenth-century origin, while others emphasize later editing or argue that the date and authorship are traditional claims that cannot be confirmed. Students should treat the Traiphumikatha as a significant Sukhothai-era tradition whose precise original form is uncertain.

The cosmology also gave teachers a way to connect an individual's choices with a universal scale of consequences. Accounts of hells and heavens could make generosity, restraint, and misconduct intelligible to listeners who could not read a long learned treatise. Sermons, recitations, illustrations, and explanations in everyday language were possible means of transmitting such ideas, although the surviving evidence does not establish precisely how this text reached every village or temple. The text's political force lay partly in this potential to make moral behavior publicly meaningful; it did not require every listener to possess a manuscript. Historians must likewise distinguish the worldview represented by the available recension from what can securely be attributed to Lithai's own pen. An influential later textual tradition is evidence of cultural reception even where the earliest wording cannot be reconstructed with confidence.

Historical Vocabulary
Karma, Merit, Traiphum, and Dhammarāja

Definition. Karma (Pali kamma) is the principle that intentional actions have consequences that shape future rebirths. Merit (bun in Thai, from Pali puñña) is the positive moral credit gained through good actions such as giving, ethical conduct, and meditation. Traiphum ("three worlds") is the cosmological scheme of the sensual, form, and formless realms. Dhammarāja is the righteous king who rules by the dhamma.

Historical context. These ideas were part of the shared Theravada tradition and found elaborated expression in Sukhothai-era texts and inscriptions. They guided donation, ordination, kingship, and ordinary moral life.

Why it matters. The concepts link religious practice to politics: a ruler's wealth and success were explained as the fruit of past merit, which encouraged generous patronage.

Potential misunderstanding. Karma is not simple fatalism, and the Traiphumikatha's vivid cosmology should not be read as the belief of every person in Sukhothai. Elite texts do not describe the beliefs of entire populations.

Section 55.16

Sukhothai Art, Buddha Images, Monastic Architecture, and Cultural Production

Sukhothai's artistic reputation rests on its religious architecture and Buddha images. Wat Mahathat, at the center of the walled town, is the principal monument. Its central tower is crowned with a lotus-bud finial, a form often considered characteristic of the Sukhothai style, and it is surrounded by smaller towers, assembly halls, and images. Other temples adopt different forms: Wat Chang Lom at Si Satchanalai has a bell-shaped relic tower resembling Sri Lankan models, while Wat Si Sawai and parts of Chaliang preserve Khmer-style sanctuaries. Brick, laterite, stucco, and bronze were the main materials, and many buildings were decorated with modeled stucco.

Sukhothai Buddha images developed a distinct vocabulary. Seated, standing, reclining, and walking figures are characteristic, and the walking Buddha, in which the figure appears to stride in a flowing line, is often cited as a Sukhothai specialty. Images typically show smooth, rounded features, elongated forms, and a flame-like finial on the head. The great bronze image cast at Sukhothai and later moved to Bangkok, and the monumental stucco and brick Buddhas of Wat Si Chum, show the scale of royal and religious investment. Dating of individual images is usually stylistic and therefore approximate.

The sources of these forms were varied. Mon and Khmer traditions supplied building techniques and iconographic models. Connections with Sri Lankan Buddhism influenced relic towers and monastic organization, and contacts with other mainland centers, Pagan's legacy among them, added further elements. Describing the result as imitation would be misleading. Artists and patrons selected, combined, and reshaped these influences into a recognizable local idiom, the way Southeast Asian societies generally localized outside forms (Chapter 19).

Cultural production extended beyond art. Inscriptions in Tai and Pali, religious texts, and the Traiphumikatha show a literate monastic and court culture. Craft specialization in bronze, stucco, ceramics, and gold supported temple building. Much of this culture was elite and religious in focus, and students should recall that the artisans and laborers who built it are rarely named. The monuments survive because they were durable, religious, and later cherished, which has shaped what we know.

The way the monuments are experienced today also reflects restoration choices. Many structures were consolidated or partly reconstructed in the twentieth century, so visitors see a combination of medieval fabric and modern conservation. This does not diminish their significance, but it means that historians must distinguish original material from later repair, and that the tidy appearance of the historical park differs from the lived, changing townscape of the fourteenth century, in which buildings were built, altered, and sometimes abandoned over time.

Section 55.17

Sukhothai after Ramkhamhaeng: Successors and Regional Competition

The generations after Ramkhamhaeng are known mainly from fourteenth-century inscriptions and later chronicles, and reconstructions of the sequence differ. Most accounts name Lo Thai as his successor, followed by one or more rulers during the 1340s, and then Lithai. The order and the dates of the rulers between Ramkhamhaeng and Lithai are not firmly established, and scholars distinguish dynastic reconstruction, which works out a plausible genealogy from fragmentary sources, from firmly dated inscriptional evidence. Where an inscription gives a date in the Saka era, that date is the strongest evidence available. Where only a chronicle supplies a reign length, it should be held more loosely.

What is clearer is the shape of the political landscape. Sukhothai, Si Satchanalai, and Kamphaeng Phet were associated with different branches of the royal family and with different ambitions. Lithai's rise from a position at Si Satchanalai to rule at Sukhothai suggests that a ruler of a secondary town could challenge or inherit the center, and that the relationship among the towns was one of competing as well as cooperating kin. Neighboring centers pursued their own consolidation. Lan Na under its Mangrai dynasty grew stronger in the north, and relations with it included both friendship and dispute. In the south, Suphanburi and Lopburi remained significant, and in 1351 Ayutthaya joined them.

Sukhothai's rulers adapted to these pressures through religion, diplomacy, and marriage. Lithai's Buddhist kingship gave the dynasty prestige, and alliances with neighbors helped to manage competition. Yet the political center of gravity on the Chao Phraya plain was shifting toward the south, where river-borne commerce and a growing military base favored Ayutthaya. Sukhothai remained prominent and culturally influential, but the balance of power was moving.

Understanding the post-Ramkhamhaeng period therefore requires caution about the idea of a single decline. The kingdom's cultural and religious activity was at its richest under Lithai, even as its political position faced new constraints. The two processes, cultural flourishing and political pressure, ran together.

Chinese records add a further complication. Missions from polities called Xian continued into the fourteenth century, and Chinese sources increasingly associated the name with the southern power centered on the lower plain. Scholars therefore hesitate to assign every recorded mission to Sukhothai, and the diplomatic evidence is better read as showing the general participation of Tai-ruled courts in the tributary system than as a precise record of Sukhothai's foreign relations.

Section 55.18

Sukhothai and the Rise of Ayutthaya

Ayutthaya, founded on an island in the Chao Phraya plain in the mid-fourteenth century and treated in detail in Chapter 57, was strategically placed for river and sea commerce and drew strength from alliances with Suphanburi and Lopburi. Its rulers expanded north, and by the later fourteenth century Sukhothai was within their reach. Traditional chronology associates an Ayutthayan campaign with 1378, when Sukhothai's ruler is said to have accepted subordination. The episode matters, but sources do not allow us to describe it precisely, and later Ayutthayan chronicles may magnify their own kings' achievements.

The relationship that followed was not a clean conquest. Sukhothai continued to have rulers, who in some sources acknowledge Ayutthaya's suzerainty while exercising local power. Royal houses intermarried, and the dynastic ties made it possible for claimants from both sides to assert rights. In the early fifteenth century Ayutthaya's king intervened in Sukhothai's succession, and Ayutthaya's influence over the choice of rulers grew. The conventional date of 1438 marks the death of Sukhothai's last ruler with independent standing, after which Ayutthaya's control became more direct. Dates from this period come mainly from later chronicles and should be treated as conventional.

Military submission, dynastic integration, and administrative absorption are different processes, and the sources show all three at different moments. Submission meant acknowledging Ayutthaya's superiority and probably supplying tribute or troops. Dynastic integration meant that marriage and succession tied the royal houses together. Absorption meant the replacement of local rulers by appointees of the southern court. Each occurred unevenly, and Sukhothai's own towns experienced them differently: Kamphaeng Phet's frontier position and Si Satchanalai's ceramic economy gave them distinct roles.

Sukhothai's institutions and elites did not vanish. Monasteries, ceramic production, and local families persisted, and in the later fifteenth century Ayutthaya's king Borommatrailokanat spent a long period at Phitsanulok, in the Sukhothai sphere, while dealing with Lan Na. Phitsanulok became a major northern center. The transformation therefore involved relocation of power and the blending of traditions, as Sukhothai's religious and administrative inheritance entered the political culture of Ayutthaya (Chapter 86).

The experience of the transition differed by group. Royal kin faced shifting prospects as claims were negotiated with the southern court. Monks continued their work, often under new patrons. Cultivators and craftspeople probably noticed the change less in daily routine than in the identity of those to whom service and produce were owed, and in the demands of military campaigns that drew men from the north. Because these experiences are poorly documented, the account remains weighted toward courts, and students should keep that bias in view.

Section 55.19

Sukhothai Rediscovered: Monarchy, Scholarship, and the Construction of Thai National History

After the fifteenth century, Sukhothai's walls and temples were gradually abandoned or reduced in importance, though some religious sites remained in use, and local memory of the old kingdom persisted. Its modern prominence began in 1833, when Prince Mongkut, then a monk, visited the site and brought Inscription No. 1 and a stone slab to Bangkok. His interest was both religious and scholarly. He studied the inscription, and the royal court began to collect and interpret ancient inscriptions. When Mongkut became king in 1851, the Bangkok court gained a new interest in the history of earlier Tai capitals, and in the reigns that followed (Chapter 113), royal scholarship shaped an account in which Sukhothai played a founding role.

In the early twentieth century, the editing and translation of the inscriptions by Coedès, who published them in the 1920s, and the historical writing of Prince Damrong Rajanubhab gave the narrative scholarly form. Sukhothai was presented as the first capital of a Thai kingdom, Ramkhamhaeng as its great king, and the Tai script attributed to him as the foundation of a Thai writing tradition. Following the political changes of 1932 (Chapter 132), and especially during the nationalist policies of the late 1930s and 1940s (Chapter 140), the state promoted national history that stressed continuity from Sukhothai through Ayutthaya and Bangkok. The name of the country was changed from Siam to Thailand in 1939, and Sukhothai's idealized kingship was invoked as a national heritage.

Archaeological and museum work reinforced the story. The Fine Arts Department undertook restoration of the Sukhothai monuments from the 1970s, the site was developed as a historical park in the 1980s, and the three associated towns were inscribed by UNESCO in 1991. These efforts preserved the monuments and made them accessible, and the interpretation offered to visitors often emphasized the first-Thai-capital narrative. The designation of the site as World Heritage honors its architecture, urban planning, and cultural significance; it is not a verdict on the historical claims of the inscriptions.

Historians therefore distinguish two Sukhothais. The first is the medieval Tai-ruled polity, studied through its sources. The second is the national symbol, constructed in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries and shaped by monarchy, scholarship, and nationalism. The second is also a legitimate subject of history, and the debates described in Section 55.6 are partly debates about it. Competing local and regional histories, those of Lan Na and the Lao kingdoms among them (Chapters 56 and 58), show that other narratives of early Tai-ruled states were available and that the choice of Sukhothai as origin was a choice.

Museum and educational presentation extended the process. School textbooks, ceremonies, and public monuments encouraged a view of Ramkhamhaeng as a national founder-figure and of the Sukhothai period as a model of benevolent rule, and festivals at the historical park drew on that imagery. Scholars who questioned the inscription's authenticity in the late twentieth century were therefore also touching a sensitive public symbol, which helps explain why the controversy aroused strong feeling beyond the specialist community.

Section 55.20

Sukhothai in Southeast Asian History: Political Formation, Cultural Adaptation, and Historical Memory

Sukhothai emerged from a complex landscape of Mon, Khmer, and Tai-speaking communities, and its early rulers built authority through alliance, kinship, and patronage in a world of mueang. Its political institutions were flexible and personal, depending on royal kin, local lords, and religious allies, not on a centralized bureaucracy or fixed frontiers. The claims of Ramkhamhaeng's inscription describe an ideal of just, accessible kingship and a wide horizon of influence, but their reliability cannot be assumed, and the dating of the inscription itself remains disputed.

Economically, the kingdom rested on rice agriculture supported by reservoirs and embankments, and on craft production and regional exchange. The famous ceramics belong largely to a later period than the reign of Ramkhamhaeng, which is a reminder that accomplishments accumulate over generations and should not be assigned to a single ruler. Religiously, Sukhothai drew on the Pali Buddhist networks that linked the mainland to Sri Lanka, and under Lithai and his successors it turned Buddhist kingship into a dominant political language. The cosmology of the Traiphumikatha, whatever its exact date, shows how that language was elaborated.

Politically, Sukhothai's relationships with Lan Na, Mon centers, Khmer-influenced towns, and eventually Ayutthaya were fluid. Its absorption by Ayutthaya was a long process of pressure, accommodation, and dynastic interconnection, and Sukhothai's religious and cultural heritage was absorbed into the later kingdom. Its later fame is the product of nineteenth- and twentieth-century scholarship and nationalism, which cast it as the beginning of a continuous Thai national history.

For the history of the mainland as a whole, Sukhothai's significance lies in its role as one of several experiments in Tai-ruled statecraft in the thirteenth to fifteenth centuries. It shows how rulers could combine kin-based lordship with Buddhist universalism, how small polities could cultivate prestige through religion and art, and how political power could shift from northern and upland-linked centers toward the commercial lower plain. Reading Sukhothai alongside Lan Na, Lan Xang, and Ayutthaya makes clear that the mainland's political future was open in these centuries, and that later outcomes were not foreordained.

Several comparisons sharpen that conclusion. Lan Na's northern valley networks, examined next, developed different balances of regional chiefs, monasteries, and overlordship. Ayutthaya's commercially connected lower-plain court ultimately incorporated Sukhothai elites and religious resources, but its political institutions were not simply copied from Sukhothai. Nor should later museums, restored monuments, and school narratives be dismissed merely because they reorganized the medieval past. They are evidence of how historical authority was constructed in subsequent periods. The appropriate historical test is to separate what surviving stones, objects, and contemporary records can establish from what later communities wished Sukhothai to represent. Doing so preserves the kingdom's importance while leaving room for alternative political futures that were possible to its inhabitants.

Historical Synthesis Study

What Was Sukhothai's Historical Significance?

Two simplified accounts of Sukhothai have circulated widely. The first treats it as an isolated golden age, a self-contained kingdom of just kings and prosperous farmers that flourished and then fell. The second treats it as a simple predecessor of the modern nation-state, the first stage of a Thai nation moving toward its present form. The evidence does not support either account.

Against the isolated golden age: Sukhothai was embedded in networks of trade, religion, and diplomacy that reached Sri Lanka, the Bay of Bengal, and China. Its institutions drew on Mon, Khmer, and Tai traditions, and its prosperity was uneven, with labor and inequality behind its monuments. The idea of a golden age rests heavily on a royal inscription of disputed authenticity.
Against the simple national predecessor: Sukhothai's people did not understand themselves as citizens of a Thai nation. Other Tai-ruled polities, including Lan Na and Lan Xang, had their own trajectories, and the identification of Sukhothai as a first capital was shaped by later political needs.
What can be said: Sukhothai was a significant Tai-ruled kingdom whose rulers developed a durable model of Buddhist kingship, whose towns preserve remarkable monuments, whose ceramic and craft traditions flourished in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, and whose inheritance influenced Lan Na, Ayutthaya, and the wider Theravada mainland.

Chapter Summary

The kingdom of Sukhothai emerged from an already complex mainland political landscape, developed distinctive institutions through regional exchange and Buddhist patronage, and underwent gradual political transformation as neighboring centers became more influential. Its prominence in Thai history reflects both its medieval achievements and the ways later generations reconstructed its meaning.

The evidence for Sukhothai is uneven. Inscriptions, archaeology, later chronicles, and foreign records each illuminate some questions and obscure others, and royal inscriptions in particular are evidence of what rulers wished to record, not neutral description. The conventional founding date of about 1238 is traditional or reconstructed.

The Yom plain was not empty before Tai-ruled Sukhothai. Mon and Khmer communities had built temples and routes, and Tai-speaking settlers lived alongside them. The founding traditions in Inscription No. 2 preserve a political memory of allied rulers and a title conferred by a peer, but they were composed after the events and cannot supply a firm chronology.

Early royal authority rested on kinship, alliance, and patronage within a world of mueang. Under Ramkhamhaeng, Sukhothai's influence expanded, but the claims in Inscription No. 1 must be distinguished from documented administration. The inscription's authenticity and dating have been vigorously debated, and recognition by UNESCO does not settle the question.

The inscription's images of the bell, free trade, and paternal kingship express an ideal. They cannot independently establish how justice or taxation worked. Sukhothai's relations with Lan Na, Mon communities, Khmer-influenced centers, and China were fluid and varied, and the claim of direct control over much of the mainland is not supported by independent evidence.

Sukhothai, Si Satchanalai, and Kamphaeng Phet formed a connected but changing urban landscape. Rice agriculture rested on reservoirs and embankments, and the famous ceramics developed chiefly in the later fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. Society was hierarchical, with households, women, craftspeople, dependents, and donors playing roles that royal inscriptions only partly reveal.

Theravada Buddhism, linked to Sri Lankan, Mon, and other networks, gave rulers a language of righteous kingship. Lithai's reign, the Wat Pa Mamuang inscriptions of 1361, and the Traiphumikatha tradition show how Buddhist ideas shaped political imagination, though the Traiphumikatha's exact date and form are uncertain. Sukhothai's art and architecture combined Mon, Khmer, Sri Lankan, and local elements into a distinctive idiom.

After Ramkhamhaeng, Sukhothai remained culturally rich while Ayutthaya's rise altered the political balance. Submission, dynastic integration, and absorption occurred over decades, and Sukhothai's institutions and elites persisted. Rediscovery in the nineteenth century, royal and nationalist scholarship, and heritage conservation then made Sukhothai a symbol of Thai national history. The medieval kingdom and the national symbol should be studied separately, and each deserves analysis.

Mueang

Chapter Reflection

Sukhothai matters because it shows, in a small compass, how a medieval kingdom is reconstructed from fragments and how its meaning changes afterward. The Tai-ruled towns of the Yom plain built on older Mon and Khmer foundations, joined a Pali Buddhist world that stretched to Sri Lanka, and developed a model of kingship that later courts admired. Their achievements in architecture, water management, ceramics, and religious literature were real, and they accumulated over generations.

The chapter also shows why method matters. A single inscription shaped a national narrative for more than a century, and then became the center of a scholarly controversy. Whether or not that controversy is ever resolved, it teaches students to ask who made a source, when, and for whom, and to separate what a document claims from what other evidence confirms.

Sukhothai's story connects to what follows. The northern Tai world of Lan Na (Chapter 56), the rise of Ayutthaya (Chapter 57), and the Lao kingdom of Lan Xang (Chapter 58) all developed in conversation with it. Its Buddhist kingship, its monasteries, and its elites entered Ayutthaya's political culture, and its memory entered the national histories of modern Thailand.

The continuity that survives is as much about memory as about institutions. Sukhothai continues to be invoked as a symbol, and understanding both the kingdom and the symbol is part of understanding mainland Southeast Asia: a region whose societies built distinctive states and cultures, and whose pasts have been repeatedly reinterpreted by those who came after.
