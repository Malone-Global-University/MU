Jayavarman VII and the Buddhist Khmer Empire

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

The reign of Jayavarman VII, conventionally dated from 1181 after a period of warfare with Champa often described as the sack of Angkor in 1177, was a military, political, religious, and institutional reconstruction of Khmer imperial power. Its victories, its Buddhist royal ideology, its monuments, its roads, and its hospitals are best understood as parts of a single project whose achievements and material costs must be weighed together.

In the center of Angkor Thom, the walled royal city of the late twelfth and early thirteenth centuries, stands a temple whose towers carry enormous carved faces. Visitors today know it as the Bayon. Its stone faces, some two hundred of them on the surviving towers, look in each of the four directions with a calm expression that has become one of the best-known images of Southeast Asia. A few hundred meters outside the walls, other foundations of the same period, Ta Prohm and Preah Khan, preserve substantial Sanskrit foundation inscriptions recording priests, villages, dancers, hospitals, rest houses, and offerings. Along the old roads that once radiated from the capital, archaeologists have identified the remains of rest houses and bridges. Almost all of this material is associated with one king.

Jayavarman VII did not inherit a secure empire. According to the conventional reconstruction, in 1177 a force from the Cham-speaking polities of the central and southern Vietnamese coast attacked Angkor by way of the Mekong and Tonlé Sap, and the Khmer ruler was killed. The route, scale, and alleged prolonged occupation are contested, as Section 38.4 explains. The attack was the climax of a generation of dynastic instability that had followed the death of Suryavarman II, the builder of Angkor Wat, examined in Chapter 37. For several years the political center of the Khmer world was contested. The king who eventually emerged from that crisis was an older man with a long military record, a Buddhist family tradition, and a talent for turning memory of national disaster into a program of reconstruction.

This chapter examines that reconstruction. It begins with the political crisis and the sequence of war, restoration, and accession that brought Jayavarman VII to power in 1181. It then examines how his government rebuilt royal authority, how it mobilized military force, and how it intervened for a generation in the affairs of Champa and dealt with its other neighbors. It turns to the great monumental program centered on Angkor Thom and the Bayon and extending through Ta Prohm, Preah Khan, and many other foundations. It analyzes the transformation of royal ideology through Mahayana Buddhism, and it studies the infrastructure and institutions, roads, rest houses, hospitals, and temple economies, through which the king's religious and political aims were put into practice. It ends with the final years of the reign and with the debate over what the building program cost and whether it could be sustained.

The evidence is unusually rich but also unusually one-sided. The principal contemporary sources are inscriptions commissioned by the king or by members of his family and court, and the monuments themselves. Chinese compilations add brief outside notices, and Cham and Vietnamese records preserve part of the other side of the war with Champa. Late in the thirteenth century a Chinese envoy, Zhou Daguan, described Angkor as a functioning city, though some eighty years after Jayavarman VII's death. No administrative archive, tax register, or independent chronicle survives. Historians therefore reconstruct the reign from royal self-presentation, from material remains, and from carefully qualified inference, and a recurring task of this chapter is to distinguish what the king's inscriptions claim from what archaeology and comparative evidence can confirm.

The chapter also resists two simplifications. The first is the tradition of describing Jayavarman VII as a unique, almost solitary genius whose personality produced Angkor's final monumental flowering. His reign depended on the labor and expertise of thousands of officials, priests, artisans, soldiers, and dependent villagers, and on institutions that existed before him. The second is the tendency to describe the reign only as a prelude to decline. Historians know that Angkor continued as a political center for more than two centuries after his death, examined in Chapter 61, and nothing in his reign made later change inevitable.

The chapter's central argument is that Jayavarman VII's reign should be understood as one ambitious project of reconstruction in which war, religion, architecture, infrastructure, and administration were connected. Victory over Champa legitimated a ruler who had come to the throne after a usurpation and an invasion. Buddhist ideology gave that ruler a language of compassion and merit through which to claim authority. Monuments, roads, and hospitals turned that language into visible institutions, and the labor and resources they required were drawn from the same agricultural and human base that supported the army. Treating these as separate stories, a military story, a religious story, and an architectural story, obscures how they supported and strained one another.

Students should keep in mind the chapter boundaries within this textbook. The rise of Angkor and its earlier kingship are examined in Chapters 33 and 34, the hydraulic and urban landscape in Chapter 35, the religious traditions of the Khmer world in Chapter 36, and Suryavarman II in Chapter 37. The wider reconstruction of households, markets, gender, agriculture, slavery, and everyday society is the work of Chapter 39. The conflict between Champa and Đại Việt is examined from a broader perspective in Chapter 45, and the later Theravada transformation of mainland Southeast Asia in Chapter 53. The present chapter concentrates on the reign itself and on the connections among its parts.

Historical Context

By the middle of the twelfth century the Khmer kingdom centered on Angkor, in the northern Tonlé Sap basin, was the most extensive political order on the Southeast Asian mainland. As examined in Chapters 33 through 37, its kings ruled through a court at the capital, usually called Yasodharapura in the inscriptions, and through networks of provincial lords, temple foundations, and dependent communities. Royal authority was expressed through Sanskrit and Old Khmer inscriptions, through the endowment of temples, and through rituals joining the king to major gods such as Shiva and Vishnu. The scale of the capital's reservoirs, canals, and rice lands is examined in Chapter 35, and the religious traditions that framed royal patronage in Chapter 36. Scholars often describe the political geography through the mandala model introduced in Chapter 1: authority radiated from a center, weakened toward the edges, and overlapped with neighboring spheres, rather than ending at a fixed border.

Suryavarman II (reigned c. 1113 to the middle of the twelfth century) had expanded Khmer power and built Angkor Wat, but his campaigns against Champa and Đại Việt had ended without permanent gain, and the empire he left faced questions of succession and legitimacy. Khmer royal succession was not governed by a strict rule of primogeniture. A claimant needed descent or marriage ties to earlier royal lines, the support of leading priestly and aristocratic families, control of the capital and its military forces, and ritual consecration. Competing claimants could therefore plausibly assert rights, and a ruler with a weak base could be displaced by a powerful official. The decades after Suryavarman II show this pattern.

The neighboring political world had also changed. Champa, which Khmer kings had attacked and briefly occupied in the 1140s, recovered under rulers who were willing and able to retaliate, and its coastal ports connected it to the South China Sea trade, examined in Chapter 22 and Chapter 45. Đại Việt, to the north of Champa, was consolidating under the Lý dynasty, examined in Chapter 43. To the west and northwest, Khmer influence extended into the Mun and Chao Phraya basins, and Buddhist communities there had long coexisted with Hindu court traditions. Students should keep in mind that modern Cambodia, Thailand, Laos, and Vietnam did not exist; the monuments and roads discussed in this chapter cross present-day borders, and the Khmer political world cannot be mapped onto any modern state.

Division I — Imperial Crisis and the Rise of Jayavarman VII · Section 38.1

The Khmer Empire after Suryavarman II

Suryavarman II's death in the middle of the twelfth century, examined in Chapter 37, left a kingdom with an enormous religious foundation at Angkor Wat, an extensive network of dependents and provincial lords, and unresolved conflicts with Champa and Đại Việt. The surviving inscriptions for the following decades are fewer and less informative than those for Suryavarman II's reign, and the dates of the next rulers are approximate. The usual reconstruction places the reign of Dharanindravarman II, a cousin or relative of the previous king, in the 1150s. Inscriptions describe him as devoted to the Buddha, which is significant because it shows a Buddhist ruler on the Khmer throne a generation before his son. He was succeeded by Yasovarman II, whose reign in the early 1160s ended when a high official, Tribhuvanadityavarman, seized the throne around 1165 and held it until 1177.

What the sources say about this usurpation comes largely from the winners. The later inscriptions of Jayavarman VII's reign describe Tribhuvanadityavarman as an illegitimate ruler who seized power by force. Historians have no inscription from his own reign presenting the case for his rule, and it would be an error to treat the hostile description as a neutral account. What can be said is that a ruler without clear descent from the previous kings could take the throne, that he governed for more than a decade, and that his regime seems to have commanded enough support to do so. The episode shows that the Khmer political order was a field of contest among court factions, priestly lineages, military commanders, and provincial lords rather than a mechanism that automatically transferred power.

The empire Jayavarman VII eventually inherited had real strengths and real problems. The agricultural and hydraulic base around Angkor was large, and the capital was surrounded by a landscape of reservoirs, channels, and temple estates. But the monumental commitments of earlier reigns, including the upkeep of foundations and their personnel, represented a standing charge on royal resources. Champa had recovered from the Khmer occupation of the 1140s and had reason for revenge. No single factor explains the political and military crisis conventionally dated to 1177; the vulnerability arose from the combination of political division, a reigning king of doubtful legitimacy, and an adversary with a capable fleet and the means to use it.

Section 38.2

Champa and the Attack on Angkor in 1177

The relationship between the Khmer and the Cham was long and varied. Inscriptions and later records show alliance, marriage, trade, and religious exchange alongside warfare, and the two polities shared Indic religious and political vocabularies while using different languages, Khmer and Cham, which belong to different language families (Austroasiatic and Austronesian, examined in Chapters 13 and 14). In the 1140s Suryavarman II's armies had campaigned in Champa, and Khmer forces had occupied the Cham capital at Vijaya for a time before being expelled. The Cham rulers who followed, among them Jaya Indravarman of Grāmapuravijaya, had both memory of that humiliation and an interest in the trade of the South China Sea, in which control of coastal and river routes was valuable.

In the conventional reconstruction, a Cham fleet traveled up the Mekong and Tonlé Sap system and attacked the Khmer capital in 1177. That route and the identification of an attack on Angkor depend on a synthesis of Chinese notices, Khmer inscriptions, and later interpretations of the Bayon reliefs; no single contemporary source securely documents the whole sequence. The sources associate a Cham king with the overthrow of Tribhuvanadityavarman, but whether Angkor was taken in the manner traditionally described, and whether any Cham occupation lasted several years, remain disputed. The extent of destruction at Yasodharapura is uncertain. Later Khmer texts emphasize the disaster, and the king who came after had reasons to dramatize it. Chinese sources of the late twelfth century refer to hostilities between Champa and Zhenla (the Chinese name for the Khmer kingdom), which confirms that the conflict was recognized outside the region, though these notices are brief.

Several explanations for the attack deserve consideration. Revenge for Suryavarman II's earlier invasions is plausible but cannot be demonstrated directly. The Khmer king's weakened legitimacy gave an invader a political opening. Maritime and riverine routes gave a coastal power an advantage that landlocked defenders could not easily counter. The success of the expedition also required Cham commanders to plan an operation over a long supply line, and the Cham ruler's ability to do so suggests that Champa had considerable resources. The immediate political consequence for the Khmer was a leadership vacuum: the king was dead, the capital was occupied, and rival claimants and regional lords had to decide where their interests lay.

Section 38.3

Jayavarman VII before the Throne

Reconstructing the life of Jayavarman VII before 1181 requires careful handling of sources. Nearly all information comes from inscriptions composed after his accession, under his authority or that of his family. These texts present his earlier life in terms that serve his later legitimacy. According to them, he was a son of Dharanindravarman II and Sri Jayarajachudamani, a member of the royal line though not the heir to the throne. He is usually thought to have been born in the 1120s, which would make him in his mid-fifties at the time of his coronation, an unusually mature age for an accession. His reign would then have lasted some thirty-seven years, which would make him very old at death; the ages involved remain estimates.

The most informative text on his early career is an inscription from the Phimeanakas, composed by his second wife Indradevi, that describes the life of his first wife, Jayarajadevi. It reports that Jayarajadevi, while her husband was away on campaign in Champa, practiced austerities and took instruction in Buddhism from her elder sister Indradevi, who was learned in Buddhist teaching and later became his wife after Jayarajadevi's death. The text also tells us that Jayavarman VII was in Champa during the political turmoil that put Tribhuvanadityavarman on the throne, and that on his return he did not immediately seize power. Whether he waited out of caution, from lack of support, or because of religious restraint cannot be determined; the inscription's own explanation is favorable to him and it should be read as such.

Even so, the picture of his career before the throne has important elements. He had military experience, probably in the campaigns against Champa; he belonged to a family with Buddhist commitments; and he had a network of connections, through family and marriage, that included learned women who would later be named as teachers in royal Buddhist institutions. The role of Jayarajadevi and Indradevi shows that royal women could be significant religious and intellectual figures and that their lives were documented by the court. It also illustrates the limits of the evidence, since the inscriptions that record their activities were produced for a ruler's purposes.

Historical Vocabulary
Stele and the "K." Numbers

Definition. A stele is an upright stone slab carrying an inscription. Khmer steles of this period are usually of sandstone and may carry text in Sanskrit verse, in Old Khmer prose, or in both. Scholars refer to inscriptions of the Khmer corpus by catalog numbers prefixed with "K." (for Cambodge), a system derived from the early twentieth-century corpus compiled by George Cœdès and later scholars.

Historical context. Khmer-period epigraphy includes Sanskrit and Old Khmer texts, but language should not be inferred from whether an inscription is administrative. The Ta Prohm stele (K. 273) and Preah Khan stele (K. 908) are composed in Sanskrit verse, including their detailed accounts of personnel, villages, and provisions.

Why it matters. The two languages serve different purposes. Sanskrit verses shape the royal image; Khmer prose records the institutional arrangements. Historians therefore read the two parts of a stele for different kinds of evidence.

Potential misunderstanding. A stele is not a neutral archive. It records what the donor wished to preserve and publicize, and a missing category of information may simply not have been considered worth recording.

Section 38.4

War, Restoration, and the Accession of 1181

In the traditional chronology, the years after the Cham attack were marked by contested control; the extent and duration of any occupation remain unresolved. Inscriptions and the later reliefs suggest that Khmer resistance was organized in several places and that Jayavarman VII became its most successful leader. The details of the campaigns are not securely established. The best-known episode is a naval engagement on the Tonlé Sap in which Khmer forces defeated the Cham fleet, an event commemorated in the Bayon reliefs, which show crowded boats, soldiers in combat, and fish and crocodiles in the water. The reliefs were carved decades later, so they record how the Khmer court wished to remember the event rather than serving as a battlefield record. Chinese and Cham sources are too brief to fix the sequence.

The conventional chronology, a Cham attack in 1177, an occupation of about four years, and a coronation in 1181, should be treated as a reconstruction rather than a documented sequence. The historian Michael Vickery challenged its certainty, questioning the traditional identification of a decisive conquest and prolonged Cham occupation of Angkor in 1177, and suggesting that Khmer-Cham alliances and overlapping conflicts have been obscured by later reconstructions. His argument relies on reassessing Khmer and Cham inscriptions alongside the Chinese accounts. The year 1181 is supported by Jayavarman VII's own regnal dating; the 1177 raid and particularly its identification with a sack and multi-year occupation of Angkor are more disputed. This chapter preserves the customary dates while distinguishing them from securely documented details of the intervening campaigns.

The restoration of the capital and the coronation of Jayavarman VII are traditionally placed in 1181, and this date is the working convention of most modern historians. The coronation transformed a successful military leader into a king whose legitimacy rested on several claims: descent from earlier royal lines, success in expelling the invader, and the religious merit and ritual consecration that Khmer kingship required. Unlike many earlier kings, he did not present himself primarily through the cult of a Hindu god. Within a few years his inscriptions presented a king whose sovereignty was linked to the Buddhist idea of compassion, a point developed in Division IV.

An important feature of the accession is how little it resembled a simple return to the old order. The capital had been sacked, and the king who took the throne was a mature, experienced commander, not a boy heir. He chose, as the following sections show, to rebuild rather than merely restore: the city, the religious program, and the institutional apparatus were substantially redesigned. Whether he planned all of this from the start or developed it as conditions allowed cannot be determined, but by the mid-1180s the main outlines were visible in the Ta Prohm stele.

Division II — Rebuilding Khmer Imperial Power · Section 38.5

Consolidating Royal Authority

A king who had retaken his capital still had to rebuild the instruments of rule. Inscriptions of the 1180s and 1190s name court officials, priests, and relatives of the king in positions of responsibility, and these names suggest a government built around a trusted circle: members of the royal family, learned Brahmins and Buddhist teachers, and officials who had served earlier rulers or the king himself. The king's queens, particularly Indradevi, served as religious teachers, and several of his sons and other relatives were associated with specific foundations and commands. Administrative institutions such as the court and its titled ranks are examined in Chapter 34, and this section concentrates on how Jayavarman VII used them.

One means was the distribution of religious and administrative responsibilities through temple foundations. The great steles show that foundations were staffed by appointees of the king, supplied by assigned villages, and linked through the ritual calendar to the capital. In this arrangement the royal temple was not only a religious institution but also a node of administration, providing the crown with a means of connecting provincial communities to the court. The distribution of Buddhist images to provincial towns, discussed in Section 38.17, served a similar integrating function.

The limits of the evidence must be stated plainly. No law code, tax roll, or register of provincial governors survives, and the administrative picture is assembled from references in religious inscriptions. We cannot say how far provincial lords retained autonomy, how loyal they were, or how much revenue reached the capital. The mandala model reminds historians that royal authority probably varied with distance and local arrangements. It also should not be applied mechanically: the concentration of foundations, roads, and personnel in this reign suggests an unusually deliberate effort to give the center practical means of reaching the periphery.

Section 38.6

Armies, Military Organization, and Imperial Mobilization

The wars of the period required a military establishment, but no source gives its size or organization in numbers. Historians rely on the Bayon and Banteay Chhmar reliefs, on inscriptions that name commanders, and on comparison with other Southeast Asian states. The reliefs show infantry with shields and spears, archers, officers mounted on elephants, and marching columns with banners and musical instruments. They also depict foreign troops, notably figures identifiable by dress and appearance as Chinese, apparently serving with the Khmer, and Cham soldiers distinguished by their headdress. The reliefs were produced for a royal audience and may idealize order and discipline; their numbers of figures should not be taken as army sizes.

Mobilization depended on obligations that the sources describe only indirectly. Households in provincial villages and temple-dependent communities probably owed labor and service, some of it military, to the king or to the foundations to which they were attached. Provincial lords may have furnished contingents under their own command. Naval operations, essential in a war fought along the Mekong, the Tonlé Sap, and the coast, required boats, crews, and shipwrights, and the 1177 attack had shown the importance of riverine control. Logistics for expeditions to Champa required food, transport, and routes. The road network and rest houses examined in Section 38.20 were part of the infrastructure that made such operations possible, even if their documented purposes were broader.

Military power was also political. A ruler who had come to power through war depended on the loyalty of commanders, and the distribution of titles, land, and temple posts bound military leaders to the crown. At the same time, the demands of war competed with the demands of building. The same agricultural surplus and labor that supported soldiers on campaign also supported temple construction, and the chapter returns to this tension in Section 38.25.

Section 38.7

Renewed Warfare against Champa

After the restoration, the Khmer court took the war to Champa. The main Khmer inscriptions record the king's victories in general terms, and the details are supplied by Cham and Vietnamese records, which are fragmentary and use different names and dates. The best-supported episode is the expedition of 1190, in which a Khmer force led by Sri Vidyanandana, a Cham prince who had been raised or had long served at the Khmer court, defeated Jaya Indravarman of Oi Vatuv at the Cham capital of Vijaya. The Cham king was captured, according to the records. Vidyanandana was installed as ruler in Vijaya under the title Suryajayavarmadeva, a king who owed his position to Khmer support.

The war's objectives were probably several. One possible objective was to neutralize Cham opponents involved in earlier campaigns against the Khmer court and prevent further attacks. A second was to secure access to the maritime trade of the South China Sea by controlling or influencing Cham ports. A third was political and ideological: the king presented as a restorer of the Khmer heartland could demonstrate power by intervening in Cham politics. A fourth, less often discussed, was to bind military leaders and the court through the rewards of war. Historians cannot say which motive predominated, and royal inscriptions give no reasoned strategic statement.

The war was not a single decisive campaign but a series of operations over many years. Cham politics was divided between the north-central region around Vijaya and the southern region of Panduranga, and Khmer forces and their Cham allies had to contend with revolts. The pattern of the conflict is better described as intermittent war and shifting political arrangements than as a straightforward conquest, a distinction taken up in Section 38.8 and in Chapter 45.

Section 38.8

Khmer Intervention and Political Authority in Champa

Khmer authority in Champa after 1190 was exercised mainly through Cham intermediaries. Rather than administering Cham territory through Khmer governors, the court seems to have installed or supported Cham princes whose position depended on Khmer arms. Vidyanandana's elevation is the clearest case. This arrangement offered advantages: it used local legitimacy, reduced the administrative burden, and allowed the king to claim overlordship. It also created a vulnerable system, because the loyalty of a Khmer-backed ruler depended on his ability to command support among his own people, and Cham elites could ally against him.

The resistance that followed demonstrates the limits of Khmer power. A revolt in Panduranga led by a Cham noble, known in the sources as Rashupati, challenged the Khmer-backed government in the early 1190s, and the political map of Champa was repeatedly redrawn. Cham records and Khmer inscriptions describe the sequence differently, and the chronology of the following decades, including the extent of direct Khmer control from the early thirteenth century, is disputed. Many historians believe that Khmer influence over parts of Champa strengthened again around 1203 and continued until about 1220, when Cham rulers reasserted independence and Khmer forces withdrew. The evidence for these dates is not firm, and the nature of the relationship, whether occupation, protectorate, or alliance, remains debated.

Khmer intervention also had cultural effects. Cham and Khmer elites already shared Indic religious traditions, and the war was accompanied by the movement of people, objects, and ideas. Cham captives and personnel appear in Khmer contexts, and Banteay Chhmar's reliefs suggest that Cham warfare was a major theme of royal commemoration. Neither side was passive: the Cham ruled their own politics, resisted, negotiated, and in the end recovered their autonomy. The episode shows that Khmer imperial power in this period was real but conditional, dependent on military success and local collaborators.

Cause and Effect

The Cham Occupation and the Reconstruction of Khmer Power

Long-term causes

Centuries of competition between Khmer and Cham polities; Khmer invasions of Champa in the 1140s; the Cham state's recovery and access to maritime trade; the absence of fixed rules of succession in the Khmer kingdom.

Short-term causes

The dynastic upheaval of the 1150s and 1160s, the usurpation of about 1165, and the Cham ruler's decision to mount a naval expedition into the Khmer heartland.

Immediate trigger and event

The Cham fleet's voyage up the Mekong and into the Tonlé Sap in 1177, the sacking of the capital, and the death of the reigning king.

Immediate effects

Political vacuum, regional resistance, the rise of Jayavarman VII as military leader, and his coronation in 1181.

Long-term consequences

Rebuilding of the capital as Angkor Thom, expansion of Buddhist patronage and infrastructure, a generation of Khmer intervention in Champa, and the commemoration of the war in monumental art.

Historical complexity

The invasion did not by itself cause the reconstruction. Jayavarman VII's own background, resources, religious commitments, and political choices shaped what followed, and the evidence for his motives is limited to royal self-presentation.
Section 38.9

Diplomacy, Frontiers, and the Wider Khmer Political World

The Khmer court's relations with other states are poorly documented, and most evidence comes from the other side. Chinese sources of the Song period record missions from Zhenla and discuss its products and customs, and a compilation of the 1220s lists many places as dependencies of the Khmer kingdom. Such lists must be handled with caution: they reflect what informants told Chinese officials, which may combine real political ties with claims and traditions, and they do not establish administrative control. Relations with Đại Việt, a neighbor of Champa to the north, appear less prominently in surviving records of this reign than the war with Champa.

The Khmer court's most reliable evidence of reach comes from its own monuments and inscriptions. Buddhist and royal foundations in the northwest, such as the temple at Phimai in what is now northeastern Thailand and the large complex at Banteay Chhmar near the present-day Thai border, indicate Khmer presence and royal investment. Images of the Buddha distributed to provincial towns, including Lavodaya (the Lopburi region) in the Chao Phraya basin, point to ties with communities to the west. Frontiers in this world were zones of overlapping influence rather than lines. Local rulers might pay tribute or accept titles from the king without being administered by him, and they could also look to other centers, a pattern examined in Chapter 54 with the rise of Tai-speaking polities.

Division III — Angkor Thom and the Monumental Building Program · Section 38.10

The Construction and Planning of Angkor Thom

Angkor Thom ("Great City" in modern Khmer) is the walled enclosure at the heart of the Angkor region, roughly three kilometers on each side and enclosing an area of about nine square kilometers. It is surrounded by a moat about a hundred meters wide and by a laterite wall, and it is entered by five gates, one on each of the north, south, and west sides and two on the east. Each gate is crowned by a tower carrying four faces. The causeways leading to the gates are lined with rows of stone figures, divine beings and demons, holding the body of a great serpent. The imagery is the Churning of the Ocean of Milk, a story long familiar in Khmer religious art, and the causeways turned the entrances into ceremonial passages.

Angkor Thom's relationship to earlier Yasodharapura is complex. Earlier kings had built extensively in the same area, and the new enclosure incorporated older structures, including the Phimeanakas, a pyramid temple connected with royal ritual and with the palace precinct. The usual reconstruction is that Jayavarman VII built or substantially remodeled the walls, moat, gates, and the central Bayon, while preserving earlier elements. How much of the city was built in the 1180s and how much was added later in the reign or afterward is debated. Some features, such as the Terrace of the Elephants, a long stone platform used for royal ceremonies, are generally attributed to this period with later modification. Chronology is difficult because inscriptions say little about the building of the city as a whole.

The plan combined defense, ceremony, and water management. A massive wall and moat protected the center from the sort of attack that had occurred in 1177, although the city's defensive value in practice is unknown. The gates and causeways framed the king's movement and display. The enclosure's relation to canals, reservoirs, and roads tied it to the wider landscape described in Chapter 35, and modern remote sensing has revealed the extent of settlement around it. Zhou Daguan's account, written in the 1290s, describes the walled city, its gates, and its palace and gives a sense of the city in operation, but it comes from a later period and should be used with that in mind. The reconstruction of Angkor Thom thus offers both an architectural and a political statement: a capital designed after a disaster as a secure, ceremonial center.

Section 38.11

The Bayon: Architecture and Royal Vision

The Bayon stands near the geometric center of Angkor Thom. It is a three-level temple in which the lower terraces carry galleries with relief carvings and the upper terrace is crowded with towers, with a central sanctuary rising above them. Its best-known feature is the face towers, which carry large carved faces oriented toward the cardinal directions. Roughly two hundred faces survive on the towers, though the original number was higher. The temple's plan is unusual among Khmer monuments: its towers are densely clustered, its galleries are irregular in plan, and its construction shows signs of changes of design during building. Mixed use of sandstone and laterite, and variations in carving quality, suggest that it was built in phases, sometimes under time pressure, and that parts were modified in later reigns.

The meaning of the faces is debated. Many scholars identify them as representations of the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara, the embodiment of compassion, whose worship was central to the king's religious program. Others argue that they are portraits of the king, either as a bodhisattva or in a manner combining royal and divine identity, since Khmer religious art had a long tradition of representing rulers and their ancestors in the form of deities. Others suggest a mixed interpretation in which the faces express a protective, universal presence that is at once divine and royal, or connect them with the idea of the four directions and the ruler's sovereignty over them. These readings are not mutually exclusive, and the evidence available, which consists of the architecture, the temple's inscriptions and images, and indirect parallels, does not settle the question. An important fact is that the king's own inscriptions identify Avalokiteśvara, under the name Lokesvara, as a principal object of royal devotion.

Three things should be kept distinct: the temple's original construction as a Buddhist royal foundation in this reign; architectural changes made during and after building; and later religious interventions. UNESCO and other scholarship describe the transformation of the Bayon into a Hindu setting under a later king, Jayavarman VIII, in the thirteenth century, with the alteration or removal of Buddhist imagery. Those interventions belong to a later reign and say little about the king's own intentions.

The Bayon's religious history was not simple. The central sanctuary housed a large Buddha image. Excavations in the 1930s found fragments of a Buddha statue in a well beneath the central tower, a discovery generally linked to a later reaction against Buddhist imagery, examined in Chapter 61. Some Buddhist carvings in the Bayon were altered or removed in later reigns, and the temple's reliefs include Hindu as well as Buddhist subjects. These facts caution against treating the Bayon as the expression of a single moment of design: it was built over time, adjusted as plans changed, and modified after the reign. It is best understood as a royal temple whose purpose was both religious and political, placing the king's devotion and the imagery of compassion at the center of the capital.

Section 38.12

The Bayon Reliefs and the Representation of Empire

The Bayon's outer gallery carries one of the largest relief programs in Southeast Asia. The reliefs are a combination of military scenes and scenes of ordinary life, and the combination is unusual. On the military side, they show processions of soldiers, elephants, and officers, land battles, and a naval battle in which boats crowded with fighters from two sides meet on water filled with fish. The two sides are distinguished by dress and headgear, and the Cham are often identified by a characteristic helmet or headdress, although identification of opposing sides is not beyond dispute. The naval scene is commonly connected to the Khmer victory on the Tonlé Sap, though the relief does not name the engagement, and it is impossible to say how far its details reflect a specific event.

Alongside warfare, the reliefs depict scenes of fishing, cooking, markets, games, cockfights, medical treatment, and processions. These scenes are valuable because they show activities of ordinary people that rarely appear in inscriptions, and historians have used them for evidence about boats, dress, food, trade, and entertainment. They also include figures often identified as Chinese through clothing and appearance, and who appear as merchants, soldiers, or residents, a reminder of the connections to the maritime world. But interpretation requires care. The reliefs were designed for a royal temple, carved by sculptors working to a plan, and the choice of scenes was shaped by what the court wished to represent. They may idealize order, harmony, and royal protection, and they leave out much that inscriptions or other sources suggest, such as dependent labor and hardship. Identification of particular scenes often rests on comparison and judgment rather than labels.

What the reliefs do offer is a record of how the court understood and presented the empire: a kingdom defended by disciplined armies, supported by a lively population, and sustained by religious order. They also reveal how war with Champa was remembered, as an episode worth displaying on the walls of the king's central temple. The relationship between the reliefs and the written record is therefore interpretive. Historians use the reliefs alongside inscriptions, archaeology, and Zhou Daguan's later account, asking in each case what the image shows, what it could not show, and for whom it was made.

Reading the Bayon as a Source

What the evidence is. The Bayon is at once a building, a sculpture program, and a historical document. Its plan, materials, and carving show how it was constructed. Its reliefs show military and everyday scenes. Its faces and images show religious aims. Alterations show what later generations did with it.

What it can establish. It shows that the king commissioned an enormous central temple within his new capital; that Buddhist imagery, especially of Avalokiteśvara, was central to its design; that war with Champa was publicly commemorated; and that Khmer artists represented the activities of ordinary people.

Section 38.13

Ta Prohm and the Commemoration of the Royal Family

Ta Prohm, east of Angkor Thom, was dedicated in 1186 under the name Rajavihara, "the royal monastery," according to the inscription found there. The temple is dedicated to the king's mother, Sri Jayarajachudamani, who is represented in the main sanctuary as Prajñāpāramitā, the Perfection of Wisdom, a Buddhist figure conceived as the mother of the Buddhas. This identification of a deceased family member with a deity continued an older Khmer practice of representing royal relatives in divine form, but it applied the practice to a Buddhist divinity. The temple was therefore both a family memorial and a Buddhist institution, tying the king's genealogy to the religion he promoted.

The Ta Prohm stele is one of the most important documents of the reign. Its Sanskrit verses trace the king's ancestry and describe his piety, and its Sanskrit verses also enumerate the personnel and resources attached to the temple. It records the numbers of priests, officiants, and assistants, including dancers, and the villages assigned to supply the temple, as well as quantities of offerings and a long inventory of precious objects. The numbers reported are very large: more than twelve thousand people are said to have lived at the site, and tens of thousands more were attached through dependent villages. Historians treat such figures with caution. They may reflect real registers, but they could include conventional or inflated totals, and the inscription does not say how the figures were compiled. Even if inexact, they show an institution of great size, whose function included religious service, resource management, and royal commemoration. The temple was later modified, and its present condition, famous for trees growing among its ruins, reflects centuries of abandonment and partial restoration rather than its original state.

Section 38.14

Preah Khan and the Monumentalization of Victory

Preah Khan, north of Angkor Thom beside the great northern reservoir known as the Jayatataka, was dedicated around 1191–1193 according to its stele (K. 908), conventionally cited as 1191; scholars differ on whether its Śaka year reads 1113 or 1114. It is dedicated to the king's father, represented in the main shrine as Lokesvara, the form of Avalokiteśvara associated with compassion. The temple's ancient name, usually read as Nagara Jayasri ("city of auspicious victory"), and the stele's content have led many scholars to link it with the commemoration of victory over the Cham, and some have suggested that it stands on or near a site of the war. This association is plausible but not secure, and it remains an interpretation rather than a documented fact.

The temple is a large rectangular complex with concentric enclosures, long galleries, and many shrines. The stele records a staff of many tens of thousands of people attached to the foundation, numbers comparable to or larger than Ta Prohm's and subject to the same cautions. It describes the king's gifts and the organization of rites and lists images placed in subsidiary shrines. It also records the distribution of an image called Jayabuddhamahanatha to a series of provincial towns, an act discussed in Section 38.17. Preah Khan combined institutional roles: it was a temple, a center of religious teaching, and, by its own description, a place with connections to the king's family. Its shrines included Buddhist, Shaiva, and Vaishnava dedications, a fact important for understanding religious continuity (Section 38.18).

Preah Khan illustrates the way monumentalization and commemoration worked together. A temple to the king's father, built beside a major reservoir and carrying a name that evokes victory, linked family, religion, and military success in a single institution. A different foundation, Preah Khan of Kompong Svay, a very large complex in the northeast of the Khmer heartland, shows that this scale of building extended to the provinces and is a reminder of how many sites of the reign lie beyond the Angkor core.

Section 38.15

Banteay Kdei, Neak Pean, Ta Som, and Other Royal Foundations

Beyond the major dedications, Jayavarman VII's reign saw the building or remodeling of many other temples, and the following examples illustrate the range. Banteay Kdei, near Ta Prohm, is a Buddhist temple with a plan of concentric galleries and carved face towers. Srah Srang, a reservoir opposite it, has an associated landing and terrace. Ta Som, a smaller temple east of Preah Khan, has gateways carved with faces and is characteristic of the secondary foundations built in this period. Their architecture shares a vocabulary of face towers, galleries, and enclosures, yet each differs in plan and scale, and attribution to this reign rests partly on style and partly on inscriptions.

Neak Pean is a small shrine on an island in a square pool, set in the middle of the Jayatataka reservoir and surrounded by four smaller pools. Its design is often interpreted as a representation of a sacred lake of Buddhist tradition associated with healing waters, and sculpture at the site includes a depiction of a horse that, in Buddhist narrative, rescues travelers, identified with Avalokiteśvara. The interpretation links the site to the hospital program discussed in Section 38.21, but this is a modern reading of the design rather than a statement in an inscription.

The same patronage extended far beyond Angkor. Banteay Chhmar, in the northwest, is a very large temple with extensive reliefs including war scenes and multi-armed images of Avalokiteśvara. An inscription associated with it commemorates a royal son and companions said to have died in battle, probably in the Cham wars, though the circumstances are uncertain. Phimai, a Buddhist temple in the Mun basin, was important in this period, and other foundations stand along the routes discussed in Section 38.20. The scale of this building across the Khmer world demonstrates that the reign's monuments cannot be understood solely through the capital. Taken together, the royal foundations form a network that tied religious, military, and commemorative aims across a wide region.

Division IV — Mahayana Buddhism and the Transformation of Kingship · Section 38.16

Jayavarman VII and Mahayana Buddhist Kingship

Earlier Khmer kings had patronized Buddhism but were generally associated with Hindu gods, especially Shiva and Vishnu. Kingship drew on Sanskritic ideas of the ruler's divine connection, and monumental temples such as Angkor Wat expressed the king's relationship to a god. Jayavarman VII altered this pattern. His inscriptions present a ruler motivated by compassion for the suffering of his subjects, a concern that echoes Mahayana Buddhist thinking about the bodhisattva, the being who postpones final liberation to help others. According to the Ta Prohm and hospital inscriptions, the king grieved at the illness of his people more than at his own. This language recurs across the reign, and it is part of how the court explained the monuments and institutions it built.

The relationship of this ideology to earlier kingship was one of adaptation rather than rejection. The Khmer practice of portraying the king and his relatives in divine form persisted, but with Buddhist figures; the king's temples continued to serve as foundations dedicated to ancestors; and the ritual role of priests and the court remained. Scholars have debated whether to describe the king as a "Buddha-king" or a bodhisattva-king, and whether the ideology amounted to a new theory of sovereignty or a rebalancing of older ideas. The sources give no systematic political treatise, and it is easy to overstate coherence. Inscriptions, sculpture, and building show a consistent emphasis on compassion, merit, and protective power, but they also continue to use traditional language of royal triumph.

A sandstone head found at Preah Khan of Kompong Svay and now in Phnom Penh, usually identified as a portrait of Jayavarman VII, shows a face with lowered eyelids in a calm, inward-looking expression. The identification is widely accepted, though not certain. If correct, it shows how the king chose to be depicted: not in the guise of a warrior but in a meditative, serene manner appropriate to a pious ruler. After his death he received a posthumous name, usually given as Mahaparamasaugata, "the supremely Buddhist," a title consistent with how his reign was remembered by the court.

Historical Vocabulary
Bodhisattva

Definition. In Mahayana Buddhism, a bodhisattva is a being on the path to buddhahood who, out of compassion, remains in the world to help others. Avalokiteśvara (called Lokesvara in Khmer inscriptions) is the bodhisattva of compassion.

Historical context. Bodhisattva devotion spread across Asia by the first millennium CE, and Mahayana forms of Buddhism were present in the Khmer world before Jayavarman VII. What was new in his reign was the prominence given to the bodhisattva in royal patronage and in the imagery of kingship.

Why it matters. The ideal of the compassionate bodhisattva gave the king a vocabulary for presenting hospitals, roads, and temples as acts of benevolence and merit.

Potential misunderstanding. Calling the king a bodhisattva does not mean that his subjects uniformly shared his religion, nor that his reign was gentle. Royal language of compassion coexisted with warfare and heavy demands on labor.

Section 38.17

Avalokiteśvara, Prajñāpāramitā, and Royal Religious Patronage

Two Buddhist figures dominate the religious program of the reign. Avalokiteśvara, in the form of Lokesvara, was the principal object of devotion in temples such as Preah Khan, where he was shown in the likeness of the king's father, and in the multi-armed images at Banteay Chhmar. Prajñāpāramitā, the personification of the Perfection of Wisdom, was honored at Ta Prohm in the likeness of the king's mother. Giving divine form to deceased parents tied personal piety to the foundation of temples and fulfilled the older Khmer practice of ancestor commemoration in a Buddhist register. It also associated the king's family with the central Buddhist concepts of compassion and wisdom.

A third element was the distribution of sacred images. The Preah Khan stele records that images called Jayabuddhamahanatha ("the Great Lord Buddha of Victory") were sent to a number of provincial towns, including places in the Chao Phraya basin such as Lavodaya. The scale of this act is notable, and the images presumably served as visible emblems of royal presence in provincial centers. Scholars interpret the distribution both as an expression of piety and as an instrument of political integration, linking local communities to the royal court through shared worship. The evidence does not say how provincial populations responded, nor whether the images were accepted as signs of loyalty or simply incorporated into local religious life.

Buddhist healing imagery also became part of the royal program. The hospital foundations were associated with the Buddha of medicine, as examined in Section 38.21. Royal family members, particularly queens and sisters, held roles as teachers and patrons, a pattern consistent with the role of Indradevi described in Section 38.3. Taken together, these patterns show that the reign's religious patronage was more than the king's private devotion. It was a public program that employed images, institutions, and personnel across the kingdom.

Section 38.18

Religious Continuity and Transformation

The reign did not replace Khmer religion with Buddhism. The inscriptions show Buddhist foundations but continued to list Hindu deities, Brahmin priests continued to serve at court and in temples, and earlier shrines remained in use. Preah Khan, with its Buddhist center and subsidiary shrines to Shiva and Vishnu, is the clearest example of a religious institution that was Buddhist in orientation but accommodated other traditions. The Khmer world had long combined Shaiva, Vaishnava, and Buddhist elements, as examined in Chapter 36, and the king's program should be understood as a shift in emphasis within an existing religious landscape.

Nevertheless, the change in emphasis was significant. A king who identified himself and his family with Buddhist figures set a different tone for the court. Buddhist monastic and teaching institutions received royal support, and the language of merit and compassion gained prominence. The effects on villagers and ordinary practice are less well documented, and local religious life, including spirit and ancestor cults, presumably continued with little regard for court theology. Some scholars suggest that the king's Buddhism reflected a broader religious change in the region, including growing interest in forms of Buddhism that were later to become dominant in the mainland, as examined in Chapter 53. However, the Buddhism of this reign was largely Mahayana in character, and the later Theravada transformation was a distinct development.

The reign's religious settlement did not survive unchallenged. After the king's death there were efforts to remove or alter Buddhist images, and the Bayon itself was modified, as discussed in Section 38.11. These later actions show that the religious change had been contested and that its patronage depended on the king. They should not be projected backward as evidence of widespread hostility during his reign, for which no contemporary evidence exists.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Khmer Kingship and Religion before and after Jayavarman VII

What Changed

  • Buddhist figures, especially Avalokiteśvara and Prajñāpāramitā, became central to royal temple dedications
  • Royal inscriptions stressed compassion, merit, and relief of suffering as qualities of kingship
  • Hospitals, rest houses, and distribution of Buddhist images became parts of a royal religious program
  • The capital's center was reorganized around a Buddhist temple with face towers

What Stayed the Same

  • Sanskrit and Old Khmer remained the languages of royal inscriptions
  • The practice of representing relatives in divine form persisted
  • Brahmin priests, Shaiva and Vaishnava shrines, and older ritual institutions continued
  • Temples remained economic and administrative centers supported by assigned villages

Pattern. Change was concentrated at the level of royal ideology and court patronage and occurred over a few decades, whereas continuity was greater in village religion and in the institutions of temple support. Court elites experienced the change most directly. After the reign, some of the change was reversed, which shows that it was contested and incomplete.

Section 38.19

Inscriptions, Royal Ideology, and the Language of Legitimacy

The inscriptions of the reign are the main source for its ideology, and they are written to persuade. Several themes recur. The king is presented as a restorer who rescued the land from disaster and as a protector who shares the suffering of his people. His genealogy is given in elaborate verse, joining him to earlier royal lines and the Buddhist merit of his parents. His foundations are described as acts of religious duty and public benefit. For a king who had come to power after a usurpation and a foreign invasion, such language had a clear function: it answered the question of why he, rather than another claimant, should rule.

The inscriptions also reveal rhetorical conventions that students should recognize. Sanskrit verses use elevated poetic language, comparisons to gods, and conventional praise, and they cannot be read as literal description. Inscriptions elsewhere also employ Old Khmer for administrative purposes, but the Ta Prohm and Preah Khan steles themselves enumerate resources in Sanskrit verse. Their numbers and lists may have symbolic as well as administrative purposes. Authorship is often attributed to named members of the royal family or court, and the learned women of the court are among the authors. The fact that several steles survive in different places allows historians to compare themes and spot repetition, but it also means that the king's own narrative is the one that has survived.

For these reasons, historians read inscriptions critically and combine them with other evidence. Archaeology can test claims about construction and layout, remote sensing can map settlement, and foreign accounts offer partial corroboration. Where such evidence is missing, the historian must say that the claim is the king's claim. This habit of distinguishing the language of legitimacy from independently verifiable fact is essential for the sections that follow, since infrastructure and institutions are described largely in the king's own words.

Division V — Roads, Hospitals, Institutions, and Imperial Resources · Section 38.20

The Royal Road Network, Bridges, and Rest Houses

Khmer roads existed before Jayavarman VII, but his reign is associated with a major expansion and organization of the network. Archaeological survey and aerial and satellite imaging have identified raised roadways radiating from Angkor toward the northwest, east, and other directions, including routes toward the Mun basin and Phimai and toward the east and southeast, in the direction of Champa. The best-known surviving structures are stone bridges, such as the corbelled bridge at Kompong Kdei on the eastern route. The roads were embankments of earth and sometimes laterite, and they crossed wetlands and rivers by causeways and bridges. They did not form a uniform highway system, and their dating and exact routes are still studied.

The inscriptions describe rest houses along the roads. The Preah Khan stele credits the king with 121 rest houses, called in Sanskrit vahnigrha ("houses of fire"), a term often interpreted as shelters where a fire was kept for travelers. Archaeologists have identified remains of buildings believed to be such rest houses at intervals along the main routes, in numbers broadly consistent with the inscription, though the interpretation of individual sites varies. Their function was probably to shelter officials, merchants, pilgrims, and soldiers, and to give royal presence along the roads, with an associated religious dimension, since building rest houses was an act of merit.

The road system served several purposes at once. It facilitated the movement of officials, orders, and armies; it supported trade and religious travel; it tied provincial centers to the capital and to the foundations where Buddhist images had been placed; and it provided a visible expression of royal care. The labor involved in building and maintaining roads would have been considerable, and no source indicates how it was organized. It is important not to read the network as a modern state's infrastructure. It was built and maintained by a court that relied on local communities, and its real effectiveness depended on the continued cooperation of those along the way.

Section 38.21

Hospitals, Healing, and Public Benefaction

One of the most distinctive features of the reign is its hospital program. The Ta Prohm stele credits the king with 102 hospitals, called arogyasala, and stone inscriptions found at many sites record the foundation of hospital chapels. Archaeologists have located the remains of numerous small laterite chapels, usually with an associated stele, in various parts of the Khmer world, and they are often identified as hospital sites. The hospital steles invoke the Buddha of medicine, Bhaisajyaguru, and express a wish that the foundation relieve the suffering of the sick. Their wording is consistent across sites, which suggests a common royal model.

What these institutions actually were is less clear than the inscriptions suggest. The stelae describe staff, including physicians and assistants, and supplies of food and medicinal substances, delivered regularly by assigned communities. They indicate that hospitals were funded by endowments and supported by the temple economy. But historians cannot say how many people were treated, whether treatment was effective by modern standards, or who had access. The inscriptions present the hospitals as for the benefit of the people at large, and the royal ideology of compassion made them a natural expression of kingship. Whether they functioned in practice as places of care, as ritual centers, or as a combination of both is difficult to determine from surviving evidence. Medical practice in the period drew on Indic medical traditions and local knowledge, and the foundations were intertwined with religious merit.

The hospital program illustrates how royal ideology and institutional practice were connected. By funding hospitals, the king gave concrete form to the claim that he shared the suffering of his subjects, and the program made his presence visible in provincial communities. It also tied those communities to the court through obligations of supply and service. Both aspects, benefaction and obligation, should be kept in mind. A hospital foundation could provide real benefit and also impose burdens on the villages assigned to support it.

Historical Vocabulary
Arogyasala and Vahnigrha

Definition. Arogyasala is a Sanskrit term for a "house of health," used in the inscriptions for the hospital foundations. Vahnigrha, literally "house of fire," is the term used for the rest houses along the roads.

Historical context. The two figures come from different inscriptions: the Ta Prohm stele records 102 hospitals, while the Preah Khan stele records 121 rest houses. They should not be attributed to a single text. Identification of surviving sites with these foundations rests on archaeological interpretation of small laterite structures and their associated inscriptions.

Why it matters. These institutions show how royal ideology of compassion was converted into permanent foundations spread across the kingdom.

Potential misunderstanding. The term "hospital" suggests a modern medical institution. The arogyasala was a Buddhist royal foundation whose precise medical practice is not documented in detail.

Section 38.22

Temple Economies and the Organization of Royal Foundations

The great foundations of the reign were economic institutions as well as religious ones. A temple like Ta Prohm or Preah Khan was endowed with villages whose inhabitants supplied rice, other foodstuffs, and goods, and with personnel who performed rituals, maintained the buildings, and ran the institution. The steles list these arrangements in detail: categories of priests and officiants, administrative staff, dancers, craftsmen, cultivators, and the amounts of regular deliveries. Some resources came from agricultural land, some from assigned labor, and some from the king's own gifts, including precious goods and cloth. The arrangement is an example of the temple-based economy described in Chapters 35 and 36, here at a very large scale.

Management of such foundations required officials, record-keeping, and regulations. The inscriptions refer to supervisors and to rules for deliveries and for the conduct of staff, and they imply that a body of administrators existed to maintain the system. Because temples were tied to the court, they served as channels through which the crown directed resources, rewarded loyal families, and gave status to appointees. At the same time, the foundations could accumulate wealth and influence, and a large temple's reliance on assigned villages might compete with other demands on those communities, including taxation and military service. The relationship between the crown's resources and the temples' endowments is poorly documented, and it is unclear how much of the wealth was new and how much was diverted from other uses.

Several cautions apply. The numbers reported are impressive but unverified; they may reflect real totals, notional allocations, or accumulated claims. The inscriptions do not show whether supplies reached their destination at the stated rates. Nor can they show how the system affected the ordinary people within it, a question taken up in Section 38.23 and in Chapter 39. What can be said is that the temple economy gave the king an institutional means of organizing labor and resources for religious ends and that its scale was large enough to affect the societies that supported it.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living and Working in a Royal Foundation

For many people the reign meant belonging to a temple or hospital as a worker, supplier, or dependent. The steles list hierarchies of personnel, from high priests and ritual specialists to dancers, attendants, cultivators, and craft workers. Some of these people lived at the temple, and others lived in villages that owed deliveries. For them the temple was an employer, a ritual center, and a claim on their labor and harvests. The Bayon reliefs show scenes of fishing, cooking, and market life, evidence of the ordinary activities carried on around the capital.

Historians must read this evidence with care. The same inscriptions that list dancers and hospital staff say nothing about how those people were recruited, whether service was inherited, or what their conditions were. The wider study of households, slavery, gender, and agriculture belongs to Chapter 39. For this chapter the point is that a program described in terms of royal benevolence rested on the daily work of many ordinary people, most of them unnamed in the sources.

Section 38.23

Labor, Construction, Taxation, and Imperial Obligations

Building the monuments and infrastructure of the reign required stone, workers, and transport. Sandstone for carving was quarried at a distance, in the hills northeast of Angkor, and moved to the building sites, probably by a combination of canals, rafts, and overland routes, while laterite was cut closer to hand. Skilled stonecutters, sculptors, carpenters, and masons were needed, along with unskilled labor to haul, dig, and build. The Bayon's variations in construction suggest that work proceeded at different paces and with different crews. The scale of workforce involved is unknown, and any specific number would be speculation.

The sources do not explain how this labor was obtained. Several mechanisms are possible and probably coexisted: obligatory service owed by villagers to the crown; the labor of people assigned to temple foundations, who were already bound to provide service; the work of professional artisans attached to the court or to foundations; and the labor of enslaved people, who are mentioned in Khmer inscriptions in other contexts and who may have included war captives. Whether and how people were paid or fed is unknown. Taxation, probably in kind, supported the court, and the demands of construction competed with those of warfare and agriculture. Historians debate how heavy the burden was, a question discussed in Section 38.25.

Imperial obligations also included the upkeep of roads, rest houses, and hospitals, deliveries to temples, and military service, all of which drew on the same population. The pattern was cumulative: a household might owe labor, supplies, and service to several institutions. Though the inscriptions present these obligations as part of a pious order, they were also claims on people's time and produce. Questions of how people experienced them, and whether they resisted, cannot be answered from the royal sources and require indirect inference.

Multiple Perspectives

Royal Building as Seen from Different Positions

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing what the evidence suggests about different groups. They are not quotations from named historical actors, and the groups did not have equal power or equal visibility in the sources.

From the perspective of the king and court: The temples, roads, and hospitals expressed religious merit, honored the royal family, answered the disaster of 1177, and demonstrated the legitimacy of the new reign. This is the perspective recorded in the inscriptions.
From the perspective of temple personnel, priests, and artisans: The foundations offered positions, status, and livelihood, and skilled workers such as sculptors and priests could gain from royal patronage. They also depended on the continuation of royal support.
From the perspective of villagers assigned to supply the foundations: Deliveries and labor were obligations that reduced the resources available to their own households, though the temples and hospitals could also offer ritual benefit and, perhaps, medical care. The sources give no direct voice to this group.
From the perspective of provincial elites: Royal foundations and distributed images could bring them prestige and ties to the court, but they could also mean closer supervision and claims on local resources.
From the perspective of Cham communities and rulers: The Khmer building program was financed in part by a power that intervened in Cham affairs and commemorated victory over Champa on its monuments, a very different significance from that seen by the Khmer court.
Division VI — The End of the Reign and Historical Assessment · Section 38.24

The Final Years of Jayavarman VII and the Succession

The chronology of the king's last years is poorly known. No inscription records his death, and the usual date, about 1218 to 1220, is inferred from the beginning of his successor's reign. The latest datable inscriptions of the reign date from the 1190s or early thirteenth century, and the building program seems to have continued for some years, though how long is uncertain. The king's age, which on the estimates given above may have approached ninety, suggests that the later years may have involved a reduced personal role for the king and a larger one for officials and family members, but this is inference.

The succession passed to Indravarman II, whose reign is dated approximately from the end of Jayavarman VII's to the 1240s. The new king is associated with Buddhist foundations, and the first years after the death show continuity of patronage. About 1220 Khmer forces withdrew from Champa, and Cham rulers regained independence. Whether the withdrawal was caused by the king's death, by Cham resistance, by the costs of the occupation, or by a combination is not established. The later thirteenth century saw changes in religious patronage, including actions against Buddhist images, and a different political context, with the rise of Tai-speaking polities and changes in the Angkor region, examined in Chapters 54 and 61. These later developments should not be read back into the last years of the reign as evidence that it was already in decline.

Section 38.25

The Benefits, Costs, and Sustainability of Imperial Reconstruction

Historians have long asked whether the building program was too ambitious. One argument is that the scale of construction, the size of temple staffs, and the continued warfare placed severe demands on labor, agricultural surplus, and administrative capacity, and that the long-term effect was to weaken the kingdom. Evidence offered for this view includes the speed and variable quality of some construction, the later religious reaction, and the changes of the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. A related argument looks to environmental and hydrological stress around Angkor, a subject examined in Chapter 61.

A different argument holds that the program was an investment in integration and legitimacy. Roads and rest houses linked the provinces to the center; hospitals and distributed images extended royal presence; temples supported priests, artisans, and administrators; and the building of a new capital restored confidence after a disaster. On this view the reign stabilized the kingdom for decades, and later changes had other causes. A third position emphasizes that the evidence is too thin to measure costs and benefits at all, since no fiscal data survive and the inscriptions are rhetorical.

A careful assessment recognizes that these positions are not entirely exclusive. The reconstruction clearly achieved military success and an enlarged institutional presence, and it also required very large resources. Whether the demands were unsustainable depends on assumptions about the size and productivity of the population that cannot be verified. What can be said is that the reign's achievements and its costs came from the same source, the capacity of the crown to mobilize labor and produce, and that evaluating one without the other distorts the history.

Historical Debate

Did Jayavarman VII's Building Program Overextend Khmer Society?

The question: Did the military campaigns, monumental construction, and institutional expansion of the reign place demands on Khmer society that contributed to later weakness, or were they a successful investment in stability and integration?

Interpretation A — overextension: The scale of construction, staffing, and warfare exceeded what the kingdom's resources could sustain over the long term, and the reign's intensity left the state and society strained. Supporters point to the number of foundations, the rapid and varied construction, and the later reaction against the king's religious program.
Interpretation B — investment and integration: The program restored legitimacy, created infrastructure, and tied a diverse territory to the court, and it left a kingdom that lasted for more than two centuries. Supporters note that Angkor remained a functioning capital well after the reign and that later changes had separate causes.
Interpretation C — the evidence cannot settle it: Without fiscal records and with inscriptions that serve royal purposes, any measure of cost or benefit is conjectural, and the question should be framed as a problem of evidence.

Evidence: The positions rely on the same material: inscriptions with figures for personnel and supplies, archaeological surveys of monuments and roads, remote sensing of the wider landscape, environmental studies, and later foreign accounts.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and evidence more than chronology. Historians differ on how far later developments can be traced to the reign and on how much weight to give the king's own figures.

Why it matters: The answer shapes whether Jayavarman VII is described as a culminating ruler, a reconstructive one, or a cause of later difficulty, and it affects how historians understand the relation between royal ambition and social cost.

Student task: Evaluate each interpretation, identify the evidence on which each depends, and decide what additional evidence would most help clarify the issue.

Section 38.26

Jayavarman VII and the Historical Transformation of Angkor

Jayavarman VII's reign transformed Angkor in several linked ways. Militarily, in the conventional account he reversed the crisis of 1177 and extended Khmer influence in Champa for a generation. Politically, he rebuilt royal authority on the basis of restoration and compassion. Religiously, he gave Mahayana Buddhism a central place in royal patronage while keeping other traditions alive. Architecturally, he rebuilt the capital as Angkor Thom and filled the Khmer world with new temples, roads, rest houses, and hospitals. Institutionally, he linked these by a temple-based economy that organized labor and resources on a large scale. The reign's meaning lies in the connections among these developments.

Students should also keep in mind the limits of what is known. Nearly everything comes from the king's own inscriptions and monuments, supplemented by foreign accounts and later archaeology. The figures are uncertain, the chronology of building is partly conjectural, and the experience of most of the population is largely absent. Scholars from the early twentieth century, working in the French colonial period, helped recover the inscriptions and monuments and created a portrait of the king as a great Buddhist ruler. Later research has revised many details and given more attention to labor, environment, and the wider region. In modern Cambodia, Angkor and its monuments have powerful symbolic meaning, and the image of the Bayon's faces has become widely recognized. These later meanings belong to the history of memory and should be distinguished from the evidence of the reign.

The reign opens questions that later chapters address. Chapter 39 examines the society and everyday life of the Angkorian world. Chapter 53 examines the Theravada transformation of the mainland, and Chapter 61 reassesses the changes of the thirteenth to fifteenth centuries without treating them as a sudden fall. The reign of Jayavarman VII neither caused nor prevented those changes, but it shaped the landscape, institutions, and memory with which later generations had to work.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Jayavarman VII's reign was an ambitious reconstruction of Khmer imperial power, in which military, political, religious, architectural, and institutional efforts were connected and in which achievements and costs came from the same sources. The reign began in crisis. After the death of Suryavarman II, succession became uncertain, a Buddhist king, Dharanindravarman II, was followed by Yasovarman II, and a usurper, Tribhuvanadityavarman, seized the throne about 1165. The political order proved vulnerable amid fighting with Champa, conventionally associated with an attack on Angkor in 1177. The familiar narrative of a fleet sailing up the Mekong, sacking the capital, and occupying it for years remains contested.

Jayavarman VII emerged from the resulting turmoil as a military leader of mature age, with experience in Champa and a family tradition of Buddhism. The evidence for his early life comes from inscriptions produced under his authority, which present his career favorably and must be read critically. After a period of resistance and campaigns, including a naval victory on the Tonlé Sap remembered on the Bayon reliefs, he was crowned in 1181. The sequence of events between 1177 and 1181 is not securely established, but the narrative of restoration became the foundation of his legitimacy.

The government of the reign rebuilt royal authority through a court drawing on family, priestly, and official networks and through temple foundations that linked provincial communities to the center. Military power rested on mobilization of troops, boats, and supplies, supported by roads and rest houses. The war against Champa resumed after the restoration. A Khmer force led by the Cham prince Sri Vidyanandana defeated Jaya Indravarman of Oi Vatuv at Vijaya in 1190, and for about a generation the Khmer court intervened in Cham politics through Cham intermediaries, facing revolts and, around 1220, a withdrawal. The pattern shows the real but conditional reach of Khmer power.

The monumental program centered on Angkor Thom, a walled city with a moat, five gates, and a central temple, the Bayon, whose face towers have been interpreted as images of Avalokiteśvara, of the king, or of a combination. The Bayon's reliefs depict warfare and daily life and are valuable but must be read as court-sponsored representation. Other foundations, including Ta Prohm, dedicated in 1186 to the king's mother, Preah Khan, dedicated in 1191 to his father, Banteay Kdei, Neak Pean, Ta Som, and Banteay Chhmar, extended the program across Angkor and the provinces and linked family commemoration, victory, and religious patronage.

Royal ideology was transformed by Mahayana Buddhism, which supplied a vocabulary of compassion, merit, and the bodhisattva. Avalokiteśvara and Prajñāpāramitā became central figures, images called Jayabuddhamahanatha were distributed to provincial towns, and the king was remembered with the posthumous name Mahaparamasaugata. This change was one of emphasis within a religious landscape that continued to include Shaiva and Vaishnava institutions, and it was partly reversed after the reign. Inscriptions served as the principal medium of the king's ideology, and historians read them for both information and persuasion.

Infrastructure and institutions gave practical form to the ideology. The road network, with bridges and 121 rest houses credited in the inscriptions, supported movement of officials, armies, merchants, and pilgrims. A program of 102 hospital foundations, dedicated to the healing Buddha and supported by assigned communities, expressed royal compassion and tied provincial villages to the crown. Temples operated as economic institutions with large staffs and dependent villages, though the reported numbers are unverified. All of these depended on labor and supplies drawn from the population, and the sources say little of how those obligations were experienced.

The end of the reign is poorly documented. The king died about 1218 to 1220, Indravarman II succeeded him, and the Khmer withdrew from Champa about 1220. Historians disagree on whether the building program overextended Khmer society, whether it was an investment in integration and legitimacy, or whether the evidence is too limited to decide. The chapter has argued that the achievements and the costs must be considered together and that later developments, examined in Chapters 53 and 61, should not be read back into the reign as inevitable.

Finally, the chapter has emphasized method. The evidence consists of royal inscriptions, monuments, reliefs, archaeological survey, and later foreign accounts, and each has strengths and limits. Students should distinguish what the king claimed from what can be corroborated, and what contemporary evidence shows from what later tradition and modern interpretation have added. The reign is best understood neither as the work of a single genius nor as a prelude to decline, but as an episode in which a Khmer court, its officials, priests, soldiers, artisans, and dependent communities reshaped the kingdom after a crisis.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a state responded to catastrophe. The military crisis conventionally associated with 1177 might have been remembered only as a defeat. Instead, the court turned it into the foundation of a long program of reconstruction, and it did so by using every available instrument: arms, ritual, architecture, administration, and a reworked royal ideology. The reign shows that political legitimacy in the classical Southeast Asian kingdoms was not given but constructed, through acts that were at once military, religious, and material.

The chapter also reveals larger processes in the Khmer world. The relationship with Champa shows how neighboring polities shaped one another through warfare, intervention, and shared religious culture. The adoption of Mahayana Buddhism in royal patronage shows Southeast Asian courts selecting and transforming religious traditions to meet local political needs, a theme introduced in Chapter 1 and developed in Chapter 19. The temple economy shows how religious institutions served as instruments of administration and resource mobilization.

The reign's consequences were long and mixed. Its monuments, roads, and inscriptions shaped the Angkorian landscape and provided the evidence on which modern history rests. Its religious program was contested after the king's death, and the changes of the following centuries, including the Theravada transformation examined in Chapter 53 and the reassessment of Angkor's transformation in Chapter 61, took place in a world that this reign had helped build. The questions of cost and sustainability remain open because the evidence is limited.

For the textbook as a whole, the chapter carries forward the argument that Southeast Asian societies were historical actors that selected, transformed, and transmitted outside influences. Jayavarman VII's court drew on Indic religious and literary traditions, on Chinese trade and diplomacy, and on a long Khmer past, and combined them in forms that were distinctively its own. Understanding that process, and the evidence and uncertainties on which it rests, is the purpose of the chapters that follow.
