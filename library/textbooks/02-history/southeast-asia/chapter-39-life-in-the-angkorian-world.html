Life in the Angkorian World

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Angkor's stone temples survive because they were built to last. The houses, workshops, boats, and markets of the people who made Angkor function were built of wood, thatch, and bamboo, and have largely vanished.

Chapters 33 through 38 followed Angkor's kings, institutions, landscapes, and monuments. This chapter asks a different question: who cultivated the rice, carried the stone, wove the cloth, tended the shrines, and paid the obligations that sustained these projects? The organizing question is how the people who cultivated Angkor's land, maintained its institutions, produced its goods, and participated in its social and religious networks sustained one of Southeast Asia's most extensive historical urban civilizations.

The evidence is uneven. Old Khmer and Sanskrit inscriptions record donations, personnel, and obligations, but they were commissioned by elites and say little about ordinary routines. Archaeology of residential mounds, ceramics, and botanical remains, together with lidar mapping, reveals where people lived and what they used. The Bayon and other reliefs depict markets, boats, and cooking, but as selective artistic representations. The Chinese visitor Zhou Daguan, who accompanied a Yuan diplomatic mission to Angkor in 1296–1297, offers the only extended eyewitness description, yet it belongs to the late thirteenth century and cannot simply be projected onto earlier centuries. Throughout, this chapter distinguishes recorded practice, archaeological reconstruction, and informed inference.

The chapter does not retell Angkor's political history, hydraulic engineering (Chapter 35), religious institutions (Chapter 36), or royal reigns (Chapters 37 and 38), nor Angkor's later transformation (Chapter 61). It treats the Angkorian world as a society of farmers, fishers, artisans, dependents, women, and religious specialists, whose experiences varied by place, status, and period.

Recent research has changed the picture. Lidar surveys show that settlement extended across a vast region, while excavations have found occupation inside temple enclosures, and these results shift attention from monuments toward the people who lived among them. Archaeology reveals habitation, diet, and manufacture, but it cannot reveal legal status or belief unaided; inscriptions add names and obligations, but only for those whom elites chose to record.

The chapter is organized in six divisions: the evidence and settlement; food and environment; households and gender; hierarchy and labor; crafts and commerce; and religion, regulation, and change. Features appear beside the sections they illuminate, and the chapter closes by asking what endured across the Angkorian centuries.

The chapter also continues this textbook's commitment to treating Southeast Asian societies as historical actors. Angkor's farmers, artisans, traders, women, religious specialists, and dependents did not simply receive Indian religion or royal command; they cultivated, produced, negotiated, and adapted within institutions that they also sustained. Understanding them therefore completes the account of Angkor begun in Chapter 33.

Historical Context

Angkor developed from the early ninth century in the northern Tonlé Sap basin, where monsoon rainfall, seasonal flooding of the great lake, and sandy and clay soils shaped farming and fishing. Chapters 33 and 34 explain the political consolidation under successive kings; Chapter 35 describes the reservoirs, canals, and settlement systems; Chapter 38 treats the late twelfth-century building programs. For ordinary people, these developments meant new demands and new opportunities: temple estates that absorbed labor, markets that linked farms to ports, and religious institutions that offered both obligation and livelihood. Because modern Cambodia, Thailand, and Vietnam did not exist, the chapter follows the Khmer-speaking and neighboring communities within the Angkorian sphere rather than any modern national territory.

Two cautions frame the discussion. First, the sources are chronologically uneven: inscriptions span centuries, but the richest descriptive source, Zhou Daguan, comes from the end of the period. Second, the people described include many whose voices are absent, including commoners, women, and the enslaved, so reconstruction depends on careful inference from material remains and on reading elite texts against their purposes.

The Angkorian economy rested on exchanges among households, temples, officials, and rulers that cannot be described through a single model. Temples were religious centers, landholders, employers, and redistributors; households combined subsistence farming with craft and market activity; and obligations to institutions overlapped. Reconstructing this system requires keeping evidence about different groups apart before combining them.

Division I

Reconstructing the Angkorian Social World

Section 39.1

How Historians Reconstruct Everyday Life

No single source allows historians to reconstruct daily life at Angkor, and each category answers different questions. Inscriptions in Old Khmer and Sanskrit, surviving in large numbers from the Angkorian centuries, record foundations, donations, genealogies, personnel, and obligations. They are precisely dated in many cases and name individuals, villages, and goods, but they were commissioned by rulers, officials, and priestly families, and they were written for religious and legal purposes. They therefore tell us much about property and service among the elite and about those attached to temples, and little about routine household life.

Archaeology supplies another view. Excavations of residential mounds, hearths, ceramic scatters, and food remains, together with lidar mapping of fields, ponds, and embankments, show where people lived and what they used. These methods can reveal diet, craft production, trade goods, and settlement layout across the landscape, but they do not name people or establish their legal status, and perishable materials such as timber, thatch, textiles, and plant fibers are poorly preserved. The temple reliefs, especially those of the Bayon, depict markets, boats, cooking, hunting, and fishing, and are valuable for visualizing activities. They are artistic programs designed to convey religious and political messages, however, and select and idealize what they show.

Zhou Daguan's account is the only extended eyewitness description. A Chinese traveler attached to a Yuan diplomatic mission in 1296–1297, he recorded markets, houses, clothing, religious practice, justice, slavery, and social life in his Record of Cambodia. His text is invaluable, but it comes from the very end of the period studied here, was written by a foreigner who interpreted what he saw through Chinese categories, and survives through a manuscript tradition with its own difficulties. Historians therefore use it as one source among several: as a check on archaeological and textual inference for the late thirteenth century, and not as direct evidence for the ninth, tenth, or twelfth centuries.

Historians gain most when sources are compared. Women's role in markets, for example, is reported by Zhou, shown in reliefs, and consistent with household craft production, yet inscriptions are silent about it, so the conclusion rests on convergence among unlike sources. The method also carries risk: analogies from modern Cambodian village life can fill gaps, but they may import later practices into earlier centuries. This chapter therefore uses analogy sparingly and flags it when it appears.

Section 39.2

Population and Settlement Geography

Greater Angkor was not a compact walled city. Archaeological survey and lidar, beginning with early mapping projects and expanded by aerial laser surveys from 2012, revealed a vast low-density settlement complex covering hundreds of square kilometers. Temples, reservoirs, and canals were interspersed with rice fields, ponds, roads, and clusters of household mounds. Within and around the great enclosures, excavations have found evidence of residential occupation, including inside the Angkor Wat enclosure, where embankments and ponds indicate a grid of inhabited plots. The monuments that survive in stone were therefore set within a densely used and continuously inhabited landscape.

Estimating how many people lived in this landscape is difficult. A 2021 model-based study published in Science Advances estimated roughly 700,000–900,000 inhabitants for the Greater Angkor region in the thirteenth century, extrapolating from lidar-mapped settlement features and assumptions about household occupancy and density. Such figures are modeled reconstructions of a defined geographic zone, not documented totals, and other estimates differ. The estimates also depend on how many household features were occupied at the same time and over how long, which are among the most uncertain variables.

The settlement pattern also changed over time. The distribution of occupation, and the construction of reservoirs and temples, shifted over the centuries from the ninth century onward, with major concentrations at different moments around Roluos, the central Angkor area, and later foundations. Settlement was organized around temples and water features, and neighborhoods probably centered on temple precincts, ponds, and roads. This pattern is described in Chapter 35 as a form of low-density urbanism. Here it matters because it meant that most residents lived amid fields and water, not within a dense urban core.

Dating adds difficulty. Occupation of residential mounds is dated by ceramics, radiocarbon samples, and stratigraphy, but few mounds have been excavated, and the sequence of occupation over the centuries remains incomplete. Surveys indicate growth and shifts in settlement, yet they cannot show whether every mapped feature was occupied at once. Whether Greater Angkor should be called a city is also debated, as Chapter 35 discusses, and the label matters less than the evidence of extensive, long-lived habitation.

Historical Vocabulary
Low-density urbanism

Definition: A settlement pattern of extensive, dispersed occupation, mixing built-up areas with fields and water features. Why it matters: It explains why Angkor lacks a single dense urban core. Misunderstanding: Low density does not mean small population.

Section 39.3

Urban Life beyond the Royal Temples

Beneath the stone monuments lay the inhabited city. Houses, workshops, and market spaces were largely built of wood, bamboo, and thatch, and so they survive only as traces: raised mounds, post holes, ceramic scatters, hearth remains, ponds, and the lines of embankments and roads visible in lidar. This is why the monuments alone give a distorted impression of Angkorian urban life. They represent a small and exceptional portion of the built environment, concentrated on religious and royal functions, while the daily activities of most residents took place in structures that have disappeared.

Excavation and mapping nevertheless allow some reconstruction of how people used space. Residential plots were commonly associated with a pond, which supplied water and probably other resources, and plots were linked by embankments and paths. Production was not separated from living, since pottery, metalworking, weaving, and food preparation likely occurred in or near households, while kiln sites and other workshop areas show specialized manufacture at some distance from the densest occupation. Zhou Daguan describes markets in the city of the 1290s, and reliefs show market scenes, but the precise locations of ancient markets are uncertain.

Inhabitants experienced the city through water and movement. Roads, canals, and embankments connected neighborhoods to temples and fields, and Zhou describes processions, officials in public display, and crowds at ceremonies. The royal city of Angkor Thom and the great temples were centers of ritual and administration, but their surrounding neighborhoods held artisans, servants, and farmers whose lives depended on these institutions. Historians should treat the elite core and the wider inhabited zones as parts of one system rather than as separate worlds.

Space reflected rank. Zhou Daguan reports that officials' houses were larger and better appointed than commoners' dwellings, and that roofing materials and permitted dimensions reflected rank. He does not establish that elite houses generally stood closer to the royal center. The royal palace and temple precincts, however, were distinguishable monumental spaces. Archaeology can confirm differences in assemblages and plot arrangement in some areas, but it cannot yet map the social geography of the whole region. The city therefore combined planned ceremonial space and unplanned, organically growing neighborhoods.

Section 39.4

Villages and Local Organization

Most Angkorian people lived in rural or semi-rural communities, not in the royal center. Inscriptions record numerous named villages, often in connection with donations of land and people to temples, and they refer to local leaders, which indicates that communities had recognized heads who dealt with institutions and officials. Lidar and survey data show that households clustered around ponds, rice fields, and embankments, forming villages of varying size dispersed across the landscape. The balance between independently cultivating families and those attached to temples cannot be determined from these sources alone.

The relationship between villages and temple estates is central but incompletely understood. Donations to temples frequently included villages and their inhabitants, producing communities tied to temple obligations for rice, labor, and goods. Such communities probably continued ordinary village life, farming, fishing, and crafting, while also supporting the foundation. Villages not attached to temples would have had other obligations to officials or rulers. The inscriptions are most informative about the first group.

Village organization itself is largely reconstructed by inference. Headmen or local officials, kin groups, shared ponds and fields, and common religious sites probably structured community life, and later Khmer village institutions are sometimes used as analogies, though doing so risks projecting later practices backward. What can be said securely is that villages were not isolated: they were linked to temples, markets, and officials, and their inhabitants moved between these settings.

Villages differed by environment and function. Settlements near reservoirs, in flood-prone lowlands, and at the edges of forest and upland would have emphasized different mixes of farming, fishing, and gathering. Inscriptions sometimes record villages assigned to temples with particular products or services, which suggests specialization in some communities. These variations make it unsafe to describe a single typical Angkorian village.

Section 39.5

Movement between Town, Countryside, and Networks

Ordinary movement depended on water in the wet season and on paths, embankments, and roads in the dry season. Canals, rivers, and the Tonlé Sap carried boats, which are depicted in the Bayon reliefs and which moved people, rice, fish, and building materials. Roads and raised causeways connected temples and settlements, and carts drawn by oxen appear in artistic evidence. These networks allowed farmers to reach markets, fishers to supply inland communities, and pilgrims and officials to travel between towns and temples.

Residents' experience of movement differed from the infrastructure planning described in Chapters 35 and 38. Rulers built and maintained roads, canals, and rest houses as part of royal and religious programs, but villagers used these routes for daily purposes: visiting kin, trading, working in fields, and attending festivals. Some movement was compelled: people assigned to temples or construction projects, soldiers, and captives were moved by authority, while others traveled voluntarily for trade or pilgrimage.

Movement also tied Angkor to regional networks. Traders and travelers moved along rivers and across the Tonlé Sap to the Mekong and the coast, and along roads toward neighboring regions. Zhou Daguan himself reached Angkor by traveling through coastal waters and the Mekong system. The mobility of goods and people links the household world of this division to the commerce discussed in §39.26, and shows that Angkor's society was not enclosed within its territory.

Travel conditions varied by season and traveler. The wet season favored boats across flooded plains and the lake, while the dry season opened roads and tracks, and the choice of route affected cost, time, and risk. Royal rest houses and roads described in Chapter 38 served official and pilgrim traffic, but ordinary travelers may have relied more on local hospitality, markets, and informal routes. Evidence for these everyday journeys is indirect.

Division II

Agriculture, Water, Food, and Environmental Life

Section 39.6

Rice and the Agricultural Year

Rice was the staple of Angkorian life and the foundation of the surplus that supported temples, officials, and specialists. The agricultural year followed the monsoon: fields were prepared as the rains began, seedlings or seed were planted, crops were tended through the wet season, and harvesting, threshing, and storage followed as the rains ended. Pollen, phytolith, and charred-grain studies, together with lidar traces of embanked fields, confirm that rice was cultivated in and around Greater Angkor, but they say less about the precise methods, varieties, and yields used by particular households.

Cultivation conditions differed across the landscape. Fields near the Tonlé Sap depended on flood and recession, fields supplied by ponds and reservoirs could receive additional water, and others relied on rainfall alone. Each setting implied different timing and labor: some plots demanded intense work at planting and harvest, while others required management of water levels. Households therefore did not follow a single agricultural calendar, and the labor of family members, neighbors, and dependents would have been concentrated at different moments of the year.

Tools and animals were central to this work. Iron implements, wooden plows, sickles, and draft cattle or buffalo are consistent with the archaeological and artistic evidence, although wood and fiber tools rarely survive. Zhou Daguan reports that in the 1290s some places yielded several harvests within a year. This does not establish multiple crops on every field: flood-recession, reservoir-fed, and rain-dependent plots had different seasons and yields, and his remark reflects what a visitor saw in particular localities.

The scale of irrigation in Angkorian rice farming is itself debated. Some scholars emphasize that reservoirs and canals supplied fields on a large scale, while others argue that much rice depended on rainfall and local ponds, with large works serving supplementary, ritual, or flood-management purposes. Chapter 35 treats this debate in detail. For households, the practical question is timing: demands for service to temples or rulers could coincide with planting and harvest, so labor obligations and the agricultural calendar had to be reconciled, although the surviving evidence does not show how.

Section 39.7

Irrigation, Water Access, and Farming Communities

Water was both a resource and a source of dependence. Lidar and excavation reveal many thousands of ponds associated with household clusters, as well as embankments, canals, and larger reservoirs. Chapter 35 explains the hydraulic engineering; the question here is how communities lived with it. Households needed water for drinking, bathing, cooking, livestock, gardens, and rice, and the siting of ponds near residential mounds suggests that access was organized at the level of neighborhoods and families as well as by large institutions.

Maintenance posed continuing demands. Ponds silt up, embankments erode, and canals must be cleared, and inscriptions occasionally refer to the upkeep of water features within temple foundations, which indicates that responsibility could be assigned to attached communities. Direct evidence for village-level rules governing water use, or for disputes between neighbors, is lacking. Historians can reasonably infer cooperation and negotiation over shared works, but the details of authority, such as who decided and who was obliged to work, remain uncertain.

Water security was also vulnerable to climatic variation. Tree-ring and other climate records indicate episodes of drought and intense monsoon rainfall in later centuries, and such variability would have tested systems built for average conditions. Whether and how these episodes affected ordinary farmers during the height of Angkor is difficult to establish, and their role in Angkor's later transformation is treated in Chapter 61, not here.

Access to water was probably unequal. Households beside reservoirs or with their own ponds had advantages over those dependent on shared or distant sources, and elite and temple holdings may have enjoyed priority. The names and dedications of major reservoirs show their religious and royal significance, but the evidence does not describe how ordinary users obtained water or resolved shortages. Historians should therefore treat equitable access as an assumption to be tested, not as a finding.

Section 39.8

The Tonlé Sap and Aquatic Resources

The Tonlé Sap shaped the livelihoods of many Angkorian households. During the monsoon, the Mekong's flow causes water to move up the Tonlé Sap river into the lake, which expands severalfold and floods surrounding forest and plain; as the flood recedes, fish concentrate and are readily caught. This seasonal pulse supports one of the world's richest inland fisheries today, and the archaeological and artistic evidence indicates that aquatic resources were important in the Angkorian period as well.

Evidence for fishing comes from several sources. Fish bones recovered in excavations at Angkor-period sites show that lake and river fish reached residential areas, and Bayon-period reliefs depict fishing with nets, traps, and lines, as well as boats of various kinds. Zhou Daguan describes fish as abundant. Because fish spoil quickly, preservation by drying, salting, or fermenting is likely to have mattered for storage and transport inland, although the specific methods used at Angkor are inferred partly from later practice.

Fisheries also tied households to exchange. Fishing families on the lake and rivers could supply markets and temples, receiving rice, cloth, and other goods in return, which linked wetland and farming communities. The social position of these families is not well documented: some may have been permanent villagers, others seasonal movers, and some attached to temples or officials. Historians should avoid treating all aquatic communities as a single group.

Fishing and farming were complementary because their labor peaks differed. Fishing intensified as the floods receded, which in the dry season followed the rice harvest, so the same households could plausibly combine both activities and distribute work across the year. This reconstruction is an inference from the seasonal environment and from later practice, not a documented arrangement. Households closer to the lake may have depended more on aquatic resources, and those farther inland more on rice and gardens, producing regional differences within Greater Angkor.

Section 39.9

Gardens, Orchards, and Livestock

Beyond rice, households probably cultivated vegetables, fruit trees, and other useful plants in gardens and compounds. Zhou Daguan mentions fruits and vegetables, and betel and areca appear among social and ceremonial goods in the evidence of his period. Palms, including sugar palms, are likely to have supplied food, drink, and building materials, though ancient documentation is thinner than for rice and varies by period.

Animals played several roles. Cattle and buffalo provided traction for plowing and carts, pigs and poultry were raised for food, and fish and game supplemented diets. Faunal remains from excavations and depictions in reliefs, such as butchery, cockfighting, and hunting scenes, support this general picture, but they cannot reveal the proportion of households keeping each animal or the quantities consumed.

Diversification reduced risk. A household that combined rice, garden produce, fishing, livestock, and gathered forest foods could withstand the failure of one source better than one relying on a single crop. Yet this reconstruction is partly inferential, since perishable plant remains survive poorly and inscriptions concentrate on goods relevant to temples and elites.

Evidence for gardens and animals is uneven across the landscape. Archaeobotanical sampling has been limited, and plant remains from tropical soils preserve poorly, so statements about particular crops or livestock proportions are provisional. Differences in soil, water access, and distance from markets likely shaped what households grew and kept. Elite and temple gardens, which inscriptions mention as endowments, may not represent ordinary household practice, and historians should avoid treating them as typical.

Section 39.10

Forests, Uplands, and Gathering

Forests and uplands supplied materials that lowland farmers could not produce. Timber, fuel, resins, honey, wax, aromatic woods, and wild game were gathered from forested areas, including the Kulen uplands northeast of Angkor, and entered markets and temple supply systems. Resins were useful for caulking boats, for lighting, and for finishing objects, while timber supported construction and daily life. Zhou Daguan lists products such as feathers, ivory, horn, beeswax, and resins as goods from the interior, though his list reflects a late thirteenth-century Chinese interest in exportable commodities.

The people who gathered and traded these products were diverse. Upland communities spoke different languages, followed different livelihoods, and maintained varying relationships with lowland centers. Some exchanged goods voluntarily, some were drawn into tribute or obligation, and some were targeted for capture, as the evidence concerning dependent labor suggests. Foreign and elite writers often described them in generalizing terms, so historians should resist treating them as an undifferentiated population or as passive suppliers.

Human use also modified forests. Fuel and construction timber, clearing for fields, and the demands of temple and urban growth altered vegetation around Angkor, and palaeoenvironmental studies indicate significant landscape change. How far these changes constrained later livelihoods is disputed and is explored in Chapter 61.

Forest and upland communities were diverse and included speakers of different languages, among them Austroasiatic-speaking groups discussed in Chapter 13. Specialized forest and iron-working communities known in northern Cambodia in later periods, such as the Kuy, suggest that comparable groups may have supplied Angkorian demand for iron and forest goods, but linking later communities to the Angkorian period requires caution. Relations between lowlands and uplands combined exchange, obligation, and coercion, and they varied by place and time.

Section 39.11

Food, Diet, and Seasonal Vulnerability

Most Angkorian households ate rice as the main food, supplemented by fish, vegetables, fruit, and, depending on access, meat and game. Cooking likely involved boiling and steaming in ceramic pots, with hearths located in or near houses. Excavated ceramics and food remains support this general picture, while ceremonial and market goods, including fermented or distilled drinks mentioned by Zhou Daguan, show that diet included more than daily staples.

Status shaped access. Elite households and temples could draw larger and more varied supplies, including meat, imported goods, and finer vessels, whereas poorer households probably relied more heavily on rice and fish. Inscriptions refer to offerings of rice and other foods to temples, which implies that large quantities were stored and redistributed, though how this affected the diets of attached communities is unknown.

Seasonal vulnerability was ever-present. Late wet-season and pre-harvest months could be periods of scarcity, and failed rains, floods, or conflict could disrupt supplies. Granaries and household storage reduced risk but did not remove it. Documentary evidence for famine in the Angkorian period is sparse and skeletal evidence limited, so historians infer vulnerability from environmental conditions rather than from recorded crises.

Storage and redistribution shaped food security. Temples received and stored rice and other supplies, as inscriptions describing offerings and provisions indicate, while households used jars and granaries. Zhou Daguan also mentions fermented drinks made from different materials. These sources show that food was processed, stored, and exchanged, but they do not show whether temple stores relieved shortage for surrounding households, and historians should not assume that they did.

Division III

Households, Family, Gender, and Community Life

Section 39.12

Houses and Material Possessions

Angkorian houses were mostly built of perishable materials, which is why they are known through traces and artistic representations rather than standing structures. Reliefs depict dwellings raised on posts, a form well suited to flooding, ventilation, and the use of the space beneath for storage or animals. Archaeological excavation recovers post holes, hearths, floors, and the debris of daily life, and these remains, together with the shape of residential mounds, indicate that households occupied small plots, each typically associated with a pond.

Differences in rank were visible in domestic architecture. Zhou Daguan reports that in the 1290s commoners' houses were thatched, while larger and better-built residences belonged to officials and the wealthy, and that tile roofing was restricted to the most privileged structures. Elite residences may have used more durable materials and more elaborate carpentry, but almost none of these buildings survive, so the contrast between commoner and elite housing is better documented in texts and reliefs than in excavated remains.

Household possessions are known mainly from ceramics, stone and iron tools, grinding and pounding stones, beads, and storage vessels. Cooking pots, jars, and bowls, many made locally and some imported, show how food was prepared, stored, and served. Cooking probably took place in or near the house, and storage jars and granaries helped households manage seasonal shortage. The contents of a typical house can be reconstructed in outline, but the proportion of wealth held in perishable goods such as cloth, baskets, and wooden items cannot be recovered.

Houses varied with environment and resources. Residential mounds differ in size and arrangement across Greater Angkor, which may reflect differences in household wealth, family size, or function, although mound size is an imperfect proxy. Near water, raised dwellings suited flooding, while elsewhere conditions may have allowed different layouts. These variations caution against treating a single reconstruction of a house as typical of the whole region.

Section 39.13

Families, Marriage, Kinship, and Inheritance

Evidence for family life is strongest for elites. Inscriptions record genealogies, marriages, and the transmission of property, offices, and religious duties through family lines, as in the Sdok Kak Thom inscription's account of a priestly lineage. Descent was traced through both men and women, and marriage alliances connected priestly, official, and royal families. These records indicate that kinship was central to status and to access to institutional positions, but they describe a small and privileged portion of society.

Property and inheritance appear in inscriptions as matters of recorded transfer, donation, and sometimes dispute. Land, goods, and dependents could pass within families, and individuals could allocate property to temples, which shows that family property could be redirected to religious purposes. How ordinary households passed on land, houses, and tools is not documented, and later Khmer customs cannot simply be assumed to apply. Historians therefore speak of likely patterns of family inheritance without claiming detailed knowledge of rules.

Zhou Daguan describes marriage and household customs of the 1290s, but some of his remarks concern rituals that are difficult to evaluate and that a foreign observer may have misunderstood or reported secondhand. His comments on family life are best treated as evidence for how a Chinese visitor understood Khmer society at the time, to be compared with inscriptions and later tradition where possible. The central point is that the Angkorian family was a basic unit of labor, property, and obligation, but its internal organization across social levels remains only partly known.

Marriage also functioned as alliance. For elite families, marriages linked priestly, official, and royal lines and transmitted status and property, as genealogical inscriptions show. For ordinary households marriage likely joined labor, land, and kin networks, but without documentation this remains inference. The unevenness of the evidence is itself a finding: Angkorian sources preserve the family histories of those who could commission stone records.

Section 39.14

Women in Angkorian Society

Women appear in Angkorian sources in several roles. Inscriptions name women as founders and donors of temples, as holders and transmitters of property, and as members of priestly and official families. Royal women are especially visible: queens such as Jayarajadevi and Indradevi, associated with Jayavarman VII, appear in inscriptions, and Indradevi is recorded as a teacher of women in Buddhist foundations. These cases show that elite women could hold religious and educational authority, although they are exceptional records.

Women's economic activity is documented in different ways. Zhou Daguan reports that women conducted most trade in the markets of Angkor, and he adds that Chinese men who settled there often took local wives partly for their commercial skills. Reliefs also show women involved in market and household tasks. Women were thus central to local exchange, and probably to textile production, food preparation, and agricultural work, though the share of labor performed by women on farms and fisheries is difficult to measure.

Women's circumstances differed widely by status. Elite women could hold property and religious roles, commoner women combined household work with farming and commerce, and enslaved or dependent women, who appear in temple personnel lists as servants, performers, or workers, were subject to control by institutions and owners. Evidence for their own experience is scarce. Historians should avoid generalizing from the visible elite women or from Zhou's remarks on trade to claim a uniform high status for women in Angkorian society.

Care is needed in interpreting women's visibility. The women named in inscriptions are mostly elite donors, and artistic images show women in market and household settings without revealing their legal standing. Evidence for women's economic activity is strong, but evidence for their protection or disadvantage under law is thin. The sources support the view that women had significant economic and religious roles without establishing equality with men or uniform conditions.

Section 39.15

Childhood, Education, and the Life Course

Childhood is poorly documented. No source gives a systematic account of how Angkorian children were raised, and archaeology rarely allows identification of children's activities, although burials sometimes include children. It is reasonable to infer that children contributed to household work, such as tending animals, gathering, and helping in fields and markets, as they do in many agrarian societies, but this is inference, not recorded fact. Mortality in infancy and childhood was probably high, though skeletal evidence from Angkor-period burials is limited and cannot yet support firm demographic conclusions.

Education was linked to religious institutions. Temples and monastic communities provided instruction in Sanskrit, Old Khmer, and religious texts, and inscriptions show that literacy existed among priestly families, scribes, and officials. Indradevi's teaching role indicates that some women also participated in teaching. Zhou Daguan reports that families sent children to monks for instruction. Literacy appears to have been limited, and the great majority of the population probably learned their skills through household and community work, not formal schooling.

Old age and care are similarly obscure. Families presumably supported elderly members, and temples and attached communities may have provided some support for dependents, but no systematic evidence describes it. The life course of an Angkorian farmer, fisher, or artisan can be sketched only in outline, using the work cycles of agrarian life, kinship obligations, and religious rites, and historians must avoid inventing specific customs where the sources are silent.

Skills were probably learned within households and communities. Farming, fishing, boat handling, weaving, pottery, and metalwork require training from an early age, and apprenticeship in families or workshops is a reasonable inference, as is instruction in ritual and recitation for religious specialists. These inferences rest on analogy with other agrarian societies and on the evidence of specialized production, not on explicit sources, and the chapter treats them as plausible but unproven.

Section 39.16

Clothing, Ornament, Hygiene, and Recreation

Reliefs and sculpture show how Angkorian people presented themselves. Both men and women are commonly depicted wearing wrapped lower garments, often with the upper body bare, and elite figures wear elaborate jewelry, headdresses, and fine textiles, while soldiers, laborers, and servants appear in simpler dress. Zhou Daguan also describes distinctions of rank in clothing and ornament, and notes the importance of cloth as a marker of status. These images are partly idealized and shaped by artistic convention, so they indicate patterns more reliably than individual practice.

Hygiene and leisure appear in several sources. Zhou Daguan reports that people bathed frequently, including in rivers and ponds, and archaeology confirms the importance of ponds and water access in households. Bayon and other reliefs depict cockfighting, wrestling, games, and performances, and Zhou describes festivals and public entertainments in the 1290s. Such evidence suggests that recreation and communal celebration were part of everyday and ceremonial life, though the frequency and social range of these activities are uncertain.

Ornament and appearance carried social meaning. Beads, bracelets, and other objects found in excavations show that jewelry was not restricted to the elite, but materials and quality varied with wealth, and gold and fine stones were associated with high rank. Social customs, including etiquette toward superiors, were also marked in public display. Because reliefs, inscriptions, and Zhou all emphasize rank, historians must remember that the lives of commoners may have been more varied and less formal than these sources suggest.

Rank regulated some forms of adornment. Zhou Daguan indicates that in the 1290s the materials and ornaments people could display, including gold, varied with status, and elite sculpture shows lavish jewelry. Such distinctions suggest that dress and ornament were socially policed to some degree, though no comprehensive sumptuary code survives. The material record of beads and metal ornaments shows that ordinary people also adorned themselves with local and imported materials.

Everyday Life and Social History

This chapter's central subject is ordinary life. The surviving record favors elites and temples; historians must read inscriptions, objects, and foreign observation together and acknowledge silences concerning commoners, women, and the enslaved.

Division IV

Social Hierarchy, Labor, Slavery, and Obligation

Section 39.17

Social Hierarchy

Angkorian inscriptions and reliefs reveal a society stratified by office, descent, religious function, and service, but the labels they preserve do not map neatly onto modern class categories. At the top stood the king and royal kin, followed by high officials and the leading priestly lineages who served as royal preceptors and ritual specialists. The Sdok Kak Thom inscription of 1052 shows how one such Brahmin family held offices, lands, and dependents across generations through service to successive rulers. Below them, local officials and temple administrators mediated between court, temples, and villages, while the large majority of the population cultivated, fished, made goods, or served institutions.

Rank was made visible. Zhou Daguan, writing in the 1290s, reports that officials' standing was displayed through palanquins, parasols, and dress, and sculpture and reliefs distinguish elite clothing and ornament from that of laborers and soldiers. Such markers suggest that hierarchy was performed in daily public life, not only recorded in texts. They also remind historians that the sources most eager to describe hierarchy were produced by those who benefited from it, so that the view from below must be reconstructed indirectly from household archaeology and from the occasional glimpse of ordinary people in reliefs.

Sanskrit vocabulary of social order, including terms associated with the four classical Indian varna categories, appears in Khmer inscriptions, but this does not demonstrate that Angkorian society operated as a rigid caste system. Scholars generally read such language as part of the selective adoption and localization of Indian concepts discussed in Chapter 19: it supplied a prestigious idiom for elite status and religious function while local Khmer institutions of kinship, service, and dependence continued to structure daily relations. Mobility was possible, particularly through royal service, yet it was unevenly distributed, and evidence for commoner mobility is almost entirely absent.

Officials formed a layered administrative group. Inscriptions record titles of court and provincial officers, temple administrators, and judges, and they show that offices could be hereditary within families or granted for service. Offices connected to temples and to the royal court overlapped, so that the same family could hold positions in both. Understanding how these roles related to local communities requires care, since titles do not always reveal functions.

Section 39.18

Farmers, Landholding, and Agricultural Rights

Access to land is among the most important and least securely documented questions in Angkorian social history. Inscriptions frequently record the donation of rice fields, orchards, and whole villages to temples, often specifying boundaries, products, and the people attached to the land. Earlier inscriptions also record sales and purchases of land among individuals, which indicates that at least some people held transferable rights. These texts concern donors, officials, and institutions, however, and offer little direct information about how ordinary cultivating families held their plots.

Several arrangements plausibly coexisted. Households probably cultivated plots inherited within families, some land was held by temples and worked by attached communities, and rulers and officials held claims over land and its produce. The boundary between ownership, use rights, and claims to a share of the harvest is difficult to draw from the surviving evidence, and the modern distinction between private and public property may mislead. Foundation texts often invoke religious penalties for anyone who diverted endowed land or its produce, a reminder that such claims were contested and required protection.

Archaeology adds a different kind of evidence. Lidar and field surveys reveal patterns of paddies, embankments, ponds, and mounds that indicate how agricultural space was organized around households and water, and they show that cultivated land extended far beyond the temple zones. A small number of inscriptions record disputes over land, which implies that rights could be negotiated and litigated at least among those with access to authorities. How secure the typical farmer's position was, and how much changed between the ninth and thirteenth centuries, remains debated.

Temple estates were major managers of land. Endowments recorded in inscriptions placed fields, orchards, and communities under temple administrators, who presumably organized cultivation and the delivery of produce. How cultivators on such land were compensated, whether they paid fixed shares, or held plots in return for service is not stated clearly in the sources. The relationship between farmer and estate probably varied by foundation, region, and period.

Section 39.19

Taxation, Tribute, and Corvée

Rulers, officials, and temples mobilized goods and labor in several ways, but historians must separate documented arrangements from inference. Foundation inscriptions list the rice, oil, cloth, and other supplies that villages or estates were to deliver to temples for rituals and upkeep, and they name the personnel assigned to produce or transport them. Some inscriptions also record royal decisions to exempt foundations from claims by officials, showing that the demands of the state and of temples overlapped and sometimes competed.

Labor service was essential to building, transport, and military activity. The scale of construction described in Chapters 37 and 38, along with the roads, reservoirs, and canals described in Chapter 35, implies large-scale mobilization of workers, yet inscriptions rarely describe how people were selected, how long they served, or whether they were compensated. Reliefs depict soldiers, carriers, and builders, but they do not reveal the terms of service. Claims that a uniform system of taxation or conscription covered the whole population exceed the evidence.

Zhou Daguan supplies limited and late information about levies and obligations in the 1290s, so it cannot be treated as a description of earlier arrangements. The most defensible conclusion is that a layered system existed: temples drew supplies and workers from attached communities, rulers and officials drew on populations through personal and institutional ties, and many households owed obligations to more than one authority. For farmers, this meant that the seasonal calendar of rice cultivation had to be balanced against claims on harvest and time.

Exemptions complicate the picture. Royal decrees recorded in inscriptions sometimes protect foundations from claims by officials, so that burdens varied among communities. It is a reasonable but unproven inference that people not attached to exempt estates bore a heavier share of general demands, but the documents do not allow a systematic comparison. The evidence shows differentiated obligations, not a single burden borne equally.

Section 39.20

Slavery, Captivity, and Dependent Labor

Slavery and dependence at Angkor cannot be reduced to a single institution. The Old Khmer term kñuṃ appears frequently in inscriptions, and scholars translate it as slave, servant, or dependent depending on context. Individuals could become dependents through inheritance, debt, purchase, donation to a temple, or capture in war, and their legal and social positions varied accordingly. Inscriptions that list temple personnel often name individual dependents by sex and sometimes by origin or function, treating them as part of an endowment alongside land and goods.

Temple dependents occupied a distinctive position. Foundation texts commonly provide that people assigned to a temple should remain in its service and not be removed or transferred, which protected the foundation's workforce but also fixed individuals and their descendants in institutional roles. Some dependents performed skilled or ritual functions, while others cultivated land or carried out manual work. Their conditions probably differed widely from those of household slaves in private homes, whose treatment depended on owners and is far less documented.

Captivity is the most contested category. War between Khmer rulers and neighboring polities, including the conflict with Champa discussed in Chapter 38, almost certainly produced captives, and Bayon-period reliefs depict prisoners and foreign troops. Zhou Daguan, however, describes household slaves primarily as people from upland communities whom he says were obtained by capture or purchase, and his language reflects an outsider's assumptions about upland peoples. Historians should avoid applying his account of the late thirteenth century to earlier periods.

Dependents also appear, in rare cases, as actors rather than objects. Legal texts and disputes imply that people sought to preserve or escape status, and the repeated protective and punitive language of foundation inscriptions suggests that attempts to remove or divert dependents occurred. The voices of the enslaved themselves are not preserved, so reconstruction depends on the writings of those who controlled them.

Section 39.21

Specialized Labor and Institutional Service

Angkor's buildings, roads, and ceremonies depended on skilled and unskilled workers whose occupations are known from inscriptions, reliefs, and material remains. Temple personnel lists name cooks, gardeners, musicians, dancers, and ritual assistants alongside cultivators, and the largest foundations employed many such people. These records describe positions within institutions, not individual experience, but they show that temples were employers and organizers of specialized labor as well as religious centers.

Building trades are visible in the monuments themselves. Quarrying, transporting, shaping, and setting sandstone and laterite required organized teams, and carpenters produced the timber roofs, doors, and scaffolding that vanished long ago. Masons' marks and unfinished carving indicate that work proceeded in stages and by specialists, and the movement of stone by waterway and cart connected quarries, workshops, and building sites. Reliefs at the Bayon and elsewhere show porters, boatmen, and soldiers, though artistic conventions simplify their activities.

Military service formed another occupation. Soldiers appear prominently in temple reliefs, including foreign contingents, and warfare required supply, transport, and local recruitment. As with builders, the conditions of service are poorly documented: some workers probably served as part of obligations to temples or officials, others as skilled specialists with higher status, and others as coerced labor. The challenge for historians is to connect these occupations to the wider social hierarchy without assuming that all workers shared a single experience.

Temple personnel included performers and ritual attendants, many of them women. Dancers and musicians appear in foundation records and in sculpture, and their status within institutions is uncertain: they may have held honored roles, but they were also assigned to the temple and not freely employed. Because these categories blur skilled service and dependence, historians should treat them as examples of the range of institutional labor, not as evidence of a single status.

Division V

Crafts, Markets, Commerce, and Material Culture

Section 39.22

Markets, Traders, and Local Exchange

Markets connected households to one another and to wider networks, but they are poorly documented in Angkorian inscriptions, which concentrate on temple foundations. Our principal description comes from Zhou Daguan, who reports daily markets held in the morning, with sellers, mostly women, sitting on mats and offering rice, fish, vegetables, cloth, and other goods. Reliefs, especially at the Bayon, show market and trading scenes. Because both sources are late and selective, historians infer earlier patterns from archaeological evidence of local manufacture, imported goods, and settlement geography.

Exchange operated at several scales. By Zhou's account, small purchases were settled in rice or grain or in cloth, while larger transactions used precious metals, so a fully monetary economy should not be assumed. No securely identified Angkorian state coinage is known, and gold and silver were used by weight. Zhou also lists Chinese goods that local people valued, including gold, silver, silk, and ceramics. His view is that of a foreign observer in the 1290s, and he may have emphasized what interested Chinese traders.

Women's prominence in trade is one of the best-supported features of this evidence, but it needs to be read carefully. It is reported by Zhou, supported by artistic depictions, and consistent with women's roles in household production, yet it does not show that women controlled the commercial economy at every level or in every period. Market supervision by officials is suggested by Zhou's account but not documented in detail. What the evidence does support is a lively local exchange system in which households sold surplus food and handmade goods and acquired items they could not produce.

Inscriptions provide indirect evidence for exchange. Some records of land purchases specify payment in goods or metals valued by weight, and temple foundations list supplies acquired or delivered. Such texts show that value was expressed in several media and that property could be bought and sold, at least among those who appear in the records. They are less informative about everyday market transactions, which probably involved small quantities and left little written trace, so Zhou's account and archaeological finds must carry much of the weight.

Section 39.23

Textiles and Clothing Economies

Textiles mattered economically and socially. Cloth was needed for clothing, hangings, temple donations, and as a store of value, and it circulated in exchange. Inscriptions record gifts of cloth to temples, and reliefs and sculpture depict a variety of garments and patterns. Spindle whorls and, rarely, textile traces in excavations indicate spinning and weaving, but cloth survives poorly, so the scale and organization of textile production have to be inferred from indirect evidence.

Zhou Daguan associates weaving with women and describes locally produced cloth, mostly of cotton, while noting that more elaborate textiles were imported. He adds that silk-raising was introduced in his time by newcomers from the west, a remark that reflects late thirteenth-century conditions and may reflect his understanding of the history of silk production. Patterned cloths from India and elsewhere appear to have been prized, and their motifs influenced artistic representation, illustrating how local production and imports coexisted.

Textile economies were tied to rank and labor. Fine fabrics signaled status, and the supply of cloth to temples and courts required labor from households or attached workers. Women's weaving was probably a significant household contribution, but because weaving left little archaeological trace, its economic weight is difficult to measure. Historians must avoid assuming that textile production was either purely domestic or fully institutional; both patterns likely coexisted.

The timing of textile work likely fitted the agricultural year. Spinning and weaving can be done in household spaces during periods of lower field demand, which would have allowed women and other household members to combine textile production with farming, though this is inference from comparison with other agrarian societies. Demand for cloth came from households, temples, and elites, and the large quantities recorded as donations imply organized production or purchase, not only household supply.

Section 39.24

Pottery, Metalworking, and Household Industries

Ceramics are the most abundant evidence of manufacture. Kiln sites in and near the Angkor region produced stoneware vessels, including glazed and unglazed jars, bowls, and other forms for household and ritual use. These locally made wares appear widely in excavations of residential mounds, while Chinese and other imported ceramics show connections with distant markets. The distribution of kilns, workshops, and finds suggests specialized production serving both local consumption and regional exchange.

Metalworking was equally important. Iron was smelted and worked in the Angkorian world, and iron sources in the uplands supplied material for tools, weapons, and construction fittings, including clamps used in temple building. Bronze was cast into bells, ritual objects, and images, and other metals such as gold and silver were worked by specialists for elite use. Iron implements such as sickles, adzes, and knives affected agriculture and craft work, linking manufacturing to the rural economy described in Division II.

The organization of production remains partly uncertain. Some pottery and metalwork may have been produced by village specialists for local exchange, some by workshops attached to temples or elites, and some by itinerant craftspeople. Inscriptions mention artisans among temple personnel, which supports the existence of institutional production, but archaeology also shows small-scale household manufacture. As with other crafts, the evidence supports a plural system without allowing precise proportions.

Production and imports changed over time. Kiln studies suggest that locally made glazed stoneware became more prominent in the later Angkorian centuries, while imported Chinese wares appear widely in contexts of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. These patterns indicate shifting supply and demand, but they depend on excavation samples that are uneven across the region. Differences between elite and ordinary assemblages also appear in some contexts, suggesting that access to imported goods was socially graded.

Section 39.25

Stoneworking, Woodworking, and Building Skills

Angkor's monuments rest on a body of practical knowledge held by quarry workers, transporters, carvers, and carpenters. Sandstone, brought from quarries in the Kulen uplands, and laterite, taken from nearer sources, were extracted using iron tools and wedges and then moved by water and land. Evidence includes quarry remains, unfinished blocks and carvings, and the canal and waterway systems that carried material, all of which show how construction depended on logistics as well as design.

Carving and assembly demanded specialist skills. Sculptors executed reliefs and images, masons dressed and fitted blocks, and carpenters built scaffolding, roofs, and doors. The forms of stone architecture often reproduce features of timber construction, which suggests that carpentry traditions influenced masonry and that skilled workers moved between materials. Iron clamps and careful joining reflect technical solutions developed through experience. Unfinished sections of temples reveal working sequences and the division of tasks among teams.

Understanding these workers requires attention to training, organization, and status. Skills were probably transmitted through apprenticeship, family lines, or institutional service, but no source describes these systems directly. Some workers were attached to temples or the court, others may have been drawn from local communities for specific projects, and their relationships to patrons varied. The monuments therefore represent the accumulated expertise of many people, whose names and conditions of work are rarely recorded.

Building materials changed over time. Earlier Khmer temples often used brick, while sandstone and laterite became more prominent in later monuments, and brick production and bricklaying were their own specialized crafts with kilns and workers. The shift in materials affected labor demands, transport, and carving skills, and it implies changing relationships between building projects and the communities that supplied materials. The evidence, however, shows that brick and stone traditions overlapped across periods.

Section 39.26

Regional and Overseas Commerce

Angkor participated in regional and overseas exchange networks. Chinese records report that Cambodian missions visited the Song court in the twelfth century, and Chinese ceramics and other imports have been found in Angkorian contexts. Indian textiles, religious ideas, and artistic motifs also circulated, and the Angkorian world linked the Mekong and Tonlé Sap systems to the Gulf of Thailand and the South China Sea. Trade was not a one-way movement of foreign goods to a passive recipient: Angkor supplied products and shaped demand through its own institutions.

Exports and imports reflected the region's resources and needs. Forest products, aromatic woods, resins, feathers, and other goods from the interior and uplands were exchanged for ceramics, metals, textiles, and luxury items, while rice and fish moved within the region. Zhou Daguan lists goods that Chinese traders sought, but his list reflects the 1290s and Chinese demand. Rulers, officials, temples, and merchants all participated, and the extent of state control over trade is uncertain.

The benefits of commerce were uneven. Elites and institutions gained access to luxury goods and revenue, merchants and intermediaries profited from exchange, and households participated through local markets and the sale of surplus. Overseas trade did not automatically raise living standards for all, and it also exposed Angkor to external competition and changing routes, themes explored in Chapter 61. For ordinary people, regional commerce mainly mattered as a source of tools, cloth, and ceramics, and as an outlet for forest and agricultural products.

Intermediaries were central to long-distance exchange. Ports along the Mekong delta and the Gulf coast, and merchants from different communities, including Chinese and Cham traders, moved goods between Angkor and overseas networks, as Chapter 22 and Chapter 38 indicate for relations with Champa. How much trade was managed by the court, by temples, or by independent merchants is uncertain, and conflicts such as those with Champa could disrupt routes. Evidence from ports themselves is limited.

Division VI

Religious Practice, Social Regulation, and Historical Change

Section 39.27

Household Religion, Local Spirits, and Ancestors

Religious life extended well beyond the royal temples. Alongside the Hindu and Buddhist traditions patronized by rulers, communities honored local spirits tied to places such as ponds, trees, stones, and settlement mounds, and families remembered their dead. These practices left less written evidence than temple foundations, because inscriptions record state and institutional religion. Later Khmer worship of territorial spirits, known as neak ta, may continue older traditions, but its use as evidence for the Angkorian period requires caution, since continuity must be argued rather than assumed.

Ancestor-oriented piety is better documented at elite levels. Inscriptions record images and shrines dedicated in memory of parents and relatives, indicating that family remembrance was religiously expressed and that individuals used donations to honor kin. Burial and cremation practices, as revealed in excavations, show varied treatment of the dead, although the evidence is limited and cannot establish uniform customs. Zhou Daguan describes religious customs of his own day, including elite and popular beliefs in spirits, but his account must be read as the testimony of an outsider in the 1290s.

The relation between household religion and the great religious systems is an open question. Local practices probably coexisted with and were sometimes absorbed into Hindu and Buddhist frameworks, and temples may have incorporated local sacred sites. Historians should avoid presenting a sharp divide between elite and popular religion, as ordinary people participated in temple festivals and rites. Yet they should also avoid assuming that the religious worlds of villagers matched those described in royal inscriptions.

Because local spirits were tied to particular places, religious practice probably varied from community to community. A sacred tree, pond, or mound in one village would have no counterpart elsewhere, and neighboring settlements may have honored different sites and observances. This variation is difficult to document, which is one reason scholars use later ethnographic parallels with caution. Local religion also was not fixed: shrines and practices may have changed as communities moved, grew, or came under temple estates.

Section 39.28

Temples, Festivals, and Community Participation

Temples were centers of community life as well as of royal religion. They employed attached personnel, received offerings of rice and other goods, held festivals, and drew pilgrims. Inscriptions list the people and supplies assigned to rituals, which shows that religious activity depended on an organized workforce. Some inscriptions also record gifts by individuals of modest standing, indicating that participation was not restricted to the elite, though the scale of non-elite giving cannot be measured.

Festivals and processions were occasions of public participation. Zhou Daguan describes seasonal celebrations in the 1290s, including processions, performances, and displays, and reliefs depict ceremonies and entertainments. Pilgrimage destinations, such as sacred hills and major temples, attracted travelers whose movement connected regions. For ordinary people, temple events offered ritual, food, spectacle, and opportunities for trade, while also reaffirming the authority of those who sponsored them.

Participation involved obligation as well as devotion. People attached to temples worked for them as cultivators, servants, or specialists, and village communities supported ritual through deliveries of goods. The religious merit associated with donation also motivated giving by those who could afford it. Social experience of temples therefore varied: for patrons they were sources of merit and prestige, for attached communities sources of both livelihood and duty, and for visitors places of worship and festivity.

Access to temple life differed by place and status. Residents near the major temples could attend frequent rituals and festivals, while rural communities more often participated through local shrines, periodic pilgrimages, or deliveries of goods to institutions to which they were attached. Some festivals and processions probably drew crowds from across the region. The experience of participation therefore ranged from regular service to occasional visits, and the evidence does not support a single model of community involvement.

Section 39.29

Brahmins, Buddhists, and Ascetics

Religious specialists occupied diverse social positions. Brahmin priests performed Vedic and Shaiva or Vaishnava rituals, served as royal preceptors and temple officiants, and belonged to lineages that held offices and property, as the Sdok Kak Thom inscription shows. Buddhist monks and teachers maintained monastic institutions, and ascetics lived in hermitages recorded in inscriptions. These specialists taught, officiated at rites, and managed endowments, making them important participants in education and in the social order.

Zhou Daguan distinguishes several kinds of religious figures in the 1290s, including scholars associated with the court, Buddhist monks, and Shaiva ascetics, and he describes their dress and customs. Scholars generally read his description of monks as indicating Theravada practice at that time, which reflects the religious change described in Chapter 36 and later in the textbook. His observations provide a late snapshot of religious diversity that cannot be projected backward without evidence.

Religious traditions coexisted and interacted. Hindu, Buddhist, and local traditions shared sacred sites, patrons, and ritual vocabularies, and rulers favored different traditions in different periods. Such patronage affected which institutions received land and people, and therefore the livelihoods of those attached to them. Historians should avoid assuming that coexistence implied a lack of competition or that doctrinal differences did not matter to the people involved.

Religious specialists were also embedded in the rural landscape. Inscriptions record hermitages and smaller religious foundations in addition to major temples, and such establishments may have functioned as local centers for teaching, ritual, and sometimes cultivation, though the evidence for their economic role is limited. Their presence suggests that religious institutions reached beyond the capital region and that communities encountered priests, monks, and ascetics in varying forms.

Section 39.30

Law, Local Authority, and Social Regulation

Evidence for law and social regulation is uneven. Foundation inscriptions contain provisions for the protection of endowments, often supported by religious penalties and curses against those who diverted property or dependents. Such texts show that rulers and donors anticipated conflict and sought to enforce obligations through religious and institutional sanctions. A small number of inscriptions record litigation and judgments, indicating that formal procedures existed, at least for disputes involving property and status among those with access to authorities.

Zhou Daguan describes judicial practices of the 1290s, including ordeal-like procedures and punishments, but he is an outsider reporting what he observed or was told, and his descriptions cannot be assumed to apply to earlier centuries. Village-level authority is almost undocumented: headmen, elders, and officials presumably settled many disputes over land, water, animals, and family matters, but their decisions left no systematic record. The absence of evidence is itself significant, since it reflects the nature of surviving sources and not necessarily the absence of ordinary legal activity.

Conflict and discipline operated through several layers. Households negotiated with neighbors, communities with temples and officials, and institutions with rulers. Obligations of labor and goods were enforced by authority and by social expectation, and the evidence for resistance, such as flight by dependents, is indirect. Historians can therefore describe the framework of regulation and some of its mechanisms but should be cautious about claiming knowledge of how often, and how fairly, it operated.

Indian legal ideas were available to Angkorian elites, and Sanskrit inscriptions use legal and moral vocabulary drawn from Indian traditions. Inscriptions also record royal decrees in Old Khmer, suggesting that law combined local custom, royal authority, and borrowed concepts. As with religion, localization is the useful frame: these ideas were adapted to Khmer conditions, and how far they governed ordinary disputes is unknown.

Section 39.31

Continuity and Change in Everyday Life

Some aspects of everyday life changed markedly across the Angkorian centuries. Settlement expanded across the landscape, the number and size of temple foundations and their attached communities grew, and religious emphasis shifted from Shaiva and Vaishnava devotion to Mahayana Buddhist patronage in the late twelfth century and, by the time of Zhou Daguan, to a visible Theravada presence. Commerce with China and other regions also developed, as Chinese records and imported goods indicate. These changes are well supported by inscriptions, archaeology, and foreign sources.

Other features show striking continuity. Rice cultivation, fishing, household gardens, the keeping of animals, village crafts, and local religious practices provided a stable base of subsistence and community across the period. The seasonal rhythm of monsoon and flood framed everyday work throughout. Continuity in subsistence does not mean continuity in social conditions, however: obligations to temples and rulers, the numbers of dependents, and the distribution of land rights may have changed in ways the evidence only partly reveals.

Many questions remain uncertain. Whether living standards improved or declined for ordinary people, how secure land rights were, how many people were dependents, and how households experienced the late changes in religion and trade cannot be resolved with current evidence. This chapter has therefore distinguished recorded practice, archaeological reconstruction, and informed inference. The later transformation of Angkor's political and ecological systems is the subject of Chapter 61, and the regional history of the next sequence begins with Chapter 40.

Change also varied across the territory. The areas around Roluos, the central Angkor zone, and provincial centers experienced different timings of growth, and settlement outside the core is less studied. Provincial communities may have experienced Angkor's institutions more lightly or on different terms, so generalizations from the core must be provisional. Future survey and excavation beyond Greater Angkor will probably refine these conclusions.

Chapter Summary

Angkor was sustained by farmers, fishers, artisans, traders, religious specialists, and dependents living in a dispersed landscape of mounds, ponds, fields, and temples. Evidence from inscriptions, archaeology, reliefs, and Zhou Daguan must be combined carefully, since each is selective and some are late.

Rice and fish formed the material base, supplemented by gardens, livestock, and forest products. Households were typically built of perishable materials, and women appear prominently in commerce and property. Hierarchy was real, but dependence took varied forms, including temple attachment, inherited service, and captivity, which should not be collapsed into a single institution.

Taxation, tribute, and labor obligations existed, but their full scale and mechanics are inferred from partial records. Crafts, local markets, and regional and overseas trade connected households to wider networks. Local religion and temple participation shaped community life, while law at village level remains poorly documented.

Everyday life showed continuity in basic subsistence and change in institutions, a pattern that sets the stage for later transformations treated in Chapter 61 and for the next regional sequence beginning with Chapter 40.

The evidence base shaped every conclusion. Inscriptions document property, service, and personnel for those whom elites recorded; archaeology and lidar reveal habitation, diet, and manufacture without naming status; reliefs offer selective images; and Zhou Daguan describes the 1290s through a foreign observer's categories. The chapter therefore separated recorded practice, archaeological reconstruction, and informed inference.

Settlement was dispersed and low-density, with households clustered around ponds, fields, and temples. Population models, such as the 2021 estimate of roughly 700,000–900,000 for the thirteenth century, are reconstructions of a defined region, not documented totals. Villages were tied to temples, markets, and officials, and movement by water and road connected them.

Economic life rested on rice, fisheries, gardens, livestock, and forest products, supported by crafts and exchange. Women's prominence in markets, the plurality of dependent statuses, the layering of obligations to temples and rulers, and the participation of ordinary people in religious life show a society whose institutions depended on household labor. Whether most farmers were independent or attached remains debated.

Change and continuity coexisted. Temple estates, commerce, and religious emphasis changed across the centuries, while subsistence routines and local religious practice persisted. Many questions, including living standards, land security, and the scale of dependence, remain unresolved, which is why the chapter treats uncertainty as part of its argument.

Chapter Reflection

Angkor's monuments invite a story of kings, but its endurance depended on the labor and skill of people whose names rarely survive. Understanding them changes how historians interpret royal power: as dependent on, and negotiated with, communities of farmers, artisans, and specialists.

The chapter closes the Angkor-focused sequence. Later developments in Chapter 61 and the mainland histories beginning with Chapter 40 build on this picture of a society whose everyday foundations proved more durable than its monumental programs.

The enduring lesson is methodological as well as historical. Social history of a premodern civilization requires combining texts, objects, landscapes, and outside observation while recognizing their limits, and the people of Angkor remind us that large states depend on small households.
