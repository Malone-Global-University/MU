Society, Religion, and Commerce in Majapahit Java

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Majapahit was not only a court, a conqueror's reputation, and a list of tributaries. It was also rice fields and river crossings, salt-makers and indigo dyers, ferry districts and village elders, ancestral shrines and mountain sanctuaries, and households whose members left almost no writing of their own. This chapter asks how that society worked, and for whom.

In the year 1358 a charter issued in the name of King Hayam Wuruk dealt with the settlements that ran river crossings along the waterways of East Java. In 1366 a very different set of documents began to record privileges granted to a community engaged in specialized work: salt-making and other trades whose practitioners needed the king's recognition to keep their rights secure. A few years earlier, in 1359, the king had traveled through the countryside of eastern Java accompanied by an entourage, visiting shrines, hermitages, and villages, and a court poet named Prapañca later wrote it up in verse. These three kinds of record, a ferry charter, a series of occupational charters, and a poem, are among the most important surviving windows onto the daily structure of Majapahit society. Each was produced because someone in authority wished to confirm a right, praise a ruler, or fix an obligation. None was produced to describe ordinary life for its own sake.

Chapter 50 examined the political power of Majapahit: the careers of Gajah Mada and Hayam Wuruk, the claims made in the Deśawarṇana, and the long debate over how far the kingdom's authority extended beyond its Javanese core. This chapter turns from reach to foundations. How did villages produce the rice that fed a capital and its dependents? How were land, labor, and revenue organized? What did merchants and craft specialists do, and what did they have to pay or promise in order to do it? How were Śaiva, Buddhist, Brahmanical, and local religious institutions woven into economic life? And how differently did a village elder, a ferry operator, a court poet, a woman who traded in a market, a dependent laborer, and a royal minister experience the same kingdom?

The chapter's central argument is that Majapahit Java was a dynamic, differentiated society whose prosperity did not depend on tight political centralization. Rural communities, religious foundations, specialist trading groups, and local elites all possessed their own interests, rules, and resources. The court recognized, taxed, rewarded, and sometimes redirected these arrangements, but it did not invent them and could not command them at will. Royal charters are therefore best read as moments of negotiation between kings and communities, not as proof that kings controlled everything they mention.

The evidence is uneven and must be handled with care. Inscriptions in Old Javanese, mostly recording royal grants and confirmations, preserve a good deal of information about taxation, occupations, and local organization, but they speak chiefly about privileged beneficiaries. The Deśawarṇana is a court poem, composed in 1365, that combines real observation with panegyric and idealization. Temple reliefs and terracotta figures from East Java show clothing, activities, and social types, but images are artistic conventions, not photographs. Chinese accounts such as those of Wang Dayuan and, a little later, Ma Huan describe Javanese ports and commerce from the viewpoint of foreign visitors. Archaeology at Trowulan and elsewhere supplies material remains: bricks, ceramics, coins, water installations, and workshop debris. Historians reconstruct the society by combining these very different kinds of evidence, and they remain aware that ordinary farmers, laborers, and most women appear in them only indirectly.

This approach places Majapahit within the wider history of Southeast Asia that this textbook has been tracing. The agrarian foundations of East Java build on the long development of wet-rice societies discussed in earlier volumes, including the Central Javanese kingdoms of Chapters 30 through 32 and the East Javanese political traditions that culminated in Singhasari and the foundation of Majapahit in Chapters 48 and 49. The commercial world described here connects to the Strait of Malacca, the Spice Islands, Bali, and China, and anticipates the Islamic and maritime transformations of Part XII. Religion, in particular, must be understood as part of the economy and the social order, not as a separate realm of doctrine.

Finally, the chapter ends by preparing, but not narrating, the changes that Chapter 52 will examine. A society as productive and as locally rooted as the one described here did not collapse because of one failure, and its dynamism was one reason that political fragmentation could occur without social disintegration. Understanding how Majapahit society worked is the necessary foundation for understanding what changed after the death of Hayam Wuruk in 1389.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Identify the principal kinds of evidence for Majapahit society, including inscriptions, the Deśawarṇana, reliefs, ceramics, and archaeology, and explain whose lives each documents and whose it obscures.
  2. Describe village communities and local authority in East Java, distinguishing local obligations from royal privileges.
  3. Explain wet-rice agriculture, agricultural seasons, and water management, and distinguish documented local practice from unproven assumptions of centralized irrigation administration.
  4. Analyze sīma grants and related privileges, explaining why communities and religious foundations sought royal recognition and why a grant did not necessarily mean total exemption from obligations.
  5. Reconstruct the main features of taxation, tolls, and obligatory service, and assess who gained or lost from changes in collection arrangements.
  6. Evaluate the Biluluk and Canggu charters as evidence for specialized occupations, ferry districts, and the intersection of commerce and government.
  7. Trace the role of rivers, ports, and merchants in linking inland East Java with the wider maritime world, and assess the debate over whether Majapahit's economy was primarily agrarian or maritime.
  8. Interpret archaeological evidence from Trowulan while distinguishing excavated remains from reconstructions of a whole city.
  9. Distinguish Śaiva, Buddhist, Brahmanical, and local religious traditions and explain how court institutions, ancestral commemoration, and village practice interacted.
  10. Assess what sources can and cannot tell historians about women, marriage, property, and household labor.
  11. Analyze social hierarchy, dependence, and unfree labor without imposing a single rigid caste model.
  12. Connect Majapahit's internal social and economic life to cultural and commercial exchange across the archipelago.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did agricultural production, commercial exchange, religious institutions, social relationships, and cultural practices sustain Majapahit Java, and how differently did people experience this society depending on their position and location?
  2. What can royal charters tell historians about ordinary communities, and what do they systematically conceal?
  3. How much autonomy did villages and specialist communities have, and how should historians measure it from sources written by or for rulers?
  4. Why did people seek royal recognition of privileges, and what did kings gain by granting it?
  5. How did rivers, ferries, markets, and ports connect inland agriculture to maritime trade?
  6. How should historians distinguish idealized court representations of religion from the practices of temples, hermitages, and villages?
  7. What does the evidence suggest about women's roles in Majapahit society, and where does it fall silent?
  8. Can a society be economically dynamic and socially unequal at the same time, and what does Majapahit suggest?
  9. Why might economic and social vitality have continued even when political centralization was limited?

Historical Context

Majapahit emerged in the lowlands of East Java in the early 1290s from the wreckage of Singhasari, whose last ruler Kertanagara was killed in a rebellion and whose political legacy was contested during the Yuan expedition of 1293, as Chapters 48 and 49 recount. Its founder, Raden Wijaya, established a new court in the Brantas delta region, near the area now known as Trowulan. By the middle of the fourteenth century, under Hayam Wuruk (r. 1350–1389), the kingdom had become the most prominent political center of Java and a claimant to influence over many islands, a story told in Chapter 50. This chapter takes that political history as given and asks about the society beneath it.

The landscape of East Java shaped the options available to its inhabitants without determining them. The Brantas River rises in the highlands south of the Malang region, flows west and north through the Kediri area, and then turns toward the coast, dividing into branches near the lowland plain around Mojokerto before reaching the sea near Surabaya. Volcanic mountains such as Kelud, Arjuno, and Penanggungan deliver fertile ash and water to the plains and also impose hazards: eruptions, floods, and shifting river channels. This is a landscape in which a rice economy could flourish, but one that required constant adjustment to water, silt, and slope. Earlier rulers of the Brantas region, notably in the eleventh century, are known from inscriptions to have sponsored dams and flood control, and the region's agricultural tradition predates Majapahit by many centuries.

The economy of East Java also rested on its position on the sea. The Java Sea gave access to the Strait of Malacca, Borneo, Bali, the Lesser Sundas, the Maluku Islands, and China. Chinese sources, which become more detailed in the Yuan and early Ming periods, describe ships calling at Javanese ports and the Javanese as active participants in maritime commerce. Inland and maritime activity were not alternatives: the surplus of rice-growing communities could be exported or used to provision ships and traders, while imported goods circulated into the interior along rivers and roads.

Religiously, East Java inherited a complex tradition. Śaiva institutions had long been prominent in Java, as visible in the Central Javanese temples of Chapter 32. Buddhism, including Mahayana and tantric forms, had been strongly patronized, as seen in Chapter 31 and in the religious experiments of Kertanagara described in Chapter 48. Brahmanical specialists, hermits, and local religious figures coexisted with these institutions. Underlying them all were local traditions of ancestral veneration, sacred mountains, springs, and village deities, which had been present long before Sanskritic religion arrived. Chapter 19 discussed why historians describe this process as selective adoption and transformation rather than passive reception.

Students should keep three cautions in mind. First, Majapahit's "territory" fluctuated and was never a modern bordered state, as Chapter 50 emphasized. Second, the terms used in this chapter for institutions such as sīma are drawn from Old Javanese inscriptions and from modern scholarship, and their meanings changed across time and place. Third, the evidence is heavily weighted toward the court, religious foundations, and the fourteenth century, so the picture offered here is inevitably partial. The chapter aims to be honest about those limits while still reconstructing as much of the society as the evidence permits.

Section 51.1

Reconstructing Majapahit Society: Sources and Their Limitations

The historian of Majapahit society works with a body of evidence that is rich in detail about a narrow range of people. The most important documentary sources are inscriptions, usually engraved on copper plates or stone, in Old Javanese and sometimes in Sanskrit. Most record a royal decision: a grant of privileged status to a village, a religious foundation, or a group of specialists; a confirmation of earlier rights; or a settlement of a dispute. They typically name the ruler, the date in the Śaka era, the beneficiaries, the officials present, the obligations that were lifted or retained, and the ritual that sealed the grant. They survive because the beneficiaries had strong reasons to preserve them. A document that protected a community's rights was a valuable possession.

The second major source is the Deśawarṇana, a poem in the Old Javanese kakawin tradition completed in 1365 by Prapañca. It describes the capital, the royal family, the king's progress through eastern Java in 1359, religious establishments, dependencies, and ceremonies. It is by far the fullest contemporary description of the court. It is also panegyric: its purpose was to praise Hayam Wuruk and the order he was said to maintain. Poets employed conventions of idealization, and what the poem leaves out is as significant as what it includes. Other literary texts, including the Pararaton and later chronicles, were composed or reworked after Majapahit's heyday and reflect later concerns; they should not be treated as contemporary records.

A third body of evidence is visual and material. Reliefs on East Javanese temples such as Panataran show clothing, tools, buildings, ships, and narrative scenes, usually drawn from religious or epic stories. Terracotta figurines, house models, and vessels from the Trowulan area depict or embody everyday objects. Ceramics imported from China, and later Vietnam and Thailand, appear in excavations, as do coins, bricks, water pipes, and workshop waste. Foreign accounts add a fourth category: Chinese writers such as Wang Dayuan (writing around 1349) and Ma Huan (whose visits occurred in the early fifteenth century) described Javanese ports and customs, and Portuguese writers such as Tomé Pires described Java in the early sixteenth century, after Majapahit's decline.

Together these sources overrepresent elites, religious institutions, and communities with enough standing to obtain written privileges. They say relatively little about the majority of cultivators who never received a charter, about laborers, or about women outside the court. Historians therefore have to infer: from the general structure of a charter, from the occupations it exempts, from the recurring problems it addresses, and from the comparative evidence of better-documented periods. Such inference is legitimate but must be labeled as inference, not presented as direct testimony. This chapter will mark the difference throughout.

Source Method

Reading a Royal Charter as Social Evidence

A royal charter is a legal and ritual document, not a social survey. To use one as evidence for society, historians ask a sequence of questions.

Who commissioned and preserved it? Charters were obtained by beneficiaries who needed proof of rights. A community that lost a dispute, or that never sought recognition, left no document.

What problem did it solve? Grants confirm rights because rights were insecure. A charter confirming tax exemption implies that officials or rivals had been, or might be, collecting taxes anyway.

Who is named, and in what order? Lists of witnesses and officials reveal the administrative categories of the time, and also the hierarchy that scribes believed worth recording.

What is repeated formula, and what is specific? Many clauses are standard, including curse formulas against violators. Specific details, such as named occupations or particular payments, are likelier to reflect local conditions.

Where does the evidence stop? A charter tells us what the king confirmed. It does not tell us how often rights were honored in practice, nor how many similar communities lacked them.

Questions for students:

  1. Why might a surviving charter exaggerate the independence of the community that received it?
  2. What kinds of communities would be least likely to appear in the epigraphic record?
  3. How could archaeology help correct an imbalance in the written evidence?
  4. Why should a poem such as the Deśawarṇana be read differently from a charter?
Section 51.2

Village Communities and Local Authority

The basic unit of rural life in Majapahit Java was the settlement, referred to in Old Javanese sources by terms such as wanua or, in some contexts, thāni. Such communities varied in size and character. Some were clusters of households around irrigated fields in the river plains. Others were hamlets on hillsides, coastal fishing and salt-making settlements, or specialist communities attached to a temple or workshop. Because the sources do not give census data, no reliable estimate of typical village population can be offered, and historians should resist inventing a representative village.

Inscriptions mention village leaders under terms such as rāma, usually translated as "elders." Charters often list the elders of a village among the persons present when a grant was proclaimed, which suggests that they were recognized spokespersons for the community and participants in local decision-making. The evidence indicates collective local governance in matters such as the allocation of land, the maintenance of irrigation channels, local ritual, and the resolution of disputes. How far this governance was formal, and how far it varied by region, cannot be fully established from the surviving record.

Villages were connected to regional officials and ultimately to the royal court, but the relationship was layered. The kingdom was organized into districts and into territories assigned to members of the royal family and other notables, as described in Chapter 50. Between a village and a king lay intermediaries whose duties included collecting revenue, adjudicating disputes, and supervising services. A community's position depended on which of these intermediaries had claims over it, what privileges it had obtained, and what religious or lay institutions drew on its production. Two neighboring villages could therefore owe different obligations.

It is useful to distinguish three kinds of obligation. First were local obligations: contributions of labor and resources to communal tasks such as maintaining channels, paths, and shrines. Second were royal or official obligations: taxes, tolls, and labor services owed to the court or to its representatives. Third were obligations to religious beneficiaries: revenue or labor redirected by grant to a temple, hermitage, or monastic community. A single household might owe all three, and charters reveal that the proportions could be renegotiated.

The extent of village autonomy should not be overstated or understated. Villages were not independent republics, since kings could and did intervene, and communities that failed to meet obligations were vulnerable. But neither were they simply passive subjects. Charters show communities and corporate groups actively seeking recognition, and the very existence of elaborate procedures for confirming privileges implies that local actors had resources and standing to bargain. The Deśawarṇana, in describing the king's tour, presents villages as orderly and loyal; this is the poet's picture of ideal order, not evidence that no conflicts occurred.

Section 51.3

Rice Cultivation, Irrigation, and Agricultural Seasons

Rice was the foundation of the East Javanese economy, but rice cultivation took several forms. Irrigated rice fields, called sawah in Old Javanese and modern Javanese, produced the most reliable yields and supported the densest populations. Dry-field cultivation, including dry rice and other crops on unirrigated land, was also practiced. Inscriptions distinguish categories of agricultural land, which indicates that contemporaries recognized different land uses and tax liabilities. Other food crops, coconuts, fruit trees, and the tapping of palms for sugar and drink supplemented the diet, and fishing, especially in coastal and river settings, provided protein.

Wet-rice farming involved a sequence of tasks. Fields were prepared by plowing, usually with draft animals such as water buffalo, and by leveling and puddling the soil. Seedlings were raised in nursery beds and transplanted into flooded fields; weeds were cleared; water levels were adjusted; and the harvest was cut by hand, threshed, dried, and stored. Iron tools, including plowshares, hoes, and knives, were part of this work, and Trowulan has yielded iron implements as well as evidence of metalworking. Temple reliefs and later descriptions suggest scenes of agricultural work, though historians should not treat any single image as a full manual of Majapahit farming.

The timing of tasks depended on the monsoon. East Java has a pronounced dry season and wet season, and its rainfall is more seasonal than that of western Java and Sumatra. Farmers had to plan planting and water use around these rhythms. Later Javanese agricultural calendars are well documented, but the particular calendar associated with the nineteenth-century courts should not be projected back into the fourteenth century. What can be said is that ritual calendars, including the Indian-derived Śaka year and several indigenous week cycles, were widely used in Old Javanese inscriptions, and that agricultural ritual likely followed seasonal rhythms.

Water management deserves particular care. The older view that Javanese kingdoms were "hydraulic" states whose power rested on royal control of large irrigation systems has not been supported by the evidence for Java. Much irrigation in rice-growing regions is organized locally: communities maintain small weirs, channels, and distribution rules among themselves. Inscriptions from the Brantas region, including those from the eleventh century, record dams and flood-control works that kings sponsored or claimed to sponsor, and some charters mention diversion weirs. This shows that rulers invested in water infrastructure and gained prestige from doing so. It does not show that a royal bureaucracy managed daily water distribution. The most defensible reconstruction is of a combination: local water-sharing arrangements, occasional large works sponsored by rulers or religious foundations, and dispute settlement when conflicts arose.

Agricultural productivity also had environmental limits. Floods could destroy crops and shift river courses, volcanic eruptions could bury fields in ash while enriching soils over time, and siltation affected channels and harbors. Archaeologists working at Trowulan have found evidence of repeated flooding and sedimentation that buried parts of the site, and this helps explain why so much of the capital region now lies below the modern ground surface. A rice economy of this kind was productive but required continual labor to maintain.

Historical Vocabulary
Sawah, Tegal, and Agricultural Landscapes

Definition. Sawah refers to a bunded, irrigated or water-fed field used mainly for wet rice. Tegal refers to a dry field, typically unirrigated, used for crops such as dry-land cereals, tubers, or vegetables. Gaga is an Old Javanese term associated with dry rice cultivation.

Historical context. Old Javanese inscriptions sometimes distinguish such categories when describing land belonging to a community or foundation. The distinctions mattered because productivity, labor requirements, and taxation differed. Fields near rivers could be irrigated; fields on slopes or far from reliable water were more likely to be dry.

Why it matters. The terms remind students that the Javanese countryside contained several agricultural landscapes and that Majapahit's economy was not just "rice paddies."

Potential misunderstanding. Modern Indonesian usage of these words is a guide but not an exact equivalent to their fourteenth-century meaning, and the existence of a term does not tell us how much land of each kind existed.

Section 51.4

Landholding, Agricultural Expansion, and Rural Production

Land in Majapahit Java was held through overlapping claims. A cultivating household had practical rights to the fields it worked, secured by custom and by the recognition of its community. Communities collectively claimed territory, and the ruler asserted a superior claim in the sense that the ruler could grant the right to receive revenue from land to officials, family members, and religious foundations. Understanding landholding therefore requires distinguishing among the right to cultivate, the right to receive a share of produce, and the right to command labor, which were not always held by the same person.

Inscriptions often concern rights to revenue and obligations attached to land, not ownership in the modern legal sense. A grant to a temple, for example, might give the foundation the income that would otherwise have gone to the king or an official, and might forbid officials from entering the territory to collect other levies. The cultivators remained on the land and continued to work it, but their payments flowed to a different recipient and, in some cases, they became subject to different rules. This distinction explains why a village could gain from a grant, by escaping some collectors, or lose from it, if the new recipient was more demanding.

The historical sources suggest agricultural expansion, though measuring it is difficult. Clearing forest and scrub, building new bunds, and settling new areas increased the cultivated area. Inscriptions refer to the establishment of new settlements and the opening of lands, and rulers and religious institutions had incentives to encourage expansion because it increased revenue. Archaeological survey in the Trowulan region indicates dense occupation, but because the evidence is spread over centuries, it does not permit precise population estimates. Claims about the total population of Majapahit Java have been made, but no figure rests on secure evidence, and this textbook does not repeat one.

A further distinction is needed between legal claims and cultivation. A charter might assign revenue from a given tract, but the tract's actual yield depended on weather, labor, disease, and political security. A grant did not create a harvest. Conversely, communities might cultivate land for which no document survives. Historians reconstructing rural production must therefore avoid equating the number of land grants with the amount of land farmed.

Rural production was also more varied than rice alone. Households grew vegetables, raised poultry and pigs or buffalo, fished, gathered forest products, and produced goods such as mats, baskets, and cloth for use and exchange. Palm sugar and salt, which appear in the charters, were important in some localities. Households were thus engaged in mixed production, and the dividing line between "farmer" and "craftsperson" or "trader" was permeable. This diversity underpins the commercial and craft activity discussed in Division II and helps explain why the countryside could support specialists.

The central lesson of Division I is that Majapahit was sustained by productive rural communities whose organization, knowledge, and labor cannot be reduced to royal command. Historian Kenneth R. Hall, in his studies of exchange and state development in early Southeast Asia, has emphasized the interdependence of local leaders, village production, and the court, a relationship in which status and exchange reinforced one another. Students should hold this interpretation as one influential scholarly view that fits the surviving evidence well, while remembering that the evidence for ordinary farmers remains indirect.

Section 51.5

Sīma Lands, Royal Grants, and Privileged Communities

One of the most common subjects of Old Javanese inscriptions is the creation or confirmation of sīma. A sīma was a territory, community, or foundation granted a special status by royal authority, typically involving the redirection or removal of certain taxes and the exclusion of some officials. The grant was usually accompanied by ritual: gifts to the officials present, a ceremony that fixed the boundaries, and curses on anyone who violated the grant. The inscription itself was the durable proof.

Beneficiaries were varied. Many sīma supported religious foundations, such as temples, hermitages, and monastic communities, whose upkeep could thus be funded by the revenues of the designated land. Others benefited villages that had performed some service, such as maintaining a shrine, building a dam, or participating in a royal ceremony, or communities whose work was needed by the crown. Some grants confirmed arrangements that had been made by earlier rulers, which reveals how beneficiaries sought to renew their privileges when a new king came to power. The motives of the grantor were mixed: religious merit, political loyalty, the building of a network of supporters, and the practical gain of securing services.

Why did communities seek sīma status? The main reasons were security and revenue. Rights confirmed in writing and ritually witnessed were harder for officials, rival claimants, or successor rulers to challenge. A community freed from certain levies could retain more of its produce, and a religious foundation could ensure a stable income for its rituals and personnel. Sīma could also confer status: being a recognized privileged community raised a group's standing relative to its neighbors.

Students should avoid the shorthand that sīma means "tax-free." Charters typically list particular obligations that were lifted, and sometimes also those that were retained or that had to be paid to the new recipient. Obligations might shift from the crown to a temple rather than disappear. In some cases the community retained duties to perform specified services. The detailed language of exemption in charters can create a false impression of total freedom, whereas the inscriptions are, in fact, careful instruments that distribute rights and duties among several parties.

Sīma also had political significance. By granting privileges, kings acknowledged and incorporated local actors into a network of obligations. The court gained legitimacy and allies; beneficiaries gained protection. At the same time, each grant reduced the revenue directly available to the crown, so kings had to balance generosity against fiscal needs. The proliferation of privileged territories in some periods could be a source of tension between the court and officials who had previously collected revenue from those lands.

Historical Vocabulary
Sīma, Royal Privilege, and Fiscal Obligation

Definition. Sīma in Old Javanese inscriptions designates land or a community whose status was fixed by royal decree, usually involving specified rights, exemptions, and responsibilities. Drawya haji refers to royal wealth or revenue, and officials who collected it appear in charters as collectors of the king's due.

Historical context. Sīma charters are known from Central Java in the ninth and tenth centuries and continue through the East Javanese period, including Majapahit. Their formulas changed over time but retained the idea of a defined territory under protected privilege. A swatantra, a territory with a special independent status, is a related concept appearing in some Majapahit-era grants.

Why it matters. Sīma charters are the principal evidence for how the Javanese state and local communities negotiated revenue and rights.

Potential misunderstanding. A sīma was not necessarily a tax holiday or a fully autonomous territory. It was a legally defined arrangement whose specific content must be read in each document.

Section 51.6

Taxation, Revenue Collection, and Obligatory Service

The Majapahit state drew revenue from several sources: shares of agricultural produce, levies on crafts and commerce, tolls on movement, fines imposed in legal disputes, and obligatory labor or service. Because the sources are fragmentary, it is impossible to reconstruct a complete fiscal system. The inscriptions record particular dues because particular dues were at issue in a grant. What can be said is that revenue collection involved multiple layers of officials and that the court regularly delegated, redirected, and negotiated its claims.

Officials who collected revenue appear in charters as a recurring category, often listed among those who must not enter a sīma to levy dues. The delegation of collection to local officials and to beneficiaries, such as temples, reduced administrative burdens on the court but created intermediaries with their own interests. Revenue might be paid in kind, in labor, or in money, and by the fourteenth century Chinese copper cash appears to have been important in commercial transactions, as discussed in Section 51.8. The proportion of payments made in each form cannot be determined for the kingdom as a whole.

Obligatory service was another form of contribution. Communities might be required to supply labor for building works, transport, or the upkeep of shrines, and specialists might owe goods or services connected with their trades. Historians use the term corvée for such labor, but the term should not be taken to mean a uniform system. The actual burden depended on the locality, the community's status, and the particular grant or arrangement in force. Ferry operators, for example, as the Canggu charter indicates, had specialized duties tied to their strategic position on the rivers.

Tolls and market taxes affected traders and travelers. Inscriptions list payments associated with commerce, crossings, and the movement of goods, and they demonstrate that the court treated movement and exchange as sources of revenue. A change in who collected a toll, or in how much was due, could alter the profitability of an entire occupation. This is one reason that specialized groups sought charters confirming their arrangements.

The effect of revenue arrangements on different groups varied. Farmers paying a share of the harvest were exposed to bad years and to the demands of multiple claimants. Privileged communities gained security, but sometimes at the cost of assuming duties. Officials benefited from collection, while religious beneficiaries gained income. Kings gained loyalty and legitimacy but surrendered direct revenue. Because many of these changes are documented only when they were formally recorded, historians must be careful not to infer from the silence of the sources that other arrangements did not exist.

Cause and Effect

Revenue, Privilege, and Royal Authority

Long-term causes

The inherited Javanese practice of delegating revenue rights to officials, family members, and religious foundations; the diversity of local arrangements; and the court's dependence on intermediaries to collect dues across a landscape of rivers, villages, and ports.

Short-term causes

The desire of communities and foundations to secure rights at the accession of a ruler or after a dispute; the court's need for loyal supporters and for religious merit; and fiscal pressures arising from building, ceremony, and military activity.

Immediate trigger or context

A petition by a community or foundation, a dispute with officials, or a royal decision to reward service, often concluded at a ceremony in which the terms of the privilege were proclaimed.

Event or transformation

The issuing or confirmation of a charter that redistributed revenue rights, fixed or redirected obligations, and recorded the settlement in an inscription.

Immediate effects

Beneficiaries gained more secure income and status; officials lost access to some revenue; neighboring communities might feel pressure to seek similar recognition.

Long-term consequences

A landscape of overlapping jurisdictions in which royal authority depended on negotiation. This pattern could build loyalty but also limited the revenue and direct control available to the crown.

Historical complexity: Privilege did not simply weaken or strengthen the crown. Grants could cement alliances and also fragment fiscal capacity, and their effect depended on who benefited, how many grants were made, and how well the court could enforce the terms. No single cause explains the pattern, and the evidence does not allow a complete accounting.

Section 51.7

Craft Production, Workshops, and Specialized Occupations

Majapahit's economy supported a wide range of specialized occupations. Inscriptions list trades and services, and archaeology adds evidence of production. Documented or strongly indicated occupations include textile production and finishing, dyeing, pottery making, metalworking, construction in brick and stone, food processing, salt-making, oil pressing, and the preparation and sale of meat. Some of these were performed within households as part of a mixed economy; others were concentrated in particular settlements or associated with religious foundations or the court.

Textiles illustrate the importance of specialization. Cloth was produced for domestic use, for ritual and court dress, and for exchange. Processes such as bleaching and dyeing, including the use of indigo, required particular knowledge and materials and are attested as occupational categories in charters. Textile work was probably carried out by both women and men, but the sources rarely specify the gender of workers, and the division of labor cannot be reconstructed with confidence.

Pottery and brickmaking are among the best-documented crafts archaeologically. Trowulan has produced large quantities of terracotta: vessels, water pipes, roof tiles, figurines, and bricks. The scale and quality of brick architecture, including gates and temples, imply organized production and construction labor. Metalworking, including the casting of bronze objects and the working of iron tools, is attested by finds and by the demand for tools in agriculture and building. These crafts show that the capital region contained workshops and specialist producers, though the organization of such workshops, whether household-based, guild-like, or attached to the court, is poorly known.

Specialist groups sought royal recognition just as villages and temples did. Charters could confirm that a group might practice its trade under defined conditions, pay specified dues, and be protected from interference. The Biluluk charters, discussed below, are the most informative examples. They show that specialist work was embedded in a system of obligation and privilege, and that trades were not simply private enterprises but arrangements negotiated with authority.

The documentary evidence tells us what occupations existed, not how many people practiced them or how prosperous they were. Specialization could provide opportunity, but it could also leave workers dependent on patrons, officials, or religious foundations. Historians should therefore avoid romanticizing craft production as an unproblematic source of prosperity, and should note that the sources largely reflect the viewpoint of those who regulated and benefited from the trades.

The sequence of the Biluluk grants makes the negotiation visible. Kenneth R. Hall distinguishes a royal letter of 1366, addressed to local aristocratic families and concerning access to a saline spring and festival privileges, from later letters dated 1393 and 1395, addressed to artisans. In his reading, the first redirected certain dues from the local elite toward royal and ceremonial payments, whereas the later documents acknowledged the continuing position of local chiefs of trade and their claims over purchases and deliveries. The charters therefore record change as well as continuity: the court could alter who received income without abolishing locally entrenched commercial authority. They should not be read as one timeless inventory of occupations or one blanket commercial license.

Section 51.8

Markets, Traders, Currency, and Local Consumption

Markets were central to the exchange of food, tools, cloth, and other goods between rural producers and specialists. Old Javanese sources use words for market (pasar) and for merchants (including banyaga), and inscriptions from earlier periods mention market regulation and traders' officials. Later Javanese practice organized markets on a rotating five-day cycle among neighboring villages, and it is plausible, though not directly proven for Majapahit, that periodic markets operated in similar fashion. Larger and more permanent markets are likely to have existed in the capital region and in port towns.

Currency in Majapahit Java combined several media. Earlier Javanese kingdoms had used small gold and silver units that persist as accounting terms in inscriptions. By the Majapahit period, imported Chinese copper cash circulated widely, and hoards of such coins have been found at Trowulan and other sites. The Chinese observer Ma Huan, writing in the early fifteenth century, reported that Chinese copper coins were used in Javanese transactions. Terracotta savings vessels, often in the form of pigs and known today as celengan, from Trowulan suggest that coins were being accumulated by households, though the interpretation of individual objects should be cautious. The widespread use of cash points to monetized exchange in at least the commercial and urban sector, though barter and payment in kind continued.

Traders ranged from itinerant peddlers and women selling produce to wealthy merchants dealing in imported goods. The sources do not allow a precise ranking, but the existence of officials supervising merchants and of tolls on trade shows that the court recognized commerce as significant. Traders purchased local produce, such as rice, salt, and cloth, and sold imported items, including ceramics and metal goods, to consumers whose purchasing power varied widely. Evidence for who bought imported ceramics at Trowulan suggests that elites and some others in the capital region had access to such goods, but the archaeology cannot tell us what proportion of households did.

Prices and taxes are poorly documented, and historians should avoid constructing precise price series. Taxes on markets and movement are attested in general terms. Consumer goods included food, cloth, cooking vessels, tools, and ritual items, and the circulation of these goods linked villages to commercial specialists. A rural household might sell surplus rice or palm sugar, buy salt or cloth, and pay dues in cash or kind, so that participation in markets was a part of ordinary life for many, even if the intensity of that participation varied by locality.

The relationship between producers and commercial specialists was one of both dependence and opportunity. Farmers needed access to markets for goods they could not produce, and traders needed producers' surplus. At the same time, traders and officials who controlled access to markets could extract a share of the gain. Different participants thus had distinct interests, which the following feature explores.

Section 51.9

The Brantas River, Ferries, and Inland Transportation

In a landscape of rivers, canals, and floodplains, movement depended on crossings. The Brantas and its branches carried boats and rafts, and travel by road repeatedly met water. Ferries linked villages on opposite banks, connected the capital region with the countryside, and allowed goods to move toward the north coast. Control over crossings therefore mattered both for communication and for revenue: anyone who moved goods, animals, or people had to pass through the places where tolls could be levied and services required.

Roads and paths in Majapahit Java are known less well than rivers. The Deśawarṇana describes the king's progress in 1359 with carts and a large entourage, which implies routes passable by wheeled vehicles at least in the dry season. Bridges and causeways are mentioned in some sources, and archaeology at Trowulan has revealed canals, brick-lined channels, and roads or pathways within the settlement zone. Beyond the capital region, travel combined rivers, tracks, and coastal sailing. No reliable figures for travel times or distances should be taken from the sources, and this textbook offers none.

Ferry operators held an unusual position. They provided a service essential to commerce and administration, they controlled a chokepoint, and they interacted daily with merchants, officials, and travelers. The Canggu charter, discussed below, shows that the crown regulated these settlements and was concerned with the revenue they paid. Historians interpret the charter as evidence that the court sought to formalize and secure its income from river crossings, while also granting the ferry communities defined rights and privileges.

Inland transport linked agricultural hinterlands to ports. Rice, salt, cloth, and other goods moved downriver to the north coast; imported goods, from ceramics to metals, moved inland. The Brantas system thus functioned as an economic artery, and the places where it branched and where routes converged were natural sites for markets and settlements. The extensive scatter of archaeological remains at Trowulan, near the point where the river divides, is consistent with the importance of this junction, although the capital's location was also shaped by political and religious factors.

Hall's analysis of the 1358 Canggu and Terung charter sharpens this picture. Approximately seventy-nine ferry-crossing districts appear in the document; that number describes listed administrative crossing districts, not a census of boats or individual docks. The older dĕrwaya haji collections had involved payments through local landed intermediaries. Under the new arrangement a levy called pamuja linked ferry communities more directly to the crown and to ceremonies honoring royal authority. Hall identifies contributions involving cash, flowers, and textiles, whose significance was fiscal but also social and ritual. This was not simply a new fare charged to passengers. By weakening an intermediary's claims and recognizing ferry operators as a distinct corporate group, the monarch could increase access to revenues while allowing the operators greater standing in the political order. The precise consequences for individual ferrymen remain unknown.

Section 51.10

East Java's Ports and Coastal Communities

The rivers of East Java led to a coast dotted with harbors and river mouths. Among the best-known commercial centers of the northeastern coast were Tuban, Gresik, and the Surabaya region, the last associated in some sources with the settlement name Hujung Galuh. These are historical places whose extent, population, and political arrangements changed over time, and they should not be confused with the boundaries of the modern cities that bear their names. Coastal geography also changed: silt from rivers extended some shorelines and closed or shifted harbors.

Foreign observers provide the most direct descriptions. Ma Huan, who traveled with the Chinese fleets in the early fifteenth century, described Tuban, Gresik, Surabaya, and the inland capital, and he reported that the population included Muslim merchants from the west, Chinese settlers, and the indigenous Javanese. His account postdates the period of Majapahit's greatest prominence, but it reflects a commercial landscape that was evidently well established. Tomé Pires, writing in the early sixteenth century, described the same ports as centers of trade under local rulers, an arrangement that belongs to the aftermath of Majapahit's fragmentation, explored in Chapter 52. Students should therefore use these sources as evidence for the port world as it appeared around and after the later Majapahit period, not as snapshots of the 1360s.

Ports connected maritime shipping with inland trade. Goods from the interior, including rice, salt, textiles, and forest products, were gathered at coastal harbors for export or for redistribution within the archipelago. Imported goods were unloaded, taxed, and carried inland by river and road. The mix of people in port towns, including traders from other Javanese regions, from Sumatra and the Malay world, from Bali and eastern Indonesia, from China, and from Muslim communities of the Indian Ocean, made them culturally varied places. The presence of Muslim burials near the capital from the late fourteenth century onward points to the early arrival of Muslim communities in the Majapahit orbit, a process addressed in Chapters 52 and 62 through 65.

The relationship between the court and the ports was not simply one of control. Port authorities and local rulers had their own interests, and the degree of their dependence on the court varied. The court benefited from the revenue and the prestige of commerce, and it also needed ports to supply goods and to connect with the wider world. Over time the balance between inland court and coastal ports shifted, a development that is central to the story of fragmentation in the next chapter.

Section 51.11

Merchants, Overseas Commodities, and Commercial Networks

East Java participated in commercial exchange with the Malay world and Sumatra, with Bali and the eastern islands, with the Maluku Islands that produced cloves and nutmeg, and with China. The kinds of goods exchanged are known from Chinese and later European sources, from archaeology, and from references in inscriptions. Java exported rice and other foodstuffs, salt, and textiles, and served as a redistribution point for products from other islands. Imports included Chinese ceramics, metals, and cash, and goods from the Indian Ocean world. The role of Java as an intermediary in the trade in eastern Indonesian spices is emphasized in sixteenth-century sources, and the evidence suggests that it was already significant in earlier centuries, a subject developed further in Chapter 71.

Merchants were not a single class. Local traders operating in markets, itinerant dealers moving among villages, specialist communities dealing in salt or sugar, and wealthier merchants engaged in overseas trade all appear in or may be inferred from the sources. The Javanese term banyaga refers to traders in Old Javanese texts. Foreign merchants, including Chinese and Muslim traders, lived in or visited port towns. Intermediaries, such as brokers and officials, mediated between visiting traders and local producers.

Ceramics are valuable evidence because they survive. Chinese stoneware and porcelain from the Yuan and Ming periods, and later wares from Vietnam and Thailand, appear in excavations at Trowulan and other East Javanese sites. These finds demonstrate access to imported goods but must be interpreted with caution. A ceramic vessel does not reveal the identity or nationality of the merchant who brought it, nor does its presence in a household prove that the household's members were wealthy or foreign-connected. This chapter therefore does not infer individual merchants' identities from the pottery they left behind.

Shipping is less well documented. Java was known for large seagoing vessels, called jong in later sources, and Javanese sailors and traders traveled widely. How many such ships existed in the fourteenth century, and how they were owned and financed, is unclear, and later descriptions from the sixteenth century should not be projected backward without qualification. What is clear is that maritime trade was woven into the economy through the demand it generated for provisions, the revenue it produced, and the goods it brought.

The relationship between foreign trade and agricultural revenue is the subject of one of the principal debates about the Javanese economy. Did the kingdom's wealth rest chiefly on rice surplus and the labor of rural communities, or on revenues from overseas commerce? The question matters because it bears on how historians understand the sources of royal power, and it is explored in the following feature.

Historical Debate

Was Majapahit's Economy Primarily Agrarian or Maritime?

The question: Did Majapahit's strength derive mainly from the agricultural productivity of the East Javanese lowlands and the manpower it supported, or from its position in maritime commerce and the revenues of trade?

Agrarian emphasis: Scholars who stress agriculture point to the fertility of the Brantas plain, the density of settlement in the capital region, the many inscriptions concerning land and rice-field revenue, and the need for a large food surplus to support courts, temples, and specialists. On this view, commerce supplemented a basically agrarian economy.
Maritime and commercial emphasis: Scholars who stress trade, among them Kenneth R. Hall in his work on maritime trade and state development in early Southeast Asia, argue that commercial exchange, port revenue, and the control of trade networks were essential to state formation and to elite wealth. Studies of earlier Javanese markets, such as those by Jan Wisseman Christie, have also shown how significant market exchange and overseas trade could be in Java's economy.
Integrated view: Many historians now see the two as interdependent. Rice surplus could be sold, used to provision ships, or converted into goods for export, while commercial income and imports strengthened elites and supported agriculture and construction. The question is then one of relative weight, which varies by period, place, and group.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on inscriptions, archaeology, Chinese and European accounts, and the geography of Java. The evidence is fragmentary, and quantitative data on production, trade volume, and revenue are lacking.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and scale: what proportion of revenue came from each source, and which sector drove political change. It is not primarily a dispute about whether both agriculture and commerce existed.

Why it matters: If agriculture was foremost, then control of land, labor, and rural elites was central to royal power. If trade was foremost, then ports, merchants, and maritime relationships were. Chapter 52 will show why this matters for understanding fragmentation.

Student task: Select two kinds of evidence discussed in this chapter and explain how each could be used to support either interpretation. Then identify what additional evidence would help to decide between them.

Section 51.12

Trowulan: Urban Space, Households, Workshops, and Archaeology

The archaeological zone of Trowulan, in the lowlands near modern Mojokerto, is widely identified as the site of the Majapahit capital or its core. Remains are scattered over a wide area rather than concentrated in a single walled enclosure, and they include brick structures, gateways, bathing and water installations, canals, wells, ceramics, terracotta objects, coins, and the debris of workshops. The site has been investigated since the colonial period; among the early researchers was the Dutch architect Henri Maclaine Pont, who surveyed the area in the early twentieth century. Indonesian and international archaeologists have worked there since, and the site appears on UNESCO's Tentative List for World Heritage.

The built remains show a capital that used brick extensively. Monumental gateways such as Bajang Ratu and Wringin Lawang, the temple known as Candi Brahu, a large rectangular pool known as the Segaran, and the water structure known as Candi Tikus are among the best-known features. These structures confirm the existence of substantial ceremonial and civic building, and they correspond in general terms to the Deśawarṇana's description of a brick-walled royal precinct with gates. But the correspondence is partial, and many details in the poem cannot be matched with excavated remains.

Domestic and craft evidence is just as informative. Excavations have produced water pipes, wells, roof tiles, cooking and storage vessels, spouted vessels, figurines, and tools. Concentrations of particular materials, such as terracotta or metalworking waste, suggest zones of specialized production. Imported ceramics demonstrate commercial connection, and Chinese coins demonstrate monetary exchange. The scale of these finds supports the conclusion that the capital region combined court, religious, craft, and commercial functions, though the precise layout of neighborhoods and the social composition of its residents remain uncertain.

Interpretation requires discipline. Because the zone is large, was occupied over a long time, and has been affected by flooding, sedimentation, brick-robbing, and modern land use, much of the original urban fabric is lost or deeply buried. Excavations sample small portions of the whole. Historians and archaeologists therefore distinguish between what has actually been excavated and the speculative reconstruction of a complete city or palace. A map of Trowulan assembled from scattered finds is an informed inference, not a record of how people experienced the city in the 1360s.

Dating these remains presents a particular challenge. A brick gate may be assigned stylistically to a Majapahit phase, yet stylistic resemblance is less secure than a dated inscription recovered in a controlled archaeological context. Likewise, an imported ceramic identifies a commercial connection and can sometimes help date a deposit, but it does not by itself identify a merchant, a household's ethnic affiliation, or an exact route of shipment. Flood deposits, the reuse of old bricks, and disturbance by later construction can mix objects from different phases. The UNESCO Tentative List description is useful for locating known remains and recognizing the site's heritage value; it is a nomination document, not independent proof of every proposed urban reconstruction. These distinctions protect the chapter's social history from appearing more precise than the excavations permit.

Archaeological Study

Reconstructing Life at Trowulan

What was found. Brick gateways and religious structures; water installations and canals; terracotta pipes, vessels, tiles, and figurines; imported ceramics; Chinese copper coins; iron and bronze objects; and workshop debris.

What can reasonably be inferred. That the capital region was densely used over a wide area; that organized brick production and construction labor existed; that water was managed through canals, wells, and pools; that households and workshops produced and used a variety of goods; and that the community had access to imported ceramics and to money.

What cannot be established. The size of the population; the proportion of residents who were elites, craft workers, farmers, or dependents; the layout of the whole city at any single date; the identity of specific individuals or households; and the details of palace architecture, which was largely built of perishable materials and does not survive.

Cautions. Finds from different centuries are mixed in the same zone. Terracotta figurines and reliefs depict stylized figures and cannot be read as portraits. Ceramics show trade but not the nationality of the traders. Reconstructions in museums and drawings should be treated as hypotheses.

Section 51.13

Court Society, Ceremony, Literature, and Artistic Patronage

The Majapahit court was the center of a hierarchical aristocratic society. At its apex stood the king, surrounded by members of the royal family, many of whom held regional titles and territories, as discussed in Chapter 50. Ministers, officials, military leaders, religious dignitaries, and retainers filled out the court. Rank was expressed through titles, dress, seating, access to the ruler, and participation in ceremonies. Status depended on kinship, office, and royal favor, and it could change with political fortunes.

Ceremony was a vehicle of political and religious life. The Deśawarṇana describes court assemblies, including an annual gathering at which representatives from various regions attended the king, as well as processions, feasts, and rites. Such events displayed royal power and bound participants into a shared order. They also required resources: food, cloth, labor, and offerings, which were drawn from the economy described in earlier sections. The ceremonial calendar thus tied the court's cultural life to the productivity of rural and commercial communities.

Literary production flourished under court patronage. The kakawin is a form of Old Javanese poetry that adapts Sanskrit metrical and literary models to the Javanese language, and its subjects include episodes from the Indian epics, religious themes, and royal praise. Earlier East Javanese works such as the Arjunawiwāha belong to this tradition, and the Majapahit period produced writers such as Mpu Tantular, whose Sutasoma is a Buddhist-themed poem that also contains a line often cited for its expression of the compatibility of Buddhist and Śaiva truths. That line was later adopted in modern Indonesia as part of a national motto, a reinterpretation that students should distinguish from its fourteenth-century meaning. Legal and other texts also circulated, including a law code associated with the Majapahit period known as the Kuṭāramānawa, whose exact date and practical application remain debated.

Patronage connected culture and political prestige. Poets, artists, and builders worked for rulers, nobles, and religious foundations, and their work glorified patrons and linked them to sacred models. The temple reliefs of East Java, which draw on epic and mythological narratives in a distinctive style, reflect this patronage. Yet cultural production was not confined to the court; textile design, performance, and craft traditions flourished in other settings, though they are less documented.

The distribution of ceremonial expense also matters. Courtly display drew on food, textiles, performers, transport workers, craft products, and labor supplied from outside the palace. It could enrich producers whose skills were valued, but could also impose compulsory deliveries on communities with little voice in court decisions. A poetic description of magnificence therefore has two readings: it illuminates the public language of royal prestige, and it prompts questions about the social arrangements that made such prestige materially possible. For students, comparison with the fiscal charters offers a way to study this connection without assuming that the poet documented every payment or worker.

Primary Source Window

Court and Ceremonial Life in the Deśawarṇana

A standard modern edition and English translation is Stuart Robson, Deśawarṇana (Nāgarakṛtāgama) by Mpu Prapañca (Leiden: KITLV Press, 1995); an earlier extensive study is Th. G. Th. Pigeaud, Java in the Fourteenth Century (1960–63).

Context: The poem praises Hayam Wuruk, describes the capital and its ceremonies, recounts the king's 1359 journey through eastern Java and his visits to shrines, lists regions regarded as dependencies, and describes the commemorative rites for Rajapatni. It survives in manuscripts preserved outside Java, and a copy discovered in Lombok in 1894 drew wide attention to the work.

Editorial paraphrase (not a verbatim translation):

The poem portrays a splendid court in which the king presides over assemblies, feasts, and religious rites, in which learned and holy persons of different traditions are honored, and in which the countryside is orderly and prosperous as the royal procession passes through it.

Limitations: It is panegyric. It idealizes the king and the kingdom, omits conflict and hardship, and says little about ordinary cultivators, laborers, or most women. Its claims about dependencies and the extent of royal authority are debated, as noted in Chapter 50. Descriptions of religious practice reflect the poet's own Buddhist court perspective and idealized representation, and should not be assumed to describe rural practice.

Perspective: A learned court insider writing for royal approval. The perspectives of villagers, merchants outside the court, rivals, or the subjects of royal demands are not represented.

Section 51.14

Śaiva, Buddhist, and Brahmanical Religious Institutions

Religious life in Majapahit Java was institutionally varied. Old Javanese sources commonly distinguish major groups of religious specialists: Śaiva priests, Buddhist clergy, and ascetics or hermits, with Brahmanical specialists connected to ritual and learning. The Deśawarṇana and other sources indicate that the court recognized religious officials of both the Śaiva and Buddhist traditions, each with its own head, and that these dignitaries took part in royal ceremonies. The recognition of both reflects political and religious pluralism at the top of the kingdom.

These traditions were not one undifferentiated religion. Śaiva institutions worshipped Śiva and related deities, with their own texts, ritual specialists, and endowed domains. Buddhist communities, including Mahayana and tantric strands, maintained monasteries, shrines, and lineages. Brahmanical specialists performed rites and preserved Sanskritic learning. Each had distinct doctrines, ritual practices, and institutional interests. At the same time, they shared ritual language, offerings, and a common culture of royal patronage, and they overlapped in participating in the same ceremonies. Historians use terms such as "Hindu-Buddhist" or "Śiwa-Buddha" for the coexistence, but caution that these labels can mask real differences and tensions.

Religious institutions had economic dimensions. Temples, monasteries, and hermitages were supported by endowments of land and revenue, by gifts from patrons, and by the labor of dependents. They employed specialists, maintained buildings, hosted ceremonies, and sometimes educated students. Religious domains, referred to in sources by terms such as dharma and related words, held privileged status through grants like those described in Section 51.5. Such institutions were thus landholders, employers, and centers of learning, as well as places of worship.

Coexistence was also a matter of royal policy and doctrine. The idea that Śiva and Buddha represent compatible paths is expressed in literary works of the period, and rulers cultivated ties with both. Yet this textbook does not assume that all religious professionals or lay worshippers accepted a uniform synthesis. The process of mutual accommodation was uneven, varied by locality and institution, and was not simply an automatic merging. The religious plurality of Majapahit is better understood as a managed coexistence of distinct traditions, sometimes combining elements, than as a single blended faith.

The religious landscape also included Muslims, particularly in commercial networks and coastal communities. Stones bearing fourteenth-century dates near Trowulan and Troloyo have sometimes been interpreted as proof of early Muslim burials at the capital, but Hadi Sidomulyo has shown that the original function and funerary use of a number of these stones cannot be assumed from their dates or present locations. His reassessment does not rule out Muslims in the Majapahit heartland; it does warn against presenting every early dated stone as a securely identified Muslim grave. The fuller transformation of port and religious networks belongs to Chapters 52 and 62–65.

The institutional boundaries within this plural religious landscape deserve attention. A royal ceremony might bring Śaiva and Buddhist dignitaries into the same public setting, while the revenues and personnel of their individual foundations remained distinct. Religious offices provided knowledge, jurisdiction, and ritual authority, but a named clerical category does not establish the number of adherents across Java. The sources are particularly thin on the worship of people who neither joined a major foundation nor received a royal grant. Material traces of shrines and local sacred places can broaden the picture, yet they rarely disclose the precise beliefs of every visitor. The political importance of religious coexistence must therefore be separated from claims that all residents held identical doctrines.

Section 51.15

Royal Ancestors, Sacred Foundations, and Religious Endowments

Commemoration of royal ancestors was a central religious and political activity. Kings honored predecessors through shrines, images, and periodic ceremonies, and in doing so affirmed their own legitimacy. The Deśawarṇana lists sanctuaries associated with earlier rulers, including those linked to Raden Wijaya, Kertanagara, and earlier Singhasari figures, and describes Hayam Wuruk's visits to them. These shrines, often called dharma foundations, linked present rulers with a sacred genealogy that extended through Singhasari and earlier East Javanese dynasties.

The commemoration of Rajapatni, a royal woman of great importance in the early dynasty and the grandmother of Hayam Wuruk, illustrates the scale of such efforts. After her death in 1350, rites were conducted, and in 1362 a major commemorative ceremony (śrāddha) was held, which the Deśawarṇana describes at length. According to the poem, the ceremony involved elaborate offerings, the participation of religious dignitaries, and gifts distributed on a large scale. The resources needed to sustain such rites, including food, cloth, labor, and revenue assigned to the foundation that preserved her memory, show how religious commemoration drew on the economy.

Endowments funded the continuing observance of rites. A foundation might receive the revenue of designated villages or lands, the labor of specified communities, and offerings from patrons, allowing it to maintain priests or monks, buildings, and regular ceremonies. The relationship was reciprocal: communities that supported a foundation might receive protection and prestige, and the foundation's prayers and merit were believed to benefit the donors. At the same time, the redirection of revenue to religious foundations meant that part of the rural surplus supported religious institutions rather than the crown or officials directly.

These arrangements tied dynastic legitimacy to religious institutions. By commemorating ancestors and endowing foundations, rulers demonstrated piety and continuity, and religious establishments gained a stake in the dynasty's survival. This does not mean that religious motives were merely instrumental; the sources suggest genuine belief and ritual seriousness. But it does mean that devotion and politics were intertwined, and that religious resources were part of the political economy.

The example of Rajapatni demonstrates why commemorative ritual must be studied across time. Her death in 1350 and the elaborate śrāddha associated with 1362 in Prapañca's account were not a single undifferentiated event. The later observances expressed the continuing place of a deceased royal ancestor in the legitimacy of Hayam Wuruk's court, while the maintenance of sacred sites required ongoing provision from land, patrons, and specialized workers. The poem describes what ceremonies were supposed to signify; charters and material remains help illuminate the resources that sustained them. Neither kind of evidence supplies a full ledger of costs or establishes that all participating communities regarded the occasion in the same way.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Royal Religion from Singhasari to Majapahit

What changed? Political centers moved within East Java and the dynasty's identity shifted from Singhasari to Majapahit. Under Majapahit, ancestral commemoration extended to new royal figures, and the court's recognition of Śaiva and Buddhist officials was institutionalized in the sources of the fourteenth century. Literary production continued to evolve, and Muslim communities appeared in ports and near the capital.

What remained continuous? The practice of honoring royal ancestors with shrines and rites; the endowment of religious foundations through grants of revenue; the patronage of both Śaiva and Buddhist institutions; the use of Sanskritic models adapted to Javanese; and the underlying role of local divinities and ancestral cults.

When did change occur? Gradually, with moments of visible shift around the fall of Singhasari and the foundation of Majapahit in the 1290s, and again in the fourteenth century as the new dynasty developed its own commemorative traditions.

Was change gradual, uneven, abrupt, contested, or cyclical? Mostly gradual and uneven. The political rupture of 1292–93 was abrupt, but religious institutions and practices largely continued across it.

Who experienced change most directly? Court elites and the foundations they patronized felt political change most sharply. Villagers who supplied resources to foundations experienced change when revenue was redirected.

Who experienced greater continuity? Rural communities whose ritual life centered on local deities, ancestors, and agricultural rites, and whose practice changed little with dynastic shifts.

Why does the pattern matter? It shows that religious life was shaped as much by institutional continuity as by dynastic change, and that Majapahit religion inherited and adapted rather than invented its forms.

Section 51.16

Village Religion, Local Deities, and Everyday Ritual

Beneath the court's institutions lay a religious world rooted in village and landscape. Communities venerated ancestors, local spirits, and deities associated with mountains, springs, rivers, fields, and sacred trees or stones. Old Javanese sources use terms such as hyang for divine or ancestral beings, and sacred places known as kabuyutan, associated with ancestral or religious significance, appear in the record. These practices predated the arrival of Sanskritic religion and continued alongside it.

Agricultural ritual connected religion to the rhythms of work. Rites accompanying planting, the management of water, and the harvest are widely attested in later Javanese societies and are likely to have had earlier forms, although direct evidence from the Majapahit period is limited. Offerings to local deities, observances at springs and shrines, and ritual specialists who mediated with unseen powers were presumably part of the lives of cultivators. Such practices should not be confused with the doctrines recorded in elite texts.

Evidence for local religious communities comes from several sources. A Walandit grant attributed to 1381 is preserved in a charter copy made in 1405, rather than in an extant original plate from 1381. The later copy refers to the sanctity of the Mount Brahma (Bromo) region and the exemption of the Walandit community from a due known as titileman. It shows how sacred upland communities invoked royal protection for specific privileges, while the interval between the purported grant and surviving copy requires care about textual transmission. The sacred mountain of Penanggungan, whose slopes hold numerous terraced sanctuaries, and later sanctuaries on Mount Lawu with distinctive imagery, indicate a landscape in which mountains were centers of devotion and in which court-linked and locally rooted religious activity overlapped. Panataran, a major temple complex with relief panels, was patronized by Majapahit rulers and attracted worship across a long period.

Interaction between court and village religion ran in both directions. Court-sponsored foundations drew on local sacred sites and communities, giving them royal recognition and resources. Villages adopted elements of court religion, such as images, rituals, and terms. But local practice retained its own character, and variation among villages, among religious establishments, and among social groups was probably considerable. The historian should resist describing Majapahit religion as merely imported Hinduism and Buddhism. Ancestral commemoration, local divinities, ritual specialists, and court institutions together shaped how religion functioned in society.

Finally, the sources describing religion are mostly elite. Prapañca's picture of piety and harmony reflects his own position, and his poem is an idealized court representation rather than an ethnography of village life. Where evidence is thin, the chapter signals inference. The central point is that religious life in the countryside and in the capital was connected but not identical, and that both were integral to the functioning of society.

Section 51.17

Women, Families, Marriage, Property, and Household Life

Women appear in the sources of Majapahit Java unevenly. Royal and aristocratic women are the most visible. The Deśawarṇana and later traditions give prominence to figures such as Rajapatni, who was honored with elaborate commemoration, and to women of the royal family who held regional titles, as described in Chapter 50. These women had status, resources, and religious influence. But the elite record does not describe the lives of most women, and this section tries to separate three kinds of evidence: documentary evidence, such as inscriptions and legal texts; literary ideals, such as the portrayal of women in poetry; and artistic representations, such as reliefs and terracotta figures.

Documentary evidence for non-elite women is sparse but not absent. Inscriptions occasionally name women among beneficiaries, donors, or witnesses, and legal texts such as the Kuṭāramānawa, whose date and application are debated, address matters including marriage, theft, debt, and sexual offenses, suggesting that women's legal and social status was a subject of regulation. These texts reflect Indian legal models adapted to Java, and they may describe ideals or elite concerns as much as general practice. Whether and how provisions applied to ordinary families cannot be determined from the text alone.

Kinship in Javanese society is generally described by scholars as bilateral, meaning that descent and inheritance could be traced through both mothers and fathers, in contrast to strictly patrilineal systems. This pattern is better documented for later periods, and its application to Majapahit is plausible but not directly proven. Where evidence on marriage, property, and inheritance exists, it suggests that women could hold and transmit property, but the sources do not allow a detailed account of rules or their variation by region and class. Historians should avoid imposing a single model of the family, and should avoid assuming that either male dominance or female autonomy was uniform.

Women's work was varied. In agriculture, women took part in planting, harvesting, and processing, as is widely attested in later periods in Java. They are likely to have been prominent in textile production, food preparation, and local marketing. Comparative evidence from many Southeast Asian societies indicates that women played a leading role in market trade, and the same may have been true in Java, though direct documentation for the fourteenth century is limited. Women also played religious roles as patrons and participants, and royal and elite women endowed and supported foundations.

Artistic representations require careful use. A study by Atina Winaya and Agus Aris Munandar, which draws on temple reliefs, sculpture, terracotta, and literature to examine the representation of women in the Majapahit period, shows that images can illuminate gendered expectations: how women were shown in dress, posture, activity, and narrative roles. But images cannot automatically establish what all women did in everyday life. Reliefs depict religious and epic stories, sculpture often portrays idealized or divine figures, and terracotta figurines may represent types rather than individuals. Students should treat such evidence as a guide to ideals and conventions, supplemented by documentary and archaeological evidence where available.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households in Majapahit Java

A household in Majapahit Java was a unit of production, consumption, and obligation. For a farming family, daily life was organized around field work, the care of animals, food preparation, the maintenance of tools and dwellings, and participation in community and religious activities. Members contributed according to age, skill, and circumstance, and the household's surplus, if any, passed into local exchange or into the dues owed to various claimants.

Households in craft settlements combined agricultural tasks with specialized work, such as pottery, cloth finishing, or oil pressing. Households in port towns and the capital region were more exposed to markets and money, and some depended on wages, trade, or service to patrons. Elite households managed larger numbers of dependents and were linked to the court through office and kinship.

The historical record, however, does not allow a representative household to be described. This feature therefore cautions against invention. Evidence such as terracotta house models, cooking vessels, and storage jars indicates the kinds of objects people used, but it does not reveal their number, their distribution among social classes, or the composition of any individual household. Archaeology gives us objects; inscriptions give us obligations; reliefs give us conventions. Combining them yields a plausible outline of ordinary life, not a portrait of named persons.

Section 51.18

Social Hierarchies, Bonded Labor, Dependence, and Mobility

Majapahit society was hierarchical, but the form of its hierarchy was complex. At the top were the king, members of the royal family, high officials, and leading religious figures. Below them were lesser officials, landholding elites, heads of religious foundations, and wealthy traders. Further down were cultivators, craftspeople, small traders, and laborers. The sources use varied terms for status, and they reflect Indian categories such as the fourfold varṇa scheme, which appear in Old Javanese texts. Scholars generally agree, however, that this scheme did not operate as a rigid caste system that bound the Javanese population into hereditary occupational groups. It functioned more as a conceptual and ritual vocabulary than as a description of lived social organization.

Status depended on several factors: kinship, office, wealth, religious role, and royal recognition. Charters that confer privileges on a community or individual raised status, while failure to meet obligations could lower it. Different regions and institutions may have used different criteria. The social structure was therefore layered and contextual, and the position of a given person might be different in the village, at the court, and in a religious foundation.

Dependence and unfree labor were part of this society. Old Javanese sources use words such as hulun, which can mean servant or slave, to refer to persons in subordinate or bonded positions, and legal texts address debt and servitude. Such persons could be attached to households, temples, or the court. Debt could lead to bondage, and captives taken in war or purchased could be put to work. The extent and the conditions of servitude varied, and it is difficult to estimate the number of dependents or to describe their treatment from the sources. Historians should neither minimize the existence of unfree labor nor assume that it took the same form everywhere. Chapter 91 compares forms of slavery and bondage across the early modern region.

Mobility was possible but constrained. Success in service to the court, in trade, or in religious office could elevate individuals and families. Marriage alliances connected elites. For farmers and laborers, movement might come through migration to new settlements, through service to a patron, or through joining a privileged community. Mechanisms of exclusion included debt, bondage, lack of kin support, and the absence of a charter protecting one's rights. Because the sources document mainly the successful and the privileged, upward mobility may appear more common than it was.

The key point is that Majapahit's social structure cannot be reduced to a single formula. It combined court rank, religious status, local community standing, occupational specialization, and relations of dependence. Understanding who held power, who bore costs, and who could change their position requires attention to specific contexts, and the evidence rarely permits generalizations beyond those contexts.

Section 51.19

Cultural and Commercial Connections across the Archipelago

Java was linked to its neighbors by flows of goods, people, ideas, and skills. Ceramics from China and mainland Southeast Asia reached Java; Javanese textiles, metalwork, and foodstuffs moved to other islands; and religious ideas, artistic styles, and literary traditions circulated in several directions. Bali, which had long-standing ties with East Java, shared religious and artistic traditions, and Javanese cultural influence there is well documented. Connections with Sumatra and the Malay world were commercial and political, and connections with eastern Indonesia were tied to the spice trade, as explored in Chapter 71.

Exchange transformed all participants. Regions that received Javanese goods and ideas adapted them to local needs, as Southeast Asian societies had long done with outside influences, a process discussed in Chapter 19. Java also absorbed influences: from Indian religious and literary traditions, from Chinese ceramics and coinage, and from the Indian Ocean networks that brought Muslim merchants. Majapahit was a participant in a many-sided exchange, not the exclusive source of innovation. Specialized knowledge, such as shipbuilding, metallurgy, textile techniques, and ritual expertise, traveled with people, and migrants and visitors carried it.

Cultural exchange also took the form of literary and artistic connection. Old Javanese literature spread to Bali, where it was preserved and adapted, and manuscripts recording Javanese works have survived there when copies on Java itself were lost. Artistic motifs and architectural forms from East Java show both continuity with earlier Javanese styles and distinctive local developments. Musical and performance traditions, partly known from reliefs, had regional variants.

The dynamics of these connections must be described carefully. Majapahit's court claimed influence over many islands, but cultural and commercial connection did not always follow political claims. Some regions had close commercial links without political subordination, and some regions the court claimed as dependencies had limited practical contact with Java, as Chapter 50 noted. Cultural exchange could flow along routes independent of the court, carried by merchants, pilgrims, scholars, and artisans.

Chronology and provenance are central to tracing these regional links. A Chinese ceramic in East Java may have been imported long before it entered the deposit where archaeologists recover it; a Javanese literary manuscript surviving in Bali may have been recopied or revised generations after its first composition. These objects reveal connections, but they cannot automatically date the movement of an individual trader or the transfer of a specific ritual. Comparing inscriptions that name places or occupational groups with ceramics, art styles, and later manuscript histories can narrow the possibilities. Such comparison also makes Southeast Asian agency visible: communities selected, reused, and transformed circulating goods and practices according to their circumstances, rather than passively reproducing a single pattern imposed from the Majapahit court.

Section 51.20

The Majapahit Social Order: Prosperity, Inequality, and Historical Change

The preceding sections allow a synthesis. Majapahit Java was supported by productive rural communities that grew rice and other crops, maintained local water systems, and supplied labor and revenue. It contained specialized craft and commercial communities that produced salt, textiles, oil, tools, and ceramics, and that sought royal recognition to protect their work. Its rivers, ferries, markets, and ports connected the interior with the maritime world, and its capital region concentrated ceremonial, craft, and commercial activity. Religious institutions, Śaiva, Buddhist, Brahmanical, and local, held land, employed personnel, preserved ancestral memory, and tied the economy to dynastic legitimacy. Households, including women's work, sustained all of these.

This society was prosperous in some respects and unequal in others. Revenue, privilege, and resources flowed toward courts, officials, religious foundations, and wealthy traders. Cultivators and laborers bore many of the costs, and dependents and bonded persons occupied the lowest positions. Prosperity as shown by monumental building, literature, and trade did not mean that its benefits were evenly shared. Historians should distinguish economic dynamism, which the evidence supports, from broad-based well-being, which it cannot demonstrate.

The social and economic dynamism of Majapahit did not require uniform political centralization. Communities, foundations, and trading groups had their own organization and resources, and the court worked through negotiation, delegation, and ritual rather than through a uniform bureaucracy. This helps explain how Java could sustain a productive economy and a vibrant culture while royal authority over distant regions remained limited, as Chapter 50 showed. It also suggests why changes in political leadership might alter relationships without destroying the underlying economy.

Change over time was present in the period covered here. Trade expanded, new communities, including Muslim ones, appeared, and relationships among the court, ports, and local rulers shifted. These changes prepare the subject of Chapter 52, which examines dynastic conflict, the movement of political centers, expanding Muslim commercial networks, and growing regional autonomy. The social order described in this chapter was not static, and its capacity for adaptation is part of the explanation for what followed.

The three central documentary examples illustrate the larger system from different angles. Canggu reveals a policy that changed the standing and payments of ferry operators; Biluluk reveals the persistence of commercial elites alongside revised obligations; and the Deśawarṇana portrays an ordered realm whose splendor depended on those kinds of local contributions. Read together, they show why neither a wholly centralized court nor a loose collection of independent villages is an adequate model. Authority was negotiated through charters, ritual occasions, commercial intermediaries, and repeated obligations. The outcome was durable political prominence for Majapahit, but not equal security or opportunity for all households. That distinction is essential when considering the later political changes of Chapter 52.

Historical Synthesis Study

How Majapahit Society Worked

Production. Rice agriculture on irrigated and dry fields, supplemented by fishing, gathering, palm products, and animal husbandry, organized mainly through households and village communities with local water management and occasional large works sponsored by rulers or religious foundations.

Revenue and privilege. Layered claims on produce, labor, and commerce, negotiated through sīma grants and charters that redistributed rights among villages, officials, foundations, and the crown.

Specialization and exchange. Craft communities and traders working in a monetized, though not fully monetized, economy, linked by markets, ferries, and ports.

Religion and legitimacy. Plural religious institutions, ancestral commemoration, and local cults, all drawing on and shaping economic resources and reinforcing social order.

Social difference. Hierarchies of rank, office, wealth, and dependence, with differing experiences by gender, occupation, and location.

Connections. Exchange across the archipelago and with China and the Indian Ocean world that transformed all participants.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Majapahit society functioned beneath the monarchy. It began from the premise that the kingdom's political power, analyzed in Chapter 50, rested on productive and organized communities that were not simply instruments of royal will. The governing question was how agriculture, commerce, religion, social relations, and culture sustained Majapahit Java and how experience varied by position and place.

The sources are uneven. Royal charters, the Deśawarṇana, temple reliefs, ceramics, coins, Chinese and European accounts, and archaeology at Trowulan all contribute, but each speaks mainly of elites, privileged communities, and religious institutions. Ordinary cultivators, laborers, and most women appear indirectly. Responsible reconstruction therefore distinguishes documented obligations from inferred practices and avoids inventing representative households or inferring individual identities from objects.

Division I showed that villages were the basic units of rural life, led by elders and organized around local obligations, with overlapping claims from officials, the court, and religious beneficiaries. Rice agriculture, both irrigated and dry, supported population and surplus, and water management combined local arrangements with occasional large works sponsored by rulers. The evidence does not support a model of centralized royal irrigation administration. Landholding involved layered rights to cultivate, to receive revenue, and to command labor.

Division II examined revenue and production. Sīma grants fixed privileges for villages, foundations, and specialist communities, but they were negotiated instruments that redistributed rights and obligations rather than guaranteeing total exemption. Taxation, tolls, and service involved multiple layers of officials and beneficiaries. Craft production and trades, especially as documented in the Biluluk charters, revealed a differentiated economy, and markets and monetized exchange, including the use of Chinese copper cash, linked producers and specialists.

Division III turned to rivers, ports, and the capital region. The Canggu charter of 1358 showed how transport, commerce, and government intersected through ferry settlements. East Java's ports, known especially through Chinese and later European observers, connected inland production to maritime trade, and merchants carried goods across the archipelago and beyond. The debate over whether the economy was primarily agrarian or maritime was resolved, in this chapter, toward an integrated view with differences of emphasis. Archaeology at Trowulan provided material evidence for a dense, craft-rich capital region, while cautioning against over-reconstruction.

Division IV addressed court culture and religion. The court was a hierarchical society with ceremonies, literature, and artistic patronage, and the Deśawarṇana is both a key source and an idealized representation. Śaiva, Buddhist, and Brahmanical institutions were distinct but coexisted under royal patronage, held endowments, and employed personnel. Commemoration of royal ancestors, notably Rajapatni, tied religious resources to dynastic legitimacy. Village religion, centered on local divinities, ancestors, and sacred places, interacted with court institutions without being reducible to them.

Division V examined households and social difference. Women's roles, though poorly documented, included work in agriculture, crafts, and probably markets, religious patronage, and, among elites, political and ritual prominence. Evidence from documents, literary ideals, and art must be distinguished. Social hierarchy was complex and not a rigid caste system, and dependence and bonded labor were real features of the society. Cultural and commercial exchange across the archipelago transformed Java and its partners alike.

The synthesis proposed that Majapahit's society was economically dynamic and unequal, and that its vitality did not require uniform political centralization. Local communities, religious foundations, and trading groups had their own organization, and the court worked through recognition, negotiation, and ritual.

Continuity and change were both present. Agricultural and religious institutions had deep roots, while commerce, the presence of Muslim communities, and relationships among court and ports were shifting. The next chapter, Chapter 52, will examine dynastic conflict, the movement of political centers, expanding Muslim commercial networks, and growing regional autonomy.

The chapter's larger lesson is methodological as well as historical. To understand a premodern kingdom, historians must read beyond the ruler's name and ask who worked, who paid, who prayed, who traded, and who left no record. The answers are partial, but they transform Majapahit from a royal reputation into a society.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 50 asked how far Majapahit's power extended. This chapter has asked what that power rested on, and the answer is a society of villages, workshops, ferries, ports, temples, and households whose activity was organized by many hands. Kings and ministers appear throughout, but they appear as participants in negotiations with communities and institutions, not as sole authors of the social order.

The chapter also reveals how historians know what they know. Charters, poems, reliefs, coins, ceramics, and excavated bricks each illuminate part of the picture and obscure another part. Understanding Majapahit means holding these partial views together and being honest about the gaps, especially where ordinary people, women, and dependents are concerned.

The social and economic patterns described here shaped later developments. A society with strong local institutions, active ports, and diverse religious communities could adapt to political change in ways that a more centralized order might not. The expansion of Muslim commercial communities, the shifting weight of ports relative to the inland court, and the persistence of local religious and economic networks all grew from the foundations examined in this chapter. They are the subject of Chapter 52.

For the textbook as a whole, Chapter 51 contributes to the argument that Southeast Asian societies were historical actors with their own institutions and innovations. The farmers, ferry operators, craft workers, merchants, clergy, and women of East Java were not passive recipients of outside influence or instruments of royal will. They built, sustained, and adapted a society whose complexity is the real subject of this history.
