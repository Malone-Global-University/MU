The Theravada Transformation

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Between about 1200 and 1500, Theravada Buddhism became an increasingly influential religious and institutional tradition across mainland Southeast Asia, not by arriving suddenly and displacing what came before, but through a gradual, uneven process in which older Buddhist inheritances, renewed monastic connections, royal patronage, and local religious practice combined in different ways in different places.

In the late 1470s, at the Mon-speaking port-capital of Pegu in Lower Burma, King Dhammaceti had a series of stone inscriptions set up at a newly consecrated ordination hall that came to be known as the Kalyani Sima. The texts, written in Pali and Mon, narrate a long history of monastic lineage: how Buddhism was said to have reached the region in the time of the emperor Asoka, how Burmese monks traveled to Sri Lanka to receive ordination in earlier centuries, how rival monastic fraternities had formed afterward, and how the king dispatched a delegation of monks across the Bay of Bengal to be reordained in a Sri Lankan lineage. Read carefully, the inscriptions are not a record of Buddhism's first appearance in Burma. They are a fifteenth-century statement about authority, discipline, and the correct transmission of a tradition that had already been present for many centuries.

A very different witness offers a glimpse of the same religious world from the opposite side of the mainland. In 1296–1297 the Chinese envoy Zhou Daguan spent roughly a year at the Khmer court at Angkor, and afterward wrote a short account of what he had seen. He described three recognizable categories of religious specialists: scholars associated with Brahmanical learning, Buddhist monks distinguished by their shaved heads and yellow robes, and ascetics who served Shiva. His report belongs to a moment when Buddhist monastic communities of a kind that later readers would call Theravada were becoming more visible at Angkor, while temples, rituals, and sacred landscapes created under Hindu and Mahayana Buddhist patronage remained deeply embedded in the capital. Taken together, the Kalyani stones and Zhou Daguan's report frame this chapter's central problem: how did a tradition with ancient roots on the mainland become so influential across such different societies, and why did it look so different from one court and community to another?

This chapter argues that the Theravada transformation was a gradual, geographically uneven process. Theravada, in the sense used here, refers to a Buddhist tradition that preserves its canonical scriptures in the Pali language and that came increasingly to look toward monastic lineages associated with the Mahavihara tradition of Sri Lanka for its model of correct discipline. It was not a wholly new import of the thirteenth century. Scholars such as Peter Skilling have shown that Pali-using Buddhist communities were probably active in Pyu and Dvaravati contexts much earlier, and that simple theories placing the "origin" of mainland Theravada in a single place or event do not fit the evidence. What changed between 1200 and 1500 was less the first presence of such Buddhism than the extent, density, and institutional organization of the networks through which it was taught, patronized, and renewed.

Those networks were institutional as well as doctrinal. They involved ordination lineages and the consecrated boundaries within which higher ordination was performed; monasteries endowed with land, labor, and revenue; kings and queens who sought merit and legitimacy through patronage; lay donors from many levels of society; scholar-monks who studied and taught Pali texts; and travelers, including monks and merchants, who crossed the Bay of Bengal and the overland routes linking the river valleys. Southeast Asian monks and rulers were not passive recipients of a Sri Lankan mission. They traveled, studied, argued, built, and sometimes rejected the claims of rival fraternities. Their choices shaped what Theravada became in each setting.

The evidence for this history is rich but uneven, and its character matters. Contemporary inscriptions, such as the thousands that survive from Pagan, record donations and dedications but say little about doctrine or ordinary belief. Travel accounts like Zhou Daguan's offer outsider impressions. Monastic chronicles written in later centuries give coherent narratives of origins, but these narratives were composed to serve the purposes of their own times. Architecture, images, and excavated remains can show institutional change but cannot by themselves reveal what every worshipper believed. Throughout the chapter, students are asked to distinguish evidence for the presence of Buddhism from evidence for a particular school, and evidence for a monastic network from later claims about its purity or antiquity.

The chapter also takes care to avoid several tempting simplifications. It does not treat Theravada as the cause of political collapse at Angkor or Pagan, a view that recent scholarship has challenged. It does not portray older Hindu, Mahayana, spirit-centered, or ancestral practices as obstacles that simply vanished. And it does not describe the sangha, the community of monks, as a single centralized organization controlled by kings. Monastic communities cooperated with royal power while pursuing interests and claims of their own. The detailed political histories of Sukhothai, Lan Na, Ayutthaya, Lan Xang, the post-Pagan Burmese kingdoms, Hanthawaddy, and post-Angkorian Cambodia belong to Chapters 54 through 61; this chapter supplies the religious and institutional framework that those chapters assume.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish the historical presence of Buddhism from evidence for a particular school, text tradition, or ordination lineage.
  2. Describe the Buddhist landscapes of the Pyu, Mon, Dvaravati, Pagan, and Angkorian worlds before the thirteenth century and explain why Theravada was not entirely new after 1200.
  3. Trace the maritime and overland connections linking Sri Lanka, the Bay of Bengal, and mainland Southeast Asia, emphasizing the participation of Southeast Asian monks and rulers.
  4. Explain how ordination lineages, consecrated boundaries, and monastic discipline created and contested religious authority.
  5. Analyze how royal patronage, merit, and the protection of Buddhism related to political legitimacy, and the limits of royal control over the sangha.
  6. Assess the economic importance of monastic endowments and the tensions they created among kings, monks, donors, and cultivators.
  7. Compare the paths of religious change in Pagan and Burma, the Mon world and Lower Burma, the Tai-speaking polities, and Cambodia.
  8. Interpret inscriptions, a travel account, and material remains as evidence, identifying authorship, purpose, perspective, and limitations.
  9. Evaluate the relationship between Buddhist institutions and ancestral spirits, local deities, and sacred landscapes, including the debate between "replacement" and "reconfiguration."
  10. Explain the Kalyani reform of the late 1470s and distinguish its chronology from the dating of the surviving inscriptions.
  11. Evaluate competing explanations for Theravada's growing influence and distinguish demonstrable mechanisms from broad claims of inherent suitability.

  3. In what ways did Southeast Asian monks, kings, and merchants shape the networks linking the mainland to Sri Lanka, rather than merely receiving influence from it?

Historical Context

By about 1200 the mainland already contained several established Buddhist worlds. In the Irrawaddy basin, the kingdom of Pagan, examined in Chapters 40 and 41, had built thousands of temples and monasteries, and its inscriptions recorded donations by kings, officials, and ordinary families. In the Chao Phraya basin and neighboring areas, the Mon-speaking communities and the cultural world associated with Dvaravati, studied in Chapter 25, had left Buddhist images, inscriptions, and sacred objects centuries earlier. At Angkor, Jayavarman VII, whose reign Chapter 38 examines, had sponsored a large Mahayana Buddhist building program, even as Shaiva and Vaishnava traditions remained part of Khmer religious life. In the Red River delta, Buddhist traditions followed a trajectory shaped by long incorporation into the Chinese imperial world and are treated in the chapters on Vietnam rather than here.

Beyond the mainland, Sri Lanka had become a distinctive center of Pali learning. The Mahavihara, the monastic establishment at Anuradhapura, had long preserved scriptural commentaries and a tradition of monastic discipline, and in the twelfth century the Sri Lankan king Parakramabahu I, who reigned from 1153 to 1186, is traditionally credited with a reform that brought the island's monastic communities under a single, Mahavihara-centered authority. The prestige of this reformed Sri Lankan sangha helped make the island an attractive source of ordination lineages for monks elsewhere, though the extent and timing of that attraction varied by region. Anne Blackburn's studies of monastic mobility show that exchanges across the Bay of Bengal grew more intensive between roughly 1000 and 1500, and that they ran in several directions.

The thirteenth century and the century that followed were also a time of political change. The kingdom of Pagan lost its imperial coherence in the late thirteenth century under pressure from Mongol-Yuan campaigns, examined in Chapter 46, and from internal fragmentation, giving way to the successor states of Chapter 59. Tai-speaking communities, whose gradual movements are the subject of Chapter 54, established new principalities in the Chao Phraya basin and the northern valleys. Angkor's political and economic landscape changed in ways that Chapter 61 examines. None of these developments was caused by religious change alone, and none had a single cause. Students should resist the idea that a new religion arrived to fill a vacuum left by collapsing empires; the evidence points instead to continuity in institutions and personnel across political upheaval.

Finally, the chapter belongs to a wider Indian Ocean and Asian setting. While Theravada networks were strengthening on the mainland, Muslim merchants and scholars were establishing new connections across the maritime world, a story told in Chapters 62 through 66. The two processes were contemporaneous, not identical, and neither should be read as a mainland-versus-island destiny. Religious change in Southeast Asia in these centuries was a set of overlapping regional transformations, in which the societies of the region were themselves the principal historical actors.

Section 53.1

Reconstructing the Theravada Transformation: Sources and Historical Problems

Any account of religious change must begin with the evidence available to reconstruct it, because the character of that evidence shapes the stories historians can responsibly tell. The history of Buddhism in mainland Southeast Asia between 1200 and 1500 survives in several quite different kinds of sources. Stone inscriptions, written in Pali, Sanskrit, Old Burmese, Mon, Old Khmer, and Tai languages, record dedications, donations, and royal religious acts. Palm-leaf and later manuscripts preserve Pali scriptures, commentaries, and local compositions, though many surviving copies are much later than the texts they transmit. Chronicles composed in Pali or vernacular languages recount the histories of kings and monastic lineages. Chinese and, later, European observers described what they saw. Buildings, sculpture, relic deposits, and excavated monastic sites supply material evidence that the texts often omit.

Each source type has strengths and limits. Contemporary inscriptions are valuable because they were set up at the time of the events they describe and were meant to be durable. The thousands of inscriptions from Pagan, for example, identify donors, name the lands and laborers given to religious foundations, and often express the hope that the donor's merit would benefit relatives or all beings. But inscriptions are formulaic, they focus on donations rather than doctrine, and their donors were disproportionately kings, queens, officials, and wealthy families. Chronicles, by contrast, offer narrative and explanation, but many were written generations or centuries after the events they describe, often within monastic or royal circles with a stake in a particular account of origins. Travel accounts, such as Zhou Daguan's, are contemporary but filtered through the observer's own categories, language limitations, and brief stay.

A central problem for this chapter is the difference between evidence for Buddhism in general and evidence for Theravada in particular. A Buddha image, a stupa, or a relic chamber establishes Buddhist devotion but not the school, text tradition, or ordination lineage of those who made or used it. Pali texts are a stronger indicator, since Pali is the canonical language of the Theravada tradition, but Pali was also used within other settings, and Sanskrit-using Buddhists also existed in the same regions. Even Pali inscriptions show that someone could read and write Pali, not necessarily that a particular institutional lineage had been established. The Theravada label itself, as Chapter 53's Vocabulary boxes explain, is a later way of naming a family of traditions, and historians use it cautiously for earlier centuries.

A related problem is the habit of treating later origin narratives as contemporary history. Monastic chronicles often describe the introduction of the "true" Buddhism by a particular missionary, a conquest that brought a complete scriptural canon, or a single reform that purified the sangha. Some of these accounts preserve genuine memories. Many also serve the legitimacy claims of the communities that produced them. Throughout this chapter, retrospective narratives are treated as important historical evidence of how later communities understood their own past, but not as straightforward descriptions of events centuries earlier. A good example is the story, examined in Section 53.10, that a single Mon-centered transmission brought Theravada to Burma.

Finally, the chapter relies on modern scholarship that has refined and sometimes overturned older accounts. Skilling's study of the advent of Theravada on the mainland, Blackburn's work on monastic mobility and Sri Lankan connections, Ian Harris's history of Cambodian Buddhism, and a large epigraphic and archaeological literature together provide the framework used here. The Source Register at the end of the chapter identifies the principal works and distinguishes contemporary inscriptions and travel accounts from later chronicles and modern reconstructions.

Section 53.2

Buddhism before the Thirteenth Century: Pyu, Mon, Dvaravati, Pagan, and Angkor

The Buddhism of the thirteenth century grew from a long inheritance. In the Pyu city-states of the Irrawaddy basin, studied in Chapter 24, Buddhist relic deposits, images, and inscribed objects survive from roughly the middle of the first millennium CE. Among the most discussed are thin gold sheets from the Pyu site of Hmawza (ancient Sri Ksetra) inscribed in Pali with passages from Buddhist texts, which palaeographers date to about the fifth or sixth century. Peter Skilling and other scholars regard such finds as strong evidence that Pali-using Buddhist communities existed among the Pyu, although the finds do not establish an organized lineage later identifiable as Theravada. The Pyu world also contained Sanskrit-using and Mahayana-associated Buddhists and Hindu devotees.

In the Mon-speaking communities of the lower Irrawaddy, the Chao Phraya basin, and neighboring regions, examined in Chapter 25, a comparable pattern appears. Dvaravati-period sites, particularly in what is now central Thailand, have yielded Buddha images, stone Wheel-of-the-Law monuments, and inscriptions in Pali and Old Mon from about the seventh century onward. The Wheel-of-the-Law, a symbol of the Buddha's first sermon, appears at several sites, and Pali inscriptions at others record verses from Buddhist texts. As with the Pyu evidence, these remains show a Buddhist presence in which Pali played a significant part, but they do not reveal the full institutional structure of the communities involved, and they coexisted with Sanskrit inscriptions and Brahmanical images. Mon-language inscriptions of the eleventh and twelfth centuries from Burma and northern Thailand show Mon speakers sustaining Buddhist patronage into the period of Pagan's rise.

Pagan, the dominant polity of the Irrawaddy basin from about the eleventh to the late thirteenth century, was the most densely Buddhist environment of the mainland. Its kings sponsored monasteries and temples, and its inscriptions refer to the Buddha, the dhamma, and the sangha in conventional formulas. But Pagan's religious life was diverse. Archaeological and iconographic evidence shows Mahayana and tantric-influenced imagery, Hindu deities in Buddhist temples, and the continued importance of spirit cults. Pali scholarship also flourished: Aggavamsa's Pali grammar, the Saddaniti, was composed at Pagan in 1154. Chronicle tradition credits King Anawrahta (traditionally r. 1044–1077) with introducing Theravada through the conquest of the Mon city of Thaton and a reform led by a monk named Shin Arahan; as Section 53.10 explains, historians regard this story as a later narrative that compresses a more complex process.

At Angkor, the older Khmer religious order centered on Shaiva and Vaishnava kingship and, under Jayavarman VII at the end of the twelfth century and the beginning of the thirteenth, on a state-sponsored Mahayana Buddhism, as Chapters 36 through 38 explain. Khmer inscriptions show that Sanskrit and Old Khmer, not Pali, dominated royal religious texts. Yet this landscape was not a uniform Mahayana monopoly. Cambodian prince Tamalinda appears in the Kalyani narrative as a companion of Chapata in Sri Lanka, suggesting contacts between the Khmer world and Sri Lankan-linked monks already in the late twelfth century, and the evidence of Zhou Daguan a century later shows a recognizable Buddhist monastic community. How far these Khmer monks should be identified with a particular lineage remains debated, and Ian Harris emphasizes that the evidence for Theravada in pre-fourteenth-century Cambodia is thin and its interpretation difficult.

The upshot is that Theravada was not new in the thirteenth century. Scholars generally agree that Pali-using Buddhism was present early on the mainland, that Sri Lankan connections existed before 1200, and that a plurality of Buddhist and non-Buddhist traditions coexisted. What changed afterward was the scale and institutional reach of particular monastic networks. This distinction, between the expansion of specific Theravada monastic networks and the first arrival of Buddhism or of Pali, underlies every section that follows.

Historical Vocabulary
Theravada

Definition: A Buddhist tradition that preserves its canon in Pali and whose monastic communities trace their discipline to what they regard as the "teaching of the elders." Historical context: The term was applied to Southeast Asian traditions in the modern era; before 1500, monastic communities described themselves with other terms, including fraternity or lineage names. Potential misunderstanding: Theravada was not a single organization, and a Buddhist community's use of Pali does not alone prove membership of a particular lineage.

Mahayana

Definition: A broad family of Buddhist traditions emphasizing the bodhisattva path and drawing on Sanskrit and, in East Asia, Chinese texts. Historical context: Mahayana images and inscriptions appear at Pagan, in the Pyu and Mon worlds, and under Jayavarman VII at Angkor. Potential misunderstanding: It was not an "outside" tradition displaced by Theravada; it coexisted with and, in places, preceded it.

Pali

Definition: The scriptural and liturgical language of the Theravada canon, related to the Middle Indo-Aryan languages. Historical context: Pali was learned by monks and a few lay scholars, not spoken as an everyday language in Southeast Asia. Potential misunderstanding: The spread of Pali learning did not replace Mon, Burmese, Tai, or Khmer as languages of daily life and local religious explanation.

Sangha

Definition: The community of ordained Buddhist monks, and by extension its institutions. Historical context: In practice, the sangha consisted of many monasteries and fraternities, not a single central body. Potential misunderstanding: Kings patronized and sometimes tried to regulate the sangha, but it was not a department of the state.

Section 53.3

Sri Lanka, the Bay of Bengal, and Transregional Religious Networks

Sri Lanka's importance for the mainland grew within a world of sea travel. Monsoon winds carried ships across the Bay of Bengal in predictable seasons, and the ports of Lower Burma, the Tenasserim coast, and the Malay Peninsula were linked to Sri Lankan harbors by merchants, pilgrims, and monks. Religious travel rode on this commercial infrastructure. Monks could join trading vessels, rulers could send embassies with gifts, and ordination lineages, texts, and relics moved along the same lanes as cloth, spices, and silver. The Bay of Bengal was therefore not merely a barrier crossed by missionaries; it was a regular communication system in which Southeast Asian ports and polities were participants.

The Sri Lankan Mahavihara tradition became more attractive as the island's own monastic communities reorganized. The twelfth-century reform associated with Parakramabahu I consolidated the monastic order around the Mahavihara and its commentarial tradition, and the resulting prestige made Sri Lankan ordination appear especially authoritative to monks elsewhere. Anne Blackburn's research indicates that between about 1000 and 1500 the mobility of monks around the Indian Ocean changed in character, with travel to Sri Lanka and exchange of ordination lineages becoming more common and more explicitly tied to claims of correct discipline. These claims were not simply imposed from Sri Lanka. Mainland monks and rulers decided whether to seek out Sri Lankan lineages, which ones, and what to do with them.

The Kalyani narrative preserves one detailed example. According to its account, the Pagan monk Chapata (known by the title Saddhammajotipala) went to Sri Lanka in the later twelfth century, studied for about a decade, received higher ordination in a Sri Lankan lineage, and returned to Pagan accompanied by four other monks, one of whom was a prince from Cambodia named Tamalinda. At Pagan, Chapata's party formed what came to be called the Sinhalese fraternity. The exact dates are debated, and the account was recorded in the fifteenth century, but it preserves the memory of a movement in both directions: Burmese and Khmer monks traveling to the island and bringing a lineage home. The Sri Lankan chronicle tradition likewise notes the presence of mainland visitors, and Southeast Asian rulers are recorded as sending gifts to Sri Lankan monasteries.

Sri Lankan connections also reached the mainland through the Malay Peninsula. The port-polity of Tambralinga, associated with Nakhon Si Thammarat, appears in the Sri Lankan chronicle tradition: its ruler Chandrabhanu is said to have attacked the island in the thirteenth century, and the region's Buddhist remains show strong Sri Lankan-style features in later centuries. Nakhon Si Thammarat may therefore have served as one conduit for Sri Lankan-linked Buddhism into the Chao Phraya basin, although this relationship is inferred partly from later tradition and its extent remains uncertain. Northern Thai chronicles, composed in the early sixteenth century, describe a Sri Lankan-lineage monk named Sumana who traveled from the Mon port of Martaban to Sukhothai and Chiang Mai in the fourteenth century, a movement that Section 53.11 examines.

The key historical point is multidirectional exchange. Texts and relics moved, monks traveled and settled, and ideas of correct ordination circulated, but each polity selected, modified, and sometimes resisted what arrived. The result was not a uniform imitation of Sri Lanka but a series of local arrangements bound together by shared references to a distant authority.

Section 53.4

Monastic Lineages, Ordination, and the Transmission of Buddhist Authority

For Theravada monastic communities, authority depended heavily on ordination. A man became a fully ordained monk (bhikkhu) through a formal act, performed by a quorum of properly ordained monks within a consecrated boundary, and the monks who performed it were themselves ordained by others in an unbroken chain traced back, in principle, to the Buddha's time. A flaw in that chain, whether an invalid ceremony, an incorrect boundary, or a lack of qualified participants, could, under the disciplinary texts, cast doubt on the validity of every ordination that followed. This gave questions of lineage a practical force that went far beyond abstract theology: monks, kings, and donors all had reasons to care which ordination was correct.

Because lineages could be disputed, they could also be renewed or replaced. A community that doubted its own pedigree might send monks to a place regarded as having a pure line, as Pagan monks did in the twelfth century and as Dhammaceti's delegation did in the fifteenth. A ruler might sponsor a new fraternity to strengthen his own position or to counterbalance an established one. And rival fraternities could claim, each on its own account, that it alone possessed the right ordination. The Kalyani narrative describes exactly this kind of competition at Pagan and afterward, listing several fraternities and the disputes among them. Such competition was normal rather than exceptional, and it is one reason historians cannot treat the sangha as a monolith.

Another distinction recurs in the sources: between monks who lived in or near villages and towns and those who preferred forest monasteries. Chronicles of Sukhothai and Lan Na refer to forest-dwelling and village-dwelling fraternities, and Sri Lankan-linked monks are often described as forest monks. The categories reflected differences of lifestyle, scholarly emphasis, and patronage, as well as competition for royal favor. They also illustrate that Theravada communities were internally diverse; adopting a shared reference to Sri Lanka did not eliminate distinctions of practice.

Historians must also be cautious about retrospective claims of purity. Later monastic writers often described a lineage as newly introduced in a pure form, replacing earlier corrupt or mixed practice. Some reforms were real, with identifiable actors and institutional consequences. But the rhetoric of purification can also hide continuity: monks ordained in an older lineage might continue to teach and hold property, and communities might maintain relationships across supposed lines of division. The task is to separate actual institutional reform, such as a new ordination hall, a documented delegation, or a recorded change in monastic organization, from the later narrative that presents any such reform as the first and only true introduction of Buddhism.

Buddhist Institutions before and after 1200

What changed?

  • Pali learning and monastic networks expanded across more courts and regions
  • Patronage patterns shifted from Sanskritic and Mahayana royal programs, in places such as Angkor, toward support for Pali-oriented communities

  • Coexistence with Brahmanical specialists, spirit cults, and older sacred sites
  • Use of vernacular languages for daily communication and local religious explanation

When, how, and for whom?

Change occurred gradually, unevenly, and at different times: earlier and more visibly at Pagan and in the Mon and Burmese world, in the fourteenth century in the Tai-speaking and Khmer regions, and with documented reform in the late fifteenth century at Pegu. Monks and royal patrons experienced change most directly, as new lineages, titles, and ordination halls altered their status. Villagers and cultivators often experienced greater continuity in household ritual and local sacred places. The pattern matters because it shows that a major institutional transformation need not rewrite everyday religious life.

Section 53.5

Kingship, Merit, and the Protection of Buddhism

Across the mainland, rulers expressed their legitimacy partly through care for Buddhism. The idea that a good king protects the Buddhist dispensation, the sāsana, and acquires great merit by doing so was widely shared, and it was expressed through concrete acts: building and repairing monasteries and stupas, commissioning Buddha images, enshrining relics, donating land and laborers, sponsoring festivals, inviting learned monks, and, at times, intervening to settle monastic disputes. Royal inscriptions from Pagan, Sukhothai, and Lan Na, and the later chronicles of Burma and northern Thailand, record such acts as central to a ruler's reputation. Royal women, including queens and princesses, also appear as donors and builders.

The language of merit gave these acts an explanation that was both religious and political. Merit, understood as the beneficial result of good actions, could be accumulated by giving to monks, and rulers who gave on a large scale could be portrayed as having extraordinary stores of it. Royal donors often transferred part of their merit to parents, ancestors, or all beings, which made gifts moral obligations as well as displays. At the same time, large public donations were visible assertions of wealth, administrative reach, and piety, and they helped bind monastic communities, local elites, and the court into relationships of reciprocal dependence.

Royal protection was not simple control. Kings depended on monks for ritual services, for learning, and for the moral authority that monastic approval conferred. They could favor a fraternity, grant it lands, or support the construction of an ordination hall, but they could not create a valid ordination by decree, and monks could withhold recognition from a royal choice or maintain parallel institutions. Rulers also had to reconcile Buddhist patronage with other demands: they relied on taxation, military recruitment, and alliances with provincial lords, and lavish giving to the sangha could strain those resources. Where chronicles describe a ruler "purifying" the sangha, as in several Burmese and Thai narratives, it is wise to ask both what changed in practice and what political advantage the ruler gained from the reputation of being a purifier.

Patronage also worked at the level of images, relics, and rituals. The commissioning of a principal Buddha image or the enshrinement of a relic in a stupa could tie a ruler's capital to a sacred center. Calendrical ceremonies, such as annual donations of robes and public sermons, drew the court and the population together. Rulers could also borrow from earlier religious traditions of kingship, retaining Brahmanical ritual specialists for consecration and royal ceremonies alongside Buddhist monks, as the Angkorian and Burmese courts did. Patronage therefore reshaped the symbols of kingship without necessarily erasing earlier ritual vocabularies.

Cause and Effect

Patronage, Legitimacy, and Religious Authority

Long-term causes

Older traditions of royal donation to Buddhist institutions, shared ideas of merit and kingship, and the established prestige of monastic learning.

Short-term causes

Political competition among successor and rival courts after the fall of Pagan and the shifting of power at Angkor, and rulers' need for new symbols of legitimacy.

Immediate context

The availability of Sri Lankan-linked monks and ordination claims gave rulers concrete projects: inviting a lineage, building a hall, or founding a monastery.

Immediate effects

Prestigious monastic communities, new temples and images, and enhanced royal reputations for piety.

Long-term consequences

A durable association of kingship with protection of the sāsana, and monastic institutions with significant property and independent claims.

Historical complexity

Patronage did not make the sangha a state instrument; monks retained disciplinary authority, while rulers remained dependent on taxation, alliances, and military resources.

Why it cannot be reduced to one cause: Royal piety, political competition, elite networks, and monastic initiative all drove patronage together; treating religion as merely a tool of rulers ignores monks' own agency.

Section 53.6

Monasteries, Ordination Boundaries, and Monastic Discipline

A Theravada monastery was not a single type of institution. It might be a small residence for a few monks near a village, a large establishment endowed with fields and dependents, or a forest dwelling on the edge of a settlement. Monks were ranked by seniority counted from higher ordination, and a community was supposed to regulate itself through the fortnightly recitation of disciplinary rules and the settlement of offenses through agreed procedures. Novices, young boys or men who had taken the preliminary vows, lived with monks, studied, and could proceed to full ordination when old enough and when proper conditions were met.

Higher ordination required not only qualified participants but a proper location. The disciplinary texts specify that formal acts of the sangha must be performed within a defined and consecrated boundary, a sīmā, whose limits are marked and whose establishment follows prescribed procedures. Ordination halls and their boundary markers therefore have a physical and legal importance. A sima could be placed on land, or in some traditions on water, and its validity depended on the correct performance of the consecration. The decision to build or consecrate a new sima, as Dhammaceti did at Pegu, could carry a declaration that previous ordinations performed elsewhere had been defective or that a new lineage deserved recognition.

Discipline also mattered in everyday terms. The rules regulated what monks might own, eat, wear, and do, and they shaped the relationship between monks and lay supporters, who provided food and robes. Reports of laxity could provoke reform movements or royal interventions. Zhou Daguan's account of Cambodian monks, for example, notes the monks' meat-eating, which is not necessarily a breach of the Pali disciplinary code, since that code permits meat under specified conditions, but which a Chinese observer familiar with a different vegetarian monastic convention found noteworthy. Evidence of this kind reminds students that standards of practice were interpreted and debated, not uniform.

Finally, the existence of rules did not mean the sangha acted as a disciplined centralized hierarchy. Monasteries often owned land and had their own relationships with local donors. Senior monks, sometimes given royal titles, could exercise influence over several monasteries, but their authority depended on the cooperation of the communities involved. Disputes about discipline were also disputes about wealth, status, and patronage, which is why the language of correct monastic practice appears so frequently in chronicles of reform.

Section 53.7

Monastic Endowments, Land, Labor, and Economic Power

Buddhist monasteries became significant economic institutions. Kings, queens, officials, and other donors gave land, cultivators, livestock, tools, goods, and money to religious foundations, and the donations were recorded in inscriptions to fix their terms and to place them under the moral sanction of merit and, often, of curses against anyone who might seize them. At Pagan, inscriptions record gifts of rice land, orchards, and families of dependents assigned to serve a temple or monastery. The persons so assigned, often called by scholars "temple slaves" or "pagoda servants" in English translations, were bound to the foundation and its upkeep; their precise legal and social status is debated, and translation as slavery may overstate or misdescribe a range of obligations.

Such endowments had real economic effects. They created reliable income for monasteries, supporting teaching, ritual, and building; they provided an organized way to channel surplus into religiously meaningful projects; and they gave donors a visible stake in the institutions they supported. Religious estates were frequently granted exemption from certain royal dues, which could make them attractive to cultivators and a valuable economic base for the monastery. Studies of Pagan have suggested that the accumulation of tax-exempt religious land may have constrained the royal revenue base, and Victor Lieberman and Michael Aung-Thwin, among others, have debated how far this explains Pagan's political weakness. Many historians now treat endowments as one factor among several, not as a sufficient cause of collapse.

Endowments also raise questions about control. Kings could, under pressure, resume land, tax religious estates, or redistribute resources, and later Burmese rulers are recorded as attempting reforms of this kind. Monks could mobilize donors, appeal to merit, and invoke the curses of the inscriptions to defend their holdings. Cultivators and servile dependents were affected by decisions made at higher levels: assignment to a monastery might offer protection or impose lasting obligation, depending on circumstances. The economic story is therefore also a story of negotiation among kings, monasteries, donors, and workers, and its details varied by period and polity.

For other regions, the evidence is thinner and more difficult. Khmer inscriptions of the Angkorian period show extensive temple economies with assigned personnel, a pattern that the Buddhist foundations of the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries inherited rather than invented. In Tai-speaking polities, inscriptions and chronicles record gifts of land and people to monasteries, but the surviving evidence is less abundant than at Pagan. In general, historians must be cautious about extending the Pagan model to every mainland society.

Section 53.8

Monks and Lay Communities: Education, Ritual, and Religious Services

The relationship between monks and villagers was reciprocal. Lay families supported monks with daily food and with robes, medicine, and shelter, and in return monks offered teaching, moral guidance, and ritual services. Monasteries were usually the only organized institutions of literacy in the countryside, so many boys received at least basic instruction there, learning to read and write and to recite protective verses. Men often entered the monkhood temporarily, a practice that expanded the number of people with some monastic education. The evidence for this pattern in the period 1200–1500 is largely indirect, coming from later sources and from the way inscriptions and chronicles assume the existence of village monasteries, but scholars generally accept that monastic education was a long-standing feature of mainland communities.

Monks also provided ritual services at key moments of life and the calendar. Sermons, chanting of protective texts, ceremonies for the dead, house blessings, and festivals associated with the agricultural year and Buddhist observances all involved monastic participation. Funerary rites in particular linked monks to families through the transfer of merit to the deceased. At the same time, many rituals were performed by lay specialists: ritual experts who consecrated houses, managed spirit offerings, healed the sick, or conducted ceremonies tied to the agricultural cycle and to the protection of villages. Monks and lay ritual specialists occupied different positions, sometimes cooperating and sometimes competing, and historians should not assume that monks controlled all religious knowledge.

Differences in region and status also shaped the relationship. In large urban centers, monasteries were integrated with court patronage and scholarly communities. In smaller villages, monks might be local men whose families supported them and who played a role in resolving disputes. Women supported monastic institutions as donors and as household managers who prepared offerings, though they could not receive higher ordination in the Theravada tradition as it existed in this period; the evidence for women's religious roles comes mostly from donor inscriptions, which favor elite women. Recognizing these variations avoids picturing a single model of Buddhist village life.

Monastic teaching also depended on material circumstances. A novice needed someone able to provide food, a residence with a teacher, and access to texts or to a community of reciters. Those conditions were easier to sustain near substantial settlements and well-endowed monasteries than in isolated hamlets. A royal donation might support advanced study by a few monks while ordinary household offerings kept a small monastery functioning throughout the year. It follows that the existence of a learned Pali author at Pagan or Chiang Mai cannot prove an identical curriculum in every village. A historical reconstruction should distinguish the literate specialist, the novice receiving practical instruction, and the lay worshipper who learned primarily by listening. Where girls and women participated through donations, oral teaching, or ritual sponsorship, they too entered Buddhist networks, though the surviving inscriptions reveal their experience unevenly. Thus religious expansion depended on everyday relationships of maintenance and instruction, not solely on celebrated ordination journeys or royal proclamations.

Everyday Life and Social History

Monks and Village Communities

For most mainland villagers between 1200 and 1500, Buddhism was encountered less through chronicles or Pali texts than through nearby monks and familiar practices: the morning round when monks received food, the recitation of protective verses at a house or field, the festivals of the Buddhist calendar, and the funeral rites that connected the living to their dead. The monastery might also be a school, a meeting place, and a store of manuscripts and medical knowledge, though the specifics varied widely. Who sponsored a particular festival, how much a household could give, and which monks were respected locally depended on local circumstances.

Historians reconstruct these patterns from inscriptions that mention village monasteries, from later chronicles and customary law, from monastic buildings and images that survive, and from comparative evidence from later centuries. Because most of these sources were created by monks, kings, and wealthy donors, ordinary villagers' own views, particularly those of women, children, and the poor, must often be inferred. This should be stated plainly rather than filled with imagined detail.

Section 53.9

Pagan and the Burmese Buddhist Inheritance

Pagan provides the institutional foundation for much of what followed in Burma. Its monastic communities, its Pali scholarship, its donation practices, and its architectural landscape remained points of reference for later Burmese courts. Chapters 40 and 41 give the history of Pagan's rise and its Buddhist society; here the focus is on how its religious institutions connected to the later transformation. Pagan's monks included several fraternities, among which the group founded by Chapata after his Sri Lankan sojourn, the Sinhalese fraternity, became prominent in the late twelfth and thirteenth centuries. Chapata himself is credited by later tradition with Pali compositions, and Pagan produced other works of grammar and commentary, including Aggavamsa's Saddaniti of 1154.

The connection with Sri Lanka at Pagan was not a one-time event. The later narrative describes continuing travel, and the increasing identification of some Pagan monks with a Sri Lankan lineage is reflected in the memory of rivalry among fraternities. King Narapatisithu (r. 1174–1211) is associated in later tradition with the Sinhalese fraternity's growth. The Pagan court also supported older fraternities, and Pagan inscriptions record donations to monks and temples across many traditions. This plurality is important: Pagan Buddhism was not simply a Theravada state church, but a layered landscape in which Pali-oriented, Mahayana-influenced, and locally rooted practices coexisted.

The end of Pagan's imperial authority in the late thirteenth century, usually dated to 1287 and the culmination of Mongol-Yuan pressure and internal weakness, did not end Buddhist institutions in the Irrawaddy basin. Monasteries and their endowments continued under the successor states, which Chapter 59 examines, and Pagan itself remained a center of pilgrimage and temple maintenance for centuries. Monastic networks and Pali learning survived political fragmentation, demonstrating that religious institutions could outlast the states that had patronized them. The post-Pagan courts also borrowed Pagan's religious vocabulary of kingship to support their own claims, one reason Pagan figures so often in later chronicles as an ideal past.

Historians therefore read Pagan in two ways. As a historical society, it left abundant inscriptions and monuments showing a thriving Buddhist community under royal patronage. As a memory, it became a model that later monastic and royal writers could invoke, sometimes accurately and sometimes through simplification. Keeping the two apart is essential for understanding the Kalyani reform, which drew on Pagan history to argue for the proper order of the sangha in the fifteenth century.

Editorial paraphrase (not a verbatim excerpt):

The inscription, in its several versions, recounts a prince's act of devotion connected with the illness and death of his royal father: the offering of a Buddha image covered in gold, the founding of an associated temple, and the expression of the hope that the merit of the act would benefit the king and others. The Pyu and Mon texts also show that long-established communities continued to be addressed in their own languages in the early twelfth century. Section 53.10

The Mon World and Lower Burma's Religious Networks

Lower Burma, the region of the Irrawaddy delta and the Gulf of Martaban, was home to Mon-speaking communities whose Buddhist traditions had deep roots. Mon inscriptions, temples, and monastic communities are documented from the first millennium into the Pagan period, and Mon speakers continued to be prominent in the region's ports and religious life. The port of Martaban, at the mouth of the Salween, and Pegu, inland on the Pegu river, became important commercial and religious centers by the fourteenth century, when the Mon-centered kingdom of Hanthawaddy took shape, a history told in Chapter 60. Because Lower Burma faced the Bay of Bengal, it was a natural gateway for contacts with Sri Lankan monastic communities.

One of the most persistent narratives about Burmese Buddhism holds that Theravada reached Burma through the Mon: that Buddhist missionaries sent in Asoka's time to a land called Suvannabhumi settled at Thaton, that Thaton preserved the true teaching, and that Anawrahta of Pagan conquered the city in the eleventh century and brought back its scriptures and monks. This story is preserved in later monastic and royal writings, including the Kalyani inscriptions, which give it extended form. Historians treat it with caution. The conquest of Thaton, as narrated, is not clearly documented in contemporary inscriptions or archaeology, and several scholars, notably Michael Aung-Thwin, have argued that it is a later construct. At the same time, there is good evidence that Mon-speaking Buddhists were active participants in Pagan's religious life, so the story may compress a real but more complex interaction into a single founding event.

The more defensible picture is one of multiple channels. Pyu communities, Mon communities, Sri Lankan-linked monks arriving by sea, Indian traditions, and Burmese-speaking communities all contributed. Assigning to the Mon a unique or exclusive role in transmitting Theravada, a tendency in some later accounts and some modern popular writing, rests on origin narratives that served the legitimacy claims of particular communities. Recognizing Mon contributions does not require accepting that narrative. Mon scholar-monks, patrons, and scribes were important, and Mon-language Buddhist texts and inscriptions form a significant body of evidence, but the Mon were one part of a larger network.

After Pagan, Lower Burma's religious networks gained new prominence. The kings of Hanthawaddy fostered monastic learning, cultivated contact with Sri Lanka, and promoted the great sacred sites of the region, including the Shwedagon pagoda at Dagon, which later tradition associates with generous donations by the fifteenth-century queen Shin Saw Pu and her successor Dhammaceti. These activities culminated in the Kalyani reform of Section 53.17. Relations between Lower and Upper Burma were not simply those of sacred periphery and political center: they involved rivalry, borrowing, and mutual influence, which Chapters 59 and 60 explore in political terms.

Section 53.11

Tai-Speaking Polities and the Growth of Theravada Patronage

Tai-speaking communities moved gradually into the Chao Phraya basin and northern valleys over several centuries, a movement examined in Chapter 54, and they entered a region that already had Buddhist and Brahmanical inheritances. The Mon-influenced Dvaravati tradition had left images and sites in the central plain; Khmer power had left temples and administrative centers, such as Lopburi; and Mon-speaking communities remained prominent in the north, notably around Haripunchai (Lamphun). Tai rulers did not introduce Buddhism to these places. They adopted, redirected, and in some cases rebuilt existing religious landscapes, and they added to them new foundations and new connections.

In the fourteenth century, Buddhist patronage by Tai rulers became increasingly visible. At Sukhothai, inscriptions associated with King Lithai (r. c. 1347 to the 1360s or 1370s, with the chronology debated; see Chapter 55) record his religious building and, according to one inscription, his temporary ordination as a monk. Lan Na's chronicles, written in the early sixteenth century, describe the arrival in the 1360s and 1370s of a Sri Lankan-lineage monk, Sumana, who traveled from Martaban to Sukhothai and then to Lan Na, where King Kue Na founded Wat Suan Dok at Chiang Mai in about 1371. Such narratives indicate royal sponsorship of imported or renewed ordination traditions, although the later date and purpose of the chronicles require caution about specific details. Forest-dwelling and village-dwelling fraternities, which Section 53.4 introduced, appear in this northern and central material.

Tai religious patronage was also notable for its use of images and temple architecture. Large Buddha images, distinctive stupa types, and temple complexes expressed royal piety and connections with Sri Lankan and Mon models. Pali learning was cultivated in monastic schools and attached to royal courts, and the production of Pali and Tai-language texts increased, a development Section 53.16 treats. Patronage was nevertheless adaptive. Tai rulers retained Brahmanical court specialists, honored local guardian spirits, and incorporated older sacred sites into new Buddhist landscapes. The emphasis of this chapter is therefore on the institutional adaptation of existing regional cultures rather than on a replacement of them.

The detailed political history of Sukhothai, including its kings, its chronology, and the contested authenticity of the Ramkhamhaeng inscription, belongs to Chapter 55; Lan Na's society and institutions belong to Chapter 56; and Ayutthaya's rise belongs to Chapter 57. For the purposes of this chapter, Sukhothai and the northern principalities illustrate how a newly prominent political elite used Buddhist monastic networks to build legitimacy and local loyalty while working within the religious geography it inherited.

Archaeological Study

Buddhist Landscapes of Sukhothai and Northern Thailand

The Historic Town of Sukhothai and Associated Historic Towns, inscribed by UNESCO as a World Heritage property, includes the ruins of Sukhothai, Si Satchanalai, and Kamphaeng Phet. The remains include monastery precincts (wat) with large stupas, assembly and image halls, Buddha images in a distinctive local style, and water-management features. At Wat Mahathat in Sukhothai, a central stupa of a form often described as a lotus-bud type stands among numerous smaller structures. Some stupas at Si Satchanalai and elsewhere have bell-shaped forms that scholars compare with Sri Lankan types. Other monuments in the region show Khmer-style architecture, a reminder that the landscape combined layers from different periods and traditions.

In the north, Wat Phra That Haripunchai at Lamphun preserves a Mon-associated tradition into the Tai period, and Chiang Mai's Wat Suan Dok and Wat Chedi Luang show the royal sponsorship of large monastic complexes in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. Archaeologists and art historians use style, construction sequence, and inscriptions to date these structures, but dating is often imprecise and many buildings were repaired, rebuilt, or expanded later.

What the evidence can show: patronage on a significant scale, ties to Sri Lankan and Mon models, adaptation of older sacred sites, and the importance of monastic precincts to the planning of towns. What it cannot show: the beliefs of every user of the spaces, the exact lineage of the monks who lived there, or the proportion of the population that participated. A monument's form and date do not establish the doctrinal views of all who worshipped there.

Section 53.12

Cambodia and the Transformation of the Angkorian Religious World

Angkor's religious landscape in the thirteenth century was rich and layered. Shaiva and Vaishnava temples, Mahayana Buddhist foundations, Brahmanical ritual specialists, and inherited sacred sites all coexisted. After the reign of Jayavarman VII, whose Buddhist building program Chapter 38 describes, the later thirteenth century saw a reaction in which some Buddhist images at temples such as the Bayon were altered or defaced, an episode traditionally associated with Jayavarman VIII (r. c. 1243–1295) and a Shaiva revival. Scholars debate how systematic and how politically motivated the alterations were and over what period they occurred, and the picture is more complicated than a simple reversal of royal religion.

It is within this setting that Theravada monastic communities became more visible. Some historians place a royal turn toward Theravada patronage in the late thirteenth or early fourteenth century, pointing to the reign of Indravarman III, also called Śrīndravarman (c. 1295–1307), during whose reign Zhou Daguan visited, though the evidence is limited and interpretations differ. Fourteenth-century inscriptions, including some in Pali, point toward a growing Theravada presence, as Ian Harris and others have argued, and archaeological work at Angkor has documented later Buddhist modifications and reuse of older temples, including Angkor Wat, which was a Hindu monument originally and became a major Buddhist site. Wider contacts with Sri Lankan-linked monks, such as the Khmer prince Tamalinda in the Kalyani narrative, supply a background to these changes.

The chapter's central caution applies strongly here: evidence does not support a simple account in which Theravada suddenly replaced Mahayana and Hindu practice at Angkor. Monastic communities of a new kind became more prominent while older institutions continued, and royal courts maintained Brahmanical ritual specialists for a long time. Equally, Theravada's growth cannot be treated as the explanation for the political and economic transformation of Angkor, which Chapter 61 examines in terms of warfare, ecological and infrastructural pressures, shifting trade, and population movement. Recent scholarship has challenged older narratives that blamed a religious change for the weakening of the Angkorian state.

Changes in monastic patronage can also be traced in the physical landscape. Over time, terraces, galleries, and chambers at Angkor temples were used to house Buddha images and as sites of Buddhist worship, while the great temple complexes retained their layouts and many of their original sacred meanings for local communities. Khmer society's relationships with guardian spirits and ancestral cults, which Section 53.14 discusses, also continued. The result was a religious world in which new monastic communities took their place within, not simply over, inherited sacred geography. The later history of Cambodia after Angkor, in Chapter 89, builds on this foundation.

Division IV · Sections 53.13–53.16

Religious Practice, Local Adaptation, and Cultural Change

Everyday religious life, spirit traditions, sacred landscapes, and learning

Section 53.13

Merit-Making, Donation, and the Participation of Lay Buddhists

Lay participation was the economic and social foundation of the monastic network. Merit-making took many forms: feeding monks, presenting robes and medicine, giving land or labor, sponsoring a Buddha image or a small shrine, contributing to the repair of a stupa, or paying for a ceremony. Inscriptions from Pagan, which survive in large numbers, show that donors included not only kings and high officials but also families of modest standing who sponsored small temples or gave a field, a garden, or a few dependents. Their records often name the donor's relatives and express the wish that the merit be shared with them, and sometimes with all beings.

Giving was also a social act. A donation could mark a household's standing in the community, strengthen ties with a monastery, and fulfill obligations to the dead. Festivals provided occasions for communal giving, and recording a donation on stone meant making it public and permanent. Because stone inscriptions required resources, they over-represent wealthier families. Many smaller gifts, offered in food and labor and never written down, are invisible in the surviving record, though historians assume they were far more numerous.

Women appear regularly in the sources as donors. Pagan inscriptions record gifts by queens, other women of the royal family, and women of non-royal households, in some cases in their own names and as owners of the property they gave. Such evidence suggests that women could hold and dispose of property and that they participated visibly in merit-making. At the same time, the evidence is largely from elite or well-off women, and it would be a mistake to assume that these donors represent the experience of all women or households. Women's roles in preparing offerings and sustaining household religious practice are probably underrepresented in written sources. Historians should therefore use inscriptions cautiously and avoid generalizing from them.

Lay participation also shaped the character of Buddhist institutions. Because monasteries depended on continued donations, monks needed to maintain the respect of lay supporters, and patrons exercised influence over appointments, building programs, and the reputation of monastic communities. The reciprocal relationship helps explain why Buddhist institutions were durable: their support was spread across many households and across different social levels, not concentrated in a single patron, even though royal giving left the most visible traces.

Section 53.14

Ancestral Spirits, Local Deities, and Religious Continuity

Southeast Asian societies had, and have, rich traditions of relations with spirits: ancestors, guardians of villages and fields, spirits of mountains and rivers, and beings associated with particular trees or places. These traditions long predate Theravada's growth and were not destroyed by it. In Burma, the cult of spirits known as nats, with shrines and festivals, remained an important part of religious life, and later tradition links the organization of a group of principal nats to the royal courts of Pagan. In Tai-speaking societies, village and territorial spirits (phi) continued to receive offerings, and in Khmer society, guardian spirits associated with places and with ancestors (neak ta) remained significant. Brahmanical specialists at court also maintained rituals tied to kingship and the agricultural cycle.

Buddhist institutions interacted with these traditions in several ways. Monastic precincts were often placed on or beside existing sacred places, so that older associations were absorbed into new foundations. Buddhist rituals of protection, such as the chanting of protective verses, could address concerns that spirit cults had long addressed. Monks might also be asked to officiate at funerals and house blessings in settings where spirit offerings were simultaneously made. Official royal religion could assign spirits a place within a cosmology presided over by the Buddha, as with the use in some courts of Indic deities as protectors of the dispensation. Doctrinally strict monks and reformers sometimes criticized spirit practices, and chronicles occasionally describe rulers suppressing particular cults, but the evidence suggests that these efforts were selective and often temporary.

The concept of religious "conversion" can mislead here. Many people participated in several ritual systems at once, calling on monks for some purposes and on spirit specialists or Brahmanical ritualists for others, without a sense of contradiction. Historians therefore speak of a layered religious field in which Buddhist institutions gained prominence while other practices persisted in changed forms. Local practices were not "survivals" doomed to vanish; they were living parts of religious life that adapted to new circumstances.

The classification of a practice as Buddhist or non-Buddhist was itself a historical judgment, not a fixed boundary visible to every participant. A family might sponsor offerings to monks while maintaining duties at an ancestral shrine; a local guardian could be understood as protecting a Buddhist foundation rather than competing with it. Conversely, a reformer seeking stricter discipline might criticize precisely the practices that a village regarded as compatible with merit-making. Inscriptions usually record the donor's preferred religious vocabulary, while ritual objects and places can have several successive uses. Neither kind of evidence establishes how a particular household understood its actions. Comparison across Burma, Tai-speaking communities, and Cambodia is therefore more useful when it asks who authorized rituals, who financed them, and whether old sacred sites were reused, than when it tries to count religious communities as belonging exclusively to one tradition. Religious transformation changed institutional relationships and local interpretations without requiring the disappearance of older obligations.

Historical Debate

Religious Replacement or Religious Reconfiguration?

The question: When Theravada monastic networks expanded between 1200 and 1500, did they replace earlier religious traditions, such as Mahayana Buddhism, Brahmanical ritual, and spirit cults, or did they reconfigure a religious field in which those traditions continued in altered forms?

Replacement-oriented interpretations: Older scholarship, and many later monastic chronicles, described Theravada as a reformed, "purer" Buddhism that displaced Mahayana and Hindu cults, sometimes through royal suppression. The defacement of Buddhist images at Angkor and the reforming rhetoric of Burmese and Thai chronicles are often cited in support.
Reconfiguration-oriented interpretations: Many recent scholars emphasize continuity: Brahmanical specialists remained at court, spirit cults persisted, older temples were reused, and monastic communities took their place within inherited sacred landscapes. On this view, Theravada's growth altered the balance and the institutional forms of the religious field without eliminating other traditions.

Evidence: Both views draw on inscriptions, chronicles, temple reuse, ritual practice, and travel accounts like Zhou Daguan's. Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and scale, and also how much weight to give later chronicles, whose rhetoric of purification may exaggerate discontinuity. It is not primarily a dispute over dates. Why it matters: If replacement is assumed, local practices appear as obstacles or remnants; if reconfiguration is assumed, they appear as participants in a continuing process. The second view fits the surviving evidence better in most regions, though specific episodes of suppression or reform did occur and should not be erased.

Section 53.15

Buddhist Architecture, Images, Relics, and Sacred Landscapes

Buildings and images are among the best evidence for religious change, because they survive where chronicles are silent and they show where resources went. Monastic complexes of the period typically included a stupa (chedi or pagoda) enshrining relics or sacred objects, an image hall or shrine, residences for monks, a library or preaching hall, and, in many cases, an ordination hall marked by boundary stones. The proportions among these elements varied by region and period. Pagan's great temples, many built before 1300, and the bell-shaped and lotus-bud stupas of the Tai-speaking regions represent different local architectural traditions responding to similar religious needs.

Changes in the placement and design of religious structures can indicate institutional change. A new ordination hall, as at Pegu, suggests a claim about lineage. A concentration of monasteries around a royal temple suggests court patronage. The reuse of older Khmer or Mon temples for Buddhist images indicates continuing appropriation of existing sacred sites. Relics, including those said to be of the Buddha himself, were important focuses of devotion, and their enshrinement in a stupa could tie a ruler and a region to a wider network of sacred places. Buddha images also traveled; claims that a particular image had come from Sri Lanka or Cambodia appear in later traditions and help explain the prestige of specific sites, though such claims need verification.

Historians nevertheless need to be careful. A monument's date and style show when and under what patronage it was built, not the beliefs of everyone who visited it. A temple dedicated by a king may have been used by villagers who understood it in their own terms. Structures were often repaired, rebuilt, or enlarged in later centuries, and styles borrowed from earlier or foreign models may not reflect doctrinal affiliation. Material evidence is most powerful when combined with inscriptions, chronicles, and excavation. It is especially useful for the religious spaces that elite chronicles overlook, such as small village shrines and monastic residences, though these are less likely to survive.

Sacred landscapes also extended beyond buildings. Hills, caves, rivers, and old trees were venerated as places associated with the Buddha's visits, with relics, or with local spirits, and pilgrimage routes linked them. Buddhist traditions regularly claimed such places by narrating that the Buddha had visited them or left footprints there. These narratives helped integrate local geography into a Buddhist cosmology and strengthened the connection between local communities and the wider Buddhist world.

Section 53.16

Pali Learning, Vernacular Traditions, and Monastic Education

Pali was the language of the Theravada scriptures and their commentaries, and learning it was a central scholarly activity for monks. Monastic libraries held Pali texts copied onto palm leaves, and teachers passed on grammar, commentary, and recitation. Pagan produced grammatical and commentarial works in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, and later centres, including Chiang Mai and Pegu, became noted for Pali scholarship. The Pali chronicles composed in the sixteenth century in northern Thailand, such as the Jinakalamali, show the continuing vitality of this tradition and its capacity to record local histories in a learned language.

Pali was not, however, the language of everyday religious life. It functioned as a liturgical and scholarly language, comparable in some respects to Latin in medieval Europe, though the comparison has limits. Sermons and explanations were given in Mon, Burmese, Tai languages, or Khmer, and many monks and laypeople understood Pali texts only through translation or explanation. In Burma, a distinctive scholarly form developed in which Pali passages were glossed word by word in Burmese, a genre known as nissaya, whose earliest forms are generally traced to the Pagan and post-Pagan periods. Comparable practices of glossing and vernacular composition appeared elsewhere. The spread of Pali learning thus occurred alongside, and encouraged, the development of vernacular religious literature.

Monastic education also reached beyond the training of monks. Monasteries taught boys basic literacy, recitation, and moral instruction, and the temporary ordination of laymen extended some monastic learning into wider society. The extent to which this was true in the period 1200–1500 varied and is hard to measure; later evidence suggests that it became well established in several regions. Knowledge was also transmitted orally, through chanting and sermons, and through arts such as painting and sculpture. Historians therefore treat Pali learning, vernacular tradition, and oral transmission as complementary channels, not as stages in a single progression.

Manuscript circulation imposed limits as well as opportunities. Palm leaves required preparation, skilled copying, protection against heat and insects, and periodic replacement; a surviving copy may therefore be centuries younger than the work it preserves. The date of a manuscript and the date of its composition cannot be assumed to be the same. In a monastery, memorization and recitation could preserve material even when few books were available, while a teacher's vernacular explanation allowed listeners with little Pali to participate. A gloss that gives the meaning of Pali words in a local language reveals one form of scholarly mediation, but it does not measure literacy across an entire kingdom. The interaction between Pali authority and local explanation helps account for how monastic networks shared texts while developing distinct regional literary practices. It also cautions against treating the later abundance of manuscripts as direct evidence for the number circulating in the fourteenth century.

Fifteenth-century reform, regional connections, historiography, and synthesis

Section 53.17

Fifteenth-Century Monastic Reform and the Kalyani Inscriptions

The reform associated with King Dhammaceti of Hanthawaddy is the best-documented fifteenth-century reorganization of Theravada monastic life on the mainland. Dhammaceti, who reigned at Pegu from about 1471 to 1492, according to the usual chronology, is remembered in later tradition as a former monk who became king. The sources describe him as concerned about the validity of ordination in his realm, where several fraternities with differing lineages existed. His response was to dispatch a delegation of monks to Sri Lanka to receive ordination in a lineage believed to be reliable, and on their return to establish a new ordination hall at Pegu, the Kalyani Sima, named after the Kalyani river and sima near Kelaniya in Sri Lanka, where the monks had been ordained.

After the monks' return, according to the inscriptions, monks from the existing fraternities were invited to receive ordination again within the new sima, with the aim of unifying the sangha under a single, validated lineage. The reform is generally dated to 1476–1479. The inscriptions that record it were engraved on a series of stone slabs, commonly counted as ten, in Pali and Mon, and set up at the site. Accounts of the engraving dates of the surviving inscriptions differ, so historians should distinguish the chronology of the reform itself, which falls in the late 1470s, from the dating of particular epigraphic records, which may have been completed during or after the events they describe.

The reform had religious and political dimensions. Religiously, it addressed the concern for correct ordination and discipline described in Section 53.4. Politically, it strengthened the king's standing as a protector of the sāsana, helped unify monastic communities within Hanthawaddy, and strengthened the kingdom's claims in competition with rival Burmese courts. The inscriptions also narrate earlier history, including the traditional account of the mission to Suvannabhumi, the Pagan monks' travels to Sri Lanka, and the divisions that followed, thus placing the reform within a long lineage story. Historians therefore read the inscriptions both as evidence about the reform itself and as a fifteenth-century interpretation of earlier history, which must be evaluated critically, particularly where it recounts origins centuries earlier.

Dhammaceti's reform did not create a unified Burmese Buddhist church, and it should not be read as the "introduction" of Theravada to Burma. It consolidated one set of monastic relationships within a particular kingdom at a particular moment, and its influence on later Burmese monastic life is a subject for Chapters 60 and 85. Other regions had their own reforms and councils, and monastic disputes recurred in later centuries. The Kalyani inscriptions nevertheless remain a central source for the study of monastic lineage, Sri Lankan connections, and the way a Southeast Asian court used the past to organize the present.

According to the narrative preserved in the Kalyani inscriptions as published by Taw Sein Ko, the royal mission included twenty-two senior monks (theras) accompanied by twenty-two disciples. The associated monument is conventionally described as ten inscribed stone slabs, with Pali and Mon versions. These are specific details of the royal account and the surviving inscriptional complex, not an independent census of everyone involved in reform throughout the kingdom.

Section 53.18

The Theravada World of the Fifteenth Century

By the late fifteenth century, monastic and royal connections linked many parts of the mainland to Sri Lanka and to one another. In Burma, Hanthawaddy's reform reflected a Mon-centered court's engagement with Sri Lankan lineage, while the courts of Upper Burma, whose histories Chapter 59 examines, continued the inherited traditions of Pagan monastic learning. In the Tai-speaking north, Lan Na's monastic communities and chronicles were closely tied to Sri Lankan-lineage fraternities and to the Pali learning later memorialized in a tradition of a council at Chiang Mai in 1477, though the sources for it are later chronicles. In the central plain, Ayutthaya, whose rise Chapter 57 describes, inherited Khmer, Mon, and Sukhothai religious cultures and supported its own monastic communities.

Cambodia's monastic communities, increasingly identified with Theravada, continued to occupy and transform the older temple landscape, as Section 53.12 described. In the Lao world, Chapter 58 examines Lan Xang, which later Lao chronicle tradition associates with its founder Fa Ngum in the mid-fourteenth century and with Khmer religious connections, including the tradition of a Buddha image, the Phra Bang, said to have come from Cambodia. These traditions are later accounts and their details are contested, but they indicate that Lao rulers and monks also took part in the regional networks.

The connections among these centers were maintained by travel and correspondence. Monks moved among monasteries to study, to teach, or to seek ordination; rulers exchanged gifts and invitations; texts were copied and circulated; and pilgrims visited sacred sites. Merchants, whose caravans and boats provided transport and sometimes patronage, formed another channel. These ties created shared institutions: common reference to Pali texts, to the sima and the ordination procedure, and to a shared repertoire of sacred stories. They did not create political unity. Courts remained rivals, and the same networks could carry competing claims about whose lineage was correct.

The regional picture was thus one of integration without uniformity. Monks of different courts might recognize each other's ordinations or dispute them; rulers might borrow from neighbors' religious institutions while fighting their armies; and local practices of ritual, language, and art varied widely. The shared tradition provided a common vocabulary through which differences were expressed and negotiated. Its influence was strongest in monastic and royal circles, and its reach into village practice varied by place.

Section 53.19

Why Did Theravada Become So Influential?

Historians have proposed several explanations for the growing influence of Theravada, and they differ in how well the evidence supports them. One explanation emphasizes royal patronage: rulers who sponsored Pali-oriented monastic communities gave them resources, status, and protection, and the prestige of the Sri Lankan lineage offered a tool of legitimacy in competitive political settings. This explanation has strong support in inscriptions and chronicles, though it explains rulers' motives better than villagers' participation.

A second explanation points to monastic organization. Communities with a clear disciplinary framework, a defined route of ordination, and a tradition of study could reproduce themselves across generations and travel to new places. The ordination hall and lineage provided portable institutions that could be established at a new court. A third explanation stresses vernacular accessibility: while the scriptures were in Pali, monks explained them in local languages and trained boys in local monasteries, so that the tradition reached communities in languages they understood. Evidence for this is strongest for later centuries, but the nissaya tradition and the language of the inscriptions suggest it began earlier.

Other explanations involve commerce and mobility. Trade routes across the Bay of Bengal and overland carried monks, texts, and relics, and ports and market towns were natural bases for monasteries and patrons. A final group of explanations points to local religious needs: protection, merit, funerary rites, and community identity, which Buddhist institutions could meet while accommodating spirit cults and older practices. These factors worked together; none by itself accounts for the pattern.

Historians should distinguish demonstrable mechanisms, such as patronage, ordination networks, endowments, and education, from broad claims that Theravada was inherently more popular, more democratic, or more suited to the mainland's political culture than other traditions. Such claims are difficult to test, often draw on later nationalist or comparative assumptions, and risk teleology. Mahayana and Brahmanical traditions also had patrons, institutions, and appeal, and their decline in some places owed much to specific political and economic circumstances rather than to any intrinsic weakness. The more cautious conclusion is that Theravada's influence resulted from a convergence of institutional strengths, political choices, and regional connections, in contexts that varied.

These explanations can be tested against the chronology and distribution of sources. A claim about royal influence should identify an actual grant, building program, or reform and ask whether monastic growth preceded it. A claim about commerce needs evidence of traveling personnel or texts, not merely the fact that a port was busy. A claim about widespread lay appeal should be compared with donations outside the court and with the changing use of sacred places, while acknowledging that such records are scarce. The same causal model need not fit Pagan, the Chao Phraya basin, and Angkor: earlier Pali traditions, court priorities, and local ritual landscapes differed. Nor was every new monastery evidence that an older tradition had been suppressed. Historical explanation is strongest when it joins specific processes to places and dates, distinguishes a ruler's announced policy from social practice, and allows several causes to interact rather than assuming that success was inevitable from a tradition's teachings.

Section 53.20

The Theravada Transformation: Regional Integration without Religious Uniformity

The Theravada transformation between about 1200 and 1500 was gradual and geographically uneven. It built on earlier Buddhist traditions in the Pyu, Mon, Dvaravati, Pagan, and Angkorian worlds; it was strengthened by renewed monastic connections across the Bay of Bengal in which Southeast Asian monks and rulers were active agents; it relied on royal and lay patronage and on endowments that made monasteries economically powerful; and it adapted to local sacred landscapes, spirit traditions, languages, and older temple complexes. Its institutional core was the ordination lineage, the consecrated boundary, and the monastic fraternity, each of which could be reinforced, contested, or renewed.

The result was a religious framework shared across multiple courts without producing a single Buddhist state. In Burma, Pagan's heritage, the post-Pagan courts, and Hanthawaddy's reform pursued overlapping but distinct paths. In the Tai-speaking regions, new elites sponsored existing monastic networks and added imported lineages. In Cambodia, monastic communities took a larger place within a long-established Hindu-Buddhist temple landscape. In each case, change interacted with political circumstances rather than determining them, and religious transformation was not a sufficient explanation for the political developments of Pagan, Angkor, or any other state. These foundations prepare for the political and regional histories of Chapters 54 through 61, in which monasteries, kings, and merchants appear repeatedly.

At the level of texts and training, a broader learned world emerged: Pali commentaries, monastic scholarship, and the prestige of Sri Lankan-linked ordination could connect communities separated by language and political allegiance. At the level of institutions, however, each monastery still depended on patrons, land, disciplinary relationships, and the practical ability to secure teachers and valid ordination. Neither texts nor lineage claims produced a centralized authority embracing the entire mainland. The repeated desire to renew ordination, visible particularly in Dhammaceti's reform, demonstrates that religious credentials themselves could remain disputed.

At the level of social experience, the transformation was less a change from one exclusive identity to another than a reorganization of practices and opportunities. Some families gained access to teachers, ceremonies, and new forms of merit-making; some cultivators bore obligations attached to monastic endowments; established sacred places and protective rituals continued to matter. Elite inscriptions illuminate sponsorship more readily than household motives, and later chronicles often interpret the past through disputes about legitimacy. These differences in source visibility mean that a regional history must resist presenting every ruler's monastic program as an equivalent measure of popular devotion.

The most defensible conclusion is therefore comparative rather than uniform. Theravada networks helped link mainland polities and created a durable vocabulary for religious authority, but their institutional forms were negotiated locally and remained subject to political and economic change. The story also has no inevitable terminal point in 1500: reform, rivalry, renewal, and regional adaptation continued. Chapter 53 establishes these mechanisms so that the next eight chapters can examine particular mainland kingdoms without mistaking the history of an individual court for the history of Buddhist life across Southeast Asia.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Theravada transformation of mainland Southeast Asia between about 1200 and 1500 was a gradual, geographically uneven process in which pre-existing Buddhist traditions, renewed monastic connections, royal patronage, and local religious practice interacted to produce new institutional configurations without eliminating regional diversity. It began with the problem of evidence, distinguishing contemporary inscriptions and travel accounts from later chronicles and modern reconstructions, and distinguishing the presence of Buddhism from evidence for a particular school or lineage.

It showed that Theravada was not new in the thirteenth century. Pali-inscribed objects among the Pyu, Pali and Mon inscriptions and Buddhist monuments in Dvaravati contexts, the Pali scholarship of Pagan, and signs of contact between Khmer and Sri Lankan-linked monks all indicate earlier presence. At the same time, these communities coexisted with Mahayana, Brahmanical, and spirit traditions, and the evidence does not permit simple identifications of lineage.

Connections with Sri Lanka, carried along the monsoon sea routes and through peninsular ports, grew in importance, and the prestige of the reformed Mahavihara tradition made Sri Lankan ordination attractive. But the exchange was multidirectional. Pagan monks such as Chapata, Khmer figures such as Tamalinda, and later Lower Burmese and Tai-speaking monks and rulers chose which lineages to seek and how to use them.

The institutional heart of the transformation was the ordination lineage, the consecrated boundary, and the monastic fraternity. Because the validity of ordination mattered to monks, kings, and donors, lineage claims could be renewed or contested, and rival fraternities competed. Retrospective claims of purity must be separated from documented reforms.

Royal patronage connected Buddhism to kingship through the idea of protecting the sāsana and gaining merit. Rulers built, endowed, and sponsored, but they did not command the sangha, which retained disciplinary authority and its own interests. Endowments of land and labor made monasteries powerful economic institutions and created tensions with royal revenue, tensions whose role in political change remains debated and should not be exaggerated.

Regional paths differed. Pagan provided an institutional foundation that outlasted its political authority. Lower Burma's Mon-speaking communities were important participants, but later narratives assigning them an exclusive role in transmitting Theravada rest on retrospective origin stories. In the Tai-speaking regions, new elites sponsored existing and imported monastic networks while adapting inherited religious landscapes. At Angkor, Theravada communities became more visible within a persistent Hindu-Buddhist landscape, and religious change should not be treated as the cause of the political transformation of the Angkorian state.

Religious change occurred at several levels. Court patronage could elevate certain monastic lineages without making village ritual, household belief, or local sacred places uniform. Spirit cults, ancestral obligations, and Brahmanical specialists persisted and changed. Architecture, images, and relics show institutional change and adaptive reuse, though they cannot by themselves establish what all users believed. Pali learning developed alongside vernacular explanation and literature.

The fifteenth century brought documented reform, most clearly in Dhammaceti's reorganization of ordination at Pegu in 1476–1479 and the Kalyani inscriptions, which both record the reform and interpret earlier history. By about 1500, monks, patrons, texts, and pilgrimage connected Burma, the Tai-speaking polities, Cambodia, Laos, and Sri Lanka, creating shared institutions without eliminating distinctions among courts and communities.

In evaluating why Theravada became influential, the chapter distinguished demonstrable mechanisms, namely patronage, monastic organization, vernacular teaching, trade and mobility, and responsiveness to local needs, from claims of inherent suitability. The chapter thus provides the religious and institutional framework for Chapters 54 through 61, which examine the political histories of Tai-speaking polities, Sukhothai, Lan Na, Ayutthaya, Lan Xang, the post-Pagan states, Hanthawaddy, and Angkor's transformation.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a long-lived religious tradition can be transformed without being newly invented. The Theravada transformation involved real institutional change, namely lineages, ordination halls, endowments, and scholarly networks, but it rested on centuries of earlier Buddhist presence and on the choices of Southeast Asian rulers, monks, and lay communities. That combination of renewal and continuity is a pattern that recurs throughout Southeast Asian history.

The chapter also reveals how historians must handle retrospective narratives. Stories of a single founding moment, a pure lineage, or an exclusive transmitter were told for reasons connected to the legitimacy of their authors. Learning to read them as evidence of how communities understood their pasts, while testing them against inscriptions, travel accounts, and material remains, is a skill students will need in every later chapter, from the origin stories of Tai kingdoms to the chronicle traditions of Lan Xang and Cambodia.

Later developments in this volume depend on the framework described here. Chapters 54 through 61 examine Tai migrations, Sukhothai, Lan Na, Ayutthaya, Lan Xang, the post-Pagan Burmese states, Hanthawaddy, and Angkor's transformation, and in each, monasteries, patrons, and ordination networks play recurring roles. The persistence of regional diversity within a shared religious vocabulary also shapes the early modern kingdoms of Volume V.

Finally, the chapter contributes to the textbook's larger argument that Southeast Asia has never been merely a passive crossroads. Sri Lankan, Indian, Chinese, and maritime connections were essential, but mainland communities selected, adapted, contested, and transmitted what reached them. The religious history of the mainland between 1200 and 1500 is therefore a history of Southeast Asian agency, and understanding it equips students to read the political and social histories that follow with greater care.
