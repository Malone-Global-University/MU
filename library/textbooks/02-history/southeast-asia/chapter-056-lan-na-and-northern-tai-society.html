The Indigenous Continent at the End of the Eighteenth Century

Indigenous Peoples of North America: A Continental History

Chapter Opening

The Indigenous continent in 1800 was not an empty map for growing empires. Its nations governed territories, markets, households, and diplomatic relationships while facing profoundly different pressures from expanding colonial states and commercial enterprises.

In 1791, an Indigenous coalition inflicted a devastating defeat upon an American army advancing into the Ohio Country. A decade earlier, few officials in the United States had imagined that the military contest for the Northwest might expose so sharply the limits of the new republic. Yet the battle was only one episode in a larger continental history. At the same time, Comanche diplomats negotiated with Spanish New Mexico, Missouri River nations rebuilt after epidemic disease, Indigenous communities in Alta California confronted expanding missions, and Nuu-chah-nulth leaders bargained with foreign ships on the Pacific coast. These events were connected by the growth of colonial power, but their outcomes were shaped by different Indigenous governments and conditions.

Chapter 59 ended with American independence and the disputed settlements of 1783–1784. The transfer of British claims to the United States did not give American officials immediate authority over villages, rivers, hunting grounds, or councils whose peoples had never accepted that transfer. British posts and commerce remained significant in the Great Lakes, while American settlers pressed west of the Appalachians. In the Northeast and Southeast, Indigenous nations considered new treaties as possible means to defend territories and secure trade. Some chose collective resistance; others negotiated under pressure; still others used competing European powers to preserve room for maneuver. Their decisions were not made on a blank map.

The western and northern parts of the continent followed different political trajectories. Horse-centered societies of the Plains developed regional power through hunting, warfare, exchange, and alliance. The agricultural villages of the upper Missouri organized important trade centers, supplying visiting peoples and creating networks that extended across great distances. In the northern interior, Cree, Anishinaabe, Dene, and other communities made the fur trade possible through labor, food, navigation, and kinship relationships. Introduced epidemic diseases could devastate communities and unsettle long-standing networks, but survivors adapted and rebuilt. Historical change was neither uniformly military nor uniformly imposed by governments.

Farther south, Spanish administrators relied on a shifting mixture of missions, presidios, trade, gifts, and military campaigns. In New Mexico, Spanish officials needed peace with powerful Comanche groups and could not simply compel Ute and Comanche leaders to accept their priorities. Pueblo, Navajo, Apache, Hopi, Zuni, and O'odham communities faced different forms of colonial intrusion and made different political choices. Alta California presented another model: missionaries and soldiers imposed labor and religious institutions on some coastal Indigenous peoples while other communities remained beyond their daily reach. The distinction between Spanish territory on a map and Spanish power on the ground matters throughout.

On the Northwest Coast, Indigenous maritime polities controlled established trade and resource systems when Spanish, British, and American vessels arrived to pursue sea-otter pelts. Foreign captains negotiated with powerful local leaders and often misunderstood the ownership practices of the people whose shores they visited. In Alaska, Russian commercial enterprises sought furs by coercing Indigenous hunters and taking hostages; those enterprises established bases while confronting resistance from diverse Alaska Native nations. Neither story can be described adequately as the simple arrival of European civilization at a previously passive coast. Indigenous authority existed before commercial intrusion and persisted within unequal relationships afterward.

The written record is substantial but partial. American treaty texts, congressional ordinances, Spanish officials' reports, missionary registers, British traders' journals, maritime logs, and Russian company records were produced for different purposes and were rarely neutral descriptions of Indigenous intentions. Indigenous oral histories, wampum traditions, place names, archaeological evidence, and the scholarship of descendant communities provide indispensable perspectives that can challenge official narratives. This chapter identifies when a document records a legal claim, when it reports an observed event, and when its author interprets or misinterprets another community's actions. Documentary windows use clearly identified editorial paraphrases unless a passage has been collated to its source.

Two misleading conclusions must be avoided. The first is that the continent had been conquered by 1800 because European states assigned it among themselves or constructed ports and forts. The second is that Indigenous political orders were unchanged or equally secure everywhere. Northwestern nations ceded substantial territory after military defeat; some Haudenosaunee communities negotiated protection yet lost land through subsequent sales; Russian coercion devastated places in Alaska; and California's mission system altered the lives of thousands. These changes matter, but they were not the same process and did not proceed at the same pace.

The governing question is how Indigenous nations exercised and defended political independence while colonial and commercial projects grew. The chapter proceeds through five divisions: the new American republic and Northwestern Confederacy; war, diplomacy, and the Northeast; southern nations; the Plains, Southwest, and northern interior; and the Pacific and Alaska. It concludes by comparing forms of political power across the continent in 1800. The objective is not to predict the nineteenth century but to understand the real options, dangers, and achievements visible at the eighteenth century's close.

Historical Context

The decades following 1760 transformed North America's imperial environment. French military power in Canada collapsed in the Seven Years' War; Britain claimed much of the former French North American sphere; and the American Revolution then produced an independent United States. Yet Indigenous peoples had never been a single subordinate population of those empires. Confederacies, village governments, kinship networks, and regional trading systems had negotiated with Europeans for generations. Chapters 55 through 59 examined these arrangements and their transformations through diplomacy, imperial war, resistance, and revolution. Chapter 60 begins with the continental consequences of those earlier struggles.

European jurisdiction was uneven and often rested on particular institutions rather than total control. American land laws sought to convert treaty claims into surveyed property and new states. British Canada retained commercial and family connections across the Great Lakes and northern forests. Spain governed settlements and missions in New Mexico, Texas, Louisiana, and California but relied on negotiation and force in differing proportions. Russian commercial crews occupied small maritime bases and imposed coercive demands upon some Alaska Native communities. The existence of an administrative map did not determine the extent to which local inhabitants obeyed external officials.

Indigenous politics were equally diverse. Haudenosaunee clan systems and the ceremonial authority of confederal councils differed from the organization of southeastern towns, Plains village networks, or coastal maritime leadership. Nevertheless, societies across the continent maintained rules governing land, exchange, kinship, justice, movement, and diplomacy. Their leaders often relied on consensus, ceremonial speech, gift-giving, or established kinship obligations, though no single mechanism can be assumed for every society. Understanding sovereignty here means examining who possessed and exercised authority in particular historical settings rather than forcing all communities into a European constitutional model.

Economic connections made independence possible and also generated vulnerabilities. Agricultural surpluses sustained Missouri River trading centers and southeastern towns. Bison, deer, salmon, sea mammals, and beaver supplied food, clothing, and valuable commodities. Firearms, ammunition, metal tools, textiles, horses, and credit altered military and household strategies. External merchants depended on Indigenous labor and knowledge, while communities sometimes became more exposed to fluctuations in imported supplies. Territorial conflicts frequently involved access to fields, hunting grounds, ports, or river routes, not land considered merely as empty surface area.

The transmission of epidemics complicated political and economic change. Smallpox devastated some northern Plains communities during 1781–1782, prompting relocation and adjustments in alliance and trade. Infectious disease, confinement, and labor demands contributed to demographic disruption in California missions. Mortality figures and causal accounts require careful source criticism: colonial records may omit nonmission populations, duplicate categories, or apply descriptions written decades after the event. Losses were profound in many places, but they did not imply the disappearance of all political institutions or the inevitability of conquest elsewhere.

This chapter's geographical divisions are analytical tools rather than sealed compartments. Great Lakes families crossed what became an international boundary; Plains horses and trade goods reached the Southwest; northern traders depended on Indigenous routes across enormous distances; coastal pelts entered markets across the Pacific. The cross-regional comparisons that conclude the chapter ask what kinds of colonial power were operating, where Indigenous communities retained negotiating leverage, and why the consequences differed. That approach restores Indigenous North America as an interconnected political continent at the end of the eighteenth century.

Indigenous Sovereignty and the New American Republic

Competing claims, confederation, and Indigenous military power

Section 60.1

Indigenous North America after 1783: A Continent of Nations

The Anglo-American Treaty of Paris in 1783 ended a contest between two states, but it did not end Indigenous authority over the interior of North America. The treaty assigned an international boundary from the Atlantic to the Mississippi, enclosing the future Old Northwest within the territory claimed by the United States. The nations living there neither signed the treaty nor participated in negotiating it. Their towns, councils, ceremonial grounds, hunting territories, and agricultural fields did not disappear when European diplomats redrew a map. Many leaders understood the agreement as a settlement between Britain and its former colonies, not as a conveyance of land that Indigenous nations had never surrendered. This difference between imperial title and effective jurisdiction is the indispensable starting point for a history of 1800.

The postwar continent possessed many political centers rather than one frontier radiating from Philadelphia or New York. Haudenosaunee communities worked to repair the divisions of the Revolution; nations in the Ohio and Great Lakes region established confederal councils; Cherokee and Muscogee towns negotiated separately and collectively with an expanding republic. Beyond them, Comanche and other Plains powers structured access to horses, markets, and routes; Pueblo settlements and neighboring Navajo and Apache communities engaged Spanish officials on terms that continually had to be renegotiated. The Mandan, Hidatsa, and Arikara controlled important Missouri River exchanges. On the Northwest Coast, Nuu-chah-nulth, Haida, Tlingit, and Coast Salish societies were political hosts and traders, not passive subjects of visiting ships.

A useful map of the period must therefore carry several layers. One layer records the claims of European governments: Britain's Canadian provinces and retained western posts; the United States' Atlantic states and vast western pretensions; Spanish New Mexico, Texas, Louisiana, and Alta California; and scattered Russian commercial establishments in Alaska. Another layer follows Indigenous settlements, confederacies, watersheds, seasonal movements, and diplomatic connections. These layers intersect without coinciding. Forts could be enclaves surrounded by Indigenous territory. Spanish authority was stronger near a mission or presidio than across nearby mountains and deserts. Russian traders could coerce hunters on one island yet remain unable to govern powerful communities beyond their supply system. This chapter treats those variations as political facts, not as temporary anomalies awaiting colonial correction.

Sovereignty in this setting was exercised through practices: councils convened, borders defended, strangers admitted or expelled, commercial relationships supervised, grievances settled, and members supported during famine or war. Institutions differed profoundly among nations. Haudenosaunee clan mothers and councils operated within a confederal constitutional tradition; southeastern towns relied on distinct kinship and ceremonial systems; Plains bands and villages made decisions through leaders who depended on persuasion, diplomacy, and reciprocal obligations. No single European term describes all these arrangements. Yet each possessed forms of legitimate authority intelligible to its members and neighbors. A European claim to exclusive sovereignty, even when accepted by rival European powers, did not by itself replace those institutions.

There had also been transformations Indigenous peoples did not choose. Smallpox and other introduced diseases could devastate communities and make the maintenance of fields, ceremonies, and defense more difficult. The demand for furs, deerskins, and sea-otter pelts drew some hunters into costly commercial schedules. Land companies and emigrant families multiplied the pressure on places that had previously been accessible through trade or treaty. Colonial commanders sometimes equated a military defeat with the right to confiscate territory. Missionaries interpreted adoption of Christianity as evidence of consent to new discipline, while Indigenous converts could instead adapt religious practices to familiar obligations. The late eighteenth century cannot be explained as untouched independence or completed conquest; it was a period of uneven, contested reorganization.

Diplomacy remained a critical Indigenous technology of governance. Haudenosaunee leaders preserved treaty belts and invoked earlier commitments, while the Ohio confederacy argued that a land boundary required agreement among affected nations rather than signatures obtained from a few representatives. Muscogee diplomats maintained contacts with Spain as well as the United States. Comanche councils considered peace with New Mexico in relation to Ute and Apache affairs. Traders around Hudson Bay and the Great Lakes relied on kinship and negotiated access. These practices allowed communities to gather information, set conditions, delay dangerous commitments, and construct alternatives when one partner proved unreliable. Even when military forces demanded concessions, the meanings of diplomatic agreements could remain contested.

To measure Indigenous political power in 1800, students must ask not simply which flags flew at coastal ports but who could make everyday decisions over land, food, labor, movement, and exchange. The answers differ by place. Some communities faced permanent garrisons or coercive missions; some had lost lands through treaties negotiated in unequal circumstances; others could still refuse colonial access and shape trading terms. A continental view does not erase violence or hardship. It makes those events more historically precise by identifying where they occurred, which Indigenous governments responded, and how local outcomes altered regional balances without determining the fate of all North America.

Section 60.2

The United States, Western Settlement, and the Politics of Territorial Expansion

Congress inherited competing ideas about western lands from the revolutionary period. Several states claimed western territory through old charters, while land speculators anticipated enormous profits from surveys and sales. The Articles of Confederation left the national government financially weak and dependent on negotiated cessions from states. Congress nevertheless proceeded as though British recognition of American independence had transferred an extensive public domain to federal control. Indigenous governments had not participated in that transfer. The resulting contradiction shaped the Northwest Territory: American officials planned organized settlement on land that Indigenous nations continued to occupy and defend. Government maps, deeds, and land-office calculations thus expressed ambitions that could not be realized by paper alone.

Congress's first postwar treaties with Ohio Country peoples reveal the difficulties. At Fort McIntosh in January 1785, American commissioners negotiated with selected Wyandot, Lenape, Odawa, and Ojibwe representatives under the shadow of American military occupation. The treaty drew boundaries, provided for reservations, and sought to arrange a western region available to the United States. Many Native leaders later challenged the authority of the signatories or the fairness of the process. The Treaty of Fort Harmar in January 1789 attempted to confirm earlier agreements but did not secure acceptance from important Shawnee, Miami, and other communities. For those opponents, an agreement with some delegates could not transfer lands held or used by many nations together. For the United States, the documents seemed to establish title; for the confederacy, they demonstrated how colonists divided peoples to obtain signatures.

The Northwest Ordinance of July 1787 was simultaneously a framework for American territorial government and a statement of claimed principles concerning Indigenous peoples. It organized the process by which the country north of the Ohio River might become territories and eventually states. Article III pledged that the United States would observe good faith toward Indigenous inhabitants, and declared that their property and lands should not be taken without consent except in lawful wars authorized by Congress. The language appears protective until set beside survey policy, land sales, settlement pressure, and military campaigns that followed. Students should not dismiss the text as meaningless, because Indigenous diplomats and later critics could invoke its promise. Nor should they mistake the promise for a guarantee that officials and settlers honored.

Historical Vocabulary

Sovereignty, Cession, Federal Authority, and Territorial Government

Source / method: Historical vocabulary study.

An imperial claim is an assertion of legal authority that may extend well beyond a government’s effective jurisdiction. An Indigenous cession is a claimed transfer of particular land rights through negotiation; its legitimacy depends upon representation, conditions, and the meanings assigned by participants.

Federal Indian affairs describes institutional claims of the United States to regulate treaties and trade, while territorial government refers to a colonial administrative structure imposed on land outside the original states. These terms explain official ambitions; they should not replace the history of the nations already living there.

The Constitution, effective under the new federal government in 1789, strengthened the national capacity to raise revenue, regulate commerce with Indian nations, make treaties, and maintain military forces. Yet it did not produce immediate consistency. Frontier settlers demanded land and security, state governments pressed their own claims, and federal officers tried to turn disputed boundaries into enforceable rules. The first federal Trade and Intercourse Act of 1790 attempted to regulate transactions involving Indigenous lands, reserving a central role for federally authorized treaty processes. This was a significant change from the often conflicting practices of individual colonies and states. Its practical enforcement, however, depended on resources, officials, and the willingness of settlers to obey.

Land surveys translated contested political geography into marketable parcels. The Land Ordinance of 1785 prescribed a rectangular survey system for federal lands, a method that made territory legible to the government and attractive to investors. Lines drawn across an Ohio forest or prairie did not reflect the boundaries of Indigenous villages, hunting ranges, portages, or sacred places. The surveyor's chain and the Indigenous council therefore represented different conceptions of legitimate territorial authority. Conflict became especially likely when settlers treated surveyed plots as private property before the surrounding nations accepted any cession. Colonial ideas about improvement and occupancy could dismiss seasonal land use, while Indigenous economies relied on movement between farms, fishing locations, sugar camps, and hunting grounds.

The federal government was not a unified actor free to implement whatever policy it preferred. Officials in the War Department sometimes sought agreements that would reduce the cost of conflict; land companies urged rapid settlement; legislators demanded retaliation after attacks on American households; and agents claimed that annuities or government trading systems could stabilize relations. The interests of Indigenous signatories were equally varied. Some hoped to protect remaining towns by drawing a defensible line, others wanted to delay surveyors while preparing collective resistance, and still others disagreed about which nation had authority to negotiate. Documents must be read with this fractured political setting in mind rather than as the voice of a single coherent American or Indigenous will.

The larger lesson is that territorial organization in the early republic was not simply an administrative prelude to statehood. It was a struggle between incompatible claims over ownership, jurisdiction, and the meaning of consent. The Northwest Ordinance's humane language and the military campaigns of the 1790s belonged to the same political order. Their juxtaposition explains both why Indigenous leaders took federal promises seriously enough to negotiate and why many refused to accept that negotiation without collective authority could secure lasting peace. American institutions expanded, but Indigenous resistance proved that institutional ambition was not the same as uncontested government.

Section 60.3

The Northwestern Confederacy and the Defense of the Ohio Country

The Northwestern Confederacy emerged from older networks of Great Lakes and Ohio diplomacy, sharpened by the cessions and violence of the American Revolution. Shawnee, Miami, Lenape, Wyandot, Odawa, Potawatomi, Ojibwe, and other nations shared grievances about unauthorized land sales and settlers crossing the Ohio River. Their political systems were not identical, and no central executive could dictate policy to all their towns. Nevertheless, councils increasingly articulated a common position: lands affecting several nations could not rightfully be conveyed by a delegation that excluded them. The collective-consent principle challenged the American practice of identifying a few available signatories and treating their agreement as sufficient for a continent-sized cession.

This principle arose from practical realities of land use. Different peoples farmed near river towns, hunted in overlapping territories, visited relatives across political boundaries, and shared routes to trading places. A line might pass through places used seasonally by several nations. American officials often preferred exclusive ownership by one named group because it made a sale easier to arrange. Indigenous diplomats could instead point to historical occupancy, kinship, alliance, and common defense. That argument did not mean all nations possessed an identical or undisputed title to every acre. It meant that disagreements should be resolved among those affected rather than exploited by an outside purchaser. In this sense, confederation was a response to both American expansion and conflicts within Indigenous diplomacy itself.

Meetings around the Detroit River in 1786 and afterward helped establish a forum for discussing a shared boundary. British merchants and officials at Detroit remained important suppliers of manufactured goods and intelligence, even after Britain acknowledged the United States in 1783. Yet the confederacy was not merely a British creation. Indigenous delegates made their own calculations, including the possibility that Britain would offer sympathy without entering another expensive war. They sought a stable Ohio River boundary that would distinguish settler lands to the south and east from Indigenous territory to the north and west. The river was politically meaningful because it had been a focus of earlier frontier conflict and offered a clear line that could be defended and monitored.

Historical Debate

Negotiations might secure a boundary, but a premature agreement signed by a smaller group could undermine the collective principle. The political culture of consultation and reciprocity meant that any durable agreement needed more than a formal ceremony in a distant fort. For American leaders, the confederacy created a problem larger than individual raids. It challenged the premise that sovereignty followed automatically from the Treaty of Paris and demanded that the new republic recognize an Indigenous political community able to bargain collectively. Some officials viewed that claim as obstruction encouraged by British agents; others acknowledged that peace would be difficult without broadly representative negotiations. The subsequent war tested not only military strength but also whether a treaty could legitimately divide peoples whose lands and livelihoods were connected. By the early 1790s, no American statement of title could erase this dispute.

Section 60.4

Harmar’s Defeat and St. Clair’s Defeat, 1790–1791

The first major federal offensives against the northwestern nations revealed the difficulty of translating settler demands into military control. In 1790 an army led by General Josiah Harmar advanced toward Kekionga, the Miami center at the Maumee headwaters. Its mission combined punishment for attacks on frontier settlements with destruction of Indigenous towns, crops, and stores. The expedition was hampered by uneven training, uncertain intelligence, disputes between regulars and militia, and terrain unfamiliar to many soldiers. Indigenous defenders used their knowledge of waterways, approaches, and forest cover to isolate American detachments. Harmar's soldiers burned abandoned structures, but their ability to occupy or hold the region was limited. The campaign showed that destruction of houses was not equivalent to defeating the political coalition that lived there.

American reports often described the 1790 actions as a sequence of engagements rather than a single battle. Separate detachments met well-directed attacks and suffered heavy losses, and the army withdrew without securing the object of its march. Indigenous success was not the result of effortless superiority. Warriors had to gather from dispersed towns, coordinate approaches, and preserve supplies while protecting families. Their military advantage lay partly in mobility and partly in the Americans' dependence on long overland communications. The defeat also carried a diplomatic message: the treaty boundaries claimed by American officials could not simply be enforced by a marching column. Communities that had doubted federal strength gained evidence that further resistance might be viable.

In 1791 the Washington administration assembled a larger force under Governor and Major General Arthur St. Clair. The expedition intended to establish a stronger American presence and punish Indigenous resistance. Its troops included regulars and short-term levies, many inadequately trained or supplied. Progress was slow, and desertion, illness, weak transport, and delays reduced the army's operational effectiveness. On November 4, near the upper Wabash in present-day western Ohio, an Indigenous coalition struck the encampment before the Americans could organize a coherent defense. Warriors attacked from multiple directions, taking advantage of cover and the confusion of troops drawn into exposed positions. Repeated American counterattacks failed to break the encirclement decisively.

St. Clair's force suffered catastrophic casualties and retreated in disorder. Contemporary enumerations differ over how many were killed and wounded, especially when camp followers and different categories of service are included, so numerical precision should be source-specific. The central conclusion is not disputed: the engagement was among the most devastating defeats in the history of the United States Army. Contemporary American observers recognized that Indigenous fighters had coordinated effectively across national lines. Little Turtle, Blue Jacket, Buckongahelas, and other leaders contributed to the coalition, though the surviving record does not support the simplistic notion that one man commanded every participant. The victory demonstrated skill in reconnaissance, timing, deployment, and the maintenance of a coalition in battle.

Military success had costs for Indigenous society. Campaigns required warriors to leave farms and families, and ammunition, food, and weapons had to be supplied by communities or traders. The danger of American retaliation remained. Victories could strengthen a coalition but also raise disagreements about whether to continue fighting, demand a negotiated Ohio boundary, or seek advantage from British aid. It would be a mistake to infer from the scale of St. Clair's defeat that all participating nations had identical long-term objectives. Some wanted a permanent barrier to settlement, others sought security for particular towns or trade routes, and some hoped to recover leverage lost in earlier treaties. The American consequences were substantial. Congress investigated the disaster; federal leaders reconsidered recruitment, discipline, equipment, and the organization of forces stationed in the West. The crisis helped justify a more systematic standing army, which would become the Legion of the United States under Anthony Wayne. Meanwhile, Indigenous defenders had demonstrated that the Northwest Territory existed as a governmental project on paper more securely than on the ground. Their victories were real but not permanent guarantees. American political and fiscal resources could support repeated attempts, and as the war continued both sides faced choices about whether battlefield success could be converted into a stable peace.

In assessing these campaigns, students should compare military evidence with territorial outcomes. Harmar and St. Clair failed to impose American control, but the communities defending the Maumee and Wabash still faced continuing settlement pressure and a state capable of raising another army. The campaigns reveal how Indigenous military coalitions could mobilize formidable power without being identical to European armies. They also demonstrate why the fate of the Northwest cannot be written as a simple procession of American victories. For several years after independence, Indigenous nations dictated the pace and costs of territorial expansion, and the United States had to reorganize before attempting another sustained offensive.

War, Treaty Diplomacy, and the Reorganization of the Northeast

Fallen Timbers, Greenville, Canandaigua, and the northern borderlands

Section 60.5

Anthony Wayne, the Legion of the United States, and Fallen Timbers, 1792–1794

St. Clair's defeat obliged the United States to reconsider an assumption that frontier militia could secure the Northwest with occasional punitive marches. President Washington's administration supported the creation of a more disciplined force, the Legion of the United States, under Anthony Wayne. Training at Legionville in Pennsylvania emphasized regular drill, coordinated infantry and cavalry, secure camps, and reliable supply. Wayne's soldiers were also taught to expect Indigenous forces to fight skillfully from wooded terrain rather than to dismiss them as a disorganized obstacle. The investment was costly for a young republic, but its leaders had concluded that territorial ambitions could not be sustained by decrees and land sales alone. Military reform was an important institutional consequence of Indigenous victories in 1790 and 1791.

Peace remained an alternative to renewed war, though the parties disagreed about its essential terms. During negotiations in 1792 and 1793, confederacy representatives repeatedly emphasized the Ohio River as a defensible boundary. American commissioners might discuss annuities or compensation, but they were unwilling to abandon settlements already north of that river or concede the collective principle in the form demanded. British agents and traders near Detroit continued to supply information and goods, which Indigenous diplomats considered when weighing their options. The British government, however, sought to avoid direct war with the United States. Confederacy leaders therefore faced uncertain support from a power that had encouraged resistance without promising to fight alongside them. The failure of diplomacy was rooted in incompatible territorial expectations, not simply personal obstinacy.

Wayne advanced by establishing a chain of fortified positions and carefully protecting communications. His method contrasted with the exposed movements of earlier campaigns. Fort Recovery, built near the site of St. Clair's defeat, was attacked in 1794 but remained in American hands. The campaign then moved toward the Maumee, a critical river corridor connecting Indigenous towns to British Detroit. For those communities, the advancing forts signified something more durable than a temporary raid. A fort could shelter troops, direct provisions, support future surveyors, and enable settlers to occupy territory formerly used by multiple nations. Wayne's policy of destroying food and settlements was likewise political as well as tactical: it aimed to remove the means by which warriors and their families could continue resistance.

The aftermath was harsh. Wayne's army destroyed homes, stored supplies, and crops in the surrounding region, reducing the capacity of communities to shelter fighters and feed displaced families. Indigenous councils now had to consider not only whether they could win another engagement but also the risk of hunger and dispersal during winter. Some leaders favored negotiation; others remained skeptical that an American boundary would be honored after earlier failures. Its diplomacy had articulated a coherent objection to piecemeal land sales. The reversal resulted from changed military conditions, vulnerable food systems, uncertain British aid, and disagreements intrinsic to a voluntary coalition. The ensuing treaty process should therefore be examined as negotiation under mounting coercive pressure, rather than as an uncomplicated recognition that American title had always been legitimate. Indigenous political actors remained present at the table and continued to shape the agreement's terms, even when their options were narrowed.

Section 60.6

The Treaty of Greenville, 1795: Peace, Territory, and Competing Sovereignties

At Greenville in the summer of 1795, Anthony Wayne met representatives of twelve named Indigenous parties: Wyandot, Lenape, Shawnee, Odawa, Ojibwe, Potawatomi, Miami, Eel River, Wea, Kickapoo, Piankeshaw, and Kaskaskia communities. The agreement concluded on August 3. Its significance came not merely from the area relinquished but from its attempt to define relations after years of warfare. Indigenous delegates met the United States as political representatives whose assent was sought, even as the American army's victories and destruction of settlements constrained their choices. The treaty's opening article declared an end to hostilities; subsequent articles addressed captives, territorial boundaries, economic provisions, travel, and future disputes. Reading only a modern map of ceded lands would obscure much of this diplomatic structure.

Article II required restoration of prisoners on both sides and provided for ten Indigenous chiefs to remain as hostages until American captives were delivered. The asymmetry is striking: it was a mechanism of enforcement backed by an army occupying the treaty site. Captivity could involve military detention, household adoption, kin relationships, and disputed personal wishes; its resolution was consequently more complicated than exchanging uniformed soldiers. Indigenous and American parties might disagree about who counted as a captive or whether an adopted individual wished to leave. The requirement illustrated that peace-making after frontier war had direct consequences for families as well as governments. It also reveals why treaty language should be read for obligations imposed unequally on the parties.

Article III drew a detailed boundary across what are now Ohio and Indiana and reserved specified tracts around forts, portages, and other strategic places for American use. These locations mattered beyond their acreage. A portage linked waterways and commercial routes; a fort could be a platform for future military or surveying activity; a river crossing enabled settlement movement. The United States obtained much of present-day Ohio while retaining strategic parcels outside the main line. In return, the treaty recognized land remaining to Indigenous nations beyond the boundary. Some communities interpreted this as a guarantee that the United States would stay on its side, a familiar form of territorial coexistence. For American officials, the cession also helped validate a system of land surveys and expanding jurisdiction.

Section 60.7

The Haudenosaunee after the Revolution and the Treaty of Canandaigua, 1794

The Haudenosaunee entered the 1790s with a history of war and displacement that could not be erased by American independence. The Revolution had divided nations and communities across the British-American boundary. The 1779 Sullivan–Clinton campaign destroyed towns and food systems; some people fled toward Niagara, while others sought to rebuild within New York. Families maintained relationships across the new border, and leaders attempted to revive the diplomatic practices that had sustained the Confederacy. The 1784 Treaty of Fort Stanwix, negotiated under unequal conditions, was contested by many Haudenosaunee participants. State-backed purchases in western and central New York created additional insecurity. The Haudenosaunee were not simply a defeated alliance of British auxiliaries; they were a confederacy negotiating the survival of homelands and governments in a transformed political environment.

Different nations faced different pressures. Oneida and Tuscarora communities that had often aided the Americans expected recognition of their wartime support, yet encountered land loss and encroachment. Seneca leaders faced demands from speculators and purchasers seeking access to fertile lands and strategic routes. Onondaga and Cayuga communities negotiated with New York over reservations and sales. Mohawk and other households who had settled at Grand River in British Canada retained connections to relatives within the United States. The political problem was therefore multilayered: federal and state authorities competed for influence, Indigenous representatives disagreed about acceptable concessions, and communities on either side of the border still sought to maintain ceremonial and kinship relationships.

In November 1794, federal commissioner Timothy Pickering met Six Nations representatives at Canandaigua. The treaty of November 11 established peace and reaffirmed specific territorial provisions. Article II acknowledged reservations of the Oneida, Onondaga, and Cayuga; Article III described lands belonging to the Seneca and pledged that the United States would not claim or disturb them. The terms included an American right of purchase if the nations chose to sell, a provision whose implications depended on how choice, pressure, and authority operated in later transactions. Article VI provided annual goods, and provisions concerning crimes and retaliation sought to stabilize relations. The text does not justify the claim that the United States granted sovereignty to Indigenous nations. Rather, American officials formally acknowledged land rights and political relationships that Haudenosaunee communities asserted already existed.

Canandaigua invites comparison with Greenville in 1795. Both were federal agreements made with Indigenous representatives; both formalized boundaries and diplomatic obligations; both contained provisions intended to reduce violence. Yet their immediate political settings differed. Greenville followed a large-scale military defeat of the Northwestern Confederacy and opened extensive territory to settlement. Canandaigua was part of an effort to stabilize relations with Haudenosaunee nations whose territories and state-level treaties presented different jurisdictional questions. Treating them as interchangeable examples of 'the Indian treaties' would flatten these differences. Section 60.8

British Canada, the Great Lakes Borderlands, and the Persistence of Indigenous Diplomacy

The new boundary between British Canada and the United States cut across Indigenous political worlds that long predated either state. Haudenosaunee relatives lived in communities on both sides, while Anishinaabe, Wyandot, and other Great Lakes peoples maintained trade routes across rivers and lakes. British officials retained western garrisons after the 1783 peace settlement, including posts at Detroit and Michilimackinac, arguing over unresolved wartime obligations and American treaty compliance. These forts allowed British merchants to maintain exchange networks and offered some Indigenous leaders possible diplomatic support. They also became sources of misunderstanding: British sympathy for Indigenous resistance did not necessarily mean that London would fight another war to preserve an Ohio River boundary.

The Grand River settlement, associated with Joseph Brant and the Haldimand Proclamation of October 1784, provided a place for many British-allied Haudenosaunee refugees and other supporters of the Crown. British officers framed the arrangement as compensation for wartime losses; Indigenous residents had their own expectations about the nature and extent of their territory. The proclamation described lands along both sides of the Grand River, creating claims and disputes that continued beyond the eighteenth century. Settling there did not dissolve relationships with New York homelands or transform Haudenosaunee identity into British citizenship. People visited relatives, retained political ceremonies, and negotiated with colonial administrators from a position they understood as Indigenous nationhood.

The Jay Treaty signed by Britain and the United States in November 1794 sought to resolve several outstanding conflicts. It provided for the withdrawal of British forces from occupied western posts, accomplished in 1796, and established rules for trade and boundary relations. Article III explicitly addressed Indigenous people living on either side of the border, permitting travel by land and inland navigation and commercial intercourse under specified conditions. The text contains exceptions, including Hudson's Bay Company territory, and restrictions concerning maritime access and customs. It should be read as a treaty provision with a particular eighteenth-century legal setting, not as an unrestricted description of modern border law. Indigenous communities were not parties to the treaty, even though its provisions concerned their mobility.

Southeastern Nations, Diplomacy, and Territorial Survival

Cherokee, Muscogee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Catawba, and everyday life

Section 60.9

Cherokee Sovereignty, Resistance, and the Treaties of the 1780s–1790s

Cherokee nations and towns emerged from the Revolution having experienced destructive expeditions, contested land transfers, and divisions over how to answer expanding settlements. Cherokee country included Lower, Middle, Valley, and Overhill communities linked by language, kinship, and ceremonial practice but not directed by one central executive. Settlements in the Watauga, Holston, and Tennessee regions continued to expand despite treaties and warnings. Some Cherokee leaders emphasized peace to protect remaining towns and trading relationships; others argued that repeated concessions invited further encroachment. Dragging Canoe led a network of resistant towns commonly identified in colonial sources as 'Chickamauga,' a geographic and political label rather than the name of a separate nation. The chapter must recognize these varying strategies as Cherokee political choices rather than assign the entire nation one wartime position.

The Treaty of Hopewell, concluded November 28, 1785, expressed the new United States' claim to establish peace and protection with Cherokee representatives. It dealt with prisoner restoration, boundaries, travel, and the treatment of offenses. The American language of protection implied a hierarchical relationship, while Cherokee delegates could view recognition of a territorial boundary as a guarantee against settlers. The agreement's effectiveness was limited by continued migration onto lands claimed by Cherokee communities and weak enforcement by distant authorities. A treaty made by national commissioners could not, by itself, compel every frontier settler or land company to abandon an advantageous claim. Nor did all Cherokee towns necessarily regard the same delegation as authorized to surrender their interests without further council deliberation.

The federal government attempted another settlement in the Treaty of Holston in July 1791. It defined new boundaries, offered annuities, addressed trade and criminal jurisdiction, and promised a form of federal protection. Its provisions were more detailed than the familiar textbook description of a simple land cession. For some leaders a recognized line and continuing goods offered tools of survival; for others, further cessions confirmed that earlier assurances had been unreliable. Settlers continued to press across agreed boundaries, leaving Cherokee diplomats to decide whether to complain through federal agents, accept further negotiations, or support armed resistance. To evaluate the treaties one must read their words alongside the behavior of state and local authorities who had material reasons to disregard them.

The economic geography behind those decisions was substantial. Cherokee households depended on corn agriculture, gardens, hunting, fishing, and commercial exchange. Women exercised important authority in farming, kinship, and social reproduction, while town councils and ceremonial institutions provided structures for collective decision-making. Roads, rivers, and passes connecting Overhill and Valley towns to American settlements also made them vulnerable to surveillance and attack. The Cherokee experience also reveals the limits of the idea that all Indigenous change was imposed. American officials later used claims about 'civilization' and agricultural improvement to justify interventions into Indigenous gender roles and property practices, but Cherokee women were already accomplished agricultural producers and communities possessed their own standards of prosperity. The late eighteenth century should therefore be studied as an arena of selective adaptation and coercion, with the distinction established by evidence rather than by assumption. Section 60.10

Muscogee Diplomacy, Alexander McGillivray, and the Treaty of New York, 1790

The Muscogee world of the late eighteenth century was organized around politically significant towns rather than a uniform state commanded from one capital. Upper Creek settlements lay principally along the Coosa and Tallapoosa rivers, while Lower Creek towns were concentrated toward the Chattahoochee and Flint systems. Their relationships with neighboring Cherokee, Choctaw, and other communities varied, as did their economic ties to Spanish markets, British merchants, and Georgia settlements. Ceremonial grounds, kinship links, military reputations, and town councils shaped authority. A respected speaker might influence a large diplomatic gathering yet still be unable to bind a town that had not agreed to its decisions. That political structure is essential for interpreting the career of Alexander McGillivray and the meaning of the 1790 treaty.

McGillivray, a prominent Muscogee leader with Scottish family connections, cultivated an ability to communicate with Spanish and American officials while seeking to protect Creek territory against Georgia's expansive claims. His position depended not only on his personal education and commercial contacts but also on support from other town leaders. British retreat after the Revolution weakened one diplomatic alternative, but Spanish officials in East and West Florida and Louisiana could still supply trade goods and recognize Creek leaders. McGillivray sought to use this rivalry to obtain leverage. His strategic choices did not represent the preferences of every Muscogee town, some of which continued their own negotiations, trade, or conflict with neighboring Americans. His authority should neither be dismissed nor inflated into that of a monarch.

Georgia leaders argued that earlier land cessions, notably those negotiated in the 1770s, opened the Oconee region to settlement. Muscogee representatives disputed the legitimacy and scope of many claimed transfers. Settlers crossed boundaries and built farms; Creek war parties sometimes attacked exposed settlements; militia retaliation increased the danger to villages. The national government's wish to avert a wider southern war complicated the relationship between Washington's administration and Georgia. Federal leaders wanted to demonstrate that treaty-making and Indian affairs could be managed under national authority, yet they could not easily enforce an agreement opposed by a state and local speculators. Creek diplomats recognized these divisions and negotiated accordingly.

Section 60.11

Choctaw, Chickasaw, Catawba, and the Southern Balance of Power

Choctaw, Chickasaw, and Catawba communities occupied different parts of the southeastern political landscape, and their histories should not be collapsed into one category of 'southern tribes.' Choctaw towns extended across broad areas of the present-day Mississippi–Alabama region, with networks of kinship, ceremonial life, agriculture, and exchange. Chickasaw communities occupied strategic routes linking the Tennessee and Mississippi valleys and controlled important access points to river travel. The Catawba lived nearer established Atlantic colonial settlements and had experienced much earlier population loss and encroachment. Each nation negotiated from a different position and with different priorities, even while confronting some of the same colonial governments and commercial pressures.

The Choctaw faced the competing influence of Spanish authorities in Louisiana and West Florida, British merchants who retained trading relationships after the Revolution, and growing American ambitions. Their political leaders weighed the availability of guns, ammunition, textiles, and credit against the risk of alienating one supplier by favoring another. Towns did not always have identical alliances, and an official's claim to speak for the Choctaw as a whole requires careful evaluation. Maintaining productive fields and hunting territories mattered as much as obtaining commercial presents. Leaders could use delays, multiple councils, or diplomacy with neighboring nations to preserve room for maneuver. Territorial pressure increased as settlements advanced toward river corridors that had long been important for Indigenous movement and exchange.

The Chickasaw were especially influential in the central Mississippi and Tennessee valley borderlands. Their strategic locations created opportunities to charge, guide, or restrict passage and to profit from connections with traders operating from multiple directions. Some leaders favored closer relations with the United States in particular circumstances, but it would be inaccurate to describe the nation as consistently or unanimously 'pro-American.' Commercial ties to older British networks survived political change, while Spanish officials attempted to cultivate Indigenous alliances along the Mississippi. The Chickasaw also managed relations with neighboring Choctaw, Cherokee, and other communities that could prove more immediate than distant disputes among European states. Internal decisions were shaped by town politics and families rather than fixed geopolitical labels.

Section 60.12

Southeastern Indigenous Societies amid Political and Economic Change

The southeastern Indigenous world around 1800 was sustained by institutions and practices that were not confined to diplomacy with colonists. Town councils managed relations among families and neighbors; ceremonial calendars coordinated public life; and agriculture, hunting, and fishing produced the resources that made political independence possible. In many Cherokee and Muscogee communities, matrilineal kinship systems helped determine descent, household membership, and rights to fields. Women controlled or directed important parts of agricultural production and social reproduction, while men often undertook hunting, diplomacy, and warfare. These descriptions are regional patterns rather than inflexible rules. Particular communities differed, and historical change created new roles and disputes. A chapter limited to treaty signers would miss the social foundation of governmental authority.

Corn, beans, squash, and other crops remained central to food systems. Women maintained fields, chose seed, stored harvests, and organized labor in ways linked to clan and town responsibilities. Men hunted deer and other game, using proceeds from deerskin exchange to obtain metal tools, cloth, firearms, and other manufactured goods. River valleys and forested uplands formed integrated economic landscapes; they were not empty acreage that could be converted to private farms without social loss. When settlers crossed treaty boundaries, they threatened access to water, fuel, hunting grounds, and places where relatives were buried or ceremonies conducted. The politics of land transfer therefore reached deeply into the tasks by which households survived each season.

Trade was important without determining every cultural change. Deerskin markets connected Indigenous hunters to British, Spanish, and American commercial houses, and fluctuations in prices or credit could alter a family's options. Traders sometimes became intermediaries in political disputes because they controlled news and supply channels. Credit could deepen dependence and generate conflicts over repayment, yet Indigenous customers compared suppliers and shifted alliances when possible. Communities also exchanged goods and knowledge with other Indigenous nations. Horse ownership, cattle raising, and new tools were not necessarily signs of surrender to a European economy; they could be adopted for reasons consistent with Indigenous goals. The meaning of adaptation depends on who made the decision, under what pressures, and to whose benefit.

Federal officials began to promote programs often described in the period as 'civilization,' urging Indigenous men to adopt plow farming and women to perform textile tasks modeled on Anglo-American households. Such proposals reflected colonial assumptions about gender and private property, not evidence that Indigenous societies lacked agriculture or industry. Cherokee and Muscogee women were already experienced agricultural producers. Captivity and slavery formed another area of change. Indigenous societies had long practiced forms of captive taking, adoption, or incorporation that varied by place and era. The two systems should not be conflated, and the late-eighteenth-century scale of African American enslavement within Indigenous communities should not be projected backward from the nineteenth century. Religious life remained equally dynamic. Ceremonial grounds, medicine traditions, seasonal festivals, and relationships to ancestral places could coexist with aspects of Christianity adopted or reinterpreted by particular individuals and communities. Epidemics, warfare, and displacement disrupted some practices while stimulating renewal in others. Women, elders, and knowledge keepers transmitted language and social memory through household work and communal gatherings. For many communities, political continuity depended upon these cultural processes as much as upon the decisions of well-known diplomats. The chapter's southeastern synthesis should therefore measure survival not simply by acres retained but by the ability to educate children, distribute food, govern kin relations, and conduct public ceremony.

By 1800 southeastern societies were changing through many overlapping causes: voluntary adaptation, commercial pressures, federal intervention, settler invasion, inter-Indigenous politics, and internal debate. Their future was not fixed by the mere presence of American institutions. Some towns retained substantial territorial space and diplomatic leverage; others lived nearer settlers and faced more immediate disruption. The relevant historical distinction is not between a 'traditional' past and an inevitably Americanized future. It is between decisions grounded in Indigenous authority and coercive arrangements that attempted to transfer control over land, labor, and family life to colonial states. That distinction gives social history a central place in the continental argument.

Section 60.13

Comanche Power and the Spanish–Indigenous Peace of 1785–1786

The Comanche dominated important stretches of the southern Plains during the late eighteenth century, but their power rested on negotiations as well as mounted warfare. Comanche bands and divisions managed access to horse herds, bison ranges, trading grounds, and raiding routes across country that Spanish officials could not easily occupy. Their military mobility enabled them to challenge settlements and rival communities; their commercial networks linked Plains products to New Mexican markets. Spanish governors, meanwhile, wanted to reduce raids on vulnerable settlements and secure alliances with Indigenous partners against other enemies. No single official could simply declare the Plains pacified. Both sides had incentives to negotiate, though the advantages and obligations of peace differed by community.

The New Mexican negotiations of 1785–1786 grew from discussions among western Comanche groups as well as Spanish initiatives. At an Arkansas River gathering in 1785, Comanche leaders debated the desirability of an agreement. Ecueracapa, associated with the Kotsoteka, emerged as a key representative of the peace faction. Delegations visited Santa Fe and considered the treatment of traders, gifts, and access to Taos and Pecos fairs. Governor Juan Bautista de Anza sought an end to violence and a more predictable frontier; Comanche negotiators demanded conditions compatible with their political dignity and economic needs. A ceremonial settlement had to be acceptable within Comanche society, not simply written into a Spanish file. The comparatively durable New Mexico peace developed through a process of councils, reciprocal expectations, and local enforcement rather than immediate colonial subjection.

The Spanish–Comanche relationship must be distinguished by region. Arrangements in Texas in 1785 were not identical to those negotiated by western Comanche leaders with New Mexico in 1786, and conflicts did not end simultaneously everywhere on the southern Plains. The New Mexico settlement incorporated trade access, exchange of gifts, and promises of restraint. A Spanish desire to enlist Comanche fighters against Apache opponents was also part of the documented negotiation. That provision should not be generalized to every Comanche band or treated as if Spanish officials designed the entire policy unilaterally. For Comanche leaders, competition over hunting and trade with Apache communities had an independent history, and diplomacy could alter a regional balance in ways useful to them.

Section 60.14

Pueblo, Navajo, Apache, Ute, and O’odham Worlds in the Spanish Borderlands

Northern New Spain was not one coherent frontier. The Pueblo villages of the Rio Grande, the Hopi mesas, Zuni country, Navajo homelands, Apache regions, Ute ranges, and the O'odham communities of the Sonoran borderlands possessed different histories and political institutions. Spanish officials governed through missions, presidios, local settlers, trade, and negotiated alliances, but those institutions were unevenly distributed. A village a short distance from Santa Fe might contend with religious supervision and labor demands, while mobile groups beyond the settled valleys could negotiate or resist from a position of considerable freedom. To reconstruct the political order around 1800, students must locate communities and travel corridors rather than assume that Spanish jurisdiction in law meant constant Spanish oversight on the ground.

The Pueblo world itself was diverse. Communities along the Rio Grande maintained languages, ceremonial institutions, irrigation agriculture, and village government through long periods of colonial pressure. Some communities had closer day-to-day contact with Spanish priests and settlers than others, and the memory of the seventeenth-century Pueblo Revolt remained part of the history through which colonial authority was interpreted. Zuni and Hopi communities pursued strategies shaped by their own geography and relations with neighboring peoples. The Spanish category 'Pueblo' described a range of societies, not one nation. A historian should distinguish particular communities and avoid implying that shared agriculture or architecture meant identical political institutions or attitudes toward missionaries.

Navajo communities developed flexible economic strategies combining farming, livestock, hunting, textile production, trade, and raiding as conditions permitted. Relationships with Spanish settlements and Pueblo villages included conflict, exchange, intermarriage, and diplomacy. The use of the broad colonial label 'Apache' is especially problematic: distinct Apache groups occupied different territories and did not follow one central policy. Some negotiated peace at Spanish establishments or entered changing trade relationships; others resisted military expeditions or sought refuge in areas beyond easy colonial reach. Spanish presidial campaigns could inflict serious damage, but they could not produce an enduring, uniform peace through force alone. Treaties, rations, and market access sometimes served as instruments of control, while Indigenous participants used them for security or advantage.

Section 60.15

The Indigenous Great Plains: Horses, Bison, Trade, and Political Competition

The phrase 'Plains Indians' conceals a broad range of societies whose politics and economies differed by river system, ecological setting, and history. The rise of mounted bison hunting transformed many communities during the eighteenth century, but villages along the Missouri and elsewhere had established agricultural and commercial systems long before horses were widely used. Lakota, Osage, Pawnee, Crow, Cheyenne, Arapaho, and other peoples adjusted to horses, firearms, and changing alliances in different ways. Some organized highly mobile hunting societies; others sustained permanent or seasonal farming settlements; many combined mobility with reliable village centers. The late eighteenth-century Plains were not merely a stage for the later wars of American expansion. They had their own inter-Indigenous geopolitics.

Horses increased the speed and range of travel, the scale of bison hunting, and the ability to move households or launch raiding expeditions. They also created new inequalities in wealth, new demands for pasture and water, and conflicts over herds. The horse did not automatically create the same social institutions everywhere. Groups with access to suitable grasslands and trade routes could accumulate large herds, while others integrated horses selectively into existing agricultural and ceremonial life. Firearms and ammunition provided other military advantages but often arrived through commercial networks that could be interrupted. Regional power depended on balancing these resources, not on one technological 'revolution' alone.

Among the Lakota and other western-moving Siouan-speaking communities, changes in hunting areas and alliances influenced expanding influence on the northern Plains. Osage power in the southern and central Plains depended on strategic locations, diplomacy, and capacity to regulate access to regional markets and hunting places. Pawnee towns along the Platte and Loup rivers combined agriculture with bison hunts and possessed political institutions that cannot be represented simply as bands of mounted warriors. Rivalries and accommodation among Plains peoples shifted as new groups moved, epidemics changed populations, and merchants altered prices or supplied guns. Indigenous wars had local causes and histories that predated direct American military presence in much of the region.

Commerce required political diplomacy. Visiting groups needed safe passage, understandings about encampment, rules for bargaining, and ways to settle disputes. Disease could abruptly alter these balances. A destructive smallpox epidemic in 1781–1782 devastated Mandan and neighboring communities, contributing to village abandonment and regrouping. Yet survivors reorganized communities and continued cultivation and exchange. A collapse in one center might benefit rivals or encourage new alliances, while affecting the availability of food for travelers. Plains history at 1800 was therefore the product of both Indigenous agency and sudden demographic shocks, neither of which can be reduced to a simple story of the rise of horse culture.

By the close of the century, there was no single dominant Plains political system. The relative weight of horses, farming, villages, trade, and war varied across regions. Indigenous nations remained major territorial powers, and most Plains societies were not governed by the United States or another colonial state. Recognizing this diversity is essential to the continental synthesis: a military treaty in Ohio tells us little about how Mandan farmers or Comanche diplomats understood their rights. Each society possessed institutions suited to particular historical challenges, and the transformation of those institutions was ongoing rather than foreordained.

Section 60.16

Epidemics, Fur Trade Networks, and the Northern Interior

The great northern interior was connected by waterways, portages, winter roads, trading posts, and kinship relationships long before it became the object of competing British commercial monopolies. Cree, Anishinaabe, Dene, and other peoples hunted, fished, gathered, traded, and governed their territories under distinct social systems. European traders relied on their geographic knowledge, food, canoes, guidance, and diplomatic support. A fur-trading company could claim exclusive rights on a charter map while remaining dependent on Indigenous providers for the commodities that made its business possible. To understand the political economy of the 1790s, students should trace who controlled transport, credit, provisions, and the timing of seasonal exchange rather than treat company records as a complete picture of Indigenous life.

The Hudson's Bay Company, chartered in 1670, built its early system around trading stations accessible from the bay. Competing Montreal merchants organized partnerships that developed into the North West Company during the period from 1779 to 1784. Both sought furs, but their routes, methods, and relationships with Indigenous traders differed. North West Company voyageurs and wintering partners moved extensively through interior canoe networks, while the Hudson's Bay Company increasingly adjusted its own inland presence. The resulting competition sometimes improved the bargaining position of Indigenous suppliers, who could shift trade between partners. It also intensified demand for furs and provisions and could deepen dependence on imported ammunition, cloth, or metal goods when older sources became less available.

Indigenous women were essential to these exchanges. Marriages and relationships between Native women and traders created households and networks linking different languages, economies, and political communities. Women prepared food, produced clothing and equipment, interpreted languages, supported travel, and helped sustain local trade. The historical emergence of Métis communities belongs within this context, but not every family of mixed ancestry can be assigned one uniform identity or political experience. Community formation took different paths in different places. The experiences of women and children deserve attention as evidence of economic and social agency, while recognizing that power within relationships could be unequal and that company archives often recorded these lives only indirectly.

Northern Cree and Dene networks also adjusted to changing markets and exposure to disease. Later observers sometimes described abandoned places as evidence of a timeless wilderness without recognizing the populations who had lived and worked there. Archaeology, Indigenous community histories, and careful readings of trader journals correct that error by showing histories of settlement, loss, and renewal.

Fur commerce had environmental consequences as well. Intensive trapping could reduce local beaver populations and force hunters to move farther, while dependence on European credit or ammunition created vulnerabilities when merchants changed policies. Yet Indigenous people were not simply laborers hired by external firms. Communities could regulate access to territory, negotiate trade, redirect suppliers, or refuse unsatisfactory conditions. Their knowledge of the seasons and rivers gave them indispensable leverage. British companies competed for supplies through relationships that could fail if local partners withdrew. It is therefore misleading to equate the presence of a post with British political control over its surrounding country.

The northern interior in 1800 combined powerful Indigenous commercial networks with growing colonial economic penetration. Company archives preserve prices, routes, contracts, and shipments, but Indigenous perspectives must also be reconstructed from oral histories, local community traditions, and the social consequences of exchange. The regional picture adds a necessary dimension to the chapter's central question: empires did not expand through armies and land treaties alone. They also depended on markets, credit, ecological change, and intimate social relationships in which Indigenous communities continued to exercise meaningful, if increasingly contested, authority.

Section 60.17

California’s Indigenous Nations and the Spanish Mission System, 1769–1800

Alta California's Indigenous societies were exceptionally diverse before the Spanish colonization begun in 1769. Along its coast and valleys lived Ohlone, Chumash, Tongva, Kumeyaay, and many other peoples whose languages, villages, trade, and resource systems differed by place. Coastal fishing, shellfish gathering, hunting, plant management, and exchange supported communities with sophisticated ecological knowledge. Spain's new missions, presidios, and civilian settlements entered these occupied landscapes, not an empty wilderness. The Franciscan missions sought Christian conversion, but they also organized land use, work, settlement, and family life under religious and military supervision. A mission bell could regulate the hours of labor while a presidio provided force to support colonial demands. These institutions transformed political authority around them.

Mission communities developed through a combination of persuasion, disruption, pressure, and compulsion, and the circumstances of entering them differed among families. Once baptized, Indigenous residents commonly faced strong restrictions on their freedom to leave, with punishments for those who fled. Men and women were assigned agricultural, pastoral, craft, and domestic labor supporting the mission economy. Spanish authorities justified such programs in religious and paternal language, but these explanations must be distinguished from the experiences of people compelled to remain and work. Mission lands often appropriated fields, gathering areas, and water that surrounding Native communities required for subsistence. As animal herds and agricultural regimes changed the environment, people outside the missions faced increasing difficulty pursuing older seasonal economies.

Disease was a devastating component of colonial expansion. Dense settlement, introduced infections, changes in diet, hard labor, and the separation of families contributed to mortality, although local demographic patterns varied and records were incomplete. Mission baptism and burial registers provide important evidence, but they categorize people according to church administration and do not fully record Indigenous motives, identities, or experiences outside the mission. It would be misleading to claim that every decline had one cause or that a register of sacraments measures willing conversion. Interpreting numbers requires comparison with settlement histories, archaeology, and the perspectives of descendants. The broader pattern nevertheless shows serious demographic disruption in mission districts before 1800.

Resistance took many forms. Some people escaped and returned to familiar villages; others protected ceremonies or languages within the constraints of mission life; still others organized open attacks. The conspiracy was discovered, its organizers were arrested, and Spanish judicial records preserved statements filtered through officials and interpreters. Her role nonetheless demonstrates that women as well as men could organize political resistance across communities. Earlier revolts at San Diego and later conflict elsewhere also refute an image of universal consent.

Indigenous people also adapted in ways that complicate a simple resistance-versus-submission framework. Some converted, worked in mission institutions, or forged relationships that could secure food or protection for relatives. Others moved between mission and nonmission worlds despite official restrictions. People used Spanish tools, animals, and crops selectively, while carrying older place knowledge into changed circumstances. Descendant communities retain traditions that do not fit neatly within the categories of the mission record. Historical analysis must avoid reducing individuals to passive victims, but it must not exaggerate agency so far that coercive structures disappear. The extent of voluntary choice depended on the local presence of soldiers, accessible alternative communities, and the consequences of refusal.

By 1800 the mission system had altered coastal and valley regions without placing every Californian Indigenous nation under identical Spanish control. Inland peoples could encounter trade goods, escaped mission residents, or colonial expeditions while retaining their own governments and resource systems. Some coastal districts experienced sustained confinement and severe demographic losses; others had fewer direct intrusions. The difference between Spanish claims and actual authority was therefore pronounced even within Alta California. California belongs in a continental history not as an inevitable preview of later American rule but as a distinct colonial system whose religious, labor, and demographic institutions produced their own combinations of violence, accommodation, and Indigenous survival.

Section 60.18

The Northwest Coast and the Maritime Fur Trade, 1774–1800

The Northwest Coast entered a period of intensified overseas commerce in the final quarter of the eighteenth century, but its political societies were already connected through extensive maritime and coastal networks. Nuu-chah-nulth, Haida, Tlingit, Coast Salish, Makah, and other peoples managed territory, fishing places, canoe routes, ceremonial relationships, and exchange with neighbors. Wealth could involve access to salmon, cedar, shells, slaves, copper, and many other resources, governed through distinct social and political institutions. Visiting European and American vessels encountered established authorities who could control landing places and set conditions for exchange. The arrival of a foreign ship did not convert a harbor into unowned space, and foreign captains often learned that safe commerce depended on local Indigenous permission.

Spanish exploration along the coast intensified in the 1770s, including the voyage of Juan Pérez in 1774. British voyages and later American merchant ships introduced new goods and expanded an international market for sea-otter pelts, which were highly valued in China. Iron tools, cloth, and other manufactured items could be exchanged for pelts, but the prices and diplomatic protocols were not imposed by Europeans alone. Coastal leaders compared offers, controlled access to hunting and trade, and used rivalry among foreign captains to increase their bargaining power. Demand for sea-otter skins also created ecological pressures and changed the value of particular coastal resources. The historical task is to reconstruct Indigenous control of property and exchange alongside the economic changes brought by global markets.

Nootka Sound on the west coast of Vancouver Island became an especially important point of contact. Maquinna, a Mowachaht Nuu-chah-nulth leader, exercised authority within a maritime world of other powerful leaders and communities. Traders sought his cooperation because their ships required wood, water, food, and access to pelts. Indigenous diplomatic conduct could include formal receptions, gift exchange, and enforcement of local property claims. European observers sometimes misunderstood these practices, treating a temporary landing agreement as a grant of sovereignty or describing Indigenous leaders as willing assistants in an imperial rivalry. Scholars emphasize that the Mowachaht, Clayoquot, and Makah had established territorial and political relationships long before the ships arrived, and those relationships continued to structure exchange.

In 1789 Spanish officials seized British vessels at Nootka Sound, provoking an Anglo-Spanish confrontation that risked wider war. Agreements known as the Nootka Conventions, concluded during the early 1790s, addressed imperial navigation and settlement claims. They were negotiated between European governments without transferring Indigenous sovereignty through an Indigenous treaty. The crisis reveals a familiar gap between state diplomacy and local authority: two empires could compromise over claims to a harbor without acquiring the right to ignore the people who already governed it. A diplomatic map showing British and Spanish claims should therefore be read alongside Indigenous place names and political territories.

Maritime commerce was not uniformly beneficial. Intensive hunting threatened sea-otter populations; visiting ships introduced diseases and new forms of violence; credit relationships, weapons, and changing access to imported goods could reshape internal hierarchies. Captains' journals preserved commercial details and descriptions of meetings, but they were written for ship owners, officials, or readers in distant ports. They rarely recorded all of an Indigenous council's deliberations, and they often interpreted social practices through unfamiliar categories. Archaeological evidence, oral histories, and modern Indigenous scholarship help restore local perspectives. Students should ask whose authority made a trading voyage possible and what the record leaves out about women's work, food production, and household decisions.

The Northwest Coast illustrates a mode of imperial involvement different from the United States' land surveys or California's mission system. Foreign vessels sought mobile resources and commercial advantage while remaining highly dependent on coastal hosts. Colonial claims expanded, but in 1800 many Indigenous communities still controlled where visitors anchored and how exchange occurred. Some retained powerful commercial leverage; others experienced severe disruptions from the pace and consequences of contact. Neither result can be inferred simply from the number of European ships. The region belongs at the center of the continental story because it shows Indigenous polities shaping a global market while contesting the territorial assumptions of distant empires.

Section 60.19

Alaska and the Expansion of Russian America

Russian expansion toward Alaska developed first through maritime exploration and the search for valuable sea-mammal furs, not through an immediate network of settled agricultural colonies. After Bering's eighteenth-century voyages, commercial expeditions crossed the North Pacific and entered Unangax̂ territories in the Aleutian Islands. Russian traders relied heavily on Indigenous maritime expertise, boats, and knowledge of animal habitats. Yet the relationship often rested on coercion. Agents seized hostages, especially relatives of hunters, to compel men to gather pelts; they used threats and force to keep labor and supplies moving. In Russian commercial correspondence, these arrangements might appear as production or tribute. For Indigenous families they could mean disrupted food systems, dangerous work, captivity, and the loss of control over seasonal movement.

The social effects extended beyond the hunt. Sea-otter and other marine mammal exploitation altered local ecologies, while outsiders' demands consumed time previously devoted to household subsistence. Violence and introduced illnesses compounded losses. Unangax̂ communities resisted and negotiated in different ways, sometimes using maritime knowledge to avoid traders or to limit their access. The availability of weapons and ships gave Russian crews advantages in particular confrontations, but logistical dependence on Indigenous food and transport remained substantial. The presence of a trading station on one island did not imply Russian control over all surrounding coastal peoples. Local power varied sharply, and the records often preserve the company's perspective more fully than that of the people compelled to work for it.

On Kodiak Island, Grigorii Shelikhov arrived with a force prepared for confrontation in 1784. Resistance by Sugpiaq/Alutiiq communities met substantial Russian violence. Historical accounts of the Awa'uq episode and related attacks differ in reported numbers, so casualty totals should not be asserted with false precision. The establishment at Three Saints Bay in August 1784 marked a durable Russian commercial base built amid conquest and coercion. Its location should not be confused with the later headquarters at the site of present-day Kodiak, to which operations moved during the early 1790s. Russian authorities organized work crews and sought to supervise Indigenous leaders through a combination of coercion, gifts, and intermediaries. The resulting colonial order required constant labor and resources that Indigenous communities had previously directed toward their own priorities.

The Library of Congress preserves collections of the company's later correspondence, records, and ship logs. The charter is significant because it formally joined commercial monopoly privileges with claims of imperial authority. Yet company formation should not be mistaken for a sudden beginning of Russian penetration, which had already reshaped parts of Alaska for decades. Nor did a royal charter remove the need for Indigenous boat crews, hunters, interpreters, and provisioners. Russian Alaska adds a final contrast to the continent's colonial systems. The American republic advanced through treaties, army posts, and surveys; New Spain used missions, presidios, and diplomacy; maritime traders elsewhere negotiated access to pelts from Indigenous-controlled harbors. Russian companies combined commercial expansion with extensive coercion and direct exploitation of Native hunting labor. These differences mattered to mortality, resistance, community rebuilding, and everyday political choices. By 1800 the North Pacific was not simply a Russian possession but a set of unequal relationships between external commercial enterprises and Indigenous nations whose authority and survival persisted under severe pressure.

Section 60.20

The Indigenous Continent in 1800: Power, Adaptation, and the Limits of Empire

A continental assessment of 1800 must resist two misleading conclusions: that Indigenous peoples had already been conquered, and that their political worlds remained unchanged. Both claims erase the variation established throughout this chapter. The Northwestern Confederacy inflicted major defeats on the United States before accepting a settlement at Greenville under severe pressure. Haudenosaunee leaders secured treaty recognition at Canandaigua even as land transactions continued to reduce some territories. Cherokee and Muscogee diplomats negotiated with an expanding republic whose settlers often ignored official boundaries. Comanche and Ute councils shaped Spanish relations through their own diplomatic initiatives. On the Northwest Coast, Indigenous trading authorities negotiated with foreign ships; in Alaska, Russian firms imposed coercive labor around selected bases. No single balance of power described all these situations.

Territorial authority depended on the means by which it was exercised. An Indigenous government could regulate settlement, control a river crossing, convene councils, maintain ceremonies, and organize labor without using the institutions of a European state. A colonial government could publish a map or a charter yet remain unable to compel a distant community to obey. Military victories mattered, but so did access to food, kinship networks, commercial partners, and the capacity to interpret or reject treaty terms. When several nations claimed use rights in one territory, an agreement with a narrow delegation often deepened conflict instead of resolving it. When an empire needed Indigenous expertise to trade or move through the country, dependence created opportunities for negotiation. The same continent therefore contained relationships ranging from relatively balanced commerce to severe military and labor coercion.

The economy of Indigenous North America was equally diverse. Missouri River villages produced agricultural surpluses that anchored long-distance trade; mounted Plains communities used horses to reorganize hunting and travel; southeastern towns sustained fields and deerskin networks; northern families exchanged furs through competing companies; California communities endured missions that appropriated labor and land; and Northwest Coast nations participated in global demand for sea otters. These activities were not peripheral to political history. They supplied the resources that made councils, defenses, refugee support, and diplomacy possible. An annuity treaty or an imported tool could have different consequences depending on who controlled its distribution and whether communities retained alternatives.

Continuity and Change

Social institutions reveal continuities missed by imperial narratives. Women maintained farms, kin networks, diplomatic authority, trade relations, and households after displacement or violence. Elders transmitted language and ceremonies when disease and war had destroyed communities. Refugees formed settlements and preserved relationships across borders newly drawn by outsiders. Some Indigenous people adopted Christianity, new crops, or commercial practices while retaining political identities and collective responsibilities. Others resisted institutions that required confinement, forced labor, or the surrender of territory. Historical analysis must distinguish selective adaptation from coercion without assuming that Indigenous cultures were frozen in an earlier period. The survival of governance was expressed through ordinary work and social continuity as well as dramatic treaties.

Demographic catastrophe shaped the century's final decades, especially in the northern Plains and mission districts. Disease could remove knowledge keepers and reduce the labor available to maintain fields and towns. Yet survival was not simply numerical. Communities regrouped, renewed alliances, cared for children, and maintained ceremonial life. It is important not to confuse the varying smallpox chronologies or project the later epidemics of the nineteenth century back into 1800. The evidence establishes profound losses in particular places but cannot justify a prediction that all Indigenous societies would necessarily face the same future. Political decisions by states and local leaders still mattered, as did ecological and commercial developments not yet determined.

The chapter's treaties demonstrate the difference between a recorded agreement and a settled relationship. Canandaigua and Greenville were made under different conditions, and their articles expressed both Indigenous rights and American restrictions. The Treaty of New York with Muscogee representatives did not ensure Georgia's compliance. The Jay Treaty addressed movement across a border that Indigenous peoples had not drawn. The Comanche and Ute cases show that some consequential negotiations took place outside European documentary control. Students should therefore examine not merely the existence of a signature but representation, coercion, the meanings assigned to words, and what happened afterward. Written documents matter enormously, but they cannot by themselves prove the disappearance of competing sovereignty.

By 1800, the continent contained expanding colonial and commercial projects alongside enduring Indigenous political worlds. The future direction of those relationships remained unsettled. The United States had shown that it could raise a powerful army, yet many nations beyond its settlements still governed extensive homelands. Spain relied on negotiated peace in New Mexico while imposing coercive missions in California. British traders depended on Indigenous labor and routes. Russian companies held selected bases while meeting formidable resistance and the limits of maritime supply. These distinctions explain why the nineteenth-century outcomes that readers may know cannot be treated as inevitable conclusions already written into eighteenth-century history. The continent remained an arena of choices, pressures, and contested possibilities.

Chapter Summary

The Indigenous continent at the end of the eighteenth century was made up of multiple political worlds, not an empty territory awaiting European or American settlement. States claimed enormous regions that their officials could neither survey securely nor govern without bargaining. Indigenous nations continued to exercise authority through councils, territorial defenses, markets, ceremonies, kinship, and the regulation of outsiders. This chapter compared those forms of authority across the continent rather than treating the early republic as its geographic or political center.

In the Ohio Country, the United States sought to convert treaty claims into territorial government and surveyed land. The Northwest Ordinance promised protection for Indigenous rights even as settlers, land speculators, and officers pressed westward. Treaties at Fort McIntosh and Fort Harmar did not win acceptance from the entire region. Confederacy diplomats therefore asserted the need for collective consent before lands used by several nations could be ceded. This was a political argument about legitimate representation as well as territorial security.

The defeats of Harmar in 1790 and St. Clair in 1791 displayed the military capabilities of Indigenous forces and forced American institutional reform. The resulting Legion under Anthony Wayne defeated a coalition at Fallen Timbers in 1794, and the subsequent destruction of farms and food stores narrowed Indigenous bargaining options. The war cannot be reduced to a progression of American victories: Indigenous success shaped the course and expense of the conflict before a new balance emerged.

The Treaty of Greenville of 1795 combined a substantial territorial cession with peace, prisoner restoration, hostages, strategic reservations, goods, and continuing territorial recognition. Indigenous political institutions were not extinguished, although American settlement could expand on relinquished lands. The Treaty of Canandaigua of 1794 arose from a different setting and acknowledged particular Haudenosaunee holdings. Both documents require reading alongside the authority of signatories, ceremonial diplomacy, and later conduct.

Great Lakes communities maintained kinship and commerce across the British-American boundary. The Grand River settlement and treaty provisions concerning cross-border movement demonstrated the inadequacy of a modern state-centered map for describing Indigenous social geography. The Jay Treaty and British withdrawal from western posts altered diplomacy without erasing established exchange networks. Indigenous political relationships survived changes in forts, international agreements, and official jurisdiction.

In the Southeast, Cherokee and Muscogee leaders negotiated with federal officials and resisted local encroachments, often while confronting disagreements among their own towns. The Hopewell, Holston, and New York treaties contained boundaries and promises that settlers and states did not necessarily honor. Choctaw, Chickasaw, and Catawba communities operated under distinct demographic and commercial conditions. The political authority of these nations depended on towns, kinship, agricultural life, and ceremonies as well as on the actions of prominent male diplomats.

The southern Plains and Southwest offered different configurations of power. Comanche leaders negotiated trade and peace with Spanish New Mexico because they possessed resources and military leverage that governors could not simply command. Ute–Comanche agreements demonstrate inter-Indigenous diplomacy whose terms were not fully preserved in Spanish records. Pueblo, Navajo, Apache, Hopi, Zuni, and O'odham communities encountered Spanish authority differently according to local geography, institutions, and access to trade.

Great Plains political life also requires distinguishing mobile horse economies from the agricultural and commercial centers of the Missouri River. Mandan, Hidatsa, Arikara, Pawnee, and other peoples developed markets, fields, and diplomatic institutions that connected broad regions. Smallpox in 1781–1782 devastated many communities but did not eliminate their governance or capacity for rebuilding. In the northern interior, fur companies relied on Indigenous labor, transport, relationships, and food; commercial penetration was not equivalent to comprehensive territorial rule.

In Alta California, the Spanish mission and presidio system imposed distinctive forms of labor control, confinement, religious pressure, and demographic disruption on many Indigenous communities. Resistance by figures including Toypurina and Nicolás José complicates narratives that treat mission participation as universally voluntary. Along the Northwest Coast, Indigenous nations negotiated maritime commerce with overseas vessels, and the Nootka dispute among European powers occurred in territories they had never ceded through Indigenous treaty.

Russian America was another distinct colonial regime. Russian companies used hostage taking and coerced hunting labor, particularly among Unangax̂ and Sugpiaq/Alutiiq communities, while remaining dependent on their maritime expertise. The 1784 Three Saints Bay establishment and 1799 Russian-American Company charter formalized commercial expansion without automatically securing control over every Alaska Native nation. Many Indigenous communities resisted, negotiated, or maintained independence beyond the reach of company bases.

The continent in 1800 contained enduring Indigenous sovereignty alongside severe and uneven forms of dispossession, epidemic disease, and commercial coercion. Military power, social resilience, household economies, and diplomatic alternatives varied from place to place. Neither a history of inevitable conquest nor a story of timeless continuity can account for those differences. The next chronological era would bring new struggles, but its outcomes were not predetermined by the eighteenth century's close.

Chapter Reflection — The Continent in 1800

What did it mean for Indigenous North America to remain politically independent in a century of expanding empires? The answer was not reducible to a treaty, battle, or map. A nation governed when it maintained councils, cared for households, defended land, enforced relationships, and decided how to encounter outsiders.

St. Clair's defeat made visible military authority; Greenville showed the costs of a subsequent defeat; Canandaigua recorded continuing Haudenosaunee territorial recognition. These events matter differently from the negotiations of Comanche and Ute diplomats, the labor policies of California missions, or the coercion of Russian trading enterprises.

Women, children, elders, and working households must remain in the historical picture. Agricultural labor, care after epidemics, cultural teaching, and the preservation of kinship relationships helped communities withstand the pressures associated with colonial states and commercial markets.

Political futures were still open in 1800. American, British, Spanish, and Russian officials possessed plans, institutions, and increasingly ambitious territorial claims; Indigenous governments retained negotiating capacity, social legitimacy, and substantial control over many places. Regional conditions made both resistance and adaptation possible under unequal circumstances.

The measure of this chapter is whether students can explain why a declaration of sovereignty in a capital city did not establish government over a distant Indigenous town, and why communities that survived great losses remained agents in their own history.

The century closed neither with a unified Indigenous victory nor with an inevitable colonial triumph. It closed with continuing relationships of power, contested claims, and choices whose consequences would be made through the struggles of the next era.
