The Śailendras and Central Java

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

In 778 CE, according to a Sanskrit inscription associated with the temple of Kalasan on the plain east of modern Yogyakarta, a Javanese king gave a village to a community of Buddhist monks. The record is brief, but it joins in one act a royal court, a circle of teachers, a goddess, a monastery, a village, and the rice-growing countryside on which all of them depended.

The stone associated with Kalasan is not a chronicle. It does not narrate a reign, describe a battle, or explain how its royal donor came to power. It records a foundation: a sanctuary dedicated to the Buddhist goddess Tārā, a residence for monks, and the assignment of a village, named in the text as Kālasa, to the monastic community. The ruler is named in the text as Panaṅkaran, and the royal teachers who urged him to act are described as gurus of the Śailendra king or kings, a lineage whose relation to Panaṅkaran himself is debated. The text also names officials who acted on the king's behalf. The date, given in the Śaka calendar as year 700, corresponds to 778 CE. Almost everything else about the political world behind the inscription has to be inferred, and the inferences have been debated for more than a century.

This chapter studies the Javanese courts of the eighth and early ninth centuries through documents of this kind. Its central question is how rulers in Central Java built powerful courts out of agrarian resources, local political relationships, and Buddhist patronage, and what the disputed history of the Śailendras reveals about the difficulty of reconstructing dynasties from inscriptions, monuments, and later king lists. The older textbook answer was simple: a Śaiva dynasty founded by a king named Sanjaya ruled Central Java, was displaced or overshadowed by a Buddhist Śailendra dynasty that built great monuments, and eventually reasserted itself through the marriage of a Śailendra princess to a Sanjaya prince. That story is not wholly baseless, but it is a reconstruction layered on a small number of fragmentary texts, and many specialists now regard it as one possible reading rather than established fact.

The chapter's second argument concerns the ground beneath the courts. The temples, monasteries, teachers, and ritual specialists documented in the inscriptions had to be fed, housed, and paid for. In Central Java that meant volcanic plains suited to intensive rice cultivation, village communities able to produce a surplus, and mechanisms, above all land grants and the redirection of revenue, through which kings could convert agricultural production into religious and political capital. Central Java was thus both an agrarian society and an outward-looking one. Its rulers received Sanskrit learning, religious doctrine, teachers, and political prestige from the wider Buddhist and Indian Ocean world, and they did so while drawing their material strength from Javanese fields.

Historians work here with an unusually narrow body of contemporary evidence: stone and copper inscriptions in Sanskrit, Old Malay, and Old Javanese; the surviving temples and sanctuary sites; and later inscriptions, especially an early-tenth-century charter that lists earlier kings. Each category has limits. Inscriptions record what donors and scribes wished to preserve, chiefly foundations, grants, and genealogies, and say little about ordinary life. Temples rarely identify exactly who built each phase. The later king lists were composed generations after the events and served the political interests of their own time. Good historical practice in this period therefore means keeping separate what contemporaries wrote, what later tradition remembered, and what modern scholars have proposed.

Foreign evidence matters as well, though it is thinner here than for Funan or Champa. A mid-ninth-century copper-plate charter from Nālandā in eastern India names a king of Suvarṇadvīpa, who claims Śailendra descent, and connects Javanese and Sumatran elites to the great Buddhist monastic centers of South Asia. The Ligor stele from the Malay Peninsula, whose Face A is dated 775 and whose undated Face B also uses Śailendra royal language, shows the name outside Java as well. Such sources show that the Śailendra name travelled, but they do not by themselves tell us where the family began or how its branches were related.

Within the architecture of this textbook, the chapter occupies a deliberately bounded place. Chapters 28 and 29 examined Srivijaya and the maritime Buddhist world; Srivijaya enters here only where it helps explain Śailendra connections. Chapter 31 will examine Borobudur as architecture, religious monument, and political statement, and Chapter 32 will turn to Prambanan, Mataram institutions, and the shift of power toward East Java. Chapter 30 explains the political, economic, and religious world that could produce a monument on the scale of Borobudur. It treats Central Javanese societies as historical actors who selected, adapted, and transformed outside traditions, the point developed in Chapter 19, and not as passive recipients of Indian or Sumatran influence.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the physical setting of the Kedu and Prambanan plains and explain how volcanic soils, rivers, and access to the northern coast shaped Central Javanese political life without determining it.
  2. Distinguish contemporary inscriptions, surviving monuments, later king lists, and modern scholarly reconstruction as different kinds of evidence for eighth- and ninth-century Java.
  3. Explain what the Canggal inscription of 732 reveals about Sanjaya, Śaiva royal ideology, and the Javanese landscape.
  4. Analyze the competing theories about the origins and identity of the Śailendras and explain why the question remains open.
  5. Interpret the Kalasan, Kelurak, and Mañjuśrīgṛha inscriptions as evidence of royal Buddhist patronage, religious teachers, and landholding.
  6. Trace the role of royal gurus and Mahāyāna and esoteric Buddhist ideas in Central Javanese court culture, keeping interpretation proportional to the evidence.
  7. Explain how wet-rice agriculture, village communities, and land grants (sīma) provided the material basis for religious foundations.
  8. Evaluate the traditional two-dynasty model of Sanjaya and the Śailendras against alternative reconstructions.
  9. Assess the limits of genealogy and king lists, including the Mantyasih charter of 907, as sources for early Javanese succession.
  10. Contextualize Pramodhawardhani, Rakai Pikatan, and Bālaputradeva within debates about political transition and interregional elite connections.
  11. Connect Central Java to the Malay Peninsula, Sumatra, India, and the maritime Buddhist world while preserving Javanese agency.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How do historians reconstruct the political history of a society from a small number of inscriptions, monuments, and much later lists of kings?
  2. What was the material basis of Central Javanese royal power, and how did rulers convert agricultural production into religious and political authority?
  3. Who or what were the Śailendras, and why have scholars disagreed so persistently about their origin and relation to other Javanese rulers?
  4. Does religious affiliation map neatly onto dynastic identity, or can one court patronize Buddhist and Śaiva institutions?
  5. What did a land grant to a monastery mean for the village that received it and for the royal treasury that surrendered revenue?
  6. How did Central Javanese rulers participate in Buddhist networks reaching India, the Malay Peninsula, and Sumatra without becoming dependent extensions of those places?
  7. Why do later narratives, including modern textbooks, favor clean stories of rival dynasties, and what is lost when contested evidence is made tidy?
  8. What does the career of Bālaputradeva suggest about the movement of elites across the maritime world?

Historical Context

By the seventh century the island of Java was already part of a long-established network of exchange. Chapters 17 and 18 traced the maritime connections that linked island and mainland Southeast Asia with South Asia and China before the best-documented historical states emerged, and Chapter 19 examined how Southeast Asian communities selectively adopted Sanskrit, religious traditions, scripts, and court practices. Chapter 27 introduced early inscriptions from western Java and Borneo, showing local rulers using Sanskrit to express kingship. Central Java enters the written record somewhat later than these sites, but its rulers inherited the same repertoire.

To the west, Chapters 28 and 29 described the rise of Srivijaya in southeastern Sumatra, a polity whose power rested on control of waterways and exchange rather than extensive agricultural land, and its connections with Chinese and South Asian Buddhist worlds. Chapter 26 treated the Malay Peninsula as a zone of transpeninsular routes and early states. These neighbors matter for Chapter 30 because Śailendra inscriptions appear on both sides of the Java Sea, and because Java's elites, whatever their origin, were visibly in touch with these centers.

Central Java differed from Srivijaya's estuarine setting. Its heartland lies in the interior, among volcanic mountains and fertile plains that supported intensive rice cultivation, a pattern outlined in Chapters 3, 4, and 5 in the discussion of volcanic landscapes and monsoon agriculture. Because the plains could support dense rural populations, Central Javanese courts could draw on agricultural revenue and village labor in ways that the more maritime courts of Sumatra could not always match. This is a tendency, not a law: Chapter 1 cautioned against dividing the region into an agrarian mainland and a commercial archipelago, and Java is the best illustration of why. Central Java was agrarian at its core, yet connected to the sea and to wider religious and commercial worlds.

Finally, the religious setting was plural. Śaiva, Vaiṣṇava, and Buddhist traditions all reached Java, and inscriptions show kings supporting more than one. Understanding the Śailendras requires abandoning the assumption that a ruler had to belong exclusively to a single religious tradition and that a change in the religion of a foundation signals a change of dynasty.

Section 30.1

Central Java: Kedu, Prambanan, and an Agrarian Heartland

The political geography of Central Java begins with volcanoes. A chain of volcanic peaks, among them Merapi, Merbabu, Sumbing, and Sindoro, runs through the interior of the island, and the rivers that descend from them, notably the Progo and the Opak, have built and watered broad plains. Repeated deposits of volcanic ash and eroded volcanic material give the soils of these plains a fertility that has long supported intensive agriculture, a general feature of Javanese environments introduced in Chapter 5. Two lowland areas matter most for this chapter: the Kedu Plain, between the Progo system and the volcanic massifs to its west and north, and the Prambanan Plain to the east, on the Opak side of the region, near the modern city of Yogyakarta.

These plains are not self-contained worlds. The Progo and other streams allow movement toward the south coast, and routes across the uplands and valleys connect the interior with Java's northern coast, where ports faced the Java Sea. Overland roads and river routes carried rice, craft goods, stone, timber, and people. The south coast of Java is exposed to heavy ocean swell and has fewer natural harbors, so the main maritime orientation of Central Java lay northward, a fact that helps explain how a heartland well inland could stay in touch with Sumatra, the Peninsula, and beyond.

The monsoon climate described in Chapter 4 shaped the agricultural year. Seasonal rainfall made rice cultivation possible, and where water could be diverted from streams to terraced or leveled fields, farmers could raise reliable crops. Volcanic landscapes also brought hazards: eruptions, ashfall, and lahars could destroy fields and settlements. The historian should therefore see the same mountains as a source of richness and risk, and should avoid treating the environment as a simple explanation for political outcomes. Chapter 32 will return to the debated role of environmental disruption in later shifts of power.

Setting Central Java beside Srivijaya's setting helps clarify what kind of power its rulers wielded. Srivijaya's core around the Musi River and estuarine southeastern Sumatra was oriented toward harbors and control of waterways. Central Java's courts could rely more heavily on landed wealth: the produce of villages, the labor of cultivators, and the capacity to assign or withhold revenue from particular lands. Yet the line should not be drawn too sharply. The inscriptions of Central Java are in languages and scripts that reached the island through maritime contacts, and the religious teachers who inspired its foundations travelled by sea. Central Java was an interior society with a long reach.

Section 30.2

Reconstructing Eighth-Century Java: Inscriptions, Temples, and Later King Lists

The history of Central Java before the tenth century is reconstructed from three kinds of evidence, each with its own strengths and distortions. The first is the contemporary inscription. These texts, engraved on stone or incised on copper plates, were created at the time of the events they record, which gives them a weight that later narratives lack. But they were written for particular purposes: to commemorate the installation of an image, to establish a foundation, to fix the terms of a land grant, or to proclaim a genealogy. They tell us what a donor wanted remembered and what scribes, working in elite literary languages, chose to include. Their silence about warfare, taxation, or village life does not mean that those things were unimportant.

The second kind of evidence is the monument. Temples, sanctuaries, and the remains of monastic buildings show where wealth and labor were directed and give a physical sense of religious life. Yet temples are difficult to date precisely, are often rebuilt or enlarged, and seldom carry inscriptions naming their builders. Associating a ruined sanctuary with a particular king, or a particular phase of construction with a particular dynasty, usually involves inference from style, from nearby inscriptions, or from later tradition. For that reason this chapter uses monuments to illuminate patronage and landscape and leaves architectural analysis, especially of Borobudur, to Chapter 31.

The third kind of evidence is the retrospective king list, and it is the most treacherous. In 907 CE, a ruler named Balitung issued a charter at Mantyasih that lists earlier kings of Mataram. Such a list is valuable because it preserves names that do not otherwise appear, but it was composed roughly a century and a half after Sanjaya and over a century after the Kalasan inscription. It reflects the genealogical claims of a later royal house and may smooth over conflict, omit rival branches, and compress a complicated sequence of rulers into a single line. Matching the names in such a list to the names in contemporary inscriptions, which often use different titles, is an act of interpretation and not simply a matter of reading.

The result is a historical record in which the dated, contemporary anchors are few: Canggal (732), Kalasan (778), Kelurak (782), Mañjuśrīgṛha (792), Karangtengah (824), and a handful of later texts. Between them lie gaps in which scholars have placed reigns, marriages, and conflicts according to their preferred reconstruction. Students should keep this fragility in view throughout the chapter. The dynastic story told in many reference works is a scholarly synthesis, and the question of how much weight it can bear is part of the history.

Section 30.3

Sanjaya and the Canggal Inscription, 732

The earliest Central Javanese inscription commonly used to open the political narrative is the Canggal inscription, also called the Gunung Wukir inscription after the hill where it was found in the Kedu region. It is written in Sanskrit and dated in the Śaka era to year 654, which corresponds to 732 CE. The text commemorates the installation of a Śiva liṅga, the aniconic emblem of the god, on a hill and records the name of the king under whom this was done: Sanjaya.

Read as a statement of royal ideology, Canggal shows how a Javanese ruler could use Sanskrit verse to present himself within a recognized idiom of kingship. In the paraphrase offered by editors, the text opens with homage to Śiva and then praises Java, described as an island rich in grain and in gold, before turning to the king's lineage. It speaks of an earlier ruler, whom it names Sanna, whose passing left the realm in disorder, and of Sanjaya as the figure who restored stability and extended his power. We should note what is being asserted and what is not. The inscription claims restoration after a period of difficulty and positions Sanjaya as a capable and religiously devoted ruler. It does not describe the political structure of his realm, the extent of his territory, or the identity of his rivals.

Two features deserve emphasis. First, the praise of Java as rich in grain and gold is court poetry rather than economic statistics. Its reference to grain is consistent with the agrarian setting established by environmental and later epigraphic evidence, while the gold motif is a conventional Sanskrit image and cannot by itself demonstrate local gold production. Second, the inscription is Śaiva. A Śiva liṅga on a hilltop was a form of religious foundation that projected the king's authority into the landscape, in line with the role of sacred places in earlier inscriptions discussed in Chapter 27. Śaiva patronage in 732 does not, however, prove that Sanjaya's family was permanently or exclusively Śaiva, nor does it establish the existence of a distinct "Sanjaya dynasty" with an unbroken identity. Those are later constructions.

Canggal gives historians a starting point rather than a foundation. Sanjaya reappears at the head of the Mantyasih list of 907, which implies that the later Mataram royal house remembered him as a founding figure. That later memory is evidence of how the later dynasty understood its origin, not independent proof of continuity. The question of how Sanjaya's reign relates to the securely dated Buddhist inscriptions of the 770s is the problem to which the following sections turn.

Section 30.4

Who Were the Śailendras? A Dynasty and a Historical Problem

The name Śailendra is a Sanskrit compound usually translated as "lord of the mountain," and it appears in inscriptions from Java, from the Malay Peninsula, and, in a retrospective context, from eastern India. In Central Java, it is attached to royal teachers and rulers associated with Buddhist foundations. What modern scholars call the Śailendra dynasty is the family or lineage implied by these references. Whether they formed a single coherent dynasty, a prestigious title borrowed by several ruling lines, or a looser kinship network is part of the problem.

Historical Vocabulary
Śailendravaṃśa

Definition: A Sanskrit expression meaning the "lineage" or "family" (vaṃśa) of the Śailendra, the "lords of the mountain." Historical context: The term appears in inscriptions from Central Java and the Malay Peninsula and in the Nālandā copper plate. It is a self-description used by donors, not a modern classification. Why it matters: Historians use it to identify a recurring claim of royal descent, but the inscriptions do not state where the family began or how all its branches were connected. Potential misunderstanding: "Śailendra dynasty" in modern books sometimes implies a single, continuous, Buddhist ruling house with a fixed territory. The sources support something more cautious: a prestigious lineage claim that appears in several places and whose boundaries are uncertain.

Older reconstructions placed the Śailendras' origin in several different regions. Some scholars proposed an Indian homeland, for example in the Kalinga region of the east coast of India, reading the Sanskrit name and the family's Buddhist patronage as signs of Indian origin. Others, influenced by the important work of the historian George Cœdès, connected the Śailendras with the earlier culture of Funan and, through it, with maritime Southeast Asia and Srivijaya, suggesting a Sumatran or mainland link. A third approach looked to the Malay Peninsula, noting the Ligor inscription. A fourth, advanced by several Indonesian and other scholars, argued that the Śailendras were fundamentally Javanese. The Old Malay Sojomerto inscription, with a difficult reading often rendered Dapunta Selendra and a date given only broadly as the seventh or eighth century, has been cited in support of this idea, though the reading and the identification are both disputed.

The difficulty is that the evidence can be read in several ways. The Śailendra name appears in Sanskrit, often in a context of Buddhist patronage, but Sanskrit and Buddhist learning were available throughout the maritime world and are not reliable markers of ethnic or geographic origin. The use of Old Malay in the later Mañjuśrīgṛha inscription might suggest a Sumatran link, but Old Malay was also a language of inter-island communication and prestige, and its use in Java is not decisive. The presence of Śailendra language on the Ligor stele, whose Face A is dated 775 but whose Śailendra-bearing Face B is undated and probably later, shows that the family or title had reached beyond Java, but not in which direction it travelled. Recent scholarship, including a working paper by Anton Zakharov, has argued that a Javanese origin is entirely plausible and that the evidence does not require a foreign dynasty displacing a native one. That position remains an interpretation, not a settled conclusion.

The wisest course for students is to treat the origin of the Śailendras as an open question and to focus on what the sources do demonstrate: that in the second half of the eighth century, rulers and royal teachers in Central Java used the Śailendra name in connection with Buddhist foundations, and that the name circulated across the maritime world. Whether the Śailendras were native or foreign matters less to this chapter's argument than how they operated: as patrons whose authority depended on local agriculture, local officials, and local acceptance.

Section 30.5

Panangkaran and the Political Transition after Sanjaya

The ruler who appears in the Kalasan inscription of 778 is called Panaṅkaran, with a fuller form in the text that scholars normally render as Kariyāna Panaṅkaraṇa. In later lists he appears as Rakai Panangkaran. The Mantyasih charter of 907 places a ruler of this name second in its sequence of kings, immediately after Sanjaya. If the Mantyasih list is taken at face value, Panangkaran was Sanjaya's successor, and the political transition between Canggal and Kalasan is a straightforward succession from one king to the next.

But the simplicity is deceptive. The Kalasan inscription describes the royal gurus as preceptors of the Śailendra king or kings and the king as patron of a Buddhist foundation, a different religious profile from the Śaiva ruler of Canggal. Scholars have therefore asked whether Panangkaran was a descendant of Sanjaya who changed religious patronage, a Śailendra ruler who had absorbed or replaced Sanjaya's line, or a ruler of a more complex lineage whose family had ties to both. The inscriptions do not say. The surviving text also does not state that Panangkaran himself was Śailendra; it can be read as describing a king guided by Śailendra teachers or as describing a Śailendra king, and both readings have supporters.

Historical Vocabulary
Rakai

Definition: An Old Javanese title, often preceding the name of a place, borne by high-ranking lords and sometimes by kings, as in Rakai Panangkaran or Rakai Pikatan. Historical context: The title seems to link its holder to a particular locality or estate, such as a village or district from which the lord derived status and revenue. Related titles appear in Old Javanese inscriptions. Why it matters: Because kings could be known by a rakai name as well as by personal or Sanskrit regnal names, the same individual may appear under different designations in different sources, complicating efforts to match names across inscriptions and king lists. Potential misunderstanding: It is not a simple synonym for "king" and not a reliable sign of dynastic membership; it points instead to the local base of aristocratic authority.

The transition between Sanjaya and Panangkaran is therefore best treated as a gap in the evidence in which several scenarios fit. What can be said is that between 732 and 778, royal patronage in the Central Javanese heartland shifted from a recorded Śaiva foundation to a recorded Buddhist foundation, and that the later Mantyasih list remembers the two rulers as consecutive. Interpreting that shift as a violent change of dynasty is one possibility; interpreting it as an adjustment in royal patronage under changing counsel is another. Chapter 30 returns to this problem in the debate of Section 30.13.

Section 30.6

Kalasan, 778: Tārā, Royal Gurus, and a Buddhist Foundation

The Kalasan inscription, dated Śaka 700 (778 CE), is the most informative single document for the Buddhist court of eighth-century Central Java. It is composed in Sanskrit, in verse, and in a script derived from North Indian models that was current in Java at the time. It belongs to the temple site of Kalasan, a sanctuary on the plain east of Yogyakarta, though the exact original relationship between the text and the surviving building has been discussed by archaeologists.

In the reading of modern editors, the inscription makes several linked statements. It records that the royal gurus of the Śailendra king or kings persuaded or requested the king, Panaṅkaran, to build a sanctuary for the goddess Tārā, and it names administrative designations, Paṅkur, Tavan, and Tirip, whose holders acted on the king's behalf in the construction. These are offices or titles, comparable to designations that later inscriptions pair with the personal names of their holders, so the Kalasan text should not be read as preserving three personal names. It then mentions a residence or monastery for monks of the Mahāyāna tradition. Finally, it records the donation of the village of Kālasa to the Buddhist community (saṅgha), with the same officials appearing as regional administrators or witnesses connected with the grant and among those charged with maintaining it. The text closes with a call to future rulers to protect the foundation, which is a typical feature of grant inscriptions in Java and across South and Southeast Asia: an act of royal piety was also an attempt to bind successors.

The goddess Tārā is a major figure in Mahāyāna Buddhism, associated with compassion and protection. Her appearance at Kalasan indicates that Mahāyāna ideas and devotional forms had reached the Javanese court by the late eighth century. The reference to monks implies a functioning monastic community, with the material needs of residence, food, and ritual that this entails. Sanskrit verse of this quality suggests educated specialists at the court, whether trained in Java, in other parts of maritime Southeast Asia, or in Indian centers of learning. The inscription does not tell us which.

The role of the royal teachers deserves emphasis. The text presents the king as acting at their request. This is not a statement that teachers governed, but it shows religious specialists as influential participants in royal decision-making and as intermediaries with wider Buddhist networks. In other societies of the period, similar figures served as ritual experts, advisers, and legitimators of kingship, and Kalasan allows us to see this kind of role in Java.

Kalasan thus shows religion, kingship, landholding, administration, and patronage working together in a single act. A Buddhist goddess received a sanctuary; monks received a home; a village received a new relationship to the court and to a monastic landlord; and the king received the merit, prestige, and ideological reinforcement that came from sponsoring the faith. The inscription repays study less for its theology than for the social and economic mechanism it records: the transfer of an agricultural community's resources to a religious institution by royal decision.

Section 30.7

What the Kalasan Inscription Reveals about Government

An inscription of this kind rewards reading for its institutional assumptions. A king who can give a village to a religious community must have recognized rights over that village: rights to its produce, its labor, or the dues owed to the court. The transfer implies a system in which land and people were assigned to institutions, and in which villages were stable units with identifiable boundaries and communities. It also implies that the grant would be respected by those on the ground, which in turn implies officials who could enforce the king's command; the text does name some.

The Kalasan text, as presently edited, is more informative about administration than it first appears. It names three administrative offices, or their holders, Paṅkur, Tavan, and Tirip, who act on the king's behalf in building the Tārā temple and the monastic residence, who are identified as regional administrators or witnesses to the grant of the village of Kālasa, and who appear again among those charged with maintaining it. This is direct evidence for early administrative personnel. It does not, however, give the long lists of officials, witnesses, and boundary descriptions found in later Old Javanese charters such as the one issued at Mantyasih, and students should be careful not to read the fuller administrative machinery of the ninth and tenth centuries back into 778. What Kalasan allows is a measured inference: by the 770s, Javanese rulers could issue binding grants of village revenue through named agents, rely on written texts to record them, and appeal to future kings to preserve them. The expansion of this practice into a detailed system of tax rights and officials, covered in Section 30.12 and in Chapter 32, builds on this early example.

The appeal to future rulers is itself evidence about political uncertainty. Kings made grants and asked successors to honor them precisely because they could not take continuity for granted. A foundation that depended on one king's favor could be cancelled by another. Religious foundations therefore sought permanence through language, through oaths and curses invoked in other inscriptions, through physical construction in stone, and through the attachment of dedicated villages to ensure a stable income. Seen this way, the great Buddhist foundations of Central Java were also institutions designed to outlive their founders.

Primary Source Window

The Kalasan Inscription, 778 CE

Paraphrase / Synthesis (not a verbatim excerpt):

The text states that the royal gurus of the Śailendra king or kings prompted the king, Panaṅkaran, to build a sanctuary for the goddess Tārā and a residence for Buddhist monks. It names Paṅkur, Tavan, and Tirip, administrative offices or their holders, as acting on the king's behalf and connected with the grant. It records that the village of Kālasa was given to the monastic community, and it asks later kings to protect the foundation.

Analysis: The inscription is evidence that by 778 Mahāyāna Buddhist devotion, represented by Tārā, had a prominent place at a Javanese court; that religious specialists could influence royal decisions; that kings could assign village resources to monastic institutions through named officials, which gives direct evidence for early administrative personnel; and that rulers expected written records to constrain their successors. It also shows that the Śailendra name was associated with royal Buddhist teachers in Java by this date.

Limitations: The text does not tell us how the village was governed before the grant, what obligations passed to the monks, how many people lived in Kālasa, or what the court gave up. It names a few officials but nothing like the long lists of later charters. It does not settle whether Panaṅkaran was himself a Śailendra, nor does it describe the relationship between his kingship and that of Sanjaya. It cannot show how ordinary villagers or non-Buddhist communities experienced the foundation. Details of reading and interpretation depend on modern editions, which should be consulted for the precise wording.

Perspective: The source represents the voice of the donor court and its advisers. It does not preserve the viewpoint of the villagers, of rival nobles, or of Śaiva communities.

Section 30.8

Kelurak, 782: Mañjuśrī and Śailendra Buddhist Kingship

Four years after Kalasan, another Sanskrit inscription documents a related act of Buddhist patronage. The Kelurak inscription, found near the Prambanan Plain, is dated Śaka 704, corresponding to 782 CE. It records the consecration of an image of Mañjuśrī, the bodhisattva associated with wisdom, and it praises the king, named Śrī Saṅgrāmadhanañjaya and called an ornament of the Śailendra dynasty, as the patron under whose authority the work was carried out. The inscription identifies the officiating teacher, Kumāraghoṣa, as a guru from Gauḍī-dvīpa, conventionally understood as Gauḍa, that is, Bengal.

Older literature derived other royal names, such as Indra or Dharaṇīndra, from a different phrase in the inscription, which explains some of the confusion in earlier reconstructions, and the equation of the king with figures in later lists has also been controversial. Whatever the outcome of those debates, the inscription yields several secure observations. It shows another Śailendra-associated Buddhist foundation within a short span of Kalasan. It shows royal Buddhism continuing as a courtly commitment, not just a single act of a single ruler. And it demonstrates the presence of learned religious specialists who may have come from, or been trained in, distant Buddhist centers, a detail that fits the broader pattern of itinerant teachers described in Chapter 18.

The language of the text draws on a poetic vocabulary of praise shared among Sanskrit court literatures of the time, in which a ruler's merit, strength, and generosity are celebrated through standard imagery. Editors have observed that this imagery overlaps with that of Brahmanical devotion. The point should not be pressed into a claim of religious fusion, but it does show that Buddhist and Brahmanical courtly Sanskrit drew from the same literary stock, and that Javanese court poets and teachers knew it.

Kelurak also begins to widen the geographical scope of Śailendra patronage. If Kalasan is a single sanctuary and village, Kelurak belongs to an increasingly dense cluster of Buddhist sites on the Prambanan Plain. The inscriptions of the 770s, 780s, and 790s give the impression of a court deliberately building a religious landscape, in which individual foundations were connected by common patrons, teachers, and ideas.

Section 30.9

Mañjuśrīgṛha and the Sacred Landscape around Sewu

A third dated inscription, associated with the Sewu complex on the Prambanan Plain, was composed in Śaka 714, or 792 CE. It is known as the Mañjuśrīgṛha inscription after the name of the foundation, the "house of Mañjuśrī," and, as read by editors, it records work on a Buddhist sanctuary that was apparently an existing or newly expanded religious complex. The inscription is written in Old Malay, in contrast with the Sanskrit of Kalasan and Kelurak.

The use of Old Malay deserves careful consideration. One possibility is that it reflects a direct Sumatran link, since Old Malay is the language of the Srivijayan inscriptions discussed in Chapter 28. Another is that Old Malay served as a prestigious or widely understood language of inter-island communication and Buddhist administration, so that its appearance in Java reveals connections without implying subordination. A third is that scribes in the Śailendra-patronized milieu used it for reasons of local practice. The evidence does not select among these. What the inscription undeniably does is show a multilingual court environment in which Sanskrit, Old Malay, and Old Javanese coexisted, each serving different functions.

The Sewu complex is generally regarded as one of the major Buddhist sanctuaries of the Prambanan Plain, and its name is commonly linked to the inscription's reference. Nearby sites suggest a plain filled with religious buildings. The hilltop complex of Ratu Boko is associated with an inscription of 792 recording a Buddhist Śailendra foundation connected with the Sri Lankan Abhayagirivihāra tradition, and with significant Śaiva activity around the middle of the ninth century, a combination that illustrates the mixed patronage discussed later in this chapter. Rather than surveying these monuments individually, which would intrude into the field of Chapter 31, it is enough here to note what they show about royal strategy: a succession of foundations by a court that wanted the countryside to carry the marks of its religious patronage, and that had the revenue and labor to support them.

Taken together, Kalasan, Kelurak, and Mañjuśrīgṛha form a sequence in which a royal Buddhist court founds a sanctuary, consecrates an image, and expands a complex within roughly fourteen years. Each text is brief, but their cumulative testimony is of a deliberate and well-resourced program of patronage. It is this program, and the economy and institutions that supported it, that the remaining sections of the chapter examine.

Section 30.10

Mahāyāna, Esoteric Buddhism, and Royal Teachers

The religious content of the Central Javanese inscriptions points to Mahāyāna Buddhism, the tradition that emphasizes the career of the bodhisattva and honors figures such as Tārā and Mañjuśrī. Both appear in the dated inscriptions of the 770s and 780s. Tārā is a compassionate and protective figure; Mañjuśrī is associated with wisdom and with learning. Their cults were expanding across South Asia and the Buddhist world in these centuries, and Java was participating in that expansion. The sources do not, however, allow a detailed account of doctrine. They record foundations and dedications, not sermons, treatises, or the content of monastic instruction.

Historians have long suggested that Buddhism at the Central Javanese court included esoteric, or tantric, currents, in which ritual practice, consecration, and the guidance of a teacher held special importance. This inference rests on the prominence of consecration ceremonies, on the role of royal teachers, and on the character of later Buddhist art and texts in Java. It should be held with proportion. The inscriptions use the language of consecration and teacher authority, but they do not describe esoteric rites, and the later textual evidence cannot be projected automatically back onto the eighth century. The safest statement is that the court's Buddhism was Mahāyāna in orientation, centered on teachers and ritual, and in contact with the wider Buddhist world.

The royal teacher is the key institutional figure. At Kalasan the teachers are credited with prompting the king; at Kelurak a teacher conducts the consecration. These figures were conduits for ideas, texts, and images. A Buddhist court could not simply order Buddhism into existence; it needed people who knew how to compose Sanskrit verse, install an image, instruct monks, and maintain a monastery. Some of these people may have been Javanese, trained locally. Others may have traveled from Sumatra, the Peninsula, or India, as the Gauḍī-dvīpa origin attributed to Kumāraghoṣa suggests and as later pilgrimage routes confirm. Because monks, pilgrims, and merchants moved along the same sea lanes, the Buddhist world of the Java Sea was a connected system in which Javanese rulers took part as patrons and sponsors, with real ability to shape what they received.

Buddhism at court did not displace other traditions. Śaiva worship continued, and later evidence shows royal support for both. The monastic community was one institution among several competing for royal attention and for the resources of the countryside. The question for students is thus not simply "what did the Śailendras believe?" but "what did royal patronage of a particular tradition accomplish for the court, the religious specialists, and the supporting villages?"

Section 30.11

Rice, Water, Villages, and the Agricultural Foundations of Power

Behind every foundation lay fields. Wet-rice cultivation, in which seedlings are grown in flooded plots that are drained for harvest, was the productive base of Central Java. It requires reliable water, careful leveling, and cooperative labor to build and maintain dikes, channels, and weirs. The streams descending from the volcanoes offered water, and the volcanic soils renewed fertility. Archaeological and later epigraphic evidence shows that Java's cultivators managed water through local means, often village or district arrangements, rather than through a single centralized irrigation bureaucracy. The scale of Central Javanese religious building implies an agricultural surplus, but the evidence does not provide numerical estimates of population or yields, and none should be invented.

Surplus did not mean that everyone ate well. Production was organized through villages whose members owed shares of the harvest and labor to lords, to the court, or to religious institutions. Canggal's praise of Java as rich in grain is conventional court poetry, but it reflects an elite perspective that valued grain as a source of wealth and power. Tax and tribute systems turned part of that grain into support for courts, officials, and monks. The distinction between growth in aggregate wealth and the distribution of that wealth is important here: monumental religious complexes tell us that rulers could mobilize resources, not that cultivators prospered.

The bilingual Karangtengah inscription of 824 helps to illustrate how the agrarian and the religious were connected on the documentary level. As read by editors, the Sanskrit portion celebrates a Buddhist foundation, while the Old Javanese portion deals with practical matters of land and rice fields assigned to support it. Together they reveal a division of linguistic labor: Sanskrit for the sacred and ideological frame, Old Javanese for the specific administrative arrangement. This is more than a literary nicety. It shows that the same text addressed both cosmopolitan religion and the concrete work of supplying a monastery from local land.

Crops other than rice, as well as fishing, livestock, forest products, and craft production, also formed part of village economies, though the inscriptions of this period give little detail. Markets and traders linked villages to courts and to the coast. Metals and prestige goods circulated, and later Old Javanese charters mention payments in gold and silver. The Central Javanese economy was therefore agrarian at its base but not closed: its surplus could be converted into goods and services of the wider world, including the Sanskrit learning, teachers, and objects that the court valued.

Everyday Life and Social History

The People behind Court Patronage

The inscriptions name kings, teachers, and sometimes officials, but the foundations they record depended on people who left no names. Cultivators prepared fields, managed water, planted, weeded, and harvested. Village leaders negotiated the demands of lords and the court, organized shared labor, and spoke for their communities. Artisans and stoneworkers, who in the later monumental phase cut, shaped, and moved large quantities of stone, worked alongside transport laborers and builders, though the inscriptions of Kalasan, Kelurak, and Mañjuśrīgṛha do not describe the labor force. Monks and attendants lived on the produce of assigned villages and relied on lay patrons for food and materials.

A land grant changed the relationship of a village to the powers around it. Dues that had gone to the court or to a local lord might be redirected to a monastery, and villagers might find themselves answering to different officials with different expectations. Whether this was a burden, a benefit, or a mix depended on the terms of the grant and on the conduct of those who administered it, and the sources are almost entirely silent about village reactions. Women, who in later Old Javanese evidence appear as traders, landholders, and participants in village life, and whose labor in agriculture and processing was undoubtedly essential, are rarely visible in these early texts. Historians must therefore use cautious inference from later sources, archaeology, and comparative evidence, and acknowledge the large gaps.

Section 30.12

Land Grants, Tax Rights, and Local Political Authority

The practical mechanism that connected court, monastery, and village is usually called a sīma grant. In the Old Javanese charters that become abundant from the ninth century onward, a sīma designates land or a village whose obligations are modified by royal decree, often by redirecting or exempting dues for a religious or public purpose. The Kalasan gift of a village is a Sanskrit-language example of the same underlying idea, and the Karangtengah inscription links it explicitly to Old Javanese land arrangements.

Historical Vocabulary
Sīma

Definition: In Old Javanese usage, a parcel of land, village, or district whose status is defined by royal grant, typically dedicating its dues or produce to a religious foundation or public work. Historical context: Documented in Old Javanese charters, especially from the ninth and tenth centuries, which record the grant, the boundaries, the beneficiaries, the officials present, and the penalties for violating it. Why it matters: It shows how kings converted agricultural revenue into religious patronage without building a uniform bureaucracy. Potential misunderstanding: It is not private ownership in the modern sense, nor simply a "tax exemption"; the rights transferred were rights to dues and labor, and the village communities remained present.

Land grants worked within a landscape of overlapping authorities. The court could issue commands, but villages had their own leaders and customary arrangements, and local lords bearing titles such as rakai held positions tied to particular places. A grant required the cooperation, or at least the acquiescence, of these local figures. Later charters show officials attending the ceremony, receiving gifts, and being named as witnesses. This implies that political authority was negotiated rather than simply imposed: the king needed local elites to recognize and enforce his decisions, and they in turn gained recognition and status.

For the religious community, a grant provided a durable income and a degree of independence. For the court, it was a way of rewarding teachers and building a base of prestige and loyalty, at the cost of surrendering revenue. For local lords, it could mean reduced income or new competitors, which is one reason why grants might be contested and why rulers sought to bind successors. The entire system should be seen as an evolving practice, not a finished institution. The mature administrative system of tenth-century Mataram belongs to Chapter 32; here the point is that its roots are visible by the late eighth century.

Cause and Effect

Why a Sacred Landscape Became Possible in Central Java

Long-term causes

Fertile volcanic plains, established rice-farming communities, and centuries of participation in maritime exchange and religious networks provided the agricultural and cultural resources on which courts could draw.

Short-term causes

Eighth-century rulers consolidated political authority in the heartland, formed relationships with Buddhist teachers, and developed the practice of assigning village revenue to religious foundations through written grants.

Immediate trigger or context

Royal teachers urged foundations such as the Tārā sanctuary at Kalasan, and the court had both the incentive, in prestige and religious merit, and the means, in revenue and labor, to act.

Event or transformation

A series of Buddhist foundations, documented in 778, 782, and 792, expanded across the Prambanan Plain and eventually involved larger monumental projects elsewhere.

Immediate effects

Villages were assigned to religious institutions, monastic communities gained a stable income, and the court gained religious legitimacy and wider connections.

Long-term consequences

Central Java acquired a durable tradition of royal religious patronage and landholding arrangements that continued, in changing forms, in later centuries.

Historical complexity: No single factor produced this result. Agricultural surplus alone does not create monuments; it requires political organization, religious motivation, and the specific legal practice of the land grant. Conversely, religious enthusiasm without agricultural support would have yielded little. The convergence of these factors is the historical explanation, and the sources allow us to see only part of it.

Section 30.13

One Dynasty or Two? The Sanjaya–Śailendra Debate

The question of how to relate the Śaiva Sanjaya of 732 to the Buddhist Śailendra-associated rulers of the late eighth century has structured the historiography of Central Java for generations. Because the problem is central to the chapter, it is presented here as a formal debate.

Historical Debate

Were the Śailendras and the So-Called Sanjaya Dynasty Really Two Rival Dynasties?

The question: Do the inscriptions of Central Java reveal two distinct ruling dynasties, one Śaiva and associated with Sanjaya, the other Buddhist and associated with the Śailendras, or can the evidence be explained by a single or overlapping Javanese ruling line?

Interpretation A: Two dynasties in competition. In the traditional model, associated with the work of the epigraphist J. G. de Casparis and widely repeated in textbooks, a Śaiva line descended from Sanjaya ruled in Central Java, was eclipsed by a Buddhist Śailendra dynasty during the second half of the eighth century and the early ninth, and reasserted itself in the mid-ninth century. Religion and dynasty are treated as aligned, and political struggle is read into the shift of patronage.
Interpretation B: Alliance, marriage, or alternating dominance. Some scholars accept two lineages but emphasize cooperation: marriage alliances, shared rule in different areas, or alternating dominance, rather than simple conquest. The later union of Pramodhawardhani and Pikatan is read as the most famous example. This view preserves the two-dynasty framework while softening the narrative of conflict.
Interpretation C: One or overlapping Javanese ruling lines. Others, including Anton Zakharov in a widely cited working paper, argue that the evidence can be read without positing a separate Sanjaya dynasty opposed to the Śailendras. On this reading, the Śailendra name designates a prestigious Javanese lineage or title system, and the apparent contrast between Śaiva and Buddhist rulers reflects patronage choices within a shared elite, not the replacement of one house by another.

Evidence: All three positions draw on the same small corpus: Canggal, Kalasan, Kelurak, Karangtengah, the Śivagṛha and Nālandā documents, and the later Mantyasih list. They differ in how they read titles, genealogical phrases, and the silence of the texts.

Points of disagreement: Chiefly interpretation and terminology: whether "Sanjaya dynasty" is a contemporary category or a later construction, whether religion identifies dynasty, and how to equate names across inscriptions and king lists. There is also disagreement over chronology, since the order and dates of reigns between 778 and 850 are partly inferred.

Why it matters: If two rival dynasties existed, Central Java's history is one of religious and political conflict and resolution. If the picture is of overlapping lines and flexible patronage, the history is one of an elite managing several religious traditions at once. The second interpretation fits the evidence of mixed patronage better, though it is not proven.

Student task: Choose two of the three interpretations. For each, identify the strongest piece of evidence supporting it and the evidence it finds hardest to explain. Then suggest what new discovery could help decide between them.

This textbook does not choose one reconstruction as proven. The more important lesson is methodological: a dynastic narrative that appears in many books may rest on a handful of readings, and the historian's task is to show where interpretation begins.

Section 30.14

Reconstructing Succession: Kings, Titles, and the Limits of Genealogy

The Mantyasih charter of 907 is the central text for any attempt to produce a continuous king list. Issued by King Balitung, it enumerates earlier rulers in sequence. In the commonly reported reading, the list begins with Sanjaya, continues with Rakai Panangkaran, and then names a series of rulers including Rakai Panunggalan, Rakai Warak, Rakai Garung, and Rakai Pikatan, before later names leading to Balitung's own time. A slightly different set of names appears in other tenth-century charters, which suggests that even the later tradition was not fully fixed.

The most important of these parallel texts is the Wanua Tengah III inscription of 908 (Śaka 830), issued a year after Mantyasih. As read by its editors, it records additional rulers and succession details beyond those in the Mantyasih sequence, so the two documents cannot simply be merged into one list. Because both were produced under Balitung and for the same royal house, the discrepancies show that even a single court could remember its predecessors in different ways, and any reconstruction of the eighth- and ninth-century sequence must compare both lists, with their published editions, rather than relying on Mantyasih alone.

The list presents several problems. It uses rakai names, which are place-linked titles, while contemporary Sanskrit inscriptions use other names, such as Samaratungga or the names in Śailendra inscriptions. Scholars have proposed numerous equations between the rakai titles in later king lists and Sanskrit royal names in contemporary inscriptions. De Casparis's two-dynasty model generally kept the Mantyasih rakai line distinct from rulers identified as Śailendras, whereas Poerbatjaraka and later scholars proposed various identifications across the two sets of names. None of these equations is independently secure, and all depend on assumptions about how titles worked. If any is wrong, the dynastic sequence changes.

A second problem concerns purpose. A king issuing a charter in 907 had reasons to emphasize a line of legitimate predecessors, and to omit branches that had lost out. A genealogy can be a political argument. It may also telescope time: a list of eight or nine reigns could cover a century or more with gaps, co-rulers, and contests that the text does not mention. Third, the list's silence on the Śailendras as a named dynasty, in the form in which the contemporary inscriptions describe them, is itself a puzzle. It could mean that the Śailendra name was a title or a branch within the same remembered line, or that later tradition chose to forget it.

The responsible conclusion is that the sequence of rulers in Central Java between 732 and 850 can be sketched but not fixed. What can be affirmed is that Javanese rulers of the period used shifting names and titles, that later courts remembered a line beginning with Sanjaya, and that contemporary inscriptions confirm the existence of Buddhist royal patronage associated with Śailendra teachers in the 770s and 780s. How these strands join remains a matter of reasoned inference.

Section 30.15

Samaratungga, Pramodhawardhani, and the Buddhist Court of the Early Ninth Century

The next dated document is the Karangtengah inscription, also called the Kayumwungan inscription, of Śaka 746, or 824 CE. It is bilingual, with Sanskrit and Old Javanese portions, and it provides the clearest view of the royal family of the early ninth century. In the reading of editors, the Sanskrit part names a king, Samaratungga, and his daughter Pramodhawardhani, and records that she consecrated a Buddhist sanctuary of the kind called a jinālaya, a dwelling of the Buddhas. The Old Javanese portion addresses the land arrangements supporting a foundation and mentions an official or lord who took part in the donation.

Several points follow. First, a woman of the royal family appears as a patron of a religious foundation in her own right, which is consistent with the broader evidence that elite women in Southeast Asian societies could hold prominent roles in religious patronage and property. Her status is nonetheless known only through texts that serve the dynasty's self-presentation. Second, the pairing of Sanskrit and Old Javanese confirms the pattern already seen: ideological language in one register and administrative substance in another. Third, the inscription connects the royal family, Buddhist patronage, and local officials in a single act, and so confirms that the system of foundations and land grants initiated in the late eighth century was still operating in the 820s.

The Karangtengah evidence belongs to the wider setting of patronage that includes the great Buddhist monument of the Kedu Plain, Borobudur. Many scholars place its principal construction in the late eighth and early ninth centuries and associate it with the same royal milieu, but this chapter does not discuss its architecture, reliefs, symbolism, or construction history, all of which belong to Chapter 31. For present purposes, the monument matters as an indicator of the scale of resources and organization that Central Javanese courts could command.

Pramodhawardhani is a figure of enduring interest because she is linked in later reconstructions to political transition. An inscription associated with the title Śrī Kahulunnan, traditionally identified by de Casparis with Pramodhawardhani, appears in the mid-ninth century, though the title has also been interpreted as "queen mother" and the personal identification remains debated, and related Buddhist sanctuaries such as Plaosan are traditionally connected with her and her husband. The next section examines how historians have used this evidence.

Section 30.16

Pramodhawardhani, Rakai Pikatan, and the Problem of Political Transition

A frequently repeated narrative holds that Pramodhawardhani, a Buddhist Śailendra princess, married Rakai Pikatan, a Śaiva prince of the Sanjaya line, and thereby reconciled two rival dynasties, after which political power passed to the Śaiva side and the Śailendras faded from Java. The story has an appealing symmetry, which is partly why it persists. It is also a reconstruction, and its components should be examined separately.

What the evidence supports is the following. A ruler called Rakai Pikatan appears in the Mantyasih list and in other tenth-century memory as a king of the mid-ninth century. Inscriptions and monuments connect a figure called Kahulunnan, whose identification with Pramodhawardhani is debated, and a lord associated with Pikatan with Buddhist and related foundations, and the Plaosan complex, with its Buddhist character, is conventionally linked with them. The Śivagṛha inscription of 856, a Śaiva foundation, shows royal patronage of Śaiva worship in the following period, and it describes conflict connected with a fortified position. De Casparis read a damaged passage as vālaputra and identified it with Bālaputradeva, which led many scholars to conclude that Bālaputra, a Śailendra heir, was defeated by Pikatan's side and fled to Sumatra. That reading has been challenged on re-examination of the stone and should not be treated as secure.

What the evidence does not clearly show is that Pikatan and Pramodhawardhani belonged to rival dynasties, that their marriage was a political settlement, or that Buddhism declined as a result. If one accepts Interpretation C of the previous debate, the pairing may instead reflect the union of different branches of an interconnected Javanese elite, with a continuing pattern of mixed patronage, in which Buddhist and Śaiva foundations flourished side by side. If one accepts the traditional model, the marriage becomes a significant political event but one whose motives and terms are unattested.

The larger point is that religious affiliation and political lineage were more complicated than a Buddhist-versus-Hindu civil war model suggests. Kings and royal women patronized multiple traditions. Temples of different religions appear in close proximity on the Prambanan Plain. The mid-ninth century is better described as a period of changing patterns of patronage and political realignment than as the replacement of one religion or dynasty by another. The Śaiva monumental program that followed, and its consequences for Mataram's institutions, belong to Chapter 32.

Multiple Perspectives

A Royal Land Grant to a Buddhist Foundation

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives, based on what inscriptions and comparative evidence allow. They are not quotations from any named historical actor, and the sources preserve the first two most fully.

The ruler and court: A grant secures religious merit, rewards influential teachers, and displays piety and generosity. It also binds a monastic community and a village to the court, and it creates a durable institution that may outlast any particular reign, though at the cost of revenue.
The Buddhist teachers and monks: The grant provides a stable livelihood, material security, and independence from the shifting favor of any one patron, and it allows the community to devote itself to ritual, learning, and teaching. It also creates obligations to the donor and to the memory of the foundation.
The local lord, rakai: A lord whose income or authority had depended on the village might lose revenue or face a new neighbor with royal backing. He might, alternatively, gain status by taking part in the ceremony or by securing a role as official or witness, depending on his relationship to the court.
The village cultivators: Their daily obligations of labor and produce might continue largely as before, merely directed to a different recipient, or they might change in ways that increased or lightened the burden. The sources do not say. The cultivators had little say in the decision, though their cooperation was essential to its success.
A Śaiva religious community: Royal support for Buddhism did not necessarily threaten other communities, since inscriptions show that kings supported several traditions. But competition for royal attention and resources may have been real, and shifts in patronage could matter to those who depended on it.
Section 30.17

Bālaputradeva, Java, Sumatra, and the Wider Maritime World

Chapter 29 introduced the Nālandā copper-plate charter, issued by the Pāla king Devapāladeva of eastern India in the mid-ninth century, in connection with Srivijaya's Buddhist world. Here the same document is read from the Central Javanese side. The charter records a request from Bālaputradeva, king of Suvarṇadvīpa, "the island of gold," to endow a monastery he had built at Nālandā, one of the great Buddhist centers of South Asia. In the usual reading, the text identifies him as the son of a king of the Śailendra lineage named Samaragrāvīra and of a mother described as a daughter of a ruler named Dharmasetu. If Samaragrāvīra is identified with a Javanese ruler of the Śailendra name, as many scholars have proposed, then Bālaputradeva was a member of the Javanese royal family who came to rule in Sumatra or in a wider Suvarṇadvīpa.

This identification, combined with de Casparis's reading of a damaged passage in the Śivagṛha inscription as vālaputra, creates the familiar story: a Śailendra prince, defeated in Java, took refuge in Sumatra and became its king. Each step in that story is plausible, and none is certain. The reading vālaputra at Śivagṛha has been challenged, so the supposed defeat in Java lacks secure epigraphic support; nor is the equation of Samaragrāvīra with a particular Javanese ruler, nor the claim that he was exiled instead of simply being heir to a lineage with branches in several places. A Javanese prince could have inherited a Sumatran throne through marriage or alliance, and a Śailendra could have ruled in Sumatra from the start.

For the study of Central Java, the question is what Bālaputra reveals about elite connections. Even if his career is only partly understood, the charter shows that a royal figure with a Śailendra pedigree was in contact with the Pāla court and with Nālandā at a time when that monastery was a center of Buddhist learning and pilgrimage. It confirms that Southeast Asian rulers could act as patrons of Indian monasteries, reversing the usual image of one-way influence, and that dynastic claims could be expressed across the Bay of Bengal. It also suggests that the Śailendra name had prestige beyond Java.

Connections with the Malay Peninsula also matter. The Ligor stele uses Śailendra royal language on its undated Face B (Face A is dated 775), and the Peninsula's transpeninsular routes, discussed in Chapter 26, linked the Java Sea and the Bay of Bengal. Whether the Śailendras of Java had a branch on the Peninsula, borrowed a prestigious title, or were linked to a Peninsula-based family remains unresolved. The safest view is that the Java, Sumatra, and Peninsula zones formed a maritime Buddhist world of related elites, in which titles, teachers, and marriages crossed water easily, and in which no single center, not Srivijaya, not Central Java, can be treated as simply dominant or dependent.

Section 30.18

From Śailendra Court to Sacred Landscape: Central Java by the Mid-Ninth Century

By the middle of the ninth century, Central Java possessed several features that distinguish it among the societies studied in this volume. It had productive agrarian foundations in the Kedu and Prambanan plains. It had courts able to mobilize revenue and labor through land grants and local officials. It had sophisticated Buddhist and Śaiva patronage, supported by teachers who may have come from many places. It had political institutions that were evolving in ways documented more fully in later charters. It had connections across maritime Asia, visible in the use of Sanskrit and Old Malay, in the possible presence of foreign teachers, and in the Nālandā charter. And it had an increasingly monumental sacred landscape.

The chapter's main argument can now be stated concisely. The political history of the Śailendra period is much less certain than conventional narratives suggest, and the story of rival Sanjaya and Śailendra dynasties is a hypothesis, not a fact. The material basis of the period, in contrast, is clearer: rice-growing plains, village communities, and the mechanism of the land grant made religious foundations possible. And the cultural and religious world of the courts was neither isolated nor derivative: it participated in a Buddhist network spanning the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea and adapted it to Javanese conditions.

This chapter has explained the political, economic, and religious world that could produce Borobudur. Chapter 31 explains Borobudur itself, as architecture, religious monument, political statement, and pilgrimage environment. Chapter 32 turns to the later developments hinted at here, including Pramodhawardhani and Pikatan, changing patterns of patronage, the Śaiva monumental program at Prambanan, the institutions of Mataram, and the eventual shift of political power toward East Java.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Patterns of Royal Patronage in Central Java, 732–c. 850

What Changed

  • The documented focus of patronage shifted from the Śaiva liṅga foundation of 732 to a cluster of Buddhist foundations in the 770s–790s
  • Languages of record broadened from Sanskrit alone to Sanskrit, Old Malay, and Old Javanese
  • Land grants became more systematically documented, with practical administrative detail appearing in Old Javanese
  • Royal women such as Pramodhawardhani appear as visible patrons

What Stayed the Same

  • The agrarian base of rice-growing plains and village communities
  • The use of Sanskrit and of land grants to express and secure royal piety
  • The coexistence of Śaiva and Buddhist traditions, rather than a simple sequence of Hindu, then Buddhist, then Hindu rule
  • The importance of teachers and maritime contacts in bringing ideas to the court

Why the pattern matters: A simple sequence of "Hindu Sanjaya, Buddhist Śailendra, Hindu Sanjaya" is not supported by the evidence. Canggal, Kalasan, Kelurak, Śivagṛha, and later texts instead suggest overlapping religious traditions and changing patterns of patronage, with change gradual, uneven, and concentrated at the court level, and continuity greatest among the cultivators whose labor sustained everything.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 30 examined the Central Javanese courts of the eighth and early ninth centuries, focusing on the Śailendras, Buddhist patronage, agricultural foundations, and maritime connections. Its central argument is that Śailendra-era Central Java cannot be reduced to a story of a Buddhist dynasty displacing or fighting a Śaiva Sanjaya dynasty. The inscriptions reveal powerful rulers, royal teachers, local officials, land grants, and increasingly ambitious sacred landscapes, but the relations among Sanjaya, Panangkaran, the Śailendras, later Central Javanese rulers, and Srivijaya remain debated.

The chapter began with geography. The volcanic plains of Kedu and Prambanan, watered by the Progo and Opak systems, supported intensive rice cultivation and provided the economic basis for courts. Their position, with routes toward Java's northern coast, kept them connected to the maritime world. Environmental conditions created possibilities and hazards but did not determine political outcomes.

The evidence was then examined critically. Contemporary inscriptions, surviving monuments, and later king lists such as the Mantyasih charter of 907 are different kinds of sources with different limitations. The dynastic narrative is a scholarly reconstruction in which dated anchors are few, and matching names across inscriptions and lists requires interpretation.

The Canggal inscription of 732 introduced Sanjaya, a Śaiva king who installed a liṅga and whose realm was praised for rice and gold. The Śailendra name appears in later inscriptions from Java and the Malay Peninsula, and scholars have proposed Indian, Funanese, Sumatran, Peninsular, and Javanese origins. The question remains open, with a Javanese origin regarded as plausible. The relationship between Sanjaya and Panangkaran, the ruler of Kalasan, is likewise uncertain.

The Kalasan inscription of 778 is the chapter's principal source. It records a Tārā sanctuary, a monastery for monks, and the gift of a village, at the urging of the royal gurus of the Śailendra king or kings, with named officials acting for the king. It shows religion, kingship, landholding, and patronage working together, and it implies a system of land assignment and local enforcement. The Kelurak inscription of 782 and the Mañjuśrīgṛha inscription of 792 extend the pattern, revealing a developing Buddhist sacred landscape, a learned teacher from Gauḍī-dvīpa (Bengal), and a multilingual court culture.

Buddhism at court was Mahāyāna in orientation and focused on figures such as Tārā and Mañjuśrī, with teachers as key mediators. Inferences about esoteric practice must remain proportional to the evidence. Buddhism did not displace Śaiva worship, and royal patronage supported multiple traditions.

Agricultural foundations were treated as indispensable. Wet-rice cultivation, village communities, and the mechanism of the sīma grant allowed rulers to convert agricultural revenue into religious institutions. The bilingual Karangtengah inscription of 824 illustrates the division between Sanskrit religious frame and Old Javanese land provision. The people who produced the surplus, cultivators, artisans, laborers, and village leaders, are largely unnamed, but their work is the precondition of every foundation.

The historical debate over one dynasty or two was presented through three interpretations: rival dynasties, alliance or alternating dominance, and one or overlapping Javanese lines. The chapter did not choose among them as proven, but showed why the second and third fit the evidence of mixed patronage. The story of Pramodhawardhani and Pikatan reconciling two dynasties was presented as a reconstruction.

Bālaputradeva and the Nālandā charter showed Central Javanese elites, or elites claiming Śailendra descent, acting across the maritime world as patrons of Indian monasteries. Connections with the Peninsula and Sumatra indicate a network of related elites with no single dominant center.

The chapter closed by handing the story to its neighbors. It has explained the political, economic, and religious world that could produce Borobudur, which Chapter 31 will examine, and it prepared the way for Chapter 32's treatment of Prambanan, Mataram institutions, and the shift toward East Java.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 30 matters because it shows both the achievement and the fragility of early Southeast Asian history. The achievement is evident in the courts of Central Java, which combined agrarian strength with learned religious patronage and wide maritime connections. The fragility lies in the evidence: a handful of inscriptions and a retrospective list bear the weight of a dynastic narrative that is often presented with more confidence than the sources permit.

The chapter also reveals a larger process, the conversion of agricultural production into religious and political authority, which recurs throughout this volume and the next. The same logic of land, labor, and patronage underlies Angkor's temples in Volume III and Pagan's foundations. Central Java offers one of the earliest and best-documented examples of how a rice-growing society could sustain monumental religious life, and it shows that such a society could be fully engaged with distant Buddhist worlds.

Finally, the chapter illustrates why the textbook insists on historical humility. Religious affiliation did not map neatly onto dynasty; names and titles shifted; elites moved across seas. The tidy story of Buddhist and Śaiva rivals gives way to a more complicated picture of overlapping patronage and changing alliances. That picture sets the stage for Chapter 31, which turns to the monument most closely associated with this world, and for Chapter 32, which follows the courts of Central Java into their next phase.
