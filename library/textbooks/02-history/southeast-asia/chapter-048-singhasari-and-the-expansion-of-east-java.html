Singhasari and the Expansion of East Java

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Singhasari lasted, on the conventional reckoning, a little over seventy years, yet it left behind a copper-plate charter that rewrote its own royal genealogy, a Buddhist statue shipped across the sea to Sumatra, a Chinese envoy returned with a marked face, and a body of temple sculpture still studied today. It was neither a mere prelude to Majapahit nor an inevitable empire, but a kingdom whose ruler tried to turn East Javanese kingship into a regional political project.

In 1255 CE (Śaka 1177), a set of engraved copper plates was issued in East Java that recorded grants of land and privilege, named officials and royal relatives, and recited the lineage of the ruling house. The plates, known today as the Mūla-Malurung inscription, were recovered in modern times and have become one of the most important documents for the history of thirteenth-century Java. Their importance lies partly in what they record and partly in what they contradict. For generations, historians had reconstructed the early rulers of Singhasari from a later chronicle, the Pararaton, which presents a tidy and dramatic sequence of five kings and a chain of murders. A close reading of this charter suggests that the dynasty's early decades were more complicated, with more rulers, overlapping authorities, and a political world less orderly than the chronicle's narrative implies.

This chapter studies the kingdom that emerged in eastern Java in the decades after 1222, when a warlord from the district of Tumapel overthrew the last ruler of the older Kediri kingdom, until 1292, when Kertanagara, the dynasty's most ambitious king, was killed in a revolt led by a Kediri prince. Its primary emphasis lies on the years from 1222 to 1292, though it draws on the political world of the preceding Kediri period. The kingdom's name, Singhasari, is itself part of the historical problem: modern specialists, notably Hadi Sidomulyo, have argued that the name was not necessarily the original contemporary designation of the realm founded in 1222, and that it gained importance in connection with a reorganization of royal authority around the middle of the 1250s. Throughout the chapter, therefore, "Singhasari" is used as a convenient scholarly label for a polity whose names and boundaries shifted.

Historians approach this kingdom through unusually uneven evidence. The Pararaton and the Deśawarṇana (commonly known as the Nagarakretagama) are literary works composed generations after the events they describe, in the courts and traditions of later Majapahit. Contemporary inscriptions, such as the Mūla-Malurung charter of 1255, the Wurare inscription of 1289, and the Padang Roco inscription of 1286, are far closer to the events but record only particular acts of royal patronage, grant, or dedication. The surviving temples and sculptures of the Malang region provide a third kind of evidence, material and iconographic, whose attribution to specific reigns is often debated. Chinese dynastic records add an outside view of one dramatic episode, the encounter between Kertanagara and the envoy of Kublai Khan. The chapter's task is to show students how these different kinds of evidence are weighed against one another.

The chapter's central historical question is: How did Singhasari transform East Javanese kingship into a wider regional political project, and why did the ambitions of Kertanagara both strengthen the kingdom's influence and expose its internal vulnerabilities? Answering it requires attention to the dynasty's difficult origins, its institutions and agrarian base, the religious program through which Kertanagara sought to elevate royal authority, and the maritime diplomacy and military enterprises that extended the court's reach toward Bali and Sumatra. It also requires attention to the opposition within East Java that the king never fully neutralized, and to the international environment, shaped by the expansion of the Mongol-ruled Yuan dynasty, in which his final decisions were made.

The chapter follows the commitment announced in Chapter 1 that Southeast Asian societies were historical actors rather than passive intermediaries between larger neighbors. The Singhasari court selected, combined, and reshaped Śaiva and Buddhist traditions that had reached Java centuries earlier; it pursued its own commercial and political interests in the Java Sea and Malacca Strait regions; and it decided, on its own calculations, how to answer an imperial demand from China. At the same time, the chapter avoids teleology. Singhasari was not a stage on a predetermined road to Majapahit, and its fall in 1292 was not inevitable. Contemporaries could not know that a Kediri revival would overthrow their king, or that a son-in-law of the dead ruler would later found a successor realm.

The chapter also takes care not to claim more than the evidence supports. Claims that Kertanagara created a Javanese "empire" in the Indonesian archipelago, found in much older writing, depend on later literary lists of dependencies and on interpretations of a handful of expeditions whose results are poorly documented. A more cautious reading distinguishes between military expeditions, diplomatic influence, ritual prestige, and enduring administrative control. These distinctions recur throughout Division IV.

The chapter has five divisions of four sections each. Division I examines the sources and the dynasty's origins. Division II follows succession, administration, and society. Division III is devoted to Kertanagara's kingship, religion, and sacred art. Division IV treats maritime expansion, and Division V examines the Yuan confrontation, the revolt of 1292, and the dynasty's legacy. It builds on Chapter 32's account of the movement of Javanese power to the east and on Chapter 47's treatment of Srivijaya's transformation, which considered some of the same events from Sumatra. It leaves the Yuan expedition of 1293 and the foundation of Majapahit to Chapter 49, and the comparative military analysis of Mongol campaigns to Chapter 46.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish contemporary inscriptions, later court literature, genealogical tradition, and modern scholarly reconstruction as sources for Singhasari history.
  2. Explain the political, agricultural, and commercial geography of the Brantas basin and why Tumapel became a base for a new dynasty.
  3. Reconstruct the events conventionally dated to 1222 while separating a probable historical core from later narrative embellishment.
  4. Analyze how the Mūla-Malurung charter of 1255 corrects the royal succession preserved in the Pararaton.
  5. Describe the institutions, landholding, labor, and everyday life of thirteenth-century East Java, distinguishing documented practice from reasonable inference.
  6. Interpret Kertanagara's Śaiva-Buddhist kingship and explain why "syncretism" requires qualification.
  7. Evaluate the Wurare inscription and the monuments of Jago, Jawi, and Singosari as evidence for royal ideology and commemoration.
  8. Assess the aims, chronology, and limits of the Pamalayu expedition, the Bali campaign, and the Amoghapāśa gift to Dharmasraya.
  9. Explain why Kertanagara refused the Yuan demand and why the incident of 1289 matters.
  10. Analyze the fall of Singhasari as the product of several interacting causes rather than a single military absence.
  11. Connect Singhasari's achievements and weaknesses to the circumstances that preceded Majapahit without treating its fall as inevitable.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. Why did a founder of obscure origins need elaborate stories of destiny, marriage, and divine favor to claim royal legitimacy?
  3. What material foundations, in rice land, rivers, villages, and ports, allowed the kings of East Java to sustain courts, temples, and armies?

  8. Why should the fall of Singhasari be explained by domestic politics as well as by overseas commitments?

Historical Context

The kingdom studied in this chapter arose in a Java whose political center had shifted decisively eastward. As Chapter 32 explained, the great central Javanese centers of Borobudur and Prambanan were succeeded, from the tenth century, by courts in the eastern river basins, where rulers drew on the productive lowlands of the Brantas valley. Later tradition associates the eleventh-century king Airlangga with the consolidation of this eastern realm and with its division between two successors, one centered on Janggala and the other on Panjalu, the polity commonly called Kediri. This division is known chiefly through later tradition and its historicity and form are debated, but the names Janggala and Kediri remained powerful political labels in the thirteenth century and appear in Singhasari-era sources.

Kediri, between the late eleventh and the early thirteenth centuries, presided over a rich literary and religious culture. Old Javanese poetic composition, the kakawin tradition, flourished at its court, and the Bharatayuddha, composed in the 1150s, is among its best-known products. Chinese writers of the twelfth and early thirteenth centuries, including Zhou Qufei in 1178 and Zhao Rugua in 1225, described Java as a wealthy land engaged in the spice and pepper trade and said to have overseas dependencies. These descriptions reflect Chinese interests and hearsay, but they confirm that eastern Java was visible in the wider commercial world. Kediri's authority over its provinces was nevertheless uneven, and local lords, such as the ruler of Tumapel, held positions that could become independent bases of power.

Beyond Java lay a maritime world in transition. Chapter 47 examined the transformation of Srivijaya, whose Palembang-centered authority had been shaken by Chola attacks in the eleventh century and whose commercial center of gravity moved northward along the Sumatran coast toward Jambi and the upper Batang Hari, where the polity called Malayu, with a center at Dharmasraya, rose to prominence. Java's rulers had long traded with and competed against these Sumatran centers. The Strait of Malacca and the Sunda Strait, the Java Sea, and the sea lanes to the eastern archipelago formed the commercial space in which Singhasari's regional ambitions developed.

Finally, the thirteenth century brought a new political force into Asian waters. The Mongol conquest of China culminated in the establishment of the Yuan dynasty in 1271 and the fall of the Southern Song in 1279. Kublai Khan's court then sought tribute and submission from the states to China's south, sending embassies and, in several cases, armies against Champa, Dai Viet, and Pagan, campaigns whose comparative military course is analyzed in Chapter 46. News of these events traveled along the same commercial routes that linked Java to Chinese ports. Kertanagara's decisions in the 1280s were made in a world in which the Yuan court's reach was a live strategic concern, and they must be understood within it.

Section 48.1

Reconstructing Singhasari: Inscriptions, the Pararaton, and the Deśawarṇana

No chapter in this textbook so clearly shows how the character of the evidence shapes the history it can tell. The history of Singhasari rests on a small number of sources of very different kinds, and a student who treats them as interchangeable will misunderstand the period. The oldest and closest to the events are inscriptions, including copper-plate charters such as the Mūla-Malurung inscription of 1255, stone and statue-base inscriptions such as the Wurare inscription of 1289 and the Padang Roco inscription of 1286, and a later inscription of 1351 that commemorates Kertanagara. These documents were produced for specific purposes, to record a grant, dedicate an image, or commemorate a ruler, and they say little about anything outside those purposes. But they were produced by participants or by institutions close to them, and they carry dates in the Śaka era, which begins in 78 CE and which modern scholars convert to the Common Era by adding 78 years.

The two great literary sources are much later. The Deśawarṇana, usually called the Nagarakretagama, is a long Old Javanese poem composed by the court poet Mpu Prapañca and completed in 1365 during the reign of Hayam Wuruk of Majapahit. It praises the Majapahit king and includes a genealogy of the ruling house from the founder Rajasa through the Singhasari kings to Majapahit's own rulers, together with accounts of Kertanagara's reign and of the temples where deceased kings were commemorated. The poem was rediscovered in the late nineteenth century on the island of Lombok and became the foundation of modern understandings of Majapahit. The Pararaton, the "Book of Kings," is a prose and verse work in Middle Javanese whose date of composition is uncertain, though it is generally placed some generations after Majapahit's peak, and it survives in later manuscript copies. It contains the famous story of Ken Arok and a narrative of the Singhasari and early Majapahit kings, but it mixes dated historical notices with legend, folk tale, and moral commentary.

Chinese sources provide a third perspective. The Yuan Shi, the dynastic history of the Yuan, was compiled in 1369–1370 under the Ming dynasty from earlier records. It describes Yuan relations with Java and records the incident of Meng Qi. These texts reflect the interests of an imperial court that wished to record tribute, submission, and defiance, and they use Chinese renderings of Javanese names that must be matched cautiously with Javanese sources. Archaeological remains, above all the temples of the Malang region, form a fourth body of evidence.

The central lesson of modern research is that these sources cannot simply be harmonized. The Pararaton and Deśawarṇana agree on many points but differ on others, and both were shaped by the political needs of a later dynasty that traced its descent from the Singhasari kings. The Mūla-Malurung charter has shown that the early royal succession preserved in later literature omits or reorders rulers. Hadi Sidomulyo's study of the charter, From Kuṭa Rāja to Singhasāri (2010), demonstrated that the early dynasty was more complex than the conventional narrative suggests, and that the capital's earlier name appears to have been Kuṭa Rāja. The consequence for students is practical. Where this chapter gives a date or sequence drawn only from the later literature, it says so, and where a contemporary inscription contradicts that literature, the inscription is given priority for the matters it actually records.

Source Method
Weighing the Pararaton, the Deśawarṇana, and the Inscriptions

Contemporary documents. Inscriptions are nearest to the events, but each serves a narrow purpose. Their strength is that their dates and named persons were produced at the time; their weakness is silence on most matters of interest, and a copper plate may record a ruler's claims rather than neutral fact.

Later court literature. The Deśawarṇana is a work of praise composed for Majapahit, and it presents Singhasari as the prelude to its patron's dynasty. Its genealogy and temple list are valuable, but it was not written to settle disputes about early succession. Its silences, such as its failure to mention the Meng Qi incident, are also informative.

Chronicle and tradition. The Pararaton preserves vivid narratives, many of which function as moral or dynastic stories. Some twentieth-century scholars, such as the Dutch philologist C. C. Berg, argued that Javanese chronicles should be read as ritual-literary compositions rather than as records of events; others have defended the use of their dated notices where corroborated. A prudent approach treats its dates and episodes as claims to be tested.

Foreign records. Chinese histories are valuable as independent outside testimony, but they record what reached the imperial court and were compiled later.

Section 48.2

Kediri, Tumapel, and the Political Geography of Eastern Java before 1222

The political world inherited by the founder of Singhasari was organized around the Brantas River. The Brantas rises on the slopes of the volcanic highlands near modern Malang, flows west and north through the plains of Blitar and Kediri, turns eastward across the lowlands of Jombang and Mojokerto, and splits into branches in a delta that reaches the Madura Strait near modern Surabaya. Its valley contains some of Java's most productive rice land, renewed by volcanic ash and river silt, and it formed a natural corridor between the inland plains and the sea. Earlier eastern Javanese rulers had invested in dams and channels along it, and inscriptions from the eleventh century show royal concern for the regulation of river crossings and the maintenance of an embankment in the delta.

Kediri, whose court lay in the middle Brantas, had been the dominant authority in the twelfth century, but its power was not uniform across the basin. The kingdom was a composite of districts governed by local lords who recognized the king's ritual primacy, provided tribute and service, and often retained their own followings. Tumapel, in the upper Brantas basin near modern Singosari, was one of these districts. Later literature calls its governor an akuwu, a regional official, and presents it as a subordinate territory of Kediri, though the actual degree of subordination is impossible to measure from the surviving sources. What matters is that Tumapel lay at the southern edge of the Kediri world, in highland-fringe country with fertile slopes, access to upland products, and routes leading north toward the delta and the coast.

Religious institutions also shaped political geography. Hermitages, Śaiva and Buddhist foundations, and the endowed religious communities that inscriptions record held land and attracted donors, and they linked rulers, brahmans, monks, and villages. The later story of Kertajaya's conflict with brahmans, taken up in section 48.3, reflects the political weight of such communities even if its details are legendary. The roads and rivers connecting the inland plains to coastal ports such as those of the delta and the northern coast carried rice, pepper, cloth, and forest products toward merchants who traded across the Java Sea, a subject developed in section 48.13.

Section 48.3

Ken Arok, the Defeat of Kertajaya, and the Foundation of the Rajasa Dynasty in 1222

The conventional account of the dynasty's origin is the story of Ken Arok, also written Ken Angrok. In the Pararaton, he is a child of obscure and miraculous parentage, abandoned and raised by a thief, who becomes a gambler and bandit before entering the service of Tunggul Ametung, the akuwu of Tumapel. Through intrigue and murder, he displaces Tunggul Ametung, takes the governor's wife, Ken Dedes, and becomes ruler of Tumapel. These episodes follow a narrative pattern, the rise of a low-born hero to kingship through trickery and divine favor, familiar from many traditions and clearly meant to dramatize legitimacy as much as to record events.

The historical core is thinner, but it can be stated with reasonable confidence. A ruler arose in Tumapel in the early thirteenth century who challenged the Kediri king, then called Kertajaya in the literature and also remembered as Dandang Gendis. Both the Pararaton and the Deśawarṇana date the decisive battle to 1222 (Śaka 1144) and place it at a site called Ganter. Kertajaya was defeated, and the former governor of Tumapel became the principal power in eastern Java, taking the royal title Śrī Rājasa Sang Amurwabhumi, from which the dynasty takes the label Rajasa. The Deśawarṇana presents him in elevated terms, calling him an epithet usually understood as "son of the Lord of the Mountain," a reference to Śiva, which reveals how the court later chose to remember its founder.

The later accounts add a political explanation. The Pararaton says that Kertajaya antagonized the brahmans, who fled to Ken Arok for protection, and that the king arrogantly claimed that even the gods would bow to him. Historians do not take the dialogue as record, but the story may preserve a memory that religious communities, whose lands and ritual standing mattered to royal authority, were among the dissatisfied constituencies of Kediri. Such a view must remain an inference. It is equally likely that the story was constructed afterward to justify the overthrow of a legitimate king.

Equally important is what the battle of 1222 did not do. The defeat of Kertajaya ended his rule but did not eliminate the claims of the Kediri line or the loyalties of its supporters. Kediri remained a place of political memory and, as later sections show, the descendants of its kings remained in the eastern landscape. The new rulers had to deal with this reality by incorporating Kediri into their realm, by installing family members there, and by watching a rival center that never entirely disappeared. The same tension reappears in the revolt of 1292, when a Kediri prince overthrew Kertanagara, and it explains why the overthrow of one dynasty by another in 1222 cannot be treated as a clean break.

Section 48.4

Ken Dedes, Dynastic Marriage, and the Construction of Royal Legitimacy

No figure in the dynasty's founding tradition is more important symbolically than Ken Dedes. The Pararaton presents her as the daughter of a brahman, Mpu Parwa, taken by Tunggul Ametung and later married to Ken Arok. When Ken Arok sees her, the story says, a radiance reveals that the man who marries her will become a universal ruler, a prophecy that motivates his seizure of her. In the same narrative, she is pregnant by Tunggul Ametung when she marries Ken Arok, and her son Anusapati is thus born to one man and raised as the child of another, a detail that shapes the later tradition of murder and vengeance. The Deśawarṇana refers to her in terms of the divine feminine, which fits the common royal practice of presenting the queen as an embodiment of auspicious power.

The historical significance of these stories lies in what they tell historians about legitimacy rather than in their literal truth. A ruler who had risen from a subordinate district and defeated an established king needed a language of right. Marriage to a brahman's daughter connected the new dynasty to religious learning and sacred lineage. Prophecy and divine favor presented the founder's rise as destined. Descent from Ken Dedes and, through other wives, from Ken Arok, created several lines of potential claimants, which is why marriage alliances and descent became so politically charged in the next generations. The genealogy in the Pararaton distinguishes the children of Ken Dedes from those of Ken Arok's other wife, Ken Umang, and the later rivalry between these branches of the family is a recurrent theme.

Students should distinguish carefully between this tradition and independently established events. The famous kris made by the smith Mpu Gandring, said to have been cursed and used in a chain of killings, belongs to the narrative logic of the Pararaton, in which a single weapon links the dynasty's violence from founder to successors. No contemporary record establishes its existence. The same applies to supernatural signs and prophecies. What can be said is that later court storytellers, working in the fourteenth century and afterward, shaped the founder's biography to answer questions that mattered to their own time: why the dynasty ruled, why it was stained by violence, and how rulers from different branches could claim the same inheritance.

One further point of terminology deserves attention. The name Singhasari was not necessarily the original designation of the realm founded in 1222. Sidomulyo argues that the capital area was earlier known as Kuṭa Rāja and that the name Singhasari became important in connection with the reorganization of royal authority around 1254. The later literature, written when Singhasari was a standard name, applied it backward. Using it for the entire period from 1222 is therefore a scholarly convenience, not a statement about what contemporaries called their kingdom.

Historical Debate

Ken Arok and the Reliability of Dynastic Tradition

The question: How much of the story of Ken Arok, Ken Dedes, and the founding of the dynasty reflects real events, and how much is a later literary construction designed to legitimize a ruling house?

Interpretation A — a historical core with legendary elaboration: Some historians treat the Pararaton as preserving a genuine core: an ambitious figure from Tumapel, a conflict with Kediri, and a battle in 1222. Legendary embellishments such as the prophecy and the kris are viewed as decoration around a recoverable sequence.
Interpretation B — dynastic story as literary and ritual composition: Others, following the tradition associated with Berg, emphasize that Javanese chronicles often shape narratives according to patterns of kingship, destiny, and cosmic order. On this view the story's details cannot be used as events, and even its framework may reflect later concerns.
Interpretation C — a selective, evidence-based approach: Many specialists adopt a middle course. They accept those points corroborated by independent evidence, such as the 1222 date appearing in more than one source, treat the biography's folkloric elements as evidence for how the dynasty was imagined, and test the rest against inscriptions.

Evidence: The 1222 date appears in both major literary sources, but both are late. The Mūla-Malurung charter shows that the Pararaton's later succession cannot be accepted uncritically, which increases caution about its treatment of earlier events.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns method and interpretation more than any single date: whether late chronicles can be used for events at all, and what counts as corroboration.

Why it matters: How historians treat the founding narrative affects whether the dynasty's violence is seen as historical fact, political memory, or literary design, and whether the "Rajasa dynasty" is treated as a stable family line or as a later construct.

Student task: Choose one episode from the Ken Arok story and list what independent evidence would be needed to treat it as history. Then explain what the episode might reveal about the later court's priorities even if it were not literally true.

Division II

Dynastic Consolidation, Political Institutions, and Society

Division II follows the dynasty through succession crises and consolidation, then turns to the administrative and material world of thirteenth-century East Java.

Section 48.5

Anusapati, Tohjaya, and the Struggles over Succession

The Pararaton narrates the years after Ken Arok as a sequence of killings. In that account, Anusapati, son of Ken Dedes, learned that Ken Arok had killed Tunggul Ametung and arranged an assassination involving the cursed kris; Anusapati then ruled until Tohjaya, Ken Arok's son by Ken Umang, killed him. The tradition has Tohjaya displaced by a movement supporting Ranggawuni and Mahisa Campaka, who subsequently shared power, with Ranggawuni assuming the royal name Wisnuwardhana. The chronologies of the later texts differ: the Pararaton places Ken Arok's death in about 1247 and makes Anusapati's reign very short, whereas the Deśawarṇana places Ken Arok's death in 1227 and Anusapati's in 1248. Neither literary sequence should be presented as an uncontested contemporary record, and the intervening succession is further complicated by the 1255 Mūla-Malurung charter.

The Deśawarṇana is quieter on these killings. It gives Anusapati's death as 1248 (Śaka 1170) and refers to a commemorative temple at Kidal, still standing, and it offers a genealogy that moves smoothly from Rajasa to Wisnuwardhana. Its silence may reflect a court poet's reluctance to emphasize violence in the ancestry of his patrons, or it may reflect different sources. The temple of Candi Kidal, associated by tradition with Anusapati, shows that his memory was sufficiently honored to be commemorated, which sits uneasily with the Pararaton's picture of an accursed murderer.

The Mūla-Malurung inscription complicates the picture further. As discussed in section 48.7, specialist readings of the charter indicate that the sequence of rulers between Anusapati and Wisnuwardhana was more complicated than the five-king list of the later chronicle. The charter refers to rulers whose names and relationships do not fit neatly into that list, including a king named Guningbhaya, and it suggests overlapping authority rather than a single line of orderly succession. The interpretation of individual names remains a subject of specialist discussion, and students should not treat any single reconstruction as settled. The safest statement is that the early dynasty experienced more reigns, more shared authority, and more political experimentation than the simplified list allows.

What the evidence does support is a picture of a dynasty struggling to stabilize succession. A ruler's death opened competition among branches of the family descending from different wives of the founder, each with its own followers, territorial bases, and religious patrons. Later tradition turned this competition into a moral drama of vengeance. A historian can instead ask what institutional conditions made succession so unstable: the absence of fixed rules of inheritance, the importance of descent through several lines, the personal nature of political loyalty, and the existence of rival centers such as Kediri. The remarkable fact is not that succession was contested but that the dynasty survived the contest.

Section 48.6

Wisnuwardhana, Narasinghamurti, and the Reunification of Royal Authority

The settlement associated with Wisnuwardhana, the name taken by Ranggawuni, and his cousin Narasinghamurti, known earlier as Mahisa Campaka, ended the period of fragmentation. Their power-sharing is described in the Pararaton in a vivid image of two rulers living in harmony, and the Deśawarṇana praises their partnership. The two came from the two principal lines of the family, Wisnuwardhana from Anusapati's descendants and Narasinghamurti from the line of Mahisa Wonga Teleng, a son of Ken Arok and Ken Dedes. Their union therefore reconciled branches that had been in conflict and gave the dynasty its first extended period of internal stability.

Around 1250 Wisnuwardhana emerged as the senior figure. His consolidation involved a reorganization of the territories that had formed the Kediri world. The later literature and the Wurare inscription treat Kediri and Janggala as formerly separate realms that were brought together under the dynasty. In 1254 (Śaka 1176) Wisnuwardhana's son Kertanagara was installed in the Kediri sphere, an act that put a member of the ruling family at the center of the old rival kingdom. This is the context in which the name Singhasari becomes prominent, as Sidomulyo argues, since the reorganization of royal authority needed a name for the unified realm.

Whether this arrangement was a true dual kingship is debatable. The Pararaton's image of a pair suggests co-rule, and the Deśawarṇana uses language of shared sovereignty. Yet the surviving records show Wisnuwardhana in the senior position, and the junior partner's role is better described as a senior royal authority with a defined sphere than as an equal throne. Historians therefore speak of a close collaboration between senior relatives, in which Wisnuwardhana held primacy, rather than a formal institution of two kings. Narasinghamurti's death in 1269, a year after Wisnuwardhana's, removed the last senior member of that generation and left the realm to Kertanagara.

The achievement of these decades was to make the dynasty durable. By placing the heir in Kediri, they converted a rival center into a dynastic appanage, and by sharing authority between family branches they reduced the incentive for violent competition. The arrangement also created a pattern with later consequences: royal authority in East Java was exercised through relatives installed in regional centers, a pattern that provided stability but depended on the loyalty of those relatives and on the continued acceptance of Kediri's subordination. That dependence proved vulnerable under Kertanagara.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Kediri to Singhasari

What Changed

  • The dynasty: a warlord from Tumapel replaced the Kediri line in 1222, introducing a new royal house with its own legitimating stories
  • The political center: authority shifted from the middle Brantas to the upper basin near Tumapel and later Singhasari, while Kediri became a dependent sphere
  • The structure of authority: from the 1250s the dynasty placed relatives in regional centers, including Kediri itself
  • Royal ideology: by Kertanagara's reign, kingship acquired a more elaborate Śaiva-Buddhist program

What Stayed the Same

  • The agrarian base in the Brantas lowlands and the villages that supported courts and temples
  • The practice of royal grants, sīma status, and religious endowments recorded in inscriptions
  • The use of Old Javanese and Sanskrit as languages of court and religion
  • The memory and political weight of Kediri, which persisted as a rival center

When and how: Change was abrupt at the level of the dynasty in 1222 but gradual and uneven in institutions. Courtiers, brahmans, and village communities experienced continuity in their daily obligations, while royal relatives and regional lords felt the changes in authority most directly.

Why the pattern matters: It cautions against reading 1222 as a clean break or the Singhasari state as wholly new. The new dynasty ruled a familiar landscape through adapted institutions.

Section 48.7

The Mūla-Malurung Inscription of 1255: Royal Grants, Officials, and Provincial Authority

The Mūla-Malurung inscription illustrates how a surviving administrative document can revise a historical chronology built from later literature. The copper-plate charter, dated Śaka 1177 (1255 CE), confirms a land grant from the senior ruler Narāryya Smining Rāt (Wisnuwardhana) to his official Prāṇarāja involving the villages of Mūla and Malurung. The sanction of regional royal figures headed by Narāryya Mūrddhaja, the young Kertanagara, was part of the charter's political setting. It is consequently inaccurate to describe Kertanagara without qualification as the sole original grantor. The text records grant and privilege, royal kinship, named officeholders, and formulas of authority: a legal instrument that is also a statement of how power was distributed. The identification of senior grantor, recipient, and participating regional rulers follows Hadi Sidomulyo's analysis of the charter (2010, especially pp. 80–83 and Appendix B).

Its value to historians is multiple. First, the charter's genealogy does not reproduce the simplified succession in the Pararaton: it names Guningbhaya and also records Mahisa Wonga Teleng (Mahisa Wong Ateleng) as a figure important to the earlier lineage, so that Tohjaya and other claimants must be placed in a more complex history. The precise order and role of individual figures remain debated. Second, it maps a distribution of regional authority: Kertanagara is identified with Daha, Wisnuwardhana retains senior authority, and other named relatives hold posts or kingships across East Java. Third, the record supplies administrative vocabulary—grant recipients, privileges, the status of villages and the ranks of royal subordinates—rather than a continuous account of warfare.

Important cautions apply. A charter issued by a ruler records the ruler's version of relationships, and a lineage recited in it is selective. Its list of names does not necessarily include every ruler, and the reading of damaged or difficult passages is still refined by specialists. The inscription also concerns a limited set of villages and says nothing directly about wider taxation, warfare, or commerce. It should be treated as a precious but partial window.

The charter also repays attention as a document of procedure. Grants of this kind were not merely announced; they were witnessed, recorded, and protected by formulas that invoked both royal power and supernatural sanction. The presence of named officials and relatives among those mentioned shows that a grant involved the consent, or at least the acknowledgment, of the people through whom authority flowed. For students, the charter is a reminder that the state of this period is visible mainly through the paperwork of patronage, and that the people who dug, planted, and paid dues on the lands in question remain largely outside the text.

Its most striking contribution is methodological. Before its study, the Pararaton list of kings had often been used as a skeleton on which other evidence was hung. The charter showed that a document produced within a few years of the events could not be reconciled with that skeleton, and it therefore shifted the burden of proof. Historians now ask not how the inscription can be fitted to the chronicle but what the chronicle's compilers did with a more complicated reality, whether through simplification, omission, or deliberate shaping of the dynasty's memory.

Section 48.8

Agriculture, Landholding, Villages, Trade, and Everyday Life in Singhasari

The material foundation of the kingdom was irrigated rice. Villages in the Brantas lowlands and upland valleys cultivated wet rice using channels, dikes, and embankments, supplemented by dry-field crops, fruit trees, livestock, fishing, and forest products. The fertile volcanic soils allowed dense settlement, and the seasonal rhythm of the monsoon governed planting and harvest. Rulers drew on this base through a mixture of tax, labor service, and tribute, but the surviving evidence rarely allows a precise reconstruction of rates or administrative machinery. Historians infer the general pattern from inscriptions of this and earlier periods, which refer to officials who assessed and collected royal revenue and to categories of dues and duties.

The land itself was held in several ways. Some was subject to ordinary royal claims; some was granted to temples, hermitages, or individuals under privileged status; and village communities exercised customary control over cultivated areas. The grant of special status, discussed in the vocabulary box below, redirected taxes and sometimes labor from the king to a religious foundation, which made such grants an instrument of both piety and political patronage. Religious endowments thus formed a significant economic institution, holding land, receiving produce, and employing specialists in ritual, maintenance, and craft.

Trade connected inland production to the coast. Rice, pepper, cloth, salt, metalwork, and forest products moved along river routes and roads to market centers and ports. Merchants, both local and foreign, handled long-distance trade, and artisans such as smiths, potters, weavers, and stone and metal workers served courts, temples, and households. Chinese copper coin was circulating in Java in these centuries alongside local measured silver and gold, though the scale and timing of monetization are still debated. Women appear in the inscriptions of this and earlier periods as landholders, donors, and participants in markets, which suggests that they could hold and manage property, though evidence for the thirteenth century is thin and later chapters on Majapahit society develop the point further.

This section is deliberately cautious. No eyewitness account describes a Singhasari village or market, and the picture here is assembled from inscriptions, from the better-documented periods before and after, and from archaeological and environmental evidence. Statements about everyday life should therefore be read as reasoned reconstruction, not as testimony.

The environment imposed both opportunities and risks. Volcanic ash refreshed soils but eruptions, such as those of volcanoes in the Brantas region, could damage fields, and floods on the river and in the delta could disrupt irrigation works. Maintaining dams, channels, and embankments required cooperation among villages and, at times, royal authority, which is one reason inscriptions of the region show concern for water management. Historians should avoid environmental determinism: the same landscape supported many political arrangements, and the evidence does not show that irrigation alone produced a strong state.

Historical Vocabulary
Sīma (royal grants and court administration)

Definition: Sīma is the Old Javanese term for land or a village whose ordinary obligations to the king were redirected or exempted by royal decree, usually to support a religious foundation or reward a person or community.

Historical context: Charters such as the Mūla-Malurung inscription record the creation or confirmation of such status. They name the grantee, the lands, the privileges, the officials who witnessed it, and the curses against violators. The system relied on a hierarchy of court and provincial officials who assessed dues, enforced rights, and recorded decisions.

Why it matters: Sīma grants show how kingship worked through patronage and negotiation as much as command, linking royal authority to temples, villages, and elites.

Potential misunderstanding: A sīma grant was not a transfer of sovereignty, and "tax-free" is too simple, since obligations were redirected rather than abolished.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Villages, Merchants, and Artisans

Most people in Singhasari's world lived in rice-growing villages and worked as cultivators, though households also fished, tended animals, wove, and gathered forest products. Village communities negotiated their obligations with local officials, and the people living on sīma land might find their dues directed to a nearby monastery or temple. Religious specialists, including brahmans, Buddhist monks, and ascetics, formed another social stratum, supported by endowments. Merchants and artisans were concentrated at courts, market towns, and ports, where foreign traders, including Chinese and others, came for Javanese products.

Because surviving texts were produced by courts and religious institutions, ordinary farmers, women, laborers, and unfree people appear only indirectly, as names in charters, categories of dues, or silent workers behind monuments. Historians combine inscriptions with archaeology, environmental evidence, and comparison with better-recorded periods to draw careful conclusions.

Division III

Kertanagara and the Transformation of Royal Kingship

Division III is the center of the chapter. It examines how Kertanagara built on the settlement of his father's generation, developed a distinctive religious program, and expressed it in inscriptions and monuments.

Section 48.9

Kertanagara's Rise and the Expansion of Royal Ambition, c. 1254–1292

Kertanagara's career had more than one stage, and separating them is essential. Around 1254 he was installed as a junior royal figure in the Kediri/Daha sphere during his father Wisnuwardhana's senior reign. The Mūla-Malurung charter of 1255 places him at the head of participating regional rulers, but the original grant it confirms belonged to Wisnuwardhana's authority. According to the Deśawarṇana (canto 41.4), Kertanagara succeeded in Singhasari following his father's death in 1268. His first known independent inscription, the Sarwadharma charter, is dated 31 October 1269. The poem associates the year 1270 not with a formal accession but with Kertanagara's defeat of an opponent called Cayarāja (canto 41.5). These are distinct stages: junior appointment, succession, an independent royal act, and a dated military achievement. They should not be collapsed into a single coronation in 1270.

His reign as independent ruler lasted until 1292 and involved sustained efforts to concentrate and extend royal authority. The Deśawarṇana attributes the defeat of Cayarāja to 1270, while the Pararaton and later traditions also describe changes among senior ministers and the posting of Arya Wiraraja to Madura. The reasons for these decisions, and the detailed circumstances of the conflict, cannot be independently reconstructed from the surviving record. Together, the dated poem and the qualified later narratives suggest a king who intervened actively in internal administration and political alignments. Wiraraja's subsequent role in the story of Raden Wijaya, examined in Chapter 49, illustrates how consequences extended beyond Kertanagara's reign.

Marriage and kinship were tools of this policy. Kertanagara had daughters who were married to relatives, including Raden Wijaya, and the dynasty continued its practice of tying regional leaders to the court through marriage. Later tradition also records that the son of Jayakatwang, the Kediri prince who would overthrow him, was married to one of his daughters, a connection discussed in section 48.18. Such alliances could bind elites but could also place rivals inside the royal family, with access to its secrets and a claim on its inheritance.

Equally important was the ambition that extended beyond East Java. Kertanagara's court developed a view of its role that was larger than that of earlier Brantas kings, one that combined religious authority, diplomatic initiative, and naval and military enterprise. The expeditions toward Sumatra and Bali, the dispatch of the Amoghapāśa image, and the confrontation with the Yuan emissary are examined in later sections. What distinguishes his program is not any single act but the coordination of ritual, diplomacy, and force into an attempt to define a sphere of Javanese preeminence. Historians debate how far this sphere was real, but the ambition itself is clear in the sources, and it set Kertanagara apart from the more locally oriented rulers who preceded him.

It is useful to ask who bore the costs and who gained from this ambition. Courtiers and religious specialists benefited from patronage and prestige; shipowners and merchants might profit from state-supported ventures; and communities near ports and along rivers may have supplied labor, provisions, and sailors. The sources rarely record these burdens directly, but expeditions and monumental building both required mobilization of people and resources, and a king who did both was drawing heavily on the productive base described in section 48.8. Whether that mobilization was welcomed or resented is not recorded, yet the reign's end suggests that it did not create a broad base of loyalty beyond the court and its clients.

Section 48.10

Śaiva-Buddhist Kingship and Tantric Religious Experimentation

The religious world of thirteenth-century Java included Śaiva and Buddhist communities that had coexisted for centuries, as Chapters 30 through 32 showed. Brahmans served kings as ritual specialists and counselors; Buddhist monks and teachers maintained their own institutions; and ascetic and tantric practitioners cultivated esoteric rituals that emphasized initiation, the manipulation of sacred power, and the identification of the practitioner with a deity. Kertanagara's reign saw these traditions drawn together in royal ideology with unusual intensity.

The Deśawarṇana portrays him as learned in religious texts and devoted to Buddhist tantric practice, and describes him as a king who studied and promoted esoteric teachings. The Pararaton adds a more hostile image, in which he and his ministers are engaged in drinking at the moment of the final attack. Whether the story records a tantric ritual that outsiders found scandalous, a moralizing invention, or a mixture is uncertain. What both traditions share is the picture of a king whose religious practice was central to his identity and, in the eyes of later authors, to his fate.

The common label for this religious program is syncretism, the merging of Śiva and Buddha worship, and the dynasty's monuments and texts do indeed associate the two. Yet the label requires qualification. Śaivism and Buddhism did not dissolve into a single religion; practitioners and institutions of each remained distinct, and sources continue to distinguish them. Thomas M. Hunter, in The Body of the King (2007), argued that the Śiva-Buddha symbolism of Kertanagara's court should be understood partly as an ambitious political-religious project rather than simply as the merger of two faiths. On this view the king's own consecrated body, identified with transcendent figures through ritual, became the point at which the two traditions were joined, and the project served to place the ruler above existing religious and political divisions.

That reading has important implications. If the program was partly political, it was addressed to audiences: regional lords, brahmans, Buddhist communities, and foreign rulers who received images and inscriptions. It could unify those groups under a common sacred authority, but it could also alienate those who regarded the king's claims as excessive or who supported rival centers. The religious program thus belongs to the same story as his political ambition, since it aimed to raise royal authority beyond the networks of kinship and patronage on which the dynasty had depended. Its success in doing so is open to question, but the effort left a lasting mark on the art and ritual of eastern Java.

Section 48.11

The Wurare Inscription of 1289: Royal Authority, Religious Legitimacy, and Political Memory

The Wurare inscription, dated 1289 (Śaka 1211), appears on the pedestal of a stone image known today as Joko Dolog, now displayed in Surabaya. The nineteen Sanskrit verses are written in Kawi script. Its opening praises Buddhist wisdom and introduces the sage Bharāḍa; verses 5–6 recall the earlier division of Java, verses 7–9 celebrate Wisnuwardhana's reunification of the land, and verses 10–14 present Kertanagara, called Jñānaśivavajra, as the king who installed an image associated with Bharāḍa and modeled on Mahākṣobhya at Wurare. This is more precise than saying merely that Kertanagara consecrated an image of himself. The interpretation of the figure's identity and the relation between priest, Buddha image and king has changed across scholarly editions; the text itself must be the starting point (Arlo Griffiths, DHARMA edition of Wurare, verses 3–14).

The inscription is a political statement as well as a religious dedication. It recalls a landscape divided into Janggala and Panjalu, then celebrates Wisnuwardhana's protection and reunification of that land (verses 5–9). Kertanagara's Buddhist titles, his pious installation, and the aspiration to benefit the royal family and all beings (verses 10–17) bring kingship, religious prestige and a history of reunification into one composition. The assertion of unity is a court claim, not proof that every regional lineage had permanently accepted the ruler: Kediri remained an important rival political center.

Later interpretation has added layers to the text. Some modern writers have inferred from the inscription that Kertanagara considered himself a Buddha, or that he fused Buddhist and Śaiva identities, while others stress that the surviving language concerns his consecration and association with a Buddha figure without establishing a doctrine of the king's identity. The Deśawarṇana, writing much later, describes his posthumous commemoration in terms of a Śiva-Buddha identity, which has encouraged retrospective readings. Careful historians separate what the inscription itself states from what later tradition said about the king, and they treat his religious identity as a question to be argued from several sources.

Section 48.12

Candi Jago, Candi Jawi, and Candi Singosari: Sacred Art and Royal Commemoration

Three temple complexes dominate the physical record of Singhasari royal commemoration. A candi in this context is a Hindu-Buddhist sanctuary, and in the case of royal monuments it could serve as a memorial where a deceased king was ritually honored in the form of a deity. The Deśawarṇana lists the sites at which Singhasari rulers were commemorated, and modern scholars have matched several of them with surviving structures, though the matching is not always certain.

Candi Jago, east of Malang near Tumpang, is associated in the Deśawarṇana with the commemoration of Wisnuwardhana at a place called Jajaghu, and the poem credits Kertanagara with the work. Its structure is a terraced base of several levels with a reduced upper sanctuary, and its walls carry narrative relief panels depicting stories from Buddhist and Hindu literature. Because the monument was later maintained or modified during the Majapahit period, its present form cannot be assigned wholly to the thirteenth century.

Candi Jawi, on the slopes of the Penanggungan massif in Pasuruan, is linked by the Deśawarṇana to Kertanagara's commemoration in a Śiva-Buddha form. Its slender tower, in which Buddhist and Śaiva features are combined, is often cited as the architectural counterpart of the king's religious program. The monument is known to have been damaged and restored, so its surviving fabric reflects more than one period.

Candi Singosari, near Malang, is a tower sanctuary with a rich sculptural program of niche figures, including images of Durga, Ganesha, and Agastya, and many of its sculptures were removed in the colonial period. It is commonly associated with Kertanagara's posthumous commemoration, and the Indonesian National Museum's study of the monument and specialist research on Javanese sculpture offer the main foundations for its interpretation. Parts of the temple appear unfinished, and its exact attribution and completion date are debated.

The sculpture of the period shows connections beyond Java. The iconography of the Buddhist figures associated with Kertanagara's reign, including the cult of Amoghapāśa discussed in section 48.16, has parallels in the art of eastern India, and Buddhist teachers and texts moved along maritime routes. Javanese sculptors worked these influences into a distinctive style rather than copying foreign models. A celebrated stone figure of Prajñāpāramitā, associated with the Singosari area and now held by the National Museum of Indonesia in Jakarta (inventory 17774), following its return from the Netherlands in 1978, is the best-known example of the school and is popularly linked with Ken Dedes, an identification that is not secure.

The commemorative practice that these monuments represent deserves emphasis. According to the Deśawarṇana, a deceased king could be honored at more than one site and in more than one form, with a Śaiva aspect at one shrine and a Buddhist aspect at another, and the poem describes ritual performed for departed kings in the Majapahit period as well. The funerary temple thus linked the dead ruler to a deity, the living dynasty to its ancestors, and the surrounding villages, which supplied the labor and offerings that sustained the cult, to royal religion. The practice also explains why later rulers took an interest in restoring and expanding such sites, and why the surviving monuments often bear the marks of several periods.

Division IV

Regional Expansion and Maritime Southeast Asia

Division IV turns from the court to the sea. It examines the economic basis of Kertanagara's regional strategy and then three episodes: the Pamalayu expedition, the Bali campaign, and the diplomatic gift to Dharmasraya.

Section 48.13

East Java's Maritime Economy and Kertanagara's Regional Strategy

Kertanagara's overseas ambitions grew out of existing economic and political networks rather than appearing as a sudden dream of empire. The Brantas basin produced rice in quantities that allowed its rulers and merchants to supply ships and ports, and the delta and northern coast contained harbors where cargoes were gathered and exchanged. Chinese descriptions from the twelfth and thirteenth centuries show Java as a commercial power, and the famous Arabic-inscribed Leran funerary stone, often dated to 475 AH/1082–83 CE, is evidence of an early Islamic inscription found in the region; its reading, original provenance, and implications for settlement have been debated, and it cannot establish that the deceased was a trader, a theme developed in Chapters 62 through 66.

Javanese ships and merchants carried rice and other goods to ports across the archipelago and brought back spices, aromatic woods, and other products from the eastern islands, while foreign merchants came to the Javanese coast. Kenneth R. Hall and other historians have stressed that East Java's rulers had economic reasons to want a secure position along the routes joining the spice-producing regions, the Java Sea, and the Strait of Malacca, since the profits of this commerce supported courts and temples. In this context Sumatra, where the center of Srivijayan commerce had moved inland and northward, and Bali, adjacent to Java's eastern shore, were natural objects of attention.

The court's relationship to merchants and ports was a matter of patronage, tolls, and alliance more than direct administration. Harbor authorities, port officials, and ships' captains operated within a framework of royal privilege, and rulers benefited from the arrival of cargoes through duties and gifts. Control of a strait or harbor could therefore matter more than control of territory, a principle that applied to Srivijaya, as Chapters 28 and 29 showed, and now to Singhasari.

Eastern Indonesia was a further horizon. The spices and aromatic products of the eastern islands reached Java through intermediary traders, and the northern-coast and delta ports served as collecting points where such goods were combined with Javanese rice and exported onward. The extent of direct Singhasari involvement in this trade is poorly documented, and later claims of dominion over the eastern islands belong to the Majapahit literature. What can be said is that the economic geography of East Java encouraged its rulers to look outward, and that a king who could secure friendly relations with ports and strait polities gained access to revenue and prestige.

This background helps explain the logic, though not the success, of Kertanagara's strategy. An East Javanese king with ambitions to influence trade routes might send an expedition to show strength, support a friendly ruler, dispatch a religious image to cement an alliance, and demand recognition from neighbors. Each of these actions can be read in strategic terms. Whether they added up to a coherent policy, and whether that policy was shaped by anticipation of the Mongol threat described later, remains a matter of interpretation.

Section 48.14

The Campaign against Bali in 1284 and Relations with Neighboring Islands

The reported expedition against Bali in 1284 (Śaka 1206) is known chiefly from the Deśawarṇana, which describes the subjugation of Bali and the capture of its ruler, who was brought to Java, and from the Pararaton, which names a commander and dates the event to the same year. The accounts treat the campaign as a successful assertion of Singhasari power over its eastern neighbor.

Bali had long had relations with Java, and Balinese rulers of earlier centuries issued inscriptions in Old Balinese and in Old Javanese-influenced forms. Java and Bali shared religious and artistic traditions, and Javanese courts regarded the island as within their sphere of cultural and political attention. A military expedition in the 1280s would therefore be an escalation of an existing relationship rather than a first contact.

The limits of the evidence are, however, severe. The accounts are retrospective and written for Majapahit audiences, who had reasons to celebrate earlier Javanese successes, and direct contemporary evidence of the political results of the expedition is limited. A military victory does not by itself establish enduring sovereignty, and the surviving sources do not show Singhasari installing a durable administration in Bali. The later history of the island under Majapahit, which belongs to Chapters 50 and 51, involved its own campaigns and arrangements, which suggests that any Singhasari success was not permanent.

Relations with other neighboring islands, including Madura, rested on kinship and appointment, as the placement of Wiraraja suggests, while more distant places named in later lists of Javanese dependencies are harder to evaluate. The Deśawarṇana's catalogue of subject lands reflects the claims of the Majapahit court in the fourteenth century and cannot be projected back onto Kertanagara's reign without corroboration. Scholars who work on Bali's inscriptions have also noted that the Balinese record for the later thirteenth century is sparse, which makes it difficult to test the Javanese claim against local evidence and is a reminder that the island's own rulers and communities are largely absent from the narrative. A raid or campaign could remove a ruler and demand submission without leaving a trace in Balinese documents, or it could have had a smaller effect than Javanese authors implied; the surviving sources do not allow a firm choice. The safest conclusion is that Kertanagara's court projected power eastward and that its reach was episodic, dependent on the success of particular expeditions and personal ties.

Section 48.15

The Pamalayu Expedition of 1275 and the Expansion of Javanese Influence toward Sumatra

The Pamalayu, a name usually translated as the expedition to Malayu, is dated by the Pararaton to 1275 (Śaka 1197). It was directed toward Sumatra, where the polity of Malayu centered on Dharmasraya in the upper Batang Hari valley had risen as Srivijaya's earlier Palembang-centered system transformed, as Chapter 47 described. The Pararaton names a commander and links the enterprise to the later story of two Malay princesses brought to Java, though that part of the tradition concerns events after 1292 and belongs to Chapter 49.

Its objectives are not stated in surviving contemporary documents. Historians have suggested that it aimed to establish Javanese influence in a region commercially vital to the Strait of Malacca, to counter or draw in a rising Sumatran power, to secure access to products of the Sumatran interior such as gold and pepper, or to present Javanese strength to a wider audience. The chronology is also uncertain. It is unclear whether the Pamalayu was a single campaign or a series of operations, and the later tradition places its conclusion only in the 1290s.

The most reliable evidence of the relationship between Java and Malayu in these years is not the campaign itself but the Padang Roco inscription of 1286, which shows cordial diplomatic and religious exchange, as section 48.16 explains. That evidence fits poorly with a picture of straightforward annexation and suggests a more complex relationship in which force, diplomacy, and religious gift all had roles.

The most useful way to evaluate the expedition is to compare the kinds of evidence that survive. The Deśawarṇana supplies a date for a Javanese initiative toward Malayu in 1275, but its praise-poem format does not give a naval itinerary, an order of battle, a surrender agreement or a sequence of garrisons. Later narrative traditions give the episode additional actors and military coloring, yet their distance from the events requires source criticism. By contrast, the 1286 Padang Roco inscription records a specific act in which Kertanagara's court dispatched an Amoghapāśa image to Dharmasraya for a Malayu ruler. The different source types answer different questions. A later military narrative is evidence for how Java remembered outward expansion; the dated inscription is evidence for an acknowledged royal relationship and a particular diplomatic-religious exchange.

There is also an important chronological gap. Eleven years separate the conventional date of the expedition from the inscription. It is tempting to fill that interval with continuous Javanese military occupation, but the sources do not authorize such a reconstruction. The expedition might have opened a negotiation, signaled strength to merchants using the straits, supported one claimant in a local struggle, or helped establish cooperation among independent courts. These possibilities should be tested rather than asserted. For a river-centered polity such as Malayu, whose rulers had their own economic interests, receiving a prestigious Buddhist image need not imply loss of autonomy. Diplomatic gifts could affirm hierarchy, reciprocity, affinity or some combination of them; the available evidence does not allow us to identify a single, stable constitutional relationship.

What Did Pamalayu Accomplish?

The question: Was the Pamalayu a conquest that brought Sumatra under Javanese control, a show of force supporting diplomatic alliance, or something else?

Section 48.16

The Padang Roco Inscription of 1286: Diplomacy, Buddhist Patronage, and Malayu–Dharmasraya

The Padang Roco inscription, dated 1286 (Śaka 1208), is carved on the pedestal of a statue of Amoghapāśa, a form of the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara, found in the upper Batang Hari region of Sumatra. A scholarly edition by Arlo Griffiths establishes that the text was composed in Old Malay and Sanskrit and written in the Kawi script, a correction that should be carried forward consistently from Chapter 47. The use of a script associated with Java for a text in Old Malay on a Sumatran site is itself significant evidence of cultural exchange.

The inscription records, in broad terms, that Kertanagara sent the image from Java to the land of Dharmasraya in Sumatra, and that the people of Malayu, and above all their ruler, received it with joy. It names the ruler as Tribhuwanaraja Mauliwarmadewa. Chapter 47 considered this document from Srivijaya's standpoint; here the focus is Javanese diplomacy.

As evidence, the inscription demonstrates that the Singhasari court had relations of exchange and religious patronage with a Sumatran ruler. It shows that Kertanagara used Buddhist images as instruments of diplomacy and that the Malay ruler accepted and commemorated the gift. It does not demonstrate that Java administered Malayu directly. The rhetoric of joyful reception could coexist with subordination, equality, or alliance, and the text, composed for an audience in the receiving court, does not specify the political terms.

The historical significance of the gift is considerable even under this cautious reading. It reveals that thirteenth-century Javanese kingship included a Buddhist dimension capable of reaching across the sea, that Malayu was a significant partner, and that political relations in the Indonesian archipelago were conducted partly through religious prestige. It supports the view that the Pamalayu, whatever its military content, was accompanied by a diplomacy of gift and alliance. The text also shows how a Sumatran court could take part in Javanese religious networks on its own terms.

The inscription also invites reflection on the position of Dharmasraya. Malayu's ruler appears in the text as an addressee who rejoiced at the gift, and the mention of his full name and title shows that he was a recognized sovereign, not a nameless dependent. Historians have asked whether the gift reflected a relationship of alliance among rulers who shared Buddhist commitments, a Javanese claim to precedence, or a calculated offer to a partner whose cooperation was valuable. The text does not choose among these, and the later history of Malayu, which falls beyond this chapter, shows that its rulers continued to pursue their own course.

Historical Vocabulary
Amoghapāśa and Royal Buddhist Patronage

Definition: Amoghapāśa ("unfailing noose") is a form of the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara, the embodiment of compassion in Mahāyāna Buddhism, commonly shown with several arms and attendant figures.

Historical context: The cult appears in Buddhist art connected to Indian, and then Southeast Asian, traditions, and in Kertanagara's reign an Amoghapāśa image became a vehicle of diplomacy, as the Padang Roco inscription records. Royal patronage of images and monasteries gave rulers religious prestige.

Why it matters: The image links Java and Sumatra in a shared Buddhist world and shows religion operating as political communication.

Potential misunderstanding: The gift of a Buddhist image should not be read automatically as a mark of conquest or subordination.

Division V

Yuan Confrontation, Domestic Crisis, and the Fall of Singhasari

Division V examines the encounter with the Yuan court, the opposition within East Java, the revolt that ended Kertanagara's reign, and the dynasty's legacy.

Section 48.17

Kublai Khan, Yuan Diplomacy, and the Meng Qi Incident of 1289

The Yuan court under Kublai Khan pursued a policy toward maritime Asia that combined diplomacy, tribute demands, and force. After the conquest of the Southern Song, the court sent envoys to the states of Southeast Asia and the Indian Ocean, seeking acknowledgment of Yuan supremacy and access to commerce, and it sent armies against Champa, Dai Viet, and Pagan when diplomacy failed, campaigns compared in Chapter 46. To Chinese officials, a ruler who received a Yuan envoy and sent tribute accepted a place within a universal order, and refusal was treated as defiance.

Java was part of this pattern. Chinese records indicate that the Yuan court's dealings with Java extended through the 1280s, and the Yuan Shi reports that in 1289 Kublai sent an envoy named Meng Qi to demand Java's submission. According to that account, Kertanagara refused and mistreated the envoy by marking his face, a punishment recorded as branding or tattooing, and sent him back. The Chinese record is the earliest and most reliable account of the incident, and later retellings that add details of mutilation or elaborate insult should be treated as embellishment. The Deśawarṇana does not mention the episode, a silence that may reflect the poem's focus on praise, or the later court's preference not to dwell on an event that preceded a Yuan invasion.

Why did Kertanagara refuse? The sources do not record his reasoning, so historians infer it. A king who had just consecrated himself in a Buddhist ritual that elevated his status, who had extended influence abroad, and who regarded Java as a sphere of his own authority might have regarded the demand as intolerable. He may also have calculated that the sea separated Java from China, that earlier Yuan expeditions to Southeast Asia had encountered severe difficulties, and that tribute would damage his claims at home. Marking an envoy's face was a deliberate act with a known meaning, and it communicated rejection in terms that both courts understood.

The consequences were far-reaching but delayed. The Yuan court ordered an expedition, which sailed in 1292–1293 and arrived after Kertanagara's death. That expedition, and its entanglement in the politics of East Java, belongs to Chapter 49, and its military course was compared with other Mongol campaigns in Chapter 46. For this chapter, the immediate point is that the decision of 1289 placed Singhasari at odds with the most powerful empire in Asia, and that it did so while the dynasty's domestic position remained unsettled.

Section 48.18

Opposition within East Java: Kediri, Jayakatwang, and the Vulnerabilities of Royal Power

The principal threat to Kertanagara came from within the Brantas world. Jayakatwang, a prince of the old Kediri line, held power at Gelang-Gelang and belonged to a political tradition that could claim authority older than Singhasari. The family relationship was not merely a late chronicle motif. The Mūla-Malurung charter (plate 7r, lines 4–6 in the DHARMA edition) names Turuk Bali, a daughter of Wisnuwardhana and therefore Kertanagara's sister, as consort of Jayakatwang. Later traditions also connect Jayakatwang's son Ardharāja with a daughter of Kertanagara. These distinct marriage connections tie the rival houses together across generations and illustrate the limits of dynastic marriage as a guarantee of political loyalty: a relative by marriage could also lead an armed challenge.

The vulnerabilities of royal power had accumulated over decades. Kediri was a political center with its own traditions of kingship, local loyalties, and religious institutions. The placement of Kertanagara himself in Kediri in 1254 had converted it into a dependency, but it had not erased the memory of independence. Local lords who resented the new dynasty or who had been displaced by Kertanagara's reshaping of the court, as the traditions about his ministers suggest, had incentives to look to Daha. A king who emphasized exalted religious authority and who pursued ambitious external projects might also have strained relations with elites who saw little benefit in those projects.

Older writing often explains the collapse by saying that the army was absent on foreign expeditions, leaving the capital undefended. The evidence does not support this as a sufficient explanation. The Pamalayu belonged to 1275 and its end is uncertain, and no source establishes that a large force was abroad at the moment of Jayakatwang's attack. Nor does absence of troops explain why a Kediri prince chose to rebel or why members of the royal family and officials did not defend the king. The domestic political conflict therefore requires its own explanation, based on the persistent rivalry between Kediri and the Singhasari center.

Geography reinforced the rivalry. Daha lay on the middle Brantas, downstream from the Singhasari heartland in the upland basin, so that the river that had made the dynasty's rise possible also connected it to its rival. Each center drew on its own agricultural districts, its own religious foundations, and its own networks of lords, and a rebellion from Kediri could mobilize a substantial base without crossing hostile territory. The distribution of political power across the basin thus made a challenge from the old center feasible in a way that a challenge from a peripheral region might not have been.

At the same time, the consequences of Kertanagara's overseas commitments should not be dismissed. Expeditions required ships, men, and resources, and a king preoccupied with distant enterprises and with confrontation with the Yuan could neglect the fragile loyalties at home. The most defensible view is that overseas ambition and domestic vulnerability were connected through the king's attention and the court's resources, but that neither alone caused the revolt.

Section 48.19

The Revolt of 1292 and the Death of Kertanagara

The Pararaton gives the fullest narrative of the attack. According to it, Jayakatwang sent a force from the north as a diversion and a stronger force from the south against the capital. Kertanagara dispatched his son-in-law Raden Wijaya and Jayakatwang's own son Ardharaja to meet the northern force. Ardharaja turned against the court and joined his father, and the southern force reached the palace, where Kertanagara and his ministers were killed, in the Pararaton's account while engaged in drinking. Jayakatwang then held power from Daha, restoring a Kediri-centered kingship, while Raden Wijaya escaped.

The Deśawarṇana tells the story differently. It presents Kertanagara's death in a framework of religious devotion and describes his posthumous commemoration, and it gives the date of his death as Śaka 1214. Its tone is that of reverent memory. The contrast between the Pararaton's image of a revelling king surprised by treachery and the Deśawarṇana's image of a devout ruler shows how differently later authors used the same event. Neither is a contemporary report.

A firmer chronological anchor comes from epigraphy. Hadi Sidomulyo identifies a 1351 inscription that places Kertanagara's death in May–June 1292, which gives the date greater security than a retrospective literary notice. Even so, the details of the fighting, the role of individual commanders, and the sequence of the assault rest on later narrative. The safest statement is that in 1292 a force acting for Jayakatwang took the Singhasari capital, that Kertanagara was killed, and that Kediri's line briefly regained royal power.

The aftermath of the capital's fall is also known mainly from the later literature. Jayakatwang took royal authority and, according to tradition, treated the surviving members of the former court with a mixture of accommodation and suspicion, an uncertain situation that Raden Wijaya later exploited. These events lead directly into the story told in Chapter 49, and for that reason this chapter stops at the point where the dynasty's survivors begin to reorganize.

The attack also illustrates how kinship networks could be turned against a ruler. The defection of Ardharaja, whom the king had trusted with a command, shows how marriage alliances placed potential enemies close to power. It was not a conquest by an outside enemy but the overthrow of one royal branch by another, and the Yuan fleet, which arrived afterward, entered a political situation that had just been transformed.

Cause and Effect

The Fall of Singhasari

Long-term causes

The unresolved rivalry between the Kediri line and the dynasty founded in 1222, the dynasty's reliance on relatives and marriage ties to hold regions, and unstable succession in earlier decades.

Short-term causes

Kertanagara's reshaping of the court and ministers, the strain of overseas ventures, and a religious program that elevated royal claims beyond the networks of kinship.

Immediate trigger or context

The confrontation with the Yuan court in 1289 and Jayakatwang's opportunity at Kediri, with an attack from two directions in 1292.

Event or transformation

The capture of the capital, the death of Kertanagara in May–June 1292, and the restoration of Kediri-centered rule under Jayakatwang.

Immediate effects

The dynasty's survivors, including Raden Wijaya, were displaced, and political authority in eastern Java was reorganized.

Long-term consequences

A successor realm, treated in Chapter 49, traced itself to Singhasari, and the memory of Kertanagara became a major part of later Javanese royal tradition.

Historical complexity: The fall cannot be reduced to a single cause. The absence of troops abroad is not adequate; the loyalty of kin, the memory of Kediri, the king's priorities, and chance in the course of the attack all mattered.

Section 48.20

The Legacy of Singhasari: Dynastic Continuity, Religious Innovation, and the Road to Majapahit

An assessment of Singhasari must balance achievement and weakness. Politically, the dynasty consolidated a realm in the Brantas basin after the overthrow of Kediri, stabilized succession after turbulent early decades, and developed a pattern of relatives governing regions. Ideologically, Kertanagara elaborated a royal program that joined Śaiva and Buddhist traditions in a claim of exalted authority, and the monuments and inscriptions associated with his reign are among the major artistic and epigraphic achievements of medieval Java. In diplomacy and maritime affairs, the court extended its influence toward Bali and Sumatra and engaged in a diplomacy of religious gift.

The structural weaknesses were equally clear. The dynasty never fully integrated Kediri, and its reliance on marriage and appointment gave relatives and regional lords the means to oppose it. The religious program may have raised royal prestige but did not create institutions able to withstand a determined revolt. The overseas enterprises extended influence but did not produce an administration capable of sustaining it, and the confrontation with the Yuan court added a danger that the dynasty's resources could not easily manage.

The legacy passed to the dynasty's survivors. Raden Wijaya, a relative of the royal house through descent and a son-in-law of Kertanagara, escaped the fall of the capital with the support of allies, including Wiraraja in Madura, according to later tradition. His role in the events of 1293, in which Yuan forces, Jayakatwang, and Wijaya himself were all involved, and the foundation of Majapahit belong to Chapter 49. It is enough here to note that the new realm took Singhasari as its ancestor: the Deśawarṇana's genealogy ties Majapahit's kings to the Rajasa line, and Kertanagara's commemoration at Jawi and elsewhere became part of the new dynasty's religious life.

Students should resist reading Singhasari as an unsuccessful draft of Majapahit. The kingdom was a distinct political experiment with its own aims and contingencies, and its fall was the result of specific events. Its importance lies in what it shows about East Javanese kingship in the thirteenth century: that rulers could combine agrarian power, ritual innovation, and maritime diplomacy, and that such combinations were fragile where internal rivalries remained unresolved. The next chapter follows the survivors as they rebuilt a kingdom from the wreckage.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how East Java's Singhasari kingdom emerged from the Kediri world, consolidated authority under the Rajasa dynasty, developed distinctive royal and religious institutions, and expanded its ambitions across maritime Southeast Asia before its overthrow in 1292. Its central question asked how Singhasari transformed East Javanese kingship into a wider regional political project, and why Kertanagara's ambitions both strengthened the kingdom's influence and exposed its internal vulnerabilities.

The chapter began with the sources, which are of unequal kind and date. Contemporary inscriptions, later court literature in the Deśawarṇana and Pararaton, Chinese dynastic records, and archaeological remains must be weighed against one another. The Mūla-Malurung charter of 1255 showed that the conventional royal succession in the Pararaton cannot be treated as uncontested chronology, and that the early dynasty was more complex than later tradition allowed. The name Singhasari itself appears to have gained importance only around the 1250s.

The dynasty arose in the upper Brantas basin, in the district of Tumapel, a part of the Kediri world. The overthrow of Kertajaya, conventionally dated to 1222, was recorded in both major literary sources, but the story of Ken Arok surrounding it combines a probable historical core with legend. The tradition of Ken Dedes, prophecy, and the cursed kris shows how a founder of subordinate origin was provided with legitimacy, and the historical debate on these traditions illustrates the difficulty of separating event from literary design.

The decades after the founder were turbulent. Struggles among descendants of different wives produced a succession more complicated than the five-king list, and the consolidation under Wisnuwardhana and Narasinghamurti around 1250 stabilized the dynasty and reorganized relations with Kediri and Janggala. Placing Kertanagara in Kediri in 1254 turned a rival center into a dynastic dependency, but it did not erase its memory. This continuity and change from Kediri to Singhasari characterizes the dynasty's early history.

The kingdom's material base was irrigated rice, supported by villages, religious endowments, sīma grants, artisans, and merchants. Landholding and labor were arranged through a mixture of royal claims, privileged religious foundations, and village custom. The evidence for everyday life is indirect, and women, farmers, and laborers appear mainly in charters and categories of dues.

Kertanagara's reign formed the chapter's center. His progress from heir in Kediri to independent king, his management of ministers, and his marriage alliances showed a ruler who sought to concentrate authority. His religious program combined Śaiva and Buddhist traditions with tantric practice, and Thomas Hunter's reading of it as partly a political-religious project corrected a simple view of syncretism. The Wurare inscription of 1289 and the monuments of Jago, Jawi, and Singosari demonstrate how the court expressed this ideology in text and stone, though many monuments had later histories.

Regional expansion was the second pillar of his ambition. The Pamalayu expedition of 1275, the Bali campaign of 1284, and the gift of an Amoghapāśa image to Dharmasraya in 1286 show a combination of force, diplomacy, and religious patronage. The Padang Roco inscription provides the best evidence for relations with Malayu and supports a cautious interpretation of influence rather than direct administration. The historical debate on Pamalayu showed how much depends on the distinction between expedition and sovereignty.

The confrontation with the Yuan court in 1289 and the mistreatment of Meng Qi placed Singhasari in conflict with the most powerful empire of the age. Yet the decisive blow came from within. Jayakatwang's revolt in 1292, aided by the defection of Ardharaja, ended Kertanagara's life in May–June 1292, a date anchored by a 1351 inscription. The cause-and-effect analysis showed that neither absent troops nor any single cause suffices; the unresolved rivalry with Kediri, the strains of the king's policies, and contingency all contributed.

The legacy of Singhasari was dynastic, religious, and artistic. The kingdom's survivors, led by Raden Wijaya, would found a successor realm that claimed Singhasari as its ancestor, and Kertanagara's memory became part of later royal tradition. At the same time, the kingdom should not be treated as a prelude whose fall was inevitable. It was a distinct political experiment whose achievements and weaknesses help explain thirteenth-century East Java and prepare for the events of Chapter 49.

Key Terms

Singhasari
The scholarly name for the East Javanese kingdom of the Rajasa dynasty from 1222 to 1292; the name became prominent around 1254 and may not have been the original designation.
Rajasa dynasty
The royal house traced to Ken Arok, who took the title Rājasa, and to his descendants, including the kings of Singhasari and, through them, Majapahit.
Kediri (Panjalu)
The dominant East Javanese kingdom of the twelfth century and early thirteenth, overthrown in 1222 and revived briefly in 1292.
Janggala
A name used in later tradition for one half of Airlangga's divided realm, retained in political usage in the thirteenth century.
Tumapel
The upper Brantas district, later governed by Ken Arok, from which the dynasty arose.
Akuwu
A regional governor of a district such as Tumapel in later literary usage.
Pararaton
The "Book of Kings," a Middle Javanese text of uncertain date mixing dated notices with legend, the main source for Ken Arok.
Deśawarṇana (Nagarakretagama)
An Old Javanese court poem completed in 1365 by Mpu Prapañca, containing a royal genealogy and descriptions of Kertanagara's reign.
Prasasti
An inscription on stone, metal, or other material, the main contemporary documentary evidence for the period.
Sīma
Land or a village whose obligations to the king were redirected by decree, usually to support a religious foundation or reward a grantee.
Śaka era
The dating system used in Javanese inscriptions, converted to the Common Era by adding 78.
Old Javanese (Kawi)
The literary and inscriptional language of Java in this period, with Kawi also naming the script used in Java and, in the Padang Roco case, for Old Malay and Sanskrit.
Yuvarāja
A junior ruler or heir, a status associated with Kertanagara's early installation.
Candi
A Hindu-Buddhist sanctuary of Java, including royal memorial temples.
Śiva-Buddha
The associated worship of Śiva and Buddha in Singhasari royal religion; a political-religious program rather than a simple fusion of two faiths.
Tantra
Esoteric ritual traditions emphasizing initiation and the identification of practitioner and deity.
Jina
A "victor," in Buddhist usage a title for transcendent Buddhas, linked to Kertanagara's consecration.
Akṣobhya
A Buddha associated with unshakability, the subject of the Wurare image.
Amoghapāśa
A form of the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara; the image sent to Dharmasraya in 1286.
Pamalayu
The expedition toward Malayu in Sumatra dated 1275 by the Pararaton; its scale and results are debated.
Malayu-Dharmasraya
The Sumatran polity centered in the upper Batang Hari valley that received the Amoghapāśa image.
Brantas
The main river of eastern Java, whose basin formed the kingdom's agricultural and commercial heartland.
Yuan dynasty
The Mongol-ruled Chinese dynasty founded in 1271 under Kublai Khan.
Meng Qi
The Yuan envoy whose mistreatment in 1289 is recorded in the Yuan Shi.
Mūla-Malurung inscription
The 1255 copper-plate charter whose evidence corrects the conventional royal succession.
Wurare inscription
The 1289 Sanskrit inscription on the Akṣobhya image now in Surabaya.
Composite polity
A political formation whose ruler's authority extended through relatives, regional lords, and varied degrees of control, as in Kediri and Singhasari.
Syncretism
The combining of religious traditions, a label that requires qualification for Singhasari because the traditions remained distinct.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 48 matters because it shows how a regional kingdom was built and how it could be lost. In seventy years, a warlord's dynasty became the center of a political and religious project that reached across the sea, and then was destroyed from within by a rival whose legitimacy was older than its own. The story reveals that kingship in medieval Java rested on land, kinship, ritual, and diplomacy, and that each of these could be a source of strength or fragility.

The chapter also teaches a method. The history of Singhasari cannot be written without deciding how to read a late chronicle against a surviving copper plate, a court poem against a stone inscription, and a Chinese record against a Javanese silence. The correction of the royal succession through the Mūla-Malurung charter is a model of how new evidence refines inherited narratives. Students who carry this habit forward will approach later chapters with a clearer sense of what historians know and how they know it.

Singhasari shaped later developments in several ways. Its genealogy became the foundation of Majapahit's royal claims, its temples became sites of continuing commemoration, and its religious program influenced later Javanese ideas of sacred kingship. Its maritime enterprises set precedents for later Javanese ambitions toward Sumatra and Bali, treated in Chapters 49 through 52. Its fall demonstrated that overseas reach without domestic consolidation was perilous.

Finally, the chapter supports the textbook's larger commitment that Southeast Asian societies were historical actors. The Singhasari court adapted Śaiva and Buddhist traditions, shaped its own maritime diplomacy, and answered a Mongol demand on its own terms. It was neither a passive crossroads nor an inevitable empire. It was a kingdom whose rulers made choices under constraint, and whose history repays study in its own right as the next chapter turns to the founding of Majapahit.
