Buddhism and Society in the Pagan Kingdom

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Pagan's Buddhism was not a single doctrine handed down from a king to his subjects. It was a working system of giving, learning, building, and dependence: kings, queens, monks, merchants, cultivators, artisans, and bondspeople each had a stake in it, and each helped to make it.

On the east bank of the Irrawaddy, in the dry zone of central Myanmar, brick temples and stupas rise from a plain of scrub, palms, and cultivated fields. UNESCO inscribed the Bagan archaeological area on the World Heritage List in 2019, citing its concentration of Buddhist monuments built chiefly between the eleventh and thirteenth centuries. Inventories disagree on the exact number of surviving structures, but all place it well above three thousand. This textbook uses the older and conventional form "Pagan" for the kingdom and its capital, and "Bagan" for the modern archaeological zone, as the outline directs.

These buildings are more than a landscape. Each was founded by someone, and many were founded with endowments of land, laborers, and prayers whose terms were cut into stone. Hundreds of stone inscriptions in Old Burmese, Mon, Pyu, and Pali survive, and most of them record gifts to the Buddhist religion. Read together with murals, sculpture, and the buildings themselves, they allow historians to ask how Buddhism worked: who paid for it, who staffed it, who learned from it, who was bound to it, and who benefited.

Chapter 40 explained how Pagan became a major kingdom, through the consolidation of irrigated land, the growth of royal power, warfare, and urban development. This chapter turns from that political narrative to the institutions that gave the kingdom much of its character. Its governing question is how Buddhism shaped the institutions, wealth, architecture, knowledge, and social relationships of the Pagan Kingdom, and how the people of Pagan, from kings and monks to donors, artisans, cultivators, and dependents, sustained and transformed its Buddhist world.

The chapter resists a familiar story in which a reforming king, Anawrahta, replaced a corrupt local religion with pure Theravada Buddhism. That account comes largely from chronicles written centuries after the events. The evidence of contemporary inscriptions, sculpture, and painting shows a more complicated religious world. Buddhism was already present in the Irrawaddy basin in the Pyu period (Chapter 24). Pagan drew on Pyu, Mon, Indian, and Sri Lankan traditions, and it supported Pali learning alongside images and ideas that scholars associate with Mahayana and esoteric Buddhism. Monastic lineages competed and changed.

The chapter also treats the economics of piety seriously. Donations of land and workers created durable religious institutions, and they may have affected royal revenue. Historians disagree about how far, and the chapter distinguishes documented transfers of property from claims about their long-term political effects. It does not treat the thirteenth century as a prelude to inevitable collapse. Mongol-Yuan intervention belongs to Chapter 46, and the successor kingdoms belong to Chapter 59.

The chapter is organized in seven divisions: sources and religious inheritance; kingship and monastic institutions; the Buddhist economy; architecture and art; literacy and learning; social hierarchy and everyday religious life; and Pagan's place in the wider Buddhist world. Features placed beside the subjects they illuminate ask students to read inscriptions, compare monuments, weigh interpretations, and recover the perspectives of people who left few records of their own.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Identify the principal kinds of evidence for Pagan's religious and social history, and explain what each can and cannot show.
  2. Trace Buddhism in the Irrawaddy basin from the Pyu period through the Pagan era without treating Pagan as its starting point.
  3. Distinguish Theravada institutions, Pali learning, and Mahayana or esoteric imagery, and explain why iconography is not proof of separate sects.
  4. Evaluate the traditional narrative of Anawrahta, Shin Arahan, and the Ari as a later chronicle tradition rather than a contemporary record.
  5. Explain how Buddhist kingship, merit, and royal patronage were connected to political legitimacy.
  6. Analyze the organization of the Sangha, including ordination, monasteries, Sri Lankan connections, and competing monastic lineages.
  7. Describe how land, labor, and goods were donated to religious foundations, and interpret the categories of dependent people in the inscriptions.
  8. Assess rival interpretations of whether religious endowments weakened royal revenue and the Pagan state.
  9. Interpret Pagan architecture, murals, and sculpture as historical evidence for patronage, belief, and artistic exchange.
  10. Explain the multilingual documentary culture of Pagan and the role of monasteries in education and textual transmission.
  11. Reconstruct the roles of women, elites, workers, and provincial communities in Pagan's Buddhist world.
  12. Connect Pagan to the wider Buddhist world while recognizing that its institutions developed locally.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How can historians reconstruct religious life when much of the evidence is donative, elite-centered, or written long after the events?
  2. Was Pagan's Buddhist history a conversion from one system to another, or a long process of accumulation, competition, and adaptation?
  3. How did merit-making connect personal devotion, family commemoration, social status, and royal legitimacy?
  4. What did it mean for monasteries to be independent religious communities that depended materially on lay donors and dependent laborers?
  5. How should historians treat inscriptional categories such as "slave" and "pagoda dependent" without assuming modern meanings of freedom?
  6. Did religious endowments weaken the Pagan monarchy, and what evidence could settle the question?
  7. What can monuments, paintings, and images reveal about religious plurality that texts alone cannot?
  8. Who is visible in Pagan's inscriptions and who is not?
  9. In what ways did Pagan participate in transregional Buddhist networks while developing its own institutions?

Historical Context

Buddhism did not arrive in the Irrawaddy basin with Pagan. The Pyu city-states (Chapter 24), including Sri Ksetra near modern Hmawza, left Pali inscriptions on gold leaf, relic deposits, stupas, and monastic remains, and Tang Chinese accounts describe a Pyu society with many monasteries. Mon-speaking communities of the lower Irrawaddy and Salween regions and the Chao Phraya basin (Chapter 25) maintained their own Buddhist traditions, and Brahmanical ritual specialists also served courts. The Burmese-speaking communities who became Pagan's rulers and majority population entered a landscape already shaped by these religious worlds.

Chapter 40 described Pagan's consolidation around irrigated and rain-fed agricultural lands, its fortified center on the Irrawaddy, and the kings traditionally associated with its rise. This chapter assumes that background. What matters here is that the same kingdom that mobilized agricultural surplus and labor for the state also directed a large share of both to religious foundations. The relationship between king and Sangha was a defining feature of Pagan's social order.

Pagan also existed inside a wider Buddhist world. Sri Lanka, with its Pali scholarship and ordination lineages, northeastern India and Bengal, with their Buddhist sites and artistic styles, and the Mon lands of Lower Myanmar were all sources of ideas, texts, and images. Chapter 19 examined how Southeast Asian societies selected and transformed outside traditions, and Pagan is a striking case. This chapter treats Pagan's Buddhism as a local tradition connected to those wider networks.

Finally, the chapter has boundaries. The wider spread of Theravada Buddhism across the mainland belongs to Chapter 53. The Mongol-Yuan campaigns belong to Chapter 46, and the political fragmentation after Pagan belongs to Chapter 59. Developments after about 1300 appear here only where needed to explain the long influence of Pagan's institutions.

Division I

Buddhism before and during the Rise of Pagan

Religious inheritance, evidence, and diversity

Section 41.1

Sources for the Religious and Social History of Pagan

The documentary foundation of Pagan's religious history is its stone inscriptions. Surviving records number in the hundreds and probably exceed a thousand, depending on how fragments and duplicate texts are counted. Most are donative: they record that a named person gave land, workers, a building, an image, or other property to a religious foundation, and they often add the donor's wishes, the date, the witnesses, and curses on anyone who might take the gift away. Old Burmese predominates, with Mon, Pyu, and Pali also represented. Scholars have also relied on translations and editions prepared over the past century, which are themselves interpretations.

Religious architecture, wall paintings, and sculpture form a second body of evidence. Hundreds of temples preserve painted programs, glazed plaques, stucco, and images, and their layouts show how worshippers moved through sacred space. These sources are valuable because they speak where texts are silent about iconography and worship. They are harder to date, and many temple names and patron attributions are modern or traditional.

A third body of evidence consists of later narratives. The Burmese royal chronicles, notably the Hmannan Yazawin compiled in the early nineteenth century and the earlier eighteenth-century chronicle of U Kala, record the reigns, reforms, and wonders of Pagan's kings. The monastic chronicle Sasanavamsa, completed in the nineteenth century, and the Kalyani inscriptions, set up at Pegu in the fifteenth century, preserve accounts of monastic lineages. Sri Lankan chronicles occasionally mention Burmese monks and rulers. These works are important, but they were composed centuries after the events and for later purposes, and they should not be read as contemporary documentation.

The evidence is also uneven in what it preserves. Religious endowments are unusually well documented because the stone was durable and donors wanted permanence. Wooden monasteries, houses, palm-leaf manuscripts, and the private lives of ordinary people have largely disappeared. Earthquakes, including those of 1975 and 2016, damaged many monuments, and restoration work has sometimes altered original fabric and been criticized by specialists. Historians therefore work with a record that is rich about gifts and patrons and thin about the people who cultivated, carried, painted, and cooked.

Section 41.2

Buddhism in the Irrawaddy Basin before Pagan

Pyu Buddhism provides the clearest evidence that the religion was established in the Irrawaddy basin well before Pagan's rise. Sri Ksetra and other Pyu cities have yielded stupas, relic chambers, Buddha images, and inscribed gold leaves bearing Pali Buddhist texts, which scholars place on paleographic grounds in roughly the fifth to sixth centuries CE. These finds show that Pali texts were known to Pyu religious communities and that Buddhist practice was supported by urban elites. Chapter 24 examines their political history.

Chinese records from the Tang period describe the Pyu as Buddhists with many monasteries and report a custom by which young people spent time in monastic life. These are outsiders' observations, filtered through the interests of Chinese compilers, but they agree with the archaeological picture of an established monastic presence. Pyu religious life was not purely Buddhist. Brahmanical and other practices appear in the material record, and Buddhism itself drew on several Indian sources.

The Pyu centers declined by about the ninth century, and the history of the transition to Pagan is debated. Pagan's population likely included descendants of Pyu communities as well as newer Burmese-speaking settlers, and the use of Pyu in some Pagan inscriptions shows that Pyu language and identity persisted. Monuments at Pagan reflect Pyu architectural ideas, though the relationship between Pyu and early Pagan forms remains a subject of research.

The point for this chapter is that Anawrahta and his successors did not introduce Buddhism to an unreligious or purely "animist" land. They inherited, competed with, and extended Buddhist institutions already in place. Local spirit worship, the nat cults associated with places such as Mount Popa, continued alongside Buddhism and became intertwined with it, a relationship that later chronicles dramatize but that probably reflects long-standing practice.

Section 41.3

Theravada Buddhism, Pali Traditions, and Religious Authority

Historical Vocabulary
Theravada

Definition. Theravada, "the way of the elders," names the Buddhist tradition that preserves its scriptures in Pali and traces its monastic discipline through a lineage associated with Sri Lanka and the Mahavihara monastery.

Historical context. In Pagan sources the word is rarely the speaker's own label. Inscriptions more often speak of the Buddha's dispensation, the sasana, and of monks as followers of the Buddha. "Theravada" is a convenient modern category for an interconnected set of practices.

Why it matters. Using the label for eleventh-century Pagan risks suggesting a single standardized institution that did not yet exist.

Potential misunderstanding. Theravada at Pagan was not purely doctrinal or purely monastic. Lay supporters, relics, images, and merit-making were central, and Pagan Buddhism also incorporated ideas and imagery from other Buddhist traditions.

Theravada Buddhism rests on the Pali canon, the Tipitaka, which contains the Buddha's discourses, the monastic rules of the Vinaya, and the philosophical analyses of the Abhidhamma. Its core religious logic, as expressed in Pagan inscriptions, is that actions have consequences across rebirths, that generosity and moral conduct generate merit, and that the Sangha is a "field of merit" in which gifts bear especially rich fruit. This logic bound monks and laypeople in a relationship of mutual dependence, in which laypeople gave material support and monks provided teaching, ritual, and a worthy object of giving.

Pali was the language of scriptural study and of many formal religious texts and inscribed verses at Pagan. Monks learned it for recitation, textual study, and the composition of commentaries and grammars. Pali learning gave monastic scholars a shared scholarly language with Buddhist communities in Sri Lanka and elsewhere, while local languages carried inscriptions, donations, and everyday religious life. The relation between Pali learning and vernacular practice recurs throughout this chapter.

Religious authority was not centralized in a single office. Senior monks, abbots, teachers, ordination preceptors, and royal patrons all exercised influence, and kings supported monks whose standing enhanced the court's prestige. It would be wrong to present Theravada at Pagan as introduced at one moment and thereafter uniform. The evidence indicates a gradual strengthening of Pali-based institutions, competition among lineages, and continued coexistence with other religious practices.

Section 41.4

Mahayana, Esoteric Buddhism, and Religious Plurality

Historical Vocabulary
Mahayana and Esoteric Buddhism

Definition. Mahayana, "the great vehicle," names a broad family of Buddhist traditions that emphasize the career of the bodhisattva and honor bodhisattvas such as Avalokitesvara. Esoteric Buddhism, often called Vajrayana or Tantric Buddhism, emphasizes ritual, deities, and initiation.

Historical context. These traditions flourished in northeastern India and Bengal in the period when Pagan's rulers and artists were in contact with that region.

Why it matters. Pagan murals and sculptures include images that art historians associate with these traditions.

Potential misunderstanding. Images alone do not prove the existence of separate, organized Mahayana or Tantric orders at Pagan. The same patrons, artists, and monks may have worked comfortably with varied iconographies.

Pagan's temples preserve a visual repertoire broader than Pali-centered Buddhism. Temples such as Abeyadana, Nagayon, Nandamannya, and Gubyaukgyi at Myinkaba contain bodhisattva figures, guardian deities, and scenes and images with parallels in Pala-period eastern India. Some temples also display Hindu deities, including Vishnu, Shiva, and Brahma, which appear in or near Buddhist settings. The Nathlaung Kyaung temple at Pagan is generally identified as Vaishnava, and a Tamil inscription of the thirteenth century attests to a community connected with Vishnu worship.

How should historians interpret this plurality? One older approach treated Mahayana and Tantric elements as evidence of a corrupt or heterodox Buddhism later purged by Theravada reformers, often identified with the Ari. Another approach emphasizes that Pagan's patrons and artists drew on available Indian models without necessarily adopting separate sects. The evidence supports the second view more readily. Temples with bodhisattva imagery also contain Jataka narratives and Pali inscriptions, and donors who expressed aspirations that scholars connect with bodhisattva ideals made gifts to monks who are described in Pali-oriented terms.

Many donative inscriptions express the hope of attaining Buddhahood or enlightenment through the donation. This orientation sits comfortably in a milieu that valued Pali scholarship, which shows that the boundary between "Theravada" and "Mahayana" ideas was more porous at Pagan than later sectarian categories imply. Material evidence allows historians to identify iconographic traditions. It does not tell us how many monks or laypeople identified with them or whether they formed separate communities.

The result is a religious landscape of coexistence. Buddhist, Brahmanical, and spirit-cult practices overlapped, and royal patrons supported a range of them. This plurality should be understood as a feature of Pagan's religious life rather than an anomaly to be explained away.

Section 41.5

The Ari Tradition and the Problem of Religious Reform Narratives

Later chronicles describe a group called the Ari as religious practitioners in Pagan before Anawrahta's reforms. In the chronicle account the Ari wore distinctive robes, held property, lived in monasteries, conducted rites involving spirits or serpents, and engaged in practices that the chroniclers portray as immoral. According to the story, Anawrahta abandoned them for a purer Buddhism taught by Shin Arahan and later suppressed or reduced them.

Modern historians have questioned almost every element of this account. The chronicles were written long after the events, in a Buddhist environment that valued narratives of purification. The depiction of the Ari conforms to a recurring pattern of polemic against rival religious groups. Earlier scholars, such as G. H. Luce, tended to identify the Ari with Tantric or Mahayana Buddhism and to treat the reform as a victory of Theravada. Later scholarship, notably that of Michael Aung-Thwin, argues that the Ari were not a distinct heterodox sect and that the story of Anawrahta's religious revolution has been exaggerated.

Recent research on early Pagan sculpture and inscriptions has further complicated the picture. The earliest dated monuments and inscriptions do not reveal a sharp divide between a supposedly corrupt past and a purified Buddhism. They suggest continuity in the use of Mon and Pali, in Buddhist imagery, and in forms of patronage across the eleventh and twelfth centuries. References to the Ari in contemporary sources are few and their interpretation is disputed.

The Ari problem is therefore as much a problem of historiography as of history. Students should treat the chronicle account as evidence of how later Burmese Buddhists remembered Pagan's past and of the kinds of purity claims that shaped that memory, while avoiding a confident reconstruction of a sect whose existence as described cannot be confirmed from contemporary evidence.

Division II

Buddhist Kingship and Monastic Institutions

Royal authority, the Sangha, and religious organization

Section 41.6

Buddhist Kingship, Merit, and the Ideal of the Dhammaraja

In Pagan, kingship was bound up with the Buddha's dispensation. Royal inscriptions present kings as builders, donors, and protectors of the religion, and Kyansittha's royal style incorporates the term Dhammaraja, "king of the Dhamma." The king's role as patron was both a religious duty and a source of prestige. A king who built temples, endowed monasteries, and supported learned monks accumulated merit, and that merit was understood to bear on his own destiny and on the welfare of his realm.

The king's religious role coexisted with older and parallel conceptions of kingship. Brahmanical specialists performed court ritual, and royal consecration drew on Indian-derived forms. Kings also presented themselves as heroes of battle and as masters of land and labor. Pagan kingship was composite, and the Buddhist element, though increasingly prominent, did not displace the others.

Patronage connected the court to the monastic community. A king's gifts to the Sangha created reciprocal obligations and a body of monks with reason to support the king's legitimacy. Patronage also offered ways to extend royal presence beyond the capital, since temples and monasteries in provincial districts linked local elites to the court. The political institutions examined in Chapter 40, including the control of irrigated lands and the mobilization of labor, provided the resources for this system.

Cause and Effect

Royal Patronage and the Growth of Religious Institutions

Long-term causes

Pyu and Mon Buddhist inheritance; Indian Ocean and Bay of Bengal religious contacts; agricultural expansion that produced surplus land and labor; a political culture in which kings sought legitimacy through religious merit.

Short-term causes

Competition among rulers and elites for prestige; the consolidation of royal authority in the eleventh century; the availability of Pali scholars and builders; royal and elite desire to secure merit.

Effects

Large-scale building programs; endowed monasteries and temples; the growth of a literate religious community; transfers of land and labor from private or royal control to religious foundations; a visible landscape of monuments.

Long-term consequences

A deep association between Buddhism and political authority that outlived Pagan; recurring royal efforts to regulate the Sangha; lasting debates over religious wealth.

Historical complexity. Patronage did not simply flow from a powerful king to a passive Sangha. Elites, queens, monks, and local communities initiated foundations of their own, and religious institutions generated claims on resources that kings sometimes supported and sometimes sought to limit.

Section 41.7

Anawrahta, Shin Arahan, and the Traditional Reform Narrative

The traditional narrative begins with Anawrahta, who is said to have come under the influence of Shin Arahan, a Mon monk from Thaton in Lower Myanmar. Shin Arahan instructed the king in the Buddha's teaching and urged him to obtain a complete set of Pali scriptures from the Mon kingdom of Thaton. When the Thaton king Manuha refused, Anawrahta is said to have conquered the city in 1057 and carried off scriptures, monks, and craftsmen to Pagan. The story then credits Anawrahta with building Shwezigon, supporting the Sangha, and subordinating the Ari.

What can be established? Anawrahta was a historical ruler whose reign is generally dated 1044 to 1077, and Buddhism flourished in the Pagan of his time and after. A monk named Arahan appears in sources of the Pagan period and in later tradition, and the Mon-language inscriptions of the early twelfth century show the importance of Mon culture at the Pagan court. Contemporary inscriptions refer to Anawrahta only briefly, and the vivid episodes of conversion, conquest, and scripture-taking are found in later chronicles.

Scholars disagree about how much of this narrative reflects real events. Michael Aung-Thwin has argued that the conquest of Thaton, as told, is a later construction and that Mon cultural primacy in early Pagan has been overstated. Other historians accept that Pagan drew substantially on Mon religious and artistic culture and that some transfer of monks, texts, and artisans from Lower Myanmar occurred, even if the specific story is a retrospective simplification.

Historical Debate

The Ari and Anawrahta's Reforms

The question: Did Anawrahta reform Pagan Buddhism by displacing a corrupt, heterodox religion with Theravada Buddhism, or is this a later narrative that conceals a more gradual and plural development?

Interpretation A — A real reform: Anawrahta, guided by Shin Arahan and enriched by Mon scholarship and scriptures, strengthened Pali Buddhism and reduced the influence of Mahayana or Tantric groups such as the Ari. Chronicles preserve genuine memories of a significant change.
Interpretation B — A retrospective narrative: The reform story reflects later Burmese Buddhist concerns with purity and royal piety. Contemporary inscriptions and monuments show continuity and plurality, the Ari cannot be identified as a distinct sect, and the conquest of Thaton may be a later invention.
Interpretation C — Gradual change: Pagan's Buddhist institutions developed over generations through the accumulation of Pali scholarship, Mon and Sri Lankan influence, royal patronage, and competition among monastic groups, and the Anawrahta narrative compresses this process into a single heroic act.

Evidence. Both sides draw on chronicles, inscriptions, temple iconography, and the relative absence of Anawrahta-period documents. Points of disagreement concern chronology, terminology, and the reliability of later sources. Why it matters: The answer shapes whether Pagan's Buddhism is seen as imported and reformed, or as a locally evolving tradition. Student task: Identify what kind of evidence, if discovered, would most strengthen or weaken each interpretation, and explain why.

Section 41.8

The Sangha: Monks, Monasteries, Discipline, and Institutional Organization

The Sangha was the community of ordained monks, bound by the rules of the Vinaya, which regulated ordination, communal life, property, and conduct. Ordination required a qualified preceptor and a recognized boundary, or sima, within which the ceremony took place; monks therefore sought to ensure that ordinations were valid by their tradition's standards. Pagan sources mention ordination halls and teachers, and the question of whose ordinations were recognized became important in monastic disputes.

Monasteries (kyaung) were residences, teaching centers, and sites for ritual. Surviving brick monasteries at Pagan were probably accompanied by many wooden ones that have vanished. Senior monks, abbots, and teachers directed monastic life, and lineages of teachers and students transmitted learning. The organization was local and personal rather than hierarchical in a bureaucratic sense, even when kings honored particular senior monks.

The Vinaya forbids monks from owning land and wealth in the way householders do, yet Pagan monasteries depended on landed endowments and dependents. The reconciliation lay in practice: donors dedicated property to the Sangha or to a religious foundation rather than to an individual monk, lay stewards or monastic officers administered it, and the produce supported the community. The ideal of an independent renunciant order existed alongside real dependence on donors, who could be kings, queens, officials, and ordinary families.

This dependence created leverage in both directions. Patrons could favor some monks and withdraw support from others, while eminent monks conferred prestige on patrons and could influence the religious life of a locality. Historians must distinguish prescriptive texts, which describe how monks ought to live, from donative inscriptions and later narratives, which show how monastic communities were actually sustained and contested.

Section 41.9

Sri Lankan Connections, Ordination Lineages, and Chapata

Pagan's relations with Sri Lanka are documented in both Burmese and Sri Lankan sources. The Sri Lankan chronicle Culavamsa reports that Vijayabahu I (r. 1055–1110), seeking to restore the Sangha after the disruptions of war, obtained monks from Burma. Later, in the twelfth century, Sri Lanka's King Parakramabahu I (r. 1153–1186) reunified and reformed the Sri Lankan Sangha, and Burmese monks traveled to the island.

The best-known figure is Chapata, also called Saddhammajotipala, whose career is told in the Kalyani inscriptions of the fifteenth century and in the Sasanavamsa. According to these accounts, Chapata, a monk of Lower Myanmar origin associated with Pagan, went to Sri Lanka, was ordained in the Mahavihara lineage, studied there for about ten years, and returned with a group of monks to establish a Sinhalese ordination lineage at Pagan. The accounts give him a significant role in Pali scholarship and in the creation of a monastic community that regarded itself as more purely descended from the Mahavihara.

Source criticism is essential. The Kalyani inscriptions were produced at Pegu in 1476 by a king who was himself engaged in reforming the Sangha and promoting a Sinhalese-derived ordination lineage. The text has a polemical interest in legitimizing that lineage by tracing it to Pagan-era figures. Details of Chapata's life, the dates, and the number of his companions should therefore be treated as later tradition. What seems clear is that contacts with Sri Lanka were real, that monks and texts moved between the two regions, and that lineage and ordination were matters of contention.

The competition did not end with Chapata. Later accounts describe several subgroups descended from his companions, and the existence of multiple lineages shows that Pagan's Sangha was not a single seamless institution.

Section 41.10

Forest Monks, Monastic Lineages, and Religious Competition

Buddhist monastic communities often distinguished village-dwelling monks, who lived in or near settlements and were more involved in teaching and ritual service, from forest-dwelling monks, who emphasized meditation and a more austere life. Pagan inscriptions and later sources refer to forest-dwelling monks (arañña in Pali) as recipients of donations, and by the later Pagan and post-Pagan periods they appear as a prominent group.

"Forest" did not mean isolation. Forest monasteries could lie near towns and could receive substantial royal and elite patronage, and the label seems to have indicated a monastic style and lineage as well as a place. The difference between village and forest groups, and between Sinhalese-descended and other lineages, created occasions for competition over patronage, prestige, and the recognition of ordinations.

Competition was fostered by the structure of patronage. Because monks depended on donors, rival communities sought the favor of kings and elites, and kings could use support for a particular lineage to signal approval. The result was a tension among royal patronage, monastic autonomy, and religious authority. Rulers who wished to affirm their role as protectors of the religion could favor or discipline monastic groups, and monks who wished to maintain independence had to navigate the dependence on lay support.

These sections show that the Buddhist community was not a single, perfectly unified institution. It consisted of overlapping lineages, patronage networks, and external connections, and later narratives of unity and purification tend to simplify a more complex situation.

Division III

Merit, Land Donations, and the Buddhist Economy

The economic foundations of religious institutions

Section 41.11

Merit-Making, Donations, and the Religious Economy

Historical Vocabulary
Merit and Religious Endowment

Definition. Merit is the positive spiritual result of good actions, especially generosity, that is believed to improve a person's future rebirths and wellbeing. A religious endowment is a gift of property, such as land, workers, or buildings, dedicated to a religious foundation in a form meant to last.

Historical context. At Pagan, donors typically expressed the hope that the merit of a gift would benefit themselves, their parents, relatives, deceased family members, and all beings, and would help bring them closer to enlightenment.

Why it matters. The idea of merit connected personal devotion, family obligation, status, and the material support of religious institutions.

Potential misunderstanding. Merit-making was not merely selfish calculation or mere display. Historians can see social recognition and religious aspiration operating together.

Gifts at Pagan ranged from food for monks and lamps for images to the endowment of whole temples. The religious logic was that gifts to the Sangha, to images, and to stupas produced especially great merit because of the worthiness of the recipient. A gift made with pure intention to a virtuous monk was understood to yield greater fruit than one given to a layperson, which is one reason monasteries became recipients of large and permanent endowments.

Donors typically shared the merit of their gifts. Inscriptions record wishes for the benefit of parents, spouses, children, teachers, and deceased relatives, and many extend the wish to all beings. Some express the aspiration to attain Buddhahood. A donation therefore functioned as a form of family commemoration and a way of linking the living and the dead. The ritual of sharing merit gave even small donors a way to participate in a religious economy dominated by wealthy patrons.

Donations also carried social meaning. A named, recorded gift announced the donor's wealth, piety, and connections, and a foundation's patrons were remembered in inscriptions and, often, in the temple's very name. The religious economy thus combined devotional motivations with social competition and with the interests of the monastic community in securing support. The two should not be separated, since contemporaries saw no contradiction between sincere religious aim and public recognition.

Section 41.12

Land Endowments and the Expansion of Monastic Property

Land was the most important form of endowment. Inscriptions record donations of irrigated rice fields, dry fields, gardens and orchards, and sometimes whole villages or shares of village production, together with water sources and other appurtenances. The Pagan economy rested on agricultural surplus from the irrigated districts of Kyaukse and Minbu and from rain-fed lands in the dry zone, and religious foundations were endowed from lands across these areas. The details of measurement and tenure require specialist study, and translations of land terms are not always secure.

Donors often acquired the land they gave. Inscriptions record purchases from named sellers, sometimes with the price and witnesses, which indicates that land was transferable and that officials and neighbors took part in legalizing transactions. The donor then dedicated the land to a temple or monastery. Boundaries were described by reference to landmarks, fields, streams, and neighbors' holdings, making the inscription a legal as well as a devotional document.

The documents emphasize permanence. Formulas bar descendants and officials from reclaiming the land, assign the produce to specific purposes such as the upkeep of a temple or the support of resident monks, and invoke curses and merit to enforce the donor's intent. Whether such formulas were effective is a separate question. Records of endowments show intentions at the moment of gift, and the actual history of an endowment, including mismanagement, encroachment, or restoration, is much harder to follow.

The cumulative effect of such donations over generations was a large body of land held by religious foundations. Historians cannot measure its precise extent, since there is no complete survey, and any numerical claim about the share of land in religious hands should be treated with great caution. What can be said is that donation transferred control of productive resources from private holders, and in some cases from the crown, to institutions with long horizons.

Section 41.13

Temple Dependents, Servants, Slaves, and Dedicated Labor

Land was of little use without people to work it. Inscriptions frequently list people given to religious foundations, often by name, sometimes with family relationships, and described by terms that translators render as slaves, servants, or dependents. The Old Burmese word kyun, usually translated "slave," and expressions that scholars render "pagoda slaves" appear in these lists. The people so designated cultivated fields, tended buildings, cooked, carried water, and performed skilled work as artisans and musicians.

These categories do not map neatly onto modern ideas of slavery or freedom. Some dependents appear to have been bound to the foundation hereditarily, with their descendants sharing their status, which is consistent with a form of bondage. Others may have been debtors or people whose obligations were transferred, and the relationship of a dependent to the foundation could involve rights and duties as well as subjection. The inscriptions rarely describe how people entered this condition, which may have included purchase, debt, capture, or a donor's own dedication of retainers.

The institutional purpose of the arrangement was to provide labor for foundations that could not otherwise sustain themselves. For donors, giving people was a visible and valued form of gift, and for the foundation it supplied a durable workforce. For the people concerned, the evidence is nearly silent. The inscriptions show a name on a list, and the experience behind it can be inferred only from context, comparison, and what is known of dependency elsewhere in the region.

Section 41.14

Tax Exemptions, Royal Revenue, and the Problem of Religious Wealth

The central economic question about Pagan's Buddhism is the relationship between religious endowments and royal revenue. Pagan's kings drew resources from taxes in kind on agricultural land, from labor service, and from other dues. Inscriptions repeatedly state that land and people given to religious foundations were free of such claims. Royal officers were forbidden to enter and exact levies, and the produce was to go entirely to the foundation. If these exemptions were widely applied and the volume of endowment grew over time, the land and labor available to the crown could have shrunk.

This is the argument associated most prominently with Michael Aung-Thwin, who identified the transfer of land and labor to religious institutions as a recurring problem in Burmese political history and argued that it eroded the resources of the Pagan state. On this view, each generation of kings and elites competed to acquire merit by donating, the Sangha and temples accumulated tax-free wealth, and the state's capacity to maintain armies, officials, and its own patronage diminished.

The argument has strengths. The inscriptional evidence of exemption is abundant, and the logic is intuitive. But there are difficulties. There is no census or complete record of landholding, so the proportion of land in religious hands, and the rate at which it grew, cannot be measured. Donated land continued to produce surplus that circulated through the economy as wages, food, building, and trade, so exemption did not remove resources from the economy as a whole. Many donors were themselves officials and elites, so endowment shifted wealth from one set of privileged holders to another, and kings were among the largest donors. Kings could also exert influence over foundations through patronage, regulation, and, in later Burmese history, periodic efforts to purify the Sangha and reclaim land.

It is also important to distinguish documented transfers from claims about their consequences. Pagan continued as a religious and political center for generations, and donation continued after the thirteenth century. The thesis that religious wealth caused political decline must contend with other factors, including the Mongol-Yuan campaigns (Chapter 46), environmental pressures, regional rivals, and dynastic politics. Religious wealth may have been one among several constraints, and its role remains open.

Section 41.15

Royal, Aristocratic, and Nonroyal Donors

Kings are the best known donors, but inscriptions show that patronage extended widely. Queens and royal relatives founded temples and endowed monasteries in their own names, and ministers and officials gave land and images. The Myazedi inscription's Rajakumar, a son of Kyansittha, is a royal donor of this kind. Such patronage allowed members of the court to display status and to build independent bases of prestige.

Below the court, merchants, local headmen, landholders, and families appear as donors. Inscriptions record gifts of modest size, a field or a pair of workers, alongside grand temples. Some donations were made jointly by family members or groups, spreading cost and sharing merit. The range suggests that merit-making was open to many, even though the surviving inscriptions are weighted toward those who could afford durable stone records.

Endowments expressed more than devotion. They commemorated parents and ancestors, announced social ambition, secured a family's name, and could serve as a form of long-term investment in a durable institution. Donors also used endowments to place relatives or retainers in positions of service connected to the foundation, linking religious and family strategies.

The distribution of patronage matters for historians because it indicates who held surplus and how it was converted into religious and social capital. It also warns against reading the monuments as the work of kings alone. The landscape of Pagan was built by a broad stratum of elites and by a large, mostly anonymous workforce.

Division IV

Temples, Stupas, Religious Architecture, and Buddhist Art

Architecture and art as historical evidence

Section 41.16

The Sacred Landscape: Stupas, Temples, and Monasteries

Pagan's religious buildings fall into several recognizable types, and distinguishing them is the first step in reading the landscape. A stupa (zedi) is a solid structure, usually bell-shaped or rising in terraces, that encloses relics or other sacred deposits and is meant to be circumambulated rather than entered. A temple or image house (often called gu or pahto in modern usage) is a hollow building that worshippers enter to reach one or more images, and many are painted inside. Monasteries (kyaung) housed communities of monks and served as teaching spaces. Ordination halls (thein) marked the consecrated boundary within which monastic ordination had to be performed. Libraries, wells, tanks, rest houses, and walls completed the religious complex. Modern terminology mixes Burmese and Pali terms and does not always follow period usage, so students should treat the labels as conventions.

Brick is the medium that survives. Pagan's builders used fired brick set in mortar, worked arches and vaults into multistory temples, and covered surfaces with plaster, stucco, and paint. The brick buildings that visitors see today represent only part of the original religious landscape. Monasteries, rest houses, pavilions, and many residences were built at least partly of wood and have vanished, and ordinary dwellings left almost no trace. The surviving monuments therefore overrepresent the permanent, merit-bearing structures that donors chose to build in brick, and they underrepresent the daily infrastructure of monastic and village life.

Sacred buildings organized movement and access. A stupa drew pilgrims around its base and terraces, with offerings made at its shrines and steps. An image temple led visitors from porches and vestibules through corridors toward a shrine, and the plan could control who saw what and in what order. Some inner spaces were open to lay worshippers and others were reserved for ritual specialists. The placement of buildings reflected patronage as much as design. Foundations stood on land donors controlled, near water and roads, and near earlier foundations whose prestige they could share. The pattern of monuments across the plain is thus partly a map of landholding and social networks.

The sacred landscape also included spaces that were not strictly Buddhist. Tradition places a shrine to a powerful nat at the gate of the old city, and the chronicle account of the thirty-seven nats gathered at Shwezigon dramatizes an incorporation of spirit worship that probably took many forms. Hindu deities appear in or near some Buddhist foundations, and the Vaishnava temple known as Nathlaung Kyaung stands in the same zone. Reading the plain as an exclusively Theravada space would flatten a landscape that included several religious practices.

Section 41.17

Shwezigon and the Development of Royal Buddhist Monumentality

Shwezigon stands at Nyaung-U, a gilded bell-shaped stupa rising from three square terraces. Tradition holds that Anawrahta began it and that Kyansittha completed it, and that it enshrines relics associated with the Buddha. These attributions come from later narratives, and the historian must separate them from what the monument and contemporary evidence establish. What can be said with confidence is that Shwezigon was an important royal and pilgrimage foundation by the Pagan period and that its form, a rounded dome on stepped terraces with ordered ornament, became a model for later Burmese stupas.

The terraces carried glazed plaques depicting Jataka scenes, and some plaques bear Mon-language captions. This detail is historically revealing. A Burmese royal foundation used Mon for religious captioning, which shows that the prestige of Mon Buddhist culture and the presence of Mon-speaking craftsmen or scholars shaped even the most prominent monuments. It also warns against treating "Burmese" and "Mon" as sealed categories in the eleventh and twelfth centuries. The plaques further show that stupas were not only reliquaries. They carried narrative programs through which visitors could encounter the stories of the Buddha's past lives as they moved around the monument.

The monument is also a case study in the biography of a building. Shwezigon has been repaired, regilded, damaged, and rebuilt many times, and later additions, shrines, and halls have accumulated around it. An earthquake in the twentieth century caused damage, and restorations have altered its surfaces. The form visitors see is therefore the cumulative product of centuries of devotion, and any reading of the "original" monument requires careful use of archaeological and art-historical evidence.

Finally, Shwezigon illustrates how royal Buddhist monumentality worked. A king who founded or completed a stupa displayed piety, secured merit, and created a focus for pilgrimage and communal ritual. The labor and materials involved required the mobilization of workers and resources, so the monument also displayed the ruler's command of people and wealth. In this combination of devotion and display, Shwezigon exemplifies a pattern that recurs through the later monuments of the plain.

Section 41.18

The Ananda Temple: Architecture, Images, and Sacred Space

Ananda is among the most admired temples at Pagan. Its plan is cruciform, with four entrance porches opening onto concentric corridors that surround a massive central core. Within the core, four standing Buddha images face the four directions, and above rises a terraced superstructure crowned by a spire. Tradition attributes the temple to Kyansittha and places its founding around the beginning of the twelfth century, and later chronicles tell of Indian monks who inspired the design. The surviving contemporary evidence for Kyansittha's patronage is less definite than the tradition suggests, so the attribution should be presented as traditional.

Architectural Evidence Study

Ananda

What to observe. The cruciform plan sends visitors from each of the four sides toward the central core, where four colossal standing Buddhas stand in niches. The images are generally understood as the four Buddhas of the present age in Pali tradition. The north and south images are generally treated as original, while the east and west figures are later replacements, so the sculptural program seen today is not entirely contemporary. Light enters through openings in the upper walls and falls on the images, shaping how they are seen. The corridors carry stone reliefs of the Buddha's life and glazed plaques, which turn circulation into a narrative experience.

What it shows. The temple combines Indian-derived temple forms with local brick technology and a distinctive program of images, reliefs, and plaques. The plan supports ritual movement similar to circumambulation, and the program functions as a teaching space.

What it cannot show. The building does not itself identify its founder, establish its construction date, or reveal the beliefs of the monks who used it. Patronage claims come from later sources, and restoration complicates interpretation.

Ananda demonstrates how architecture communicates religious ideas through plan, light, scale, and image. The four Buddhas presented a sequence of past and present enlightened teachers, placing the visitor within a long Buddhist history. The use of directional symmetry gave the temple a ritual center that could be approached from any side. It is also a lesson in the limits of evidence. The temple also shows the interplay of local and external influence. The idea of a hollow temple with a central core has Indian antecedents, but the brick construction, the proportions, the use of light, and the combination of reliefs and plaques are Pagan solutions. Ananda should be read as a local creation within a wider Buddhist architectural tradition, not as a copy of an imported model.

Section 41.19

Thatbyinnyu, Dhammayangyi, Sulamani, and Monumental Expansion

Between the early twelfth and the thirteenth centuries Pagan's builders produced temples of increasing size and complexity. Thatbyinnyu, associated with Alaungsithu, is a very tall multistory temple that dominates the skyline of the old city. Its name is generally connected with the Buddha's omniscience. Shwegugyi, whose construction is recorded in an inscription dated commonly dated 1131, although other specialist readings give 1141, is notable because the text gives a rare dated account of royal building. Dhammayangyi, traditionally linked to Narathu, is the largest of the temples by mass, and its interior corridors were blocked, apparently before completion, for reasons that are not documented. Sulamani, traditionally connected with Narapatisithu, is a broad two-story temple with a refined elevation. Htilominlo, Gawdawpalin, and later stupas such as Mingalazedi continued the sequence.

Several features of this sequence matter for social history. First, size required organization. Second, monumental building required patrons with resources. Kings and royal relatives built the largest foundations, but the plain also contains many smaller temples founded by officials and local elites. Together they show a competition for merit and prestige in which building was a principal currency. The chronicle stories attached to individual monuments, such as the tale that Narathu built Dhammayangyi to atone for a crime, express later moral readings. They cannot be tested against the buildings, and historians should treat them as memory rather than evidence.

Third, the later monuments reflect technical development. Builders refined vaulting, enlarged interior spaces, and elaborated exterior ornament. The unfinished state of Dhammayangyi shows that ambitions could outrun completion, whether because of political change, shifting resources, or other causes the record does not explain. Reading the sequence as a steady march toward grandeur would be teleological. The evidence shows variation, interruption, and changing priorities.

Section 41.20

Murals, Jataka Narratives, and the Teaching of Buddhism through Art

Painted murals survive in many Pagan temples, and glazed terracotta plaques and carved reliefs add further narrative programs. Common subjects include the Jataka stories of the Buddha's previous lives, episodes from the Buddha's final life, and rows of Buddhas representing a long succession of enlightened teachers. Captions accompany many scenes, written in Pali, Mon, or Burmese, which identify stories and guide the viewer. The use of several languages for captions reflects the multilingual culture described in Division V and shows that image and text worked together.

Visual Source Study

Jataka Narratives in Pagan Art

What to observe. Narratives are arranged in sequence, with captions identifying episodes. Figures appear in lively scenes with architecture, dress, animals, and tools, so the images are evidence for material culture as well as religion. Mon captions on some plaques show that Mon was used for religious texts in Burmese royal foundations.

What it shows. Art taught moral and doctrinal lessons through story, linked donors to the merit of the temple, and gave literate and illiterate viewers access to the same narratives.

What it cannot show. Images do not reveal how audiences understood them or how many could read the captions. Painted programs vary in date and quality, and later repainting complicates analysis.

The teaching function of the murals depended on who looked at them. Monks and educated visitors could read captions and recognize episodes from the canon and its commentaries. Lay worshippers, many of whom could not read, would have understood the stories through familiarity, oral retelling, and the sequence of images. A painted Jataka cycle thus served as a kind of visual sermon, and it allowed ideas about generosity, discipline, and sacrifice to reach audiences beyond the monastery. The Jataka stories were especially suited to this role because they present moral choices in narrative form, often centered on figures whose virtues model the qualities a donor hoped to cultivate.

Painting also reveals the organization of artistic work. Walls were prepared with plaster, designs were laid out, and colors were applied by painters who must have trained within workshops and traditions. Their names rarely survive. Patrons chose or approved subjects, so murals register the tastes and religious priorities of donors as well as the learning of monks. Changes in the preferred subjects and in the balance of languages over time, with Pali and Burmese captions gaining prominence relative to earlier Mon usage, are among the clues historians use to trace developments in religious and artistic culture.

Murals also record aspects of social life that texts omit. Costume, houses, boats, musical instruments, and tools appear in scenes meant to illustrate stories set in distant times and places, and artists naturally drew on the world they knew. Historians must be careful in using this evidence, since conventions of representation and borrowed Indian models mix with local observation. Used critically, however, the paintings offer glimpses of everyday material life that complement the inscriptions.

Section 41.21

Buddhist Sculpture, Iconography, and Artistic Diversity

Buddha images at Pagan were made in stone, bronze, wood, brick, and stucco, and they range from small portable figures to colossal temple images. Common postures include the seated Buddha with one hand touching the earth, recalling the moment of enlightenment, and standing figures such as those at Ananda. Bodhisattva figures, protective guardians, and Hindu deities appear in some foundations. Styles show connections with eastern India and with Pyu and Mon artistic traditions, and also local development over time.

The temples of Abeyadana, Nagayon, Nandamannya, and the Gubyaukgyi at Myinkaba are especially valuable because their programs combine varied imagery. A single temple may include Jataka scenes, rows of Buddhas, bodhisattvas, and deities. This mixture challenges interpretation. One possibility is that it expresses a deliberate synthesis that patrons and monks understood as coherent. Another is that it accumulated through the tastes of successive patrons and the availability of models. A third is that different groups within the religious community valued different imagery. The evidence does not require choosing among these, and the temples probably reflect all three.

Iconography also shows connections. Images and motifs with parallels in Pala-period eastern India point to the movement of artists, models, and ideas across the Bay of Bengal and overland routes. Yet Pagan artists adapted what they received. They changed proportions, combined elements, and developed local conventions, so the result is a distinct Pagan style within a wider Buddhist visual world. Interpretation must avoid two errors: treating the art as merely derivative, and assuming that the presence of a given image proves that a particular sect existed at Pagan.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Buddhist Artistic and Religious Plurality

What changed? Scale and technique developed from smaller early temples to large multistory monuments. Mural and sculptural programs became more elaborate, and Pali and Burmese captions grew more prominent relative to earlier Mon usage. What remained continuous? Coexistence of varied iconographies, Jataka narrative traditions, the central role of donors, and the cult of relics and images. When did change occur, and how? Gradually and unevenly across the eleventh to thirteenth centuries, with no single break corresponding to a reform. Who experienced change most directly? Patrons and artisans who commissioned and produced new forms, and monks who used them for teaching. Who experienced greater continuity? Villagers and everyday worshippers whose practices of offering and merit-making are only indirectly visible. Why does the pattern matter? It argues against a simple reform-and-purification narrative and supports a view of Pagan's Buddhism as an evolving, plural tradition.

Division V

Literacy, Monastic Education, and Textual Cultures

Language, learning, and the transmission of religious knowledge

Section 41.22

Inscriptions, Pali, Mon, Pyu, and Burmese

Pagan's documentary culture was multilingual. Pali served for scriptural verses and formal religious texts, Mon appears in important early royal and religious inscriptions, Pyu appears in some, and Old Burmese became the principal language of donation records and administration. Sanskrit appears rarely, and a Tamil inscription of the thirteenth century records a South Asian community. Each language carried different prestige and served different audiences. Pali connected Pagan to a pan-Buddhist scholarly world, Mon recalled a prestigious regional Buddhist culture, Pyu preserved an older local tradition, and Burmese reached the people who held and worked the land.

The development of Burmese writing belongs to this setting. The Burmese script is Indic in origin, like the scripts used for Mon and Pyu, but its precise line of descent is debated. The conventional reconstruction derives it from the Mon script, while Michael Aung-Thwin has argued for an earlier connection through the Pyu script. Scholars continue to discuss the paleographic evidence, and the question is best presented as an open problem involving regional Indic-derived writing traditions rather than a settled lineage. What is clear is that dated Old Burmese inscriptions appear in the eleventh century and become the principal medium for donation records in the twelfth.

The shift toward Burmese was also a shift in who could be addressed. Donation inscriptions in Burmese spoke directly to local landholders, officials, and neighbors who needed to know the terms of an endowment. Pali verses and Mon passages could coexist with the Burmese text, so a single stone might address several audiences at once. Language choice therefore expressed learning, piety, and social reach together.

Section 41.23

Monasteries, Education, and the Transmission of Knowledge

Monasteries were the principal centers of religious learning at Pagan. Novices and monks learned to read Pali, memorized and recited texts, studied the discipline of the Vinaya, and engaged with doctrine and commentary. Teachers and students formed lineages, and the continuity of instruction from teacher to pupil was how knowledge moved across generations. Later Burmese Buddhism distinguishes learning from practice, and while one should not project that later vocabulary onto Pagan, the combination of study and meditation or ritual discipline probably characterized monastic life.

The evidence for the scale of education is limited and must be handled with care. Tang accounts describing Pyu youths entering monasteries suggest an older custom of temporary monastic training, but there is no census of Pagan-era students. It would be an error to assume universal literacy. Reading and writing were concentrated among monks, officials, scribes, and some elites, and many laypeople learned through listening, ritual, and images. Monastic education also produced specialists: scholars who could compose commentaries and grammars, scribes who copied texts, and teachers who trained successors.

Education depended on endowment. Teachers and students needed food, shelter, writing materials, and books, and donors who built monasteries or gave books supported learning directly. Education was therefore embedded in the same economy of giving that sustained temples. This dependence shaped who studied, since families with resources could place sons in monasteries more easily and patrons could direct support to particular teachers or lineages.

Monasteries also linked Pagan to external centers of learning. Monks who traveled to Sri Lanka brought back texts and teaching, and scholarly exchange helped maintain Pali study at a high level. The creation of grammars and other works at Pagan shows that monastic education reached advanced stages. At the same time, the survival of knowledge depended on copying and on patronage, which meant that periods of disruption could weaken transmission.

Section 41.24

Manuscripts, Religious Texts, and Buddhist Intellectual Life

Texts were written on palm leaves with a stylus, and sacred works and commentaries were copied in monasteries. Almost none of Pagan's manuscripts survive from the period itself. Palm leaf is vulnerable to climate, insects, fire, and war, and texts known today were preserved by repeated copying in later centuries. Reconstructing Pagan's libraries therefore depends on indirect evidence: inscriptions that mention gifts of books, depictions of monks reading, references in later works, and surviving texts that scholars attribute to Pagan authors. The distance between the original collections and the evidence we have is large, and historians should avoid describing the libraries as if their contents were known.

Pali scholarship at Pagan was substantial. The grammar Saddaniti, dated 1154 and credited to Aggavamsa, is one of the best-established works associated with the Pagan monastic community. Chapata is credited by later sources with grammatical and Abhidhamma works. Religious narratives, Jataka collections, and commentaries circulated, and monks debated discipline and doctrine. These works show that Pagan was not only a place of devotion and building but also a center of textual scholarship in the Pali tradition.

Textual culture also operated in local languages. Donation records, verse, and religious narratives in Old Burmese and Mon expressed the religion in the languages of the community. The combination of Pali learning and vernacular writing is characteristic of Buddhist societies in which monastic scholarship and lay devotion coexisted.

The loss of manuscripts means that silence in the surviving record reflects destruction as much as absence. Historians can say that a literate religious culture with advanced Pali study existed, that it produced works that traveled and were copied, and that it had material support. They cannot reconstruct its libraries, the range of its readers, or the full scope of its intellectual debates. Responsible description acknowledges both the achievements that can be documented and the large areas of loss.

Section 41.25

Religious Inscriptions as Records of Society

Donation inscriptions are among the best sources for the social history of Pagan. A trained reader can extract the donor's name and family connections, the offices or occupations mentioned, the property given and its boundaries, the dependents attached, the witnesses, the date, the merit wishes, and the formulas of blessing and curse. Taken together, thousands of such details allow historians to reconstruct kinship networks, patterns of landholding, occupational categories, and the vocabulary of status.

Inscriptions are also selective, and a critical reader asks what they leave out. They record those who could afford durable records, they use formulas that flatten variation, and they were created for religious and legal purposes, so they emphasize permanence, merit, and protection of property. They do not record the poor, the landless, most women of modest means, or the dependents whose names appear only as items on a list. A study that counts donors by category risks mistaking the sample that survives for the structure of the whole population. Inscriptions are a record of gift-giving, not a census.

Method matters. Historians compare inscriptions across sites and periods, test their claims against monuments and landscapes, and use later records, such as those of the Konbaung period and the colonial era, with caution to suggest how institutions may have worked. Translations and editions are themselves interpretations, so careful scholars return to the original languages and to the physical stones.

Division VI

Social Hierarchy, Gender, and Everyday Religious Life

The human consequences of religious organization

Section 41.26

Religious Specialists, Elites, and Social Hierarchy

Pagan society was hierarchical, and its religious institutions both reflected and reinforced that hierarchy. At the top stood the king and the royal family, followed by officials, aristocratic households, and wealthy donors. Monks occupied a distinctive position, because their standing derived from discipline and learning rather than birth, and senior monks and royal teachers commanded great respect. Below them stood landholders, cultivators, artisans, and the dependent workers attached to foundations. This ordering can be inferred from the titles, honorifics, and relationships recorded in inscriptions, though no single document lays out the system.

The relationship between religious status and economic power was complicated. A monk's prestige did not depend on wealth, yet monasteries accumulated resources through endowment, and eminent monks could influence patrons and the flow of gifts. Wealthy families used foundations to secure standing for themselves and their descendants, and some placed relatives in positions of service connected to a temple or monastery. Hereditary privilege and institutional privilege overlapped, so that a family's connection to a prominent foundation could become a form of social capital passed from one generation to the next.

Officials and local elites formed a middle layer that mediated between the court and the countryside. They appear as donors, witnesses, and administrators of endowments, and their participation in religious life tied the monarchy to provincial communities. Inscriptions record titles that show how offices and ranks were recognized, though the meaning of many terms is debated and translations vary. Historians should avoid imposing the vocabulary of later Burmese administration on Pagan without evidence.

The population was also diverse. Inscriptions identify people as Pyu, Mon, or Burmese and record foreign communities, including South Asians. These labels show a society of varied origins living within a shared religious order, but they do not map neatly onto modern ethnic categories, and their meaning shifted over time. Religious institutions provided common ground for people of different backgrounds, while also preserving distinctions through language and custom.

Section 41.27

Women as Donors, Religious Practitioners, and Members of Religious Communities

Women appear frequently in Pagan inscriptions as donors, as co-donors with husbands and relatives, and as holders of property in their own right. Queens and royal women endowed temples and monasteries, and women of lesser rank gave fields, workers, and images. Gifts were often made in commemoration of parents, children, and deceased spouses, so women's patronage was closely tied to family memory. The pattern is consistent with a society in which women could hold and dispose of property, though the details of law and custom require careful specialist study and cannot be assumed from the donation formulas alone.

The prominence of women as donors had consequences. A woman's gift allowed her to create a lasting memorial, to gain merit for herself and her family, and to establish a relationship with a monastic community. For widows and women with property, endowment could be a way of asserting social position. Women's gifts also supported the institutions on which monks depended, so the Sangha's wealth was partly a product of women's decisions. It would be a mistake to treat Pagan's religious economy as a purely male affair.

Women also appear as religious practitioners. Some inscriptions refer to women who lived as renunciants, and scholars have asked whether a few women may have received full ordination as Buddhist nuns. The evidence is limited and disputed. Evidence for female renunciants is stronger than evidence for a continuously functioning order of fully ordained bhikkhunis, and the lineage of full nuns' ordination had lapsed in the Sri Lankan tradition from which Pagan drew. Later claims that women were wholly excluded from religious life should be distinguished from the contemporary record, which shows women active as patrons and, in some form, as renunciants.

Section 41.28

The Lives of Temple Workers and Monastic Dependents

Everyday Life and Social History

Temple Workers

Building and maintaining Pagan's religious landscape required many kinds of work. Cultivators grew rice and other crops on endowed land and delivered produce to the foundation. Brickmakers and builders produced and laid brick, painters and sculptors made images, and goldsmiths and other artisans produced ornaments and ritual objects. Attendants, cooks, water-carriers, watchmen, and musicians kept foundations running and supported ceremonies. The inscriptions list such workers by name, sometimes with family relationships, and describe them as dependents or slaves of the foundation. These lists are the main evidence for their existence. Historians can infer from them that many workers belonged to families, that skills were passed within households, and that the foundation provided a framework of obligation. They cannot easily recover how workers experienced their condition.

The first question is what the inscriptional categories meant. Dependents given to a foundation were bound to it, and in later Burmese history groups of hereditary "pagoda slaves" persisted into the nineteenth century, which suggests that status could pass from parents to children. One cannot assume that the rules of the Konbaung era applied at Pagan, but the later evidence helps historians frame questions about inheritance, obligation, and the possibility of release. Whether a dependent could leave, marry outside the group, hold property, or be sold is unknown for Pagan, and the answers may have varied between foundations and over time.

The second question is how work was organized. A single large temple drew on specialists and on mass labor. Skilled workers such as painters, sculptors, and masons probably worked in teams under masters, and their training would have required years. Cultivators worked on foundation land in the agricultural cycle of the dry zone and the irrigated districts, delivering shares of the harvest. Attendants and servants performed daily tasks in monasteries. Some workers may have been attached to a single foundation for life, while others may have worked on several, depending on the terms of their dedication.

The third question is how workers appear in the record and how they do not. They appear as names, numbers, and occupational labels, always in documents written by and for donors. Their own voices, their view of the merit that their labor generated for others, and their resistance or accommodation to their situation are absent. The inference that some people fled, negotiated, or sought better conditions is reasonable, given what is known of dependent labor elsewhere, but the surviving documents rarely mention such actions. Historians must therefore describe what is known, flag what is inferred, and avoid both romanticizing the monuments as products of voluntary devotion and reducing the workers to passive victims.

Finally, the human scale deserves attention. The splendor of Pagan was the collective achievement of large numbers of people, most of whose names are lost and whose lives are accessible only through fragments. Recognizing them does not diminish the religious aims of the donors, but it places those aims within a social order in which merit for some depended on the labor of others. Any honest account of Pagan's Buddhism must hold both facts together.

Section 41.29

Lay Buddhism, Offerings, Pilgrimage, and Festivals

For most people, Buddhism was lived outside the monastery. Lay Buddhists took the precepts, observed holy days, and offered food, flowers, lamps, and water to monks and images. Visiting temples and stupas, walking around them, and venerating relics were forms of devotion that required no ordination. Households connected themselves to nearby foundations by supporting resident monks, and the daily offering of food to monks was a basic expression of the relationship between laity and Sangha.

Pilgrimage extended devotion beyond the home district. Pagan's major shrines attracted visitors from the surrounding countryside, and journeys to more distant sacred sites, including those in Sri Lanka and India, linked individuals and communities to a wider sacred geography. A pilgrim could acquire merit by traveling, by worshipping at the shrine, and by making offerings, and the movement of pilgrims carried news, objects, and ideas across regions.

Ceremonies marked the founding of monasteries and temples. Evidence from inscriptions and later tradition indicates that dedications involved feasts for monks, the sharing of merit through ritual acts, music, and the presentation of gifts. Such occasions drew communities together and gave non-elite participants a role in events sponsored by wealthy donors. Festivals associated with relics and holy days offered further occasions for communal celebration and exchange.

Lay religion also included practices that were not strictly Buddhist. The veneration of nat spirits, ancestral rites, and Brahmanical ritual coexisted with Buddhist devotion, and many people probably saw no conflict among them. The sources for these practices are indirect. They consist of temple spaces designed for worship, depictions in murals, mentions in inscriptions, and later descriptions, so reconstruction depends on inference and requires humility about what cannot be known.

Section 41.30

Religion and Community beyond the Royal Capital

Religious life was not confined to the capital. Inscriptions and monuments are found in the agricultural districts of Kyaukse and Minbu, in the Sale area, and in other provincial centers, and religious foundations in these districts linked local elites to the court. Local headmen and landholders endowed temples and monasteries, and provincial foundations drew resources from nearby fields. These establishments mattered for the kingdom because the irrigated districts were central to its agricultural base, so religious investment there was also investment in the economy.

In smaller settlements, village temples, monasteries, and shrines gave Buddhism a local presence. The evidence for them is thinner than for the capital, partly because the monuments are smaller and more often built of perishable materials, and partly because fewer inscriptions survive. Still, the pattern of inscriptions and monuments in provincial districts indicates that Buddhism was embedded in agricultural communities through local worship, monastic patronage, and social obligations. A village that supported a monastery enjoyed access to teaching, ritual services, and a center for community events.

Regional variation is likely. Access to irrigation, distance from the capital, and the presence of Mon, Pyu, or other communities shaped local religious life, and foundations in different districts probably followed different patterns of endowment and patronage. The relationship between provincial foundations and the capital also varied, from close royal supervision to substantial local autonomy.

Provincial evidence corrects a common bias. The monuments of the capital area, magnificent and well studied, can suggest that Pagan's religion was primarily a court phenomenon. Provincial foundations show that it was also a rural and local one, sustained by communities whose names rarely appear in the grand narratives but who provided the labor, produce, and devotion on which the system depended.

Division VII

Religious Networks, Institutional Change, and Historical Assessment

Connections and synthesis

Section 41.31

Pagan and the Wider Buddhist World

Pagan's Buddhist community belonged to a network of religious centers. Sri Lanka supplied Pali texts, ordination lineages, and models of reform, and Burmese monks traveled there. Northeastern India and Bengal were sources of artistic and iconographic influence, and the Mahabodhi temple at Bodh Gaya, the site of the Buddha's enlightenment, held special importance. Pagan contains a replica of the Mahabodhi temple, and Burmese rulers and officials are recorded as sponsoring repairs at the original. The Mon lands of Lower Myanmar contributed language, texts, and personnel. Monks, texts, relics, images, and artists moved along river and sea routes and along overland paths.

These connections did not make Pagan a passive recipient. Pagan monks produced original Pali scholarship, local artisans developed distinctive temple forms, and Burmese became a language of religious record. The result was an institution shaped by outside influence yet recognizably local, consistent with the processes of selection and adaptation described in Chapter 19. Connections also ran in both directions. Burmese monks and rulers supported religious institutions abroad, and Pagan's reputation as a center of Buddhism reached other regions.

The Bodh Gaya repairs illustrate the point. Inscriptions record a Burmese repair campaign at the site that began in December 1295 and was completed in October 1298, and earlier Burmese involvement is also recorded. That the campaign took place after the political troubles examined in Chapter 46 shows that Pagan's religious networks continued even as its political circumstances changed. The wider regional transformation of Theravada Buddhism is the subject of Chapter 53.

Section 41.32

The Growth of Religious Institutions and the Limits of Pagan's Resources

Religious institutions grew in number and size over the period. More foundations meant more demands for maintenance, more land and workers committed, and more competition among patrons and monastic groups. Large establishments required continuing resources: brick decays in monsoon rains, roofs need replacing, images need care, and monks and dependents need food. A foundation that depended on a single family could fall into difficulty if that family declined, and neglect or encroachment on endowed land is likely to have occurred, though the record rarely documents it.

Institutional growth also produced competition. Rival monastic lineages sought the favor of kings and elites, and patrons competed to found the grandest temples. Competition could stimulate building and scholarship, but it also divided resources among many establishments. The proliferation of smaller foundations suggests that merit-making extended widely, and it meant that the burden of maintenance was spread across many patrons of varying means.

Interpretations of what this implied for the kingdom differ. Some historians stress the burden of religious wealth on royal resources, as discussed in §41.14. Others stress that religious institutions provided social cohesion, administrative capacity, and legitimacy, and that wealth moved through the economy as wages, provisions, and trade. Evidence of continued donation at Pagan after the height of royal power shows that religious life remained vigorous, and that later rulers, including those of successor kingdoms, continued to honor Pagan as a sacred center. This chapter does not narrate Pagan's political decline, which belongs to Chapters 46 and 59, and it avoids treating the growth of religious institutions as a prelude to inevitable collapse.

Historical Debate

Religious Wealth and Institutional Sustainability

The question: Were Pagan's religious institutions a source of strength, a drain on resources, or both?

A burden: Accumulated endowments reduced royal revenue and manpower and encouraged competition that strained the system.
A source of strength: Foundations legitimized rulers, organized local communities, preserved learning, and redistributed resources through employment and ritual.
Dependent on circumstances: The effect varied with royal power, the ability to regulate foundations, external pressures, and economic conditions.

Evidence. Inscriptions of donation, monuments, later chronicles, and comparative cases. Points of disagreement concern scale, causation, and chronology. Why it matters: The answer affects how Pagan's place in Burmese history is understood. Student task: Identify what additional data, such as landholding surveys, would help resolve the question.

Buddhism and Society in the Pagan Kingdom: A Historical Synthesis

Buddhism at Pagan was a network of institutions and practices embedded in the social order, and it was more than the religion of its rulers. Kings acted as patrons and sought legitimacy through merit. Monks formed communities of learning and ritual that depended on donors. Land and labor endowments supported foundations, and dependents performed much of the work. Architects, painters, and sculptors created a religious landscape that taught and displayed the faith, and scholars produced Pali works that connected Pagan to the wider Buddhist world.

The tradition was plural. Pali scholarship, Mon and Pyu inheritance, Sri Lankan lineages, eastern Indian iconography, and local spirit worship coexisted. The chronicle story of purification by a reforming king simplifies a gradual and contested process in which institutions, lineages, and practices developed over generations. Recognizing this plurality does not diminish the importance of Pali learning or monastic discipline. It places them within a wider religious field.

The social consequences were broad. Religious foundations provided occupations, structured status, gave women a field of patronage, and connected provinces to the court. They also bound people into hereditary dependence and concentrated resources in institutions whose effect on the state remains debated. The same system that produced extraordinary monuments and scholarship depended on the labor of people whose own experience is largely lost.

Seen this way, Pagan's Buddhism was neither a mere instrument of royal power nor an independent spiritual realm separate from economy and society. It was a system of reciprocal obligation that both sustained and constrained those who took part in it. Its legacy for later Burmese history, in ideas of kingship, in the relationship between monarchy and Sangha, and in the continuing veneration of Pagan as a sacred landscape, makes it a foundation for the chapters that follow.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 41 has examined how Buddhism shaped the institutions, wealth, architecture, knowledge, and social relationships of the Pagan Kingdom. It began with evidence: inscriptions, monuments, paintings, and later narratives, each with strengths and limits.

It showed that Buddhism in the Irrawaddy basin predated Pagan, with Pyu and Mon traditions providing an inheritance on which Pagan built.

It treated Theravada, Mahayana, and esoteric imagery as parts of a plural religious landscape in which iconography does not prove separate sects, and it examined the Ari and the Anawrahta reform narrative as later constructions whose reliability is contested.

It explained Buddhist kingship, in which patronage and merit connected rulers to the Sangha, and described the organization of the monastic community, including ordination, monasteries, Sri Lankan connections, and competing lineages.

It analyzed the religious economy: merit-making, land endowments, dependent labor, tax exemptions, and the debate over whether religious wealth weakened royal power, concluding that documented transfers should not be confused with proven political consequences.

It read architecture and art as evidence, from Shwezigon and Ananda to the later monumental temples, and from Jataka murals to varied sculpture.

It examined multilingual literacy, monastic education, manuscripts, and the use of inscriptions as selective records of society.

It recovered, as far as evidence permits, the roles of elites, women, workers, lay worshippers, and provincial communities.

It placed Pagan within wider Buddhist networks and argued that its Buddhism should be understood as a network of institutions and practices embedded in its social order.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown that Buddhism at Pagan cannot be understood as doctrine alone. It was an arrangement of obligations among kings, monks, donors, workers, and dependents, expressed in stone, brick, paint, and inscribed words.

The chapter also revealed how much of Pagan's religious history rests on interpretation. Chronicles supplied a story of reform, while contemporary evidence points to gradual, plural development. Learning to hold both in view is one of the central skills of historical study.

Pagan's institutions shaped later Burmese religious and political life, including ideas of the king as protector of the religion and recurring concern about the relationship between the Sangha and the state. The Sri Lankan connections explored here prepare the ground for Chapter 53, and the economic questions bear on the transitions examined in Chapters 46 and 59.

Above all, the chapter has treated the people of Pagan as historical actors. They selected, adapted, and sustained a Buddhist world, and in doing so made a landscape that continues to teach about the past.
