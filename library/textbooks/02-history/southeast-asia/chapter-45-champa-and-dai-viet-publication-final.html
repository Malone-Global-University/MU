Champa and Đại Việt

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

In 1371, Vietnamese chronicles report, a Cham force reached Thăng Long, the capital of the Trần dynasty, and the court withdrew before it. Exactly a century later, in 1471, a Vietnamese army under the Lê emperor Lê Thánh Tông captured Vijaya, the principal Cham political center of the central coast. Read alone, the second event looks like the culmination of a long southward march; read beside the first, it looks like one episode in a contest whose outcome was open for generations.

This chapter studies the relationship between Cham polities and Vietnamese states across roughly five centuries, from the tenth-century emergence of locally governed Vietnamese regimes through the fall of Vijaya in 1471, and then follows the continuing history of Cham political authority, communities, and culture after that date. It is the first chapter in this textbook to treat the relationship from both sides with equal analytical weight. Chapters 42 to 44 narrate the Early Lê, Lý, and Trần regimes chiefly from within Vietnamese political history; Chapter 22 examines the origins of Champa; this chapter asks what happened where the two political worlds met.

The meeting place was a long, narrow coastal region of river plains separated by spurs of the Trường Sơn mountains, running from the passes south of the Red River delta to the dry southern coast around Phan Rang. Cham-speaking societies, whose language belongs to the Austronesian family, organized political life around several river valleys and ports, each with its own ruling lineages, temples, and commercial connections. Vietnamese-speaking societies, whose language belongs to the Austroasiatic family, developed in the Red River delta and its margins under dynasties that governed through Literary Chinese, Buddhist and Confucian institutions, and a wet-rice agrarian economy. Both worlds traded with China, both drew on older Indian Ocean and East Asian religious and political ideas, and both were repeatedly drawn into the wars and diplomacy of larger neighbors, including Song China, the Khmer empire of Angkor, the Mongol-Yuan court, and Ming China.

Historians reconstruct this relationship from sources that rarely agree. Cham inscriptions in Sanskrit and Old Cham record royal genealogies, temple endowments, and religious dedications, but they seldom narrate wars against Vietnamese rulers. Vietnamese chronicles narrate campaigns in detail, but they were compiled long after the events and were shaped by the interests of the courts that commissioned them. Chinese dynastic histories record envoys, tribute, and strategic concerns. Archaeology adds temples, citadels, kilns, and shipwreck cargoes. Where these sources can be compared, they sometimes describe different rulers, different centers, or different causes, and the chapter treats these disagreements as part of the history rather than obstacles to be smoothed away.

The chapter also confronts two interpretive habits. One treats Champa as a single kingdom with a fixed capital and a single agenda. The other treats Vietnamese expansion as an inevitable "march to the south" (Nam tiến) that began in the tenth century and ended with Cham disappearance. Neither habit survives close examination of the evidence. Cham authority was regional and shifting; Vietnamese advances were interrupted by Cham victories, negotiated transfers, and long periods of coexistence; and Cham polities and communities continued after 1471.

The central historical question is therefore this: how did changing political, military, commercial, and cultural relationships between Cham polities and Vietnamese states transform the central Vietnamese coast, and why must that transformation be understood as a contested historical process rather than the inevitable disappearance of Champa? The chapter's eight divisions move from evidence and geography through the early wars, twelfth-century diplomacy, the Yuan era and the marriage of 1306, the fourteenth-century Cham resurgence, the fifteenth-century campaigns culminating in 1471, social and commercial interaction, and the long aftermath. The later early-modern history of Vietnamese expansion into former Cham and Khmer lands belongs to Chapter 88 and is introduced here only to establish continuity.

  7. Evaluate the fall of Vijaya in 1471, including its violence, its aftermath, and scholarly debate over how to interpret it.
  8. Examine the roles of captives, migrants, women, merchants, and religious communities in frontier societies.

Historical Context

Chapter 22 traced the emergence of Cham-speaking polities along the central and southern Vietnamese coast, their maritime orientation, and their Hindu and Buddhist institutions, documented from the first millennium CE in inscriptions and monuments. By the late first millennium several centers existed, including Indrapura in the north, where an inscription of 875 records a Buddhist foundation at Đồng Dương, and the Thu Bồn valley, whose temple complex at Mỹ Sơn was an enduring religious site.

Chapters 23 and 42 explained that the Red River delta had been governed within successive Chinese imperial systems for roughly a millennium until the tenth century. After the political break of 939, a succession of local regimes, the Ngô, Đinh, and Early Lê, and then the Lý dynasty (Chapter 43) and the Trần dynasty (Chapter 44), built independent institutions. Their rulers wanted security on the southern frontier, access to coastal commerce, recognition from China, and prestige. Cham rulers had their own purposes: defending ports and river valleys, maintaining ritual authority, and managing competition among regional lineages.

The two worlds were never isolated from the wider region. Angkor intervened in Cham affairs in the twelfth century (Chapters 37 and 38 treat the Khmer side). Song China, then the Mongol-Yuan empire, then the Ming dynasty were all diplomatic partners and sometimes military threats. These larger systems gave both Cham and Vietnamese rulers resources and risks, and they are essential to understanding why particular campaigns occurred when they did.

Section 45.1

Reconstructing Champa–Đại Việt Relations: Inscriptions, Chronicles, Chinese Records, and Archaeology

Three kinds of evidence underlie any reconstruction of Cham history, and Michael Vickery's survey in Champa Revised (2005) is a useful guide to what each can and cannot do. The first is physical remains: brick temples, sculpture, citadels, and material from excavation. The second is inscriptions in Sanskrit and Old Cham. The third is the references to Cham polities in Chinese and Vietnamese histories. Each has a different distribution in space and time, and the differences matter for a bilateral history.

Cham inscriptions are carved on stone steles and temple doorways and are usually dated in the Śaka era, which begins in 78 CE. They record royal genealogies, temple foundations, endowments, and religious claims. Their distribution is uneven. From the fifth to the late eighth century about twenty inscriptions survive, nearly all in Sanskrit and nearly all at or near Mỹ Sơn. A group of eight from 774 to 854 is found in the south, around Phan Rang and Nha Trang. From 875 to 965 a group of about twenty-five is associated with Đồng Dương and the Indrapura region. After 991 most inscriptions are in Cham rather than Sanskrit, and the last dated one is from 1456. The Bình Định plain, where Vijaya lay, has produced only seven short inscriptions, all late, and the major inscriptions of rulers believed to have controlled that region before the thirteenth century were carved at Mỹ Sơn. The largest coherent body of texts, at least a dozen, concerns the often hostile relations with Cambodia in the twelfth and early thirteenth centuries. In other words, the inscriptions give an unusually detailed Cham view of the wars with Angkor and a very thin Cham view of the wars with Đại Việt, and they say almost nothing about the fourteenth-century campaigns. Vickery also cautions that some translations of Old Cham texts, especially the earliest, are not fully reliable and that interpretations resting on them may require revision.

The Chinese and Vietnamese records pose different problems. The Chinese dynastic histories were compiled long after the events and at second hand. Geoff Wade's translation of the Song huiyao, a compilation assembled between the early eleventh and mid-thirteenth centuries and later partly lost and reworked, differs in significant details from the Song shi on which earlier historians relied. For the Vietnamese side, Vickery stresses that two chronicle traditions diverge on some events involving Champa: the Đại Việt sử ký toàn thư and the later Khâm định Việt sử thông giám cương mục follow one line, while the Việt sử lược follows another. The French historian Georges Maspéro, whose 1928 Le Royaume de Champa shaped most later accounts, chose between these versions without explicit criteria and fitted Cham royal names from inscriptions onto the names in Chinese and Vietnamese texts. Vickery argues that very few such identifications are secure, since the Chinese and Vietnamese records almost never reproduce the Sanskrit royal names found in Cham inscriptions, and he suggests that this may be because the Chinese and Vietnamese were not always dealing with the same Cham polity as the inscriptions record.

Dating of monuments is a further difficulty. Vickery points out that many architectural and stylistic dates rest on reasoning that is no longer accepted, so that temples and sculpture can provide only loose relative dating. He also notes the odd silence of the Bình Định and Quy Nhơn towers, which date chiefly to the eleventh through thirteenth centuries, in the epigraphic record. For the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, for which there are essentially no Cham inscriptions, the narrative rests on Chinese and Vietnamese histories, and Wade's translation of the Ming shi account (2003) supplies the one extended Chinese record that includes Cham envoys' own statements. This chapter therefore treats every narrative of a campaign as a claim by a particular source, identifies the source, and says so when a Cham voice is available only through a Chinese or Vietnamese filter.

Section 45.2

The Cham Polities: Indrapura, Amaravati, Vijaya, Kauthara, and Panduranga

Cham inscriptions and later tradition associate Champa with five regions named after centers or river valleys. Indrapura lay in the north, in the area of present-day Quảng Nam, and is linked to the Buddhist monastery of Đồng Dương. Amaravati, in the Thu Bồn basin, included the religious center of Mỹ Sơn and the old ritual landscape of the Quảng Nam plain. Vijaya, in present-day Bình Định, became increasingly prominent in the eleventh and later centuries. Kauthara, around Nha Trang, was dominated by the shrine of Po Nagar, and Panduranga, around Phan Rang, was the southernmost region. The framework is a useful geographical guide, but it should not be mistaken for a fixed administrative structure.

The physical and epigraphic evidence supports a more complicated picture. Vickery identifies architectural regions that developed at about the same time, in roughly the eighth and ninth centuries: the Thu Bồn valley with Mỹ Sơn, Trà Kiệu, and Đồng Dương; the Nha Trang region with Po Nagar; and the Phan Rang region. To these he adds the Quy Nhơn region, whose towers date from the eleventh to thirteenth centuries; the Trà Khúc river in Quảng Ngãi, with the citadels of Châu Sa and Cổ Lũy; the Đà Rằng or Ba river valley, the largest river valley in Vietnam, with the citadel of Thành Hồ; and a northern zone in Quảng Trị and Quảng Bình, where Mahayana sanctuaries such as Đại Hữu and Mỹ Đức show that Cham building extended far north of the Hải Vân Pass in the ninth and tenth centuries. All of these were river-mouth ports or river sites not far from the sea. The map of Cham power was, in effect, a string of river-mouth polities, not a single territory.

How these polities related to each other is the central problem. William Southworth, whose 2001 dissertation Vickery cites, argued that Kauthara and Panduranga were independent states that flourished in the late eighth and early ninth centuries, when the decline of early Tang maritime trade and the shift of South China Sea commerce toward the Red River ports displaced trade wealth southward along the coast. Vickery accepts that there was no single transfer of a capital from north to south and back again, and that the shifts in prominence among the Thu Bồn valley, the south, and the Đồng Dương region reflect changing commercial routes. He also rejects Maspéro's thesis of a long rivalry between an "Areca clan" in Panduranga and a "Coconut clan" in the north, noting that only one inscription, from Mỹ Sơn and dated 1080, mentions both clans, in the genealogy of a king Harivarman, and gives no geographical location for either.

This regional character matters for Cham-Vietnamese relations. A Vietnamese campaign against one center did not defeat Champa as a whole. Chinese records, which speak of a single kingdom called Zhancheng, and Vietnamese chronicles, which followed the convention, simplified a more complex reality. When this chapter says "Champa" it usually means the political world that sources describe as such, and it identifies specific regions or rulers wherever the evidence allows.

Historical Debate

Was Champa One Kingdom?

The question: Should Champa be understood as a unified kingdom with changing capitals, or as a loose set of regional polities that shared language, religion, and royal titles?

Interpretation A — a kingdom with shifting centers: Many older accounts followed Chinese and Vietnamese usage and described a single Cham state whose court moved between Indrapura, Vijaya, and Panduranga.
Interpretation B — regional polities and shifting alliances: Other scholars, including Michael Vickery in his revisionist work, argue that inscriptions point to regional lineages and that unified kingship was episodic, so that Chinese and Vietnamese sources imposed unity.
Southworth and Taylor: William Southworth argued that Champa consisted of independent entities whose relations varied from alliance to hostility, and Keith Taylor has described it as a generic term for polities organized by Austronesian speakers along the coast.
Middle positions: Some historians accept that shared language, ritual, and royal titles gave Champa cultural coherence while insisting that political unity varied greatly over time.

Student task: Identify one type of evidence that could support unity and one that could support regionalism. What would count as proof that a single court controlled several regions at the same time?

Section 45.3

The Geography of Contact: Coastal Plains, Mountain Passes, Rivers, and Maritime Routes

The central Vietnamese coast is a series of narrow alluvial plains separated by spurs of the Trường Sơn range that reach the sea. Rivers run short distances from upland to coast. Vickery notes that all of the major Cham sites were at river mouths or on rivers not far from the sea, and that the great river valleys of Trà Khúc and Đà Rằng, still relatively little studied, were routes into the interior as well as outlets to the sea. The northern rivers, including the Gianh, drain the region south of the Red River delta; the Hoành Sơn spur, crossed today by the Ngang Pass, marks a long-remembered boundary zone; and the Hải Vân Pass, between present-day Huế and Đà Nẵng, separates the northern plains from the Thu Bồn basin and Quảng Nam. Farther south the plains of Bình Định, Phú Yên, and Khánh Hòa are separated by additional spurs, among them the headland the French called Cap Varella, and the climate becomes drier toward Phan Rang.

These features shaped military movement. Vickery argues that three great barriers, the Ngang Pass, the Hải Vân Pass, and the Cap Varella headland, make it implausible that a force from Phan Rang attacked the Chinese provinces of Hoan and Ái, in present-day Nghệ An and Hà Tĩnh, overland in the early ninth century, as some older accounts had it, except by sea. An army marching south depended on coastal roads, river transport, and supply from ships; the passes were defensible choke points; and monsoon winds affected naval operations (Chapter 4). The Vietnamese record of the campaign of 982 preserves a revealing detail. The Toàn thư says that the route to Chiêm Thành by the Đồng Cổ mountain and the Bà Hoa river was dangerous and difficult, and that a new canal on the sea route was completed in 983. Modern editors place those two sites in Thanh Hóa, which shows how much of the early Vietnamese effort went into overcoming the difficult northern stages of the journey before an army could reach any Cham center.

Control of a citadel, port, or river mouth did not guarantee control of surrounding villages, upland communities, or inland trade routes, which is one reason campaigns repeatedly captured capitals without establishing lasting authority. Geography also shaped commerce. Nha Trang lay on what Southworth called the main maritime route from India through Southeast Asia to northern Vietnam and southern China, and the sea-route between Java and northern Vietnam passed the ports of Nha Trang and Phan Rang. Upland products such as aromatic woods reached the coast by river. The northern districts contested in the eleventh century lay between the Red River delta and the first Cham plains, and so were both a frontier and a corridor.

Section 45.4

Two Political and Cultural Worlds: Language, Religion, Trade, and Royal Authority

Cham is a Chamic language within the Austronesian family, closely related to Acehnese and Malay and more distantly to languages of the Philippines, Indonesia, Polynesia, and Madagascar (Chapter 14). Vickery summarizes the linguistic consensus that Austronesian languages spread by sea, and that the ancestors of the Cham reached the central Vietnamese coast from the direction of Borneo at a time estimated, very approximately, between 500 BCE and the early centuries of the Common Era. Some archaeologists link the Sa Huỳnh culture of the central coast with the earliest Cham, though this may be revised, and there was probably more than one landfall. On arrival the Cham found a region already inhabited by Mon-Khmer (Austroasiatic) speakers, and the later highland Chamic languages such as Jarai, Rhade, Chru, and Roglai appear, in the linguist Gérard Diffloth's analysis, to be descendants of Cham spoken by Mon-Khmer populations who adopted it, since their structure retains Bahnaric features. Vietnamese is also an Austroasiatic language, of the Vietic branch, whose origin linguists place in the Nghệ An region (Chapter 13). The two societies thus spoke languages from unrelated families, but their ancestors had been neighbors, and in some areas intermingled, for a very long time.

Their political cultures differed. Vietnamese courts governed through Literary Chinese and increasingly through Confucian examination institutions, while patronizing Buddhism and local cults. Cham rulers used Sanskrit for formal religious texts in the early centuries and increasingly Old Cham afterward, a shift visible in the inscriptions, where almost all texts after 991 are in Cham. Religion varied by region and period. The Thu Bồn sanctuaries of Mỹ Sơn centered on a Shaiva cult associated with a king Bhadravarman, while Đồng Dương was a Mahayana Buddhist foundation whose 875 inscription nonetheless also praised the Mỹ Sơn lingam. Southworth has argued that the early Võ Cạnh inscription, near Nha Trang, shows donations typical of a matrilineal society, which suggests that kinship patterns differed from those of the Vietnamese court; this is an interpretation of an isolated text, not a general rule for all later Cham society.

These contrasts should not be reduced to a clash of civilizations. Both societies were connected to the wider world: Vietnamese rulers to Song, Yuan, and Ming China; Cham rulers to Indian Ocean and Chinese maritime commerce, Angkor, and the Malay world. Both included diverse populations, and political identities were fluid. The Chinese name for Champa after the ninth century, Zhancheng, meant "city of the Cham," and the Vietnamese Chiêm Thành is a rendering of the same idea, evidence that outsiders took their terms from what the Cham called themselves.

Section 45.5

The Historiography of Nam tiến: Expansion, Conquest, and Historical Inevitability

The phrase Nam tiến, "advance to the south," describes the long process by which Vietnamese rule and settlement extended from the Red River delta to the Mekong delta. Twentieth-century historians often presented it as a continuous movement beginning in the tenth century: each dynasty inherited the same objective, and each war with Champa added territory until the Cham were absorbed. Michael Cotter's 1968 article on the "Vietnamese Southward Movement" became a standard statement, and writers such as Paul Mus described the Vietnamese as a flood spreading wherever rice could be grown. The narrative was attractive because it was simple, because it connected events across a thousand years, and because it served nationalist accounts of Vietnamese identity.

Later scholars questioned the narrative on several grounds. Vickery's Champa Revised challenged its premises directly. He argues that the Chinese records of the old polity of Linyi record constant Linyi aggression against the Chinese provinces to its north, so that the earliest pattern was a northward movement; that the first war between independent Vietnam and Champa, in the tenth century, began with Cham intervention in Vietnamese internal politics; that thereafter the conflicts were mostly between equals; that in the last quarter of the fourteenth century the Cham very nearly conquered all of Vietnam; and that Nam tiến, if the term is accurate at all, applies only from the early fifteenth century. Vickery also cites historians of Vietnam who reject the concept. Li Tana describes the expansion as a series of different episodes responding to particular events or opportunities, and Keith Taylor has written that he does not believe such a movement ever took place. John K. Whitmore's studies of Lê Thánh Tông's campaigns examine the fifteenth-century reality of state building and military mobilization. These positions differ among themselves, and Vickery's own revisionism is not universally accepted, but together they have shifted the field away from the older story.

Different dynasties had different goals. Lý rulers sought security and prestige, Trần and Hồ governments sought defense and resources, and the Lê emperor Lê Thánh Tông pursued a program of centralization. Territorial gains were often reversed, and Cham armies occasionally attacked deep into Vietnamese territory. This chapter therefore uses Nam tiến as a historiographical term rather than as an explanation. It acknowledges real territorial change and real violence, but it treats the sequence of events as contingent: different decisions or military outcomes could have produced a different frontier.

Section 45.6

The Emergence of Independent Vietnamese States and the Cham Frontier

The political separation of the Red River region from Chinese imperial rule, traced in Chapter 42, changed its relationship with neighboring polities. Under imperial administration, the southern frontier had been a Chinese concern; once local regimes ruled, their rulers negotiated directly with Cham polities, and the Song court, which did not reconquer the region, became a diplomatic partner and arbiter rather than a governor. Early Vietnamese rulers needed to secure recognition, defend their southern approaches, and acquire prestige goods.

The first confrontation for which the sources give any detail is the campaign of 982 under Lê Hoàn, founder of the Early Lê dynasty. Even this event is reconstructed from fragments. Maspéro, drawing on Chinese and Vietnamese sources, told the story as follows: the Cham seized a Vietnamese ambassador, Vietnam invaded in 982, the Cham king was killed, the Vietnamese sacked the capital, which Maspéro took to be Indrapura or Đồng Dương, and a successor fled south. Vickery observes that the Toàn thư says only that the Vietnamese attacked Chiêm Thành, seized much booty, and gives no location for the capital, and that the claim that the successor fled to Phan Rang has no support in either the Chinese and Vietnamese sources or the inscriptions. In 978, when Maspéro's king would still have been alive, Chinese records note a Cham king with a different name sending tribute, which is not a problem once it is recognized that there was no single Champa.

A second set of details involves a Vietnamese figure named Lưu Kế Tông. The Toàn thư reports under the year 983 that, when the king attacked Chiêm Thành, Lưu Kế Tông fled to that country and someone was sent to seize him. On the basis of Chinese sources, Maspéro described him as a usurper in Champa until 988, under whom many people fled to Hainan, while others took a national claimant to a new center. Vickery interprets the episode as the first war between independent Vietnam and Champa having begun with Cham intervention in Vietnamese internal politics, an interpretation that rests on a brief chronicle entry and should be treated as a reading, not a settled fact.

The significance of 982 lies less in conquest than in what followed. The campaign showed that Vietnamese rulers could project force along the coast. It did not establish Vietnamese authority, and its relationship to later shifts in the prominence of Cham centers is uncertain.

Seen from the northern delta, the frontier was not merely a line separating two courts. Hoa Lư and, later, Thăng Long governed districts that were themselves connected with mountain communities, coastal settlements, and routes to Thanh Hóa and Nghệ An. Officials could describe a southern campaign as punishment for an affront to royal dignity, but the practical considerations were more complicated: the security of a port, the capture of people and movable property, or the need to display the authority of a newly established dynasty. From the viewpoint of a Cham river-valley ruler, the same encounter could threaten access to shipping, temple revenue, and the support of regional dependents. The sources rarely record both explanations side by side. Consequently a study of the frontier must recover interests, institutions, and material consequences rather than adopting the political vocabulary of the victorious court.

The reign of Lê Hoàn illustrates that distinction. A successful expedition might bring prisoners and recognition to the northern ruler without producing a garrisoned province to the south. The survival of distinct Cham centers after the campaign is itself an important limit on what the campaign accomplished. Later dynastic histories often connect a celebrated victory with a wider story of national consolidation. Historians should ask narrower questions: which place was attacked, what route was possible, what objects or people returned with the army, and what continuing administration is actually attested? None of those questions can be settled by the mere statement that one king defeated another.

Section 45.7

Cham Political Change in the Tenth and Eleventh Centuries

Evidence from inscriptions, monuments, and Chinese records indicates that, during the tenth and eleventh centuries, the prominence of individual Cham regions shifted, but the nature of the shift is disputed. The older account, following Maspéro, held that after the Vietnamese attack of 982 the Cham court abandoned the northern capital and moved south, eventually settling at Vijaya in Bình Định around the year 1000. Vickery questions almost every link in that chain. The northern Indrapura or Đồng Dương polity had expanded as far as Quảng Bình, with nearly half its inscriptions found north of Huế, and the only inscription ascribed to that dynasty in the south is one at Po Nagar dated 965, so the supposed southward move of its court is an inference, not a documented event. The place to which a Cham king retreated, which Vietnamese and Chinese sources call Phật Thệ or Phật Thành, "Buddha city," is not clearly identified. A Vietnamese annal reports that a king with titles Cham inscriptions record as yāï po ku vijaya śrī withdrew to Phật Thệ about 700 li from the former capital. The place may have been Đồng Dương, a great Mahayana site, or it may have been the center later called Vijaya. Vickery notes that the Sino-Vietnamese transcription rendered the title vijaya recognizably but did not render the city's name that way, which weakens the equation of Phật Thệ with Vijaya, and Pelliot read the Chinese name as "Bhoja," not Vijaya.

Other evidence points in different directions. A Cham-language inscription at Mỹ Sơn is dated 991, and Mỹ Sơn continued to receive royal inscriptions until 1263. According to Vickery, the major inscriptions of rulers believed to have controlled Bình Định before the thirteenth century were engraved at Mỹ Sơn, which suggests continuing ties between that region and the Thu Bồn sanctuaries. Bình Định itself has brick towers dated mainly to the eleventh to thirteenth centuries but few inscriptions. Southworth, as Vickery notes, regards the earliest surviving reference to Vijaya as an inscription of about 1153 to 1184, and therefore considers the use of the name for earlier periods an anachronism, although Vickery accepts that Bình Định is a reasonable location for the Vijaya of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries.

It would be a mistake to interpret these changes as the migration of a single court. Cham political life consisted of lineages competing for influence, and a ruler at one center might receive recognition from Chinese envoys without exercising authority elsewhere. This internal structure shaped relations with Đại Việt. Vietnamese expeditions targeted whichever center was prominent or accessible, and Cham rulers might respond by seeking Chinese support, retreating to defensible locations, or negotiating. The disunity of Cham polities made decisive Vietnamese conquest harder, because no single defeat removed all rival centers.

Changes in the relative prominence of Cham regions also affected the northern frontier. Royal titles and temple inscriptions associated with the Thu Bồn basin cannot automatically be treated as a record of events in Bình Định or Panduranga. Archaeological concentrations around Đồng Dương, Mỹ Sơn, Po Nagar, and the southern towers point to several histories unfolding simultaneously. Vickery's critique of the old scheme of a single capital migrating from region to region is therefore more than a technical correction to a map: it changes whose actions are being narrated. A military setback in one valley may have opened commercial or political opportunities in another. Conversely, a ruler who patronized an important sanctuary may have claimed a wider legitimacy without exercising everyday authority at all the other ports.

The early centuries also illustrate the disparity between political and documentary geography. Some places preserve religious inscriptions but little evidence of fortification; some citadels leave impressive physical traces with few royal texts; and Chinese notices often designate the entire coast by one familiar name. It would be misleading to use any one distribution as a census of power. The task is to connect the different forms of evidence and to leave open the question of whether a named king commanded a broad coalition, one river basin, or a shifting network of patrons and allies.

Section 45.8

Diplomacy, Tribute, and Relations with Song China

Both Cham and Vietnamese rulers sent missions to the Song court, and Song records preserve lists of envoys and gifts. Cham missions are recorded bringing goods such as ivory, aromatic woods, and other products of the coast and forest, and Song officials granted Cham rulers titles. Vietnamese rulers also received titles from the Song court, which recognized them as local kings under imperial suzerainty even as Vietnamese rulers used imperial titles at home.

The Chinese sources themselves have a history that affects how they should be read. Wade's translation of the Song huiyao includes a statement, noted by Vickery, that in previous dynasties Zhancheng had rarely had contact with China, which suggests that Song historians did not regard Champa as the same polity as the older Linyi. The Song records for Champa were compiled over more than two centuries, partly lost, and reworked, which is why Vickery cautions against insisting that every detail for the tenth and eleventh centuries must be accepted as fact.

This pattern allowed Cham and Vietnamese rulers to negotiate with China independently while competing with each other. A Cham envoy at the Song court could present grievances about Vietnamese actions, and a Vietnamese envoy could present Vietnamese claims. The Song court generally avoided military involvement in the south, preferring to maintain trade and a stable southern frontier, but diplomatic recognition conveyed prestige and sometimes restrained or encouraged particular actions. External recognition therefore functioned as a political resource, not as a measure of real power. A ruler recognized by the Song might have limited authority at home, and a ruler who dominated a region might be unrecognized. The same logic, in a more fully documented form, appears in the Ming records of the fifteenth century treated in Divisions V, VI, and VIII.

A tribute mission was an opportunity to represent a court internationally, to convey complaints about neighboring states, and to establish regular channels of exchange. Song records therefore deserve close reading as diplomatic communications, not merely as dates in a list of embassies. Envoys might present a ruler as deserving a title, ask for gifts, or seek permission to trade. Chinese compilers sorted such encounters through institutional categories of hierarchy and submission, but the Cham sender could have understood the transaction as a practical negotiation between governments with unequal resources. Wade's translation of the Song huiyao is particularly useful because it preserves notices and administrative language that do not always agree with the later Song shi.

Neither Vietnamese nor Cham diplomacy toward Song China should be interpreted as proof of exclusive loyalty. Rival courts could both maintain contact with the Song while fighting on their own frontier. This triangular setting also complicates claims about territorial sovereignty: a Chinese title was not a survey of the districts a ruler actually administered. The contrast between ceremonial recognition and effective control is important when interpreting later reports that a Cham ruler acknowledged Vietnamese superiority and then resumed independent action.

Section 45.9

The Lý Campaign against Champa in 1044

According to the Vietnamese chronicles, in 1044 the Lý emperor Lý Thái Tông led a large expedition against Champa. The Toàn thư describes preparations by land and sea, a major battle in which the Cham king, named Sa Đẩu in Vietnamese sources, was killed, and the capture of a Cham citadel the chronicle calls Phật Thệ. It reports that the Vietnamese army seized large numbers of captives, including members of the Cham court, and returned northward with spoils.

Several aspects of the account deserve attention. The Việt sử lược, which belongs to the other chronicle tradition identified by Vickery, records an attack on Chiêm Thành and then the arrival at the city of Phật Thệ, and Maspéro took this to mean that the Vietnamese "arrived in Vijaya." That reading depends on the equation of Phật Thệ with Vijaya in Bình Định, which is plausible but not demonstrated. Southworth's argument that the name Vijaya is not securely attested before the second half of the twelfth century means that "the capture of Vijaya in 1044" is a convenient modern shorthand, not what the sources themselves say. The expedition illustrates the reach of Lý military organization, including naval transport, and the reported captives show that campaigns served to acquire labor and prestige. Later records mention Cham-derived music and dance at the Vietnamese court, an indication that captives or cultural transfer enriched Lý court life, though the evidence is indirect. The timing, early in Lý Thái Tông's reign, suggests that victory strengthened the new dynasty's legitimacy.

The account is also limited. It reflects a court that saw the Cham king as a subordinate, and it says little about Cham reactions or the long-term effects on the population. Cham inscriptions from the period do not narrate the event. Historians therefore treat the campaign as probable in outline while recognizing that its scale, motives, and consequences, and the identity of the center attacked, are known mainly from Vietnamese records.

The expedition's consequences can be studied more closely through the movement of people. The Vietnamese tradition associates the 1044 victory with a large body of captives who were resettled in northern territories. Such totals are not reliable demographic measurements, but the reported practice is historically consequential. Prisoners could supply labor, specialized craft knowledge, performance traditions, or household service to a court seeking to enlarge its resources. Their removal simultaneously injured households and production in the districts from which they had been taken. Sources that celebrate the return of trophies are much less informative about the families separated or the legal position of the resettled people. Any account of cultural transmission through captives needs to retain that inequality.

The name Phật Thệ is another example of the problems created when later geography is projected onto an earlier text. Identifying it with Vijaya provides an intelligible narrative, but a name transmitted through Literary Chinese and Vietnamese phonetic conventions is not the same thing as a securely located archaeological site. The route and travel time need to be compared with nautical conditions and coastal landfalls before the location can be called established. Vickery's objections to an automatic equation do not make the campaign imaginary; they require historians to separate an event strongly attested in one tradition from a particular modern reconstruction of its geography.

Section 45.10

The War of 1069, the Capture of Chế Củ, and the Three Northern Districts

Vietnamese chronicles report that in 1069 the Lý emperor Lý Thánh Tông, with the general Lý Thường Kiệt, led an expedition against Champa. The expedition reportedly captured the Cham king, whom the chronicles call Chế Củ, and the king is said to have secured his release by ceding three districts: Địa Lý, Ma Linh, and Bố Chính, in the region of present-day Quảng Bình and northern Quảng Trị. The chronicles themselves give only this Vietnamese rendering of the ruler's name. Later historians have commonly correlated Chế Củ with Rudravarman III, a ruler known from Cham sources; that identification is a modern scholarly reconstruction, not a statement found in the chronicles.

Vickery shows that the two chronicle traditions describe the campaign very differently. The Toàn thư and the Cương mục record very briefly that the king attacked Chiêm Thành, location unspecified, and captured Chế Củ, holding him until the three districts were surrendered as ransom. The Việt sử lược has a much more detailed account in which the Vietnamese arrived at a port called Thị Lợi Bì Nại, a rendering of the Cham Śrī Banöy, and then pursued the king to Phật Thệ. Maspéro accepted the port as the port of Bình Định. Vickery notes that the name Śrī Banöy comes from Cham legendary histories, that the research of Po Dharma and Pierre-Bernard Lafont places the first legendary capital of that name in Panduranga, and that other contexts place Binnai in Quảng Ngãi, so the geography of the 1069 campaign is not settled. Vickery also argues that the reconstruction of the Cham royal sequence built on the Vietnamese chronicles conflicts with the epigraphy. In the detailed discussion of the campaign, Vickery proposes a different target in the Trà Khúc basin and disputes the identification of Chế Củ with Rudravarman. These are hypotheses based on contrasting chronicle routes, travel times, and the inscriptions, not a replacement eyewitness account.

Students should therefore see the disagreement rather than receive a composite account. The identification of the Cham king, the sequence of Cham rulers, the location of the Cham center attacked, and the exact circumstances of the cession are debated. The territorial consequence, however, is not in doubt: a frontier zone that had once been Cham became a contested borderland under Vietnamese administration, with a population that included Cham-speaking residents and, over time, Vietnamese settlers. The chronicles' claim of a ruler's surrender and a negotiated cession is the court's own narrative; the later administrative evidence shows only that the districts did come under Vietnamese authority.

Vickery's discussion of inscription C.30A1, dated to 1084 and associated with the Nha Trang–Panduranga region, makes the identification especially difficult. Its retrospective narrative describes prolonged political disorder involving rival leaders in the south and the subsequent consolidation associated with Paramabodhisatva. It can be read in relation to turmoil near the period of the Vietnamese campaign, but it does not straightforwardly describe a Vietnamese victory over Rudravarman or identify a captured king named Chế Củ. Inscriptions from Mỹ Sơn describe another set of struggles, temple damage, restoration, and conflict involving Cambodia. Combining these separate records into one seamless dynastic sequence would eliminate precisely the regional distinctions that the evidence reveals.

Vickery therefore proposes that the Vietnamese attack was directed against Châu Sa in the Trà Khúc river region, rather than accepting the conventional attack on a capital in Bình Định; he also argues that Chế Củ should not simply be equated with Rudravarman. These are reasoned hypotheses, not facts established by the texts alone. They depend on a comparison of travel times, place-name identifications, the two Vietnamese chronicle traditions, and what the inscriptions omit as well as what they say. For students, the critical lesson is the difference between a military event, which the Vietnamese annals report, and an entire geography and royal genealogy constructed by later historians around that event.

Section 45.11

The Contested Frontier after 1069 and the Campaign of 1104

A formal territorial transfer did not automatically produce permanent and uncontested administration. The districts of Địa Lý, Ma Linh, and Bố Chính lay in Quảng Bình and Quảng Trị, the very zone in which, according to Vickery, Cham rulers of the ninth and tenth centuries had built Mahayana sanctuaries and set up a large share of their inscriptions. The ceded districts therefore contained Cham-speaking inhabitants, local elites, and religious sites, and the Lý court had to establish administration, collect taxes, and attract settlers. The Toàn thư reports that the court settled people in the area in the following years, and that Cham rulers made efforts to regain the districts. A Mỹ Sơn inscription dated 1080, catalogued as C90A, names a king Harivarman, Prince Thãï, usually numbered Harivarman IV, whose genealogy joined two old lineages; the inscriptions describe disputes, temple restorations, and conflict involving Cambodia, but do not explicitly record a war against the Vietnamese in these northern districts.

In 1104, Vietnamese chronicles report, a Cham ruler named Chế Ma Na attempted to recover the districts, and Lý Thường Kiệt led a campaign that defeated the effort and restored Vietnamese control. The general died the following year. The campaign indicates that the districts remained contested more than thirty years after 1069, and that Lý rulers regarded the frontier as important enough to commit their most experienced commander. Vietnamese historical syntheses accept the basic chronology, but the chronicle account remains court-centered, the identification of Chế Ma Na with a ruler named in Cham sources is uncertain, and the chronicles' claim to have restored control tells us what the court asserted, not how far its officials could in practice collect taxes or hold the villages.

The pattern is typical of frontier history: negotiated or imposed transfers created new categories of contest. Control depended on garrisons, settlement, local cooperation, and the military balance. For Cham rulers, the northern districts were symbolically and strategically significant; for Vietnamese rulers, they provided a buffer.

Control of the three northern districts should be studied through settlement and administration as much as warfare. Royal chronicles could announce new names and the appointment of officials, but a village's attachment to a state depended on the collection of revenue, settlement of disputes, protection of crops, and cooperation by local leaders. Cham sacred landscapes did not vanish because a court renamed a district. A household might use a Cham language, participate in local cults, and at the same time meet obligations demanded by officials acting for the Lý monarchy. The resulting frontier society was unlikely to fit a neat division between populations that had been entirely conquered and populations that remained wholly independent.

The 1104 campaign is informative precisely because it reveals how precarious a celebrated transfer could be. After more than three decades, the court still needed to send Lý Thường Kiệt to recover or defend districts it represented as its own. The narrative should not turn this into proof of continuous occupation from 1069 to 1104, nor assume that a successful counterattack produced permanent peace afterward. Rather, the incidents reveal a recurring dynamic: rulers announced rights over territory, while local allies, rival chiefs, soldiers, and settlers negotiated the practical meaning of those claims over generations.

Section 45.12

Twelfth-Century Diplomacy, Royal Marriage, and the Language of Tributary Relations

After the campaigns of the eleventh century, Cham and Vietnamese rulers continued to exchange envoys, and the twelfth century is the period in which the language of tribute is most visible. Vietnamese chronicles record Cham missions bearing gifts, which the chronicles describe as tribute, and they record Vietnamese court rituals for receiving envoys. The Song court, for its part, conferred on the Lý ruler in 1164 the title of king of An Nam, a formal recognition that placed the Vietnamese ruler within the Chinese hierarchy of kings while he continued to use imperial titles at home. Cham rulers likewise sought Song titles. Each court was therefore a participant in two hierarchies at once, the one it maintained toward China and the one it claimed over its neighbor.

Historians distinguish between ritual language and political reality. The terms used in Vietnamese records presented the Cham ruler as a subordinate, while Cham rulers dealt with Vietnamese courts as neighbors with whom peace was often more advantageous than war. A Cham mission recorded as tribute may have combined diplomacy, trade, and the exchange of prestige goods, and the same envoys might carry a message to the Song court. The same pattern appears in relations with China, where both sides used a hierarchical vocabulary that did not determine their actual strength. The Chinese sources themselves are shaped by this logic. Zhou Qufei's Lingwai daida, written in 1178 in the south of China, and the Song records compiled in the Song huiyao describe Zhancheng chiefly as a source of tribute and commodities, and they say little about its internal politics.

Marriage alliances were a recognized tool of diplomacy in Southeast Asia, and the best-documented marriage between the two courts, that of 1306, is discussed in Division IV. For the twelfth century the sources do not record comparable alliances between Cham and Vietnamese rulers, and this chapter does not assume marriages that the evidence does not mention. What the evidence does show is that, in the decades after the northern districts were transferred, the Cham and Vietnamese courts were not in continuous conflict. Vietnamese chronicles of this century are more concerned with Khmer attacks, and Cham inscriptions are almost entirely concerned with Cambodia, which suggests that for much of the period the northern frontier was relatively quiet.

Royal diplomacy during the twelfth century operated across several scales. A message to the Vietnamese court could seek security from raids along a river valley; an embassy to the Song might pursue access to commerce or a prestigious title; an agreement with an Angkorian ruler could arise from an entirely different strategic calculation. Those channels could overlap without constituting a permanent alliance. One ruler might send customary gifts in one season and prepare an expedition in another. Contemporary language of friendship, peace, and tribute therefore needs to be read alongside evidence of fortifications, migrations, and temple donations.

Marriage diplomacy is especially difficult to reconstruct for this century. Court genealogies favored relationships considered legitimate by later writers, while agreements involving women of subordinate lineages may have left no surviving record at all. A dynastic marriage should be examined as a political relationship linking several households rather than assumed to signal the union of two coherent nations. The fuller documented marriage of 1306 in Division IV offers a later comparison, but its details cannot be retrojected to every earlier diplomatic exchange.

Section 45.13

Champa, Angkor, and the Changing Political Balance of Mainland Southeast Asia

The twelfth century drew Champa and Đại Việt into the politics of Angkor. Chapters 37 and 38 treat Khmer history in detail; this section outlines what is needed for the Cham-Vietnamese relationship, and it relies on the epigraphic evidence that Vickery summarizes. The largest coherent body of Cham inscriptions, at least a dozen texts, concerns the often hostile relations with Cambodia from the twelfth to the early thirteenth century. They record that Cham rulers resisted Khmer armies, and one of them, catalogued as C17 and dated by its contents between 1147 and 1160, relates that the ruler Jaya Harivarman, attacked by the Cambodian army together with the army of a place called Vijaya, defeated them at Caklyang. Early Chamists did not know what Vijaya meant here, and later scholarship settled on the plain of Bình Định. The inscriptions and Khmer texts agree that in the late twelfth and early thirteenth centuries Angkor exercised hegemony over a region called Vijaya, written in Sanskrit, Khmer, and Cham alike.

Vietnamese chronicles report that in 1128 and the following years Khmer forces under Suryavarman II attacked Nghệ An, sometimes with Cham support, and were repelled. Khmer sources and later inscriptions record Angkorian military ambitions in Champa, including operations against Vijaya in the 1140s. The Song huiyao reports a Cham boat-borne attack on Cambodia in 1177, which later historians interpreted as the capture of Angkor by a fleet traveling through Cambodian waterways. Vickery disputes whether this source identifies Angkor as the target and notes that surviving Cham inscriptions do not directly attest the celebrated occupation. The later campaigns of Jayavarman VII and periods of Khmer intervention in Champa are better supported, but the exact sequence leading to them remains debated. Vickery adds an important historiographical point. Maspéro had assigned some Cambodian interventions in Champa to the late ninth or early tenth century, relying on an interpretation of the Banteay Chhmar inscription that Coedès showed in 1928–1929 to be wrong, since the events belong to the twelfth century. A part of the older narrative of early Cham history therefore rested on a misdating of Khmer history.

These wars made Champa both a victim and an aggressor, and they gave Đại Việt a period of relative freedom from Cham pressure while Cham energies were committed elsewhere. They also changed the geography of Cham power. If Bình Định was the Vijaya of the Khmer wars, then the region's large brick towers of the eleventh to thirteenth centuries, which Vickery notes have been described as showing Angkorean influence although he finds this hard to see, reflect an area that was prominent, wealthy, and contested in precisely this period.

One celebrated episode requires explicit caution. In the conventional narrative a Cham expedition ascended Cambodian waterways and took Angkor in 1177, before Jayavarman VII organized the Khmer recovery. The existence of prolonged and violent Cham–Khmer conflict is not in question, but Vickery disputes the secure identification of a conquest of Angkor in that year. The Song huiyao describes a Cham attack by boat on Cambodia, without necessarily locating the action at Angkor itself; the surviving Cham inscriptions do not directly attest the much-retold occupation; and Khmer commemorative writing is difficult to align with the later chronological reconstruction. A narrative that states simply that Angkor fell in 1177 conceals the difference between a recorded attack and an inferred capture of the capital.

The debate is consequential for Cham–Vietnamese relations. Vietnamese chronicles also report Cham action against Nghệ An in 1177. If several regional Cham centers acted independently, simultaneous activity against different neighboring states is not impossible. If the records refer to different years or have been rearranged by compilers, an apparently coordinated imperial strategy might be a modern creation. The evidence supports a period of intensive conflict and changing alliances, but the scale, command structure, and exact locations of individual operations require qualified reconstruction.

Section 45.14

Cham Political Authority after the Angkorian Wars

Khmer control in Champa did not last. By the early thirteenth century Cham leaders had regained authority, and inscriptions and temple construction show a period of religious patronage and reorganization. Cham-language inscriptions in the south become more numerous in this period: Vickery counts thirty-two in the south after the early thirteenth century, compared with only six at Mỹ Sơn, where the last dated inscription is from 1263. The shift is consistent with a gradual movement of ritual and political weight toward the southern regions, though it should not be read as the abandonment of the north. Inscriptions associated with the reigns of rulers such as Jaya Paramesvaravarman II, in the first half of the thirteenth century, record temple endowments and restoration. Panduranga gained prominence, and later temple monuments there, including Po Klong Garai, are linked by tradition and inscription to rulers of the late thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries.

Recovery was uneven. Some regions had suffered damage, populations had moved, and lineages competed. The brevity and later date of Cham inscriptions in Bình Định make it hard to tell how the Vijaya region, the object of Khmer hegemony, was reintegrated into Cham political life. The relationship among Cham courts and neighboring states shifted, and the northern frontier with Đại Việt remained a concern.

The thirteenth century also brought a new regional environment as the Trần dynasty replaced the Lý, and as Mongol power expanded across Asia. For nearly a century, Vickery notes, the Chinese and Vietnamese paid little attention to Champa during the period of Angkorian intervention, and the sources begin to give Cham rulers' names again only in the early fourteenth century, so the intervening decades have to be reconstructed from inscriptions that are rarely narrative. The recovery of Cham authority is therefore known more from its results, the Cham capacity to resist the Yuan in the 1280s, than from a continuous account.

The aftermath of the Angkorian interventions offers an opportunity to examine the resilience of regional centers rather than tell a story of one court's destruction and restoration. Religious foundations and inscriptions reveal claims to legitimacy that could be revived through renovation of sanctuaries, the re-endowment of temple property, and the naming of victorious ancestors. These activities were political as well as religious: patrons sought the cooperation of priests, administrators, and families capable of organizing labor and resources. Where a royal inscription declares restored order, historians should ask whether it demonstrates authority throughout the coast or only within the sanctuary's regional network.

It is particularly important not to merge the later record of Panduranga with the history of the Thu Bồn or Bình Định areas. The availability of more inscriptions from one region can distort the apparent political weight of another, just as the disappearance of inscriptions does not itself prove social collapse. Trade and the survival of local cults could continue when a particular dynasty ceased to sponsor texts. A careful history of this period is consequently both regional and comparative, distinguishing a change in the location of surviving evidence from a demonstrable change in the location of power.

Section 45.15

The Trần Campaign of 1252 and the Return of Vietnamese Military Pressure

Vietnamese chronicles report that in 1252 the Trần emperor Trần Thái Tông, whose dynasty had been established in 1225 (Chapter 44), led an expedition against Champa. The Toàn thư says that Cham raiding or failure to send tribute prompted the campaign and that the army captured the Cham ruler's wife and many people before returning. The campaign is treated by historians as a plausible demonstration of the new dynasty's military capacity, but its details depend heavily on retrospective Vietnamese records, and the two main chronicle traditions diverge on some events involving Champa, so the entry should be read as one court's account.

The campaign needs to be set in its context. The Trần court was still consolidating its authority, and a successful southern expedition could reinforce legitimacy, as Lý campaigns had done. Its geography and timing are not detailed in the sources, and no lasting territorial change is reported. The Cham side is nearly silent. The inscriptions of this period are largely concerned with temple endowments, and the Cham kings of the mid-thirteenth century, as far as the sources allow, were still reorganizing after the Khmer occupation. For the Trần, captives and spoils increased prestige and signaled that Cham-Vietnamese hostility would continue.

The consequences were limited but not trivial. The campaign re-established the pattern of Vietnamese pressure after the long interval in which Khmer attacks had dominated the sources, and it probably contributed to the wariness with which Cham rulers received Trần envoys in the following decades, though the sources do not say so directly. It is best understood as a demonstration of capability, not as a step toward annexation.

Little in the Vietnamese account of the 1252 expedition permits a detailed reconstruction of every engagement. The choice to march or sail south is nevertheless revealing: the newly established Trần monarchy sought to demonstrate that it could act beyond the borders inherited from the Lý, and the raid or expedition joined political prestige to the acquisition of people and goods. The reported capture of members of the Cham court is significant not because the numbers can be trusted, but because elite prisoners were instruments of bargaining and could also transfer valuable skills and knowledge northward. Their treatment, return, or settlement is difficult to reconstruct from chronicles primarily interested in the emperor's success.

For Cham regional rulers the episode occurred in a landscape already altered by Khmer interventions and competing local courts. A loss suffered by one center need not have represented submission by every port and valley. Nor does the sparse record justify treating the 1252 campaign as the first stage of a coherent Trần policy of territorial annexation. The evidence supports a military confrontation and a shift in relations; its durable administrative consequences are much less clear.

Section 45.16

Trade, Ports, Artisans, and Cultural Contact across the Maritime Frontier

Military rivals continued to share commercial networks. Chinese works of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries describe Zhancheng as a source of aromatic woods, ivory, and other products, and Cham ports connected the interior with Chinese, Malay, and Indian Ocean merchants. Vickery stresses that until the twelfth century navigation and trade in Southeast Asia were dominated by local groups, among them Cham speakers, and that Champa consisted of port cities at the mouths of major rivers. Vietnamese ports in the north exported to China and received southern goods, and the borderland districts lay on the route between them. Ceramics produced in the Vijaya region, including kilns at Gò Sành in Bình Định that date to a later period, show craft production for regional markets.

Trade did not require peace, but it created incentives for diplomacy. Rulers benefited from port revenue, and merchants moved among jurisdictions. A Chinese or Malay merchant would encounter a Cham ruler's customs officials at one port and a Vietnamese one at another, and the same ship might call at both. Religious patronage also crossed boundaries: artistic forms circulated among temples, and Vietnamese Buddhist and Cham Hindu-Buddhist art show occasional parallels. Such parallels cannot be used to identify the ethnicity of artisans, a caution that Section 45.40 reiterates.

The relationship between trade and war should not be simplified. Plunder of ports, seizure of ships, and captives were a part of warfare, and trade routes could be disrupted for years. Yet the sources suggest that even rival courts relied on the same maritime system. When Cham and Vietnamese envoys alike traveled to China, they traveled on the same sea routes, in the same monsoon seasons, and often through the same ports on the Chinese coast, where their missions were received side by side.

Harbors on the Cham coast formed part of the infrastructure linking the Java Sea, the Malay Peninsula, the Gulf of Tonkin, and the ports of southern China. Winds and currents affected sailing seasons, while river mouths supplied fresh water, local food, timber, and access to inland producers. Such ports were not only entry points for distant merchants; they were places where rulers could regulate exchange, provide protection, and extract resources. The same maritime connections that allowed luxury goods to reach temple patrons also helped armies move quickly along the coast, so political competition and commerce repeatedly intersected.

Craft production should not be represented as a simple importation of styles from India or China. Cham temples and sculpture were built within local workshops and religious communities, even when their forms referred to Sanskritic narratives and wider regional fashions. Ceramics, beads, metalwork, and shipborne cargoes can document networks of circulation without allowing historians to assign a single ethnicity to every maker or merchant. Material objects also survive differently from documents: a kiln can testify to production over decades without identifying its patrons, whereas an inscription can name patrons without representing the ordinary workers who made the endowment productive.

Division IV

Yuan Expansion, Cham–Trần Diplomacy, and the Marriage Alliance of 1306

External pressure, diplomatic adaptation, and negotiated territorial change

Section 45.17

Mongol-Yuan Expansion and the Strategic Position of Champa and Đại Việt

After the Mongol-Yuan conquest of the Southern Song in 1279, Khubilai's court turned to the states of the south and demanded submission, envoys, and cooperation. Both Champa and Đại Việt were targets, but their situations differed. Đại Việt shared a land frontier with China and had a centralized court capable of mobilizing large forces; Champa was a coastal polity reachable by sea and more difficult to coordinate under a single command. Each court therefore faced different options: submission in form, delay, resistance, or a combination.

The Yuan court's approach was to send envoys demanding that the ruler present himself or a close relative, supply troops and provisions, and accept Yuan officials. Champa's position on the sea route to the south probably gave it practical value to the Yuan as well as symbolic value, though the sources do not set out Yuan motives in detail. In neither case did a ruler's formal acceptance of Yuan terms settle what he would actually do. Rulers of both states understood that diplomatic language could be used to buy time.

This section establishes the context only; the invasions of Đại Việt are reconstructed in Chapter 44, and the comparison of Yuan strategies across the region belongs to Chapter 46. What matters here is that Yuan pressure changed the strategic environment of both polities, giving each a new external threat and a new reason to calculate their relations with one another.

The Yuan court's southern policy involved multiple overlapping objectives. Its rulers wanted deference from neighboring courts, security for routes linking Yunnan and the coast, and leverage over ports and maritime connections. Champa and Đại Việt did not occupy the same strategic position: the northern Vietnamese delta bordered corridors through which armies could advance from China, whereas Cham centers were distributed along a coastline vulnerable to naval intervention but difficult to hold without reliable provisioning. For both rulers, nominal acceptance of Yuan ceremonial demands might be preferable to invasion, yet concessions could encourage further requests for troops, hostages, passage, or administrative access.

The distinction between a court's submission on paper and obedience in the field is central to the record. Yuan officials often described resistance in the language of rebellion against imperial authority; local rulers could view those same acts as the preservation of their own governments. The available Chinese accounts reveal the imperial decisions in much greater detail than the negotiations inside Cham councils. They should be used to reconstruct the Yuan perspective without turning it into a neutral account of every participant's intentions.

Section 45.18

Cham Resistance to Yuan Military Intervention, 1282–1285

Yuan sources record that in 1282 and 1283 the court sent an expedition by sea under the general Sogetu to Champa after Cham officials had failed to satisfy Yuan demands. The expedition landed on the Cham coast, and the Yuan record reports that the Cham ruler, whom Chinese sources name Indravarman, withdrew from the main citadel into the hills while sending messages of submission. Resistance continued under the ruler's son, known in Cham sources as Harijit and later as King Jaya Simhavarman III (Chế Mân in Vietnamese sources), who adopted a strategy of avoiding pitched battles and harassing Yuan supply lines.

Several features of the campaign are worth noting. The landing was a seaborne operation, which exposed the Yuan force to the sea route's dependence on monsoon winds and to a long line of supply that the defenders could harass. The Cham chose not to defend the citadel, a decision that preserved their forces, and negotiation continued while resistance proceeded, which may have allowed the Yuan court to be told that submission was under way while its army was losing strength. Yuan forces suffered from disease, supply problems, and the unwillingness of Đại Việt to grant passage for a second front. By 1285 the expedition had achieved no durable occupation.

Cham rulers eventually offered nominal submission to the Yuan court, which allowed both sides to claim success. The campaign shows how a regional polity could withstand a major power by combining negotiation with guerrilla resistance and the advantages of local geography. The Cham leaders who emerged from it, above all Harijit, owed their authority to the resistance, and that authority was a basis for the diplomacy that followed. The Cham sources do not narrate the war, so this account rests on the Chinese record and the Vietnamese chronicles.

Yuan intervention against Champa exposed the mismatch between an empire's ambition and the costs of making its authority effective in the tropical coastal environment. A fleet might seize a harbor or royal settlement, yet the capacity to supply its crews and hold routes into the interior depended on food, navigable waterways, cooperation from local intermediaries, and the physical survival of transport vessels. Cham forces could avoid a decisive battle while retaining influence over inland valleys and alternative centers. The campaigns therefore cannot be evaluated solely by which side held the principal city at one moment. Political control was a continuing logistical problem.

Chinese records give the invaders' chain of command and reports of tactical progress, but the motives of individual Cham leaders appear mostly through hostile descriptions. A negotiated accommodation by one ruler may have been a temporary expedient rather than consent to permanent occupation. Likewise, resistance need not have been uniform throughout the Cham coast. Regional lineages had different interests, and the disruption of trade threatened some communities more directly than others. This complexity also cautions against describing the struggle as a modern national liberation movement with an unvarying population-wide consensus.

Section 45.19

Cham–Vietnamese Relations during and after the Yuan Wars

Both Champa and Đại Việt resisted Yuan demands, but the sources provide little evidence of a formal alliance. Vietnamese chronicles mention Cham missions and the Yuan request to cross Vietnamese territory; the Trần court refused passage in 1282 and later fought Yuan armies in 1285 and 1287–1288. Cham resistance reduced the force available against Đại Việt, and Trần resistance prevented Yuan forces from approaching Champa through the north. These were parallel resistances with overlapping interests rather than coordinated strategies, and a historian should avoid reading a formal alliance into a convergence of interests.

The wars changed attitudes. For Trần rulers, Champa was now a potential partner in managing Yuan power; for Cham rulers, Đại Việt was both a neighbor that had resisted Yuan demands and a power that might again press the frontier. Cham rulers also had reasons for caution. A Yuan army had landed on their coast, they had paid in destruction for the choice, and they had no assurance that the Yuan would not return. The Trần court, having defeated Yuan armies, had less reason to be conciliatory but had its own reasons to prefer a quiet southern border.

The visit of 1301 and the marriage of 1306 can be read as outgrowths of this changed diplomatic climate, though that reading is an inference from the sequence of events and not a statement found in the sources. The chronicles present the visit as a retired emperor's personal journey, and the marriage as a courtly arrangement. Whether the court understood them as steps in a common strategy against a renewed Yuan threat is a matter on which the records are silent.

The two neighboring courts shared an interest in avoiding effective Yuan domination, but that shared interest does not establish identical strategy. A Cham ruler might hope that the northern court would obstruct an army's route to the south; a Trần ruler might prefer to preserve his own strength and avoid becoming a conduit for imperial operations. The evidence for mutual contact and the evidence for a formal joint military command are not interchangeable. Distinguishing parallel resistance from coordinated planning is particularly important when campaigns against Đại Việt and Champa are described retrospectively as a single Southeast Asian victory.

After the invaders withdrew, old issues remained. The disputed northern districts still mattered; court prestige depended on the management of tribute, marriage, and envoys; and merchants needed stability in the ports. Warfare against a common external power could alter the bargaining position of each neighbor without erasing earlier grievances. The events of 1301 and 1306 should be understood in that postwar diplomatic setting: they are evidence that rulers were exploring a workable relationship, not proof that centuries of frontier disagreement had ended.

Section 45.20

Trần Nhân Tông's Visit to Champa and the Diplomacy of 1301

In 1301, after his abdication in 1293 and his turn to Buddhist practice, the former emperor Trần Nhân Tông traveled to Champa. Vietnamese sources report that he visited Buddhist and religious sites, stayed several months, and received a lavish welcome from the Cham ruler Chế Mân. The accounts connect the visit with later marriage negotiations, reporting that the former emperor promised a princess in marriage, though the exact terms and timing are described differently in different sources.

The visit demonstrates the intertwining of religion and diplomacy. A retired ruler who had become a monk could travel as a religious figure and still convey political messages; the Cham court could receive him with honor and test the possibility of a new relationship with the Trần. A visit of this kind also created a personal bond between two rulers, one of them a monk and the other a king whose own resistance to the Yuan had been the foundation of his authority, and it gave the Cham court an opportunity to display its hospitality and its religious patronage to a visitor from a Buddhist court.

The sources are Vietnamese and were written after the fact, which makes it hard to separate the court's later interpretation from the motives at the time. The later promise of a princess, in particular, appears in accounts that were written once the marriage had occurred, and the connection between the visit and the marriage may be a retrospective narrative. A Cham account of the visit has not survived in comparable form.

The visit traditionally dated to 1301 is noteworthy because Trần Nhân Tông was a former emperor whose retirement did not extinguish his political influence. The senior-emperor system meant that an abdicated ruler could still act as a prominent negotiator, and his Buddhist commitments made travel and religious contact intelligible alongside statecraft. Later narratives associate the visit with an understanding that a Trần princess would marry the Cham king, but historians should distinguish what an account explicitly records at the time of the journey from later attempts to explain the marriage of 1306. A plausible diplomatic connection is not evidence that every term was negotiated five years in advance.

The journey also reveals the inadequacy of viewing the frontier solely through army movements. Elite travelers, monks, envoys, merchants, and escorts could traverse the same passes and coastal approaches that soldiers used in wartime. Their ability to move depended on local protection and hospitality. The source record tells us little about those arrangements, yet the episode implies a period in which political leadership on both sides considered high-level contact possible despite the recent disturbances of the Yuan era. The specific intentions of the Cham hosts remain more difficult to recover than those ascribed to the Vietnamese visitor.

Section 45.21

Princess Huyền Trân and King Chế Mân: The Marriage of 1306

According to Vietnamese chronicles, in 1306 the Trần court agreed to the marriage of Princess Huyền Trân, sister of the emperor Trần Anh Tông, to the Cham ruler Chế Mân, known from Cham inscriptions as Jaya Simhavarman III. Chế Mân is reported to have sent betrothal gifts, including territory, and the chronicle records that some court officials opposed the match while others supported it. The princess traveled south and became queen of Champa.

The event was a significant diplomatic undertaking. It took place within a regional context of Yuan pressure, Trần security needs, and Cham interest in stable relations with the north. Recent scholarship stresses that the marriage should be seen in its wider East Asian political context, not merely as a bilateral romance. Royal women could be diplomatic instruments, but they also acted with some agency, and the princess's later experience shows the risks involved. Chế Mân's reign, begun in the resistance against the Yuan, is also the reign in which Panduranga temples such as Po Klong Garai are traditionally dated, which indicates that the king had resources for religious patronage even as he negotiated with the north.

Chế Mân died in 1307. Vietnamese chronicles and later Vietnamese narratives report that the queen was expected to share the fate of the king's body in a funerary rite, and that the Trần court sent an envoy, Trần Khắc Chung, who brought her back to Đại Việt by sea in 1308. This account is known only through Vietnamese tradition. No Cham source confirms such a requirement, and it should not be treated as independently verified Cham custom; it may reflect how Vietnamese writers explained the princess's return. Later literary traditions have made Huyền Trân a symbol of sacrifice, embellishing the sea journey and the rescue. Cham memory of these events is much less documented, and the princess's own experience is not recoverable. Disputed Cham succession accounts after Chế Mân form the immediate background of the conflict dated to 1311–1312; the names and relationships of the figures involved are not settled.

For the Cham court, marriage to a Trần princess could affirm a relationship with a powerful northern neighbor without requiring the ruler to abandon his own legitimacy or connections to the wider maritime world. For the Trần court, it could reduce pressure on a vulnerable frontier and bring an agreement over districts that previous campaigns had failed to settle permanently. Whether the territorial provisions were understood as a marriage gift, a negotiated concession, or a later interpretation is precisely why the absence of a contemporary Cham account matters. A court chronicle records the political meaning that its compilers wished to preserve; it does not provide a transcript of negotiations between two parties.

Huyền Trân's experience also poses a historical-method problem. The sources preserve her name because of her royal status and because later literature made her a symbol of dynastic sacrifice. They do not preserve her own testimony. The reported danger after Chế Mân's death has frequently been presented as proof of a uniform Cham funerary custom, but no surviving Cham text establishes that claim. A responsible account explores both the political uses of the story and its human implications, without inventing the princess's feelings or accepting later embellishment as eyewitness evidence.

Section 45.22

Châu Ô and Châu Rí: Territorial Transfer and the Formation of Thuận Hóa

Vietnamese sources identify the territories connected with the marriage as Châu Ô and Châu Rí, lying south of the earlier contested districts in the area of present-day Quảng Trị and Thừa Thiên–Huế. Later scholarship has proposed Cham counterparts to these names, but those identifications are not sufficiently established here to be treated as certain medieval toponyms. After the marriage, the Vietnamese court reportedly renamed them Thuận Châu and Hóa Châu in 1307 and sent officials to register households and establish administration. The name Thuận Hóa came to designate the combined area.

The reported marriage arrangement of 1306 should be distinguished from subsequent measures to establish Vietnamese authority. A transfer, whether a gift or a cession, was only the first step; administration required surveying, settling people, and managing the existing inhabitants. Those inhabitants included Cham-speaking residents, and the sources suggest that some were incorporated while others moved south. The Vietnamese tradition that settlers from the north came to the region, and that the area of present-day Huế developed from them, reflects later accounts of that settlement. The region became a frontier zone from which later wars would be launched, and as the conflict of 1312 shows, Cham rulers did not accept the transfer as permanent.

The territory's later history confirms that the frontier moved only partially. In 1402–1403 the Hồ regime reportedly obtained further districts to the south, and the Ming administration in the early fifteenth century organized part of the area as a prefecture. Thuận Hóa was thus the first in a series of administrative layers that different regimes placed on the same ground. What is certain is that the districts were in Vietnamese hands in later centuries; what is not certain is how many Cham villages remained, and in what status, in the decades after 1307.

Incorporating Châu Ô and Châu Rí required officials to translate a court-level agreement into daily administration. Registrations, the assessment of land and labor, and appointment of local authorities could generate new obligations for established Cham-speaking households. Settlers arriving from farther north encountered landscapes with existing place names, sacred places, agriculture, and commercial routes. The historical record is richer for the proclamation of a new administrative unit than for the negotiations that would have taken place in individual villages. The absence of such records should not be mistaken for the absence of local residents or for instant cultural assimilation.

Territorial names themselves reveal changing layers of authority. Cham toponyms could be rendered through Vietnamese sounds and Literary Chinese characters, then replaced or incorporated into official district names. The historian must separate the linguistic history of a name from the legal history of the land: a new name does not establish that a governor was continuously effective in the same area. The later wars of the fourteenth century demonstrate that possession, allegiance, and administrative reach remained open to challenge long after the marriage agreement was celebrated in court histories.

Section 45.23

The Crisis of 1312 and the Renewed Contest for the Frontier

Chế Mân died in 1307, and the arrangement made in 1306 was tested soon afterward. In the familiar later chronology the Cham throne passed to a ruler called Chế Chí, and the Trần court is said to have captured him in an expedition usually dated 1312. Brian Zottoli's closer reading instead distinguishes the successor Chế Đa Da from a Cham camp leader named Chế Chí or Phan Trắc and dates the attack against the encampment at Câu Chiêm to 1311. These are competing reconstructions, not interchangeable names for the same securely identified ruler. The court then installed a Cham figure, reportedly a relative of the former ruler, as a subordinate ruler of Champa, and Chế Chí died in Vietnamese custody.

The 1312 campaign was a different kind of event from the transfer of 1306. The earlier agreement rested on negotiation between two courts; the campaign reflected a decision to impose Vietnamese influence over Cham succession. A ruler placed on the throne by a foreign court faced obvious difficulties, since Cham legitimacy depended on lineage, temple patronage, and the support of regional elites, none of which a Trần appointment could supply. The subordinate arrangement did not hold. Vietnamese sources report renewed conflict in the following years, which led to the separate campaign of 1318 under Trần Minh Tông, when the Cham ruler, named Chế Năng in the chronicles, is reported to have fled overseas, with Java the destination named in the Vietnamese record. A ruler known as Chế A Nan then appears in the sources.

The chronology commonly repeated in later histories assigns the capture of Chế Chí to 1312 and follows it with the installation of Chế Năng, but it is not a secure identity-by-identity reconstruction of all Cham regional rulers. Zottoli's reading instead distinguishes the heir Chế Đa Da from the encampment leader Chế Chí or Phan Trắc. A modern dissertation by Brian Zottoli (2011) proposes a different reconstruction, which distinguishes the Cham heir Chế Đa Da from the head of an encampment at Câu Chiêm, named Chế Chí or Phan Trắc, and dates the Trần expedition to 1311. That interpretation challenges the simple conventional succession narrative. The differences between Zottoli's reading and the conventional dynastic sequence are an example of why titles, locations and dates must be traced to the specific chronicle version that supplies them. It is important to keep the successive Cham rulers and campaigns distinct. The capture of Chế Chí in 1312, the installation of a subordinate, and the flight of Chế Năng in 1318 are separate developments, known chiefly from Vietnamese chronicles compiled long afterward. Cham inscriptions of the period do not narrate these events, and the identification of the Cham rulers named in Vietnamese sources with rulers named in Cham sources is uncertain. What the sequence shows is that Trần military success could capture rulers and compel temporary compliance, but could not create a durable Cham client state.

Zottoli's account, based on a close comparison of Vietnamese textual traditions, makes the conventional succession story less secure than a simple list of kings suggests. He distinguishes Chế Đa Da, presented as the heir who sent tribute after Chế Mân's death, from Chế Chí or Phan Trắc, described as the leader of a Cham encampment at Câu Chiêm. In his reconstruction the Trần attack took place in 1311, not simply in the year 1312 as often reported, and the camp was distinct from the royal center visited in the earlier mission. These differences matter: the defeat or submission of an encampment's leader cannot automatically be narrated as the capture and deposition of the paramount ruler of every Cham region. The chronicle versions should be presented as competing accounts, with their terminology and chronology preserved rather than fused into one supposed eyewitness sequence.

The episodes also show the political significance of borderland intermediaries. A court might confer a title on a claimant and record the act as a restoration of order; for communities in the coastal valleys, the result could be a rival authority competing for taxes, armed followers, and ritual status. The later report of Chế Năng's flight in 1318 belongs to this continuing contest, not to the same moment as the early expedition. A history organized around rulers alone can obscure the deeper problem: foreign military intervention could disrupt one succession but could not guarantee that local elites, coastal merchants, and temple networks would accept the nominee indefinitely.

Section 45.24

Champa between Đại Việt and the Wider Asian Political System, c. 1320–1360

Between the 1320s and the 1360s the sources are thin, and the period is best understood through what changed at its end rather than through a continuous narrative. Vietnamese chronicles report that Cham tribute to Đại Việt lapsed by the mid-1320s, which suggests that Cham rulers no longer accepted the subordination the Trần court claimed. Some accounts also place an unsuccessful Trần action against Champa in 1326. Because this episode is less securely documented in the materials examined here, it is treated as a reported event rather than the basis for a detailed battlefield reconstruction. Cham rulers maintained maritime and diplomatic contacts with China, and Chinese travelers and compilers of the fourteenth century described Zhancheng's ports and products, including aromatic woods, ivory, and rhinoceros horn, in works that were later drawn on by the Ming-era compilers of the Ming shi account.

The Trần dynasty meanwhile faced internal strain. Chapter 44 traces the fiscal and agrarian problems of the mid-fourteenth century, rural disorder, and the dynastic crisis of 1369–1370 in which a usurper, Dương Nhật Lễ, briefly held the throne before being overthrown. These conditions reduced the court's capacity to maintain a southern military presence and made the frontier districts of Thuận Hóa difficult to hold.

Within Champa, succession struggles and regional competition continued. Panduranga, Kauthara, Vijaya, and the northern regions each had their own leaders and interests, and a ruler's ability to mobilize forces from several of them depended on personal authority and agreements with regional elites. The long period of Cham disengagement from the Vietnamese court therefore did not mean passivity; it meant that no ruler had yet assembled a coalition capable of challenging Đại Việt, and that conditions in the north were becoming more favorable for one that could.

The limited source record for the middle decades of the fourteenth century should be treated as a problem to investigate rather than filled with an imagined continuous sequence of reigns. A gap in Cham inscriptions does not prove a gap in political activity. Chinese notices about trade and embassies, Vietnamese reports of conflict, and the material survival of ports can reveal different parts of the same period. When the Trần court recorded the absence of tribute, that was a statement about a relationship it regarded as obligatory; it was not proof that Cham commerce or diplomacy elsewhere had ceased. Similarly, a reported unsuccessful Vietnamese expedition deserves a specific annal citation before it is used to explain the rise of a later ruler.

The balance of resources was shifting in both realms. The Trần government faced the costs of aristocratic competition, rural distress, and recurring problems of court succession. Cham rulers could draw on coastal exchange, military followers, and their connections with regional valleys, but none of those resources was uniform or unlimited. It is possible to explain why a southern offensive became strategically attractive without claiming that the sources tell us precisely when a unified Cham army was assembled. The history becomes clearer when structural conditions are separated from the event narratives that are actually documented.

Section 45.25

The Rise of Chế Bồng Nga and the Reassertion of Cham Military Power

Cham tradition remembers a ruler called Po Binasuor, and Vietnamese chronicles call him Chế Bồng Nga. Geoffrey Wade's annotated translation of the Ming shi account of Champa records a Cham ruler whom Chinese sources name A-da-a-zhe, who sent envoys to the new Ming court in 1369 and was enfeoffed by it. Wade notes that this name has never been convincingly reconstructed, that it may be a title rather than a personal name, and that it apparently refers to the same person as Chế Bồng Nga in the Vietnamese texts. The identification is a scholarly inference, not a statement in any one source, and the three traditions, Cham, Vietnamese, and Chinese, differ on his origins, his accession, and the manner of his death.

The Ming shi account gives a Cham-originated view of the early years. In 1371–1372 a Cham memorial, delivered in the Cham script on a sheet of gold leaf, thanked the Ming court for the gold seal of enfeoffment and complained that Annam had been attacking the Cham frontier, killing officials and plundering the people. The memorial asked for weapons and for musicians; the Ming court refused both, reasoning that weapons would only fuel the war. The exchange reveals that, from the Cham side, the 1370s were already a period of open warfare with Đại Việt, and that Cham leaders were seeking recognition from the Ming as a counterweight, exactly as Vietnamese rulers were.

Chế Bồng Nga's success rested on several conditions. Cham fleets could move quickly along the coast and, as the 1371 raid showed, into the Red River approaches. Cham rulers could draw on the coastal ports for ships and crews, and on the wealth of maritime trade, including the forest and sea products that Chinese sources associate with Zhancheng. The Trần court, by contrast, suffered from dynastic instability and thin finances. John K. Whitmore's essay on Chế Bồng Nga and fourteenth-century Champa, published in the volume The Cham of Vietnam, is the principal modern study of the reign, and students should consult it for an account based on the Vietnamese and Chinese sources; this textbook's summary has not been checked against it. It would be a mistake to attribute the resurgence to one man alone, since it required the cooperation of regional commanders and the weakness of an opponent, but the sources agree that for about three decades Cham military initiative was a central fact of Southeast Asian politics.

The reputation of Chế Bồng Nga was formed through the memory of repeated advances against a formerly dominant neighbor. Vietnamese chronicles describe an adversary whose forces crossed the frontier and threatened royal centers; Ming materials record a Cham ruler known by a different transcription and situate his rule within a wider diplomatic system. Identifying the same person across these accounts is plausible in context, but it remains an act of historical reconstruction. The political foundations of his campaigns are even less fully documented. A leader could draw forces from several coastal regions, negotiate with local elites, and benefit from discord among opponents without necessarily ruling a territorially uniform state.

His rise should therefore be explained through changing opportunities as well as personal initiative. The Trần monarchy was strained by succession disputes and the difficulties of maintaining garrisons far from the Red River court. Coastal and river approaches made rapid attacks feasible, while a successful campaign could acquire booty, prisoners, and political prestige. Yet the exact constituency of the Cham armies is not given in surviving Cham testimony. The temptation to assign them a single ethnic or political identity resembles the temptation to imagine an equally unified Vietnamese defense. The campaigns are better understood as contests among courts and regional forces whose allegiance and capacity changed over time.

Section 45.26

The Cham Offensive of 1371 and the Sack of Thăng Long

Vietnamese chronicles report that in 1371 a Cham fleet sailed north, entered the Red River system, and reached Thăng Long. The Trần emperor Trần Nghệ Tông, who had taken the throne only the previous year after the overthrow of Dương Nhật Lễ, left the capital, and Cham forces plundered the palace and city before withdrawing with their spoils. The expedition was a naval and riverine operation. Coastal winds, the river mouths of the delta, and the capital's position within reach of tidal waters made it vulnerable to a force that could move by water faster than a land army could be assembled to oppose it.

The consequences were material and political. The raid damaged Trần prestige and demonstrated the fragility of the capital's defenses. It strengthened the position of officials associated with military reorganization, among them Hồ Quý Ly, whose rise is traced in Chapter 44. The human costs were considerable, including loss of property and lives in and around the capital, but the sources provide no reliable figures.

The source situation is notable. The 1371 attack is known principally from Vietnamese chronicles. Wade's translation of the Ming shi shows Cham-Ming exchanges in precisely these years, with Cham envoys complaining of Vietnamese aggression, but it does not record the sack of Thăng Long. The silence may mean only that the Cham envoys did not report it, or that the Chinese compilers did not include it. It should not be read as evidence that the raid did not happen, but it does limit corroboration, and students should treat the narrative details as the Vietnamese court's account.

The march or voyage that brought Cham forces to Thăng Long in 1371 was politically devastating because the capital represented dynastic authority, court ritual, and the concentration of elite resources. Its seizure or plunder did not mean that every district of Đại Việt had submitted to Champa, just as earlier Vietnamese victories over Cham centers had not extinguished Cham government. The court's withdrawal protected the persons through whom the dynasty could continue to claim rule, but the decision exposed residents and property to violence. Chronicles written for the court were more likely to record the emperor's escape and the loss of prestige than the experience of markets, households, and temples left behind.

The geography of the Red River system helps explain both the opportunity and the limits of the attack. A force supported by boats could approach through riverine channels and move faster than a land army burdened by supplies, especially where the defending government failed to coordinate responses. Yet the same waterways required local pilots, food, and secure routes for withdrawal. Without evidence for permanent garrisons or administration after the raid, the event should be described as a severe military and symbolic success rather than a lasting Cham conquest of the entire northern kingdom. Its decisive lesson was the vulnerability of the Trần political center.

Section 45.27

The Campaign of 1377 and the Death of Trần Duệ Tông

In 1377 the Trần emperor Trần Duệ Tông led a large expedition against Vijaya. Vietnamese chronicles report that the army advanced by sea and land, that the Cham ruler appeared to offer little resistance, and that the Vietnamese force was ambushed near the Cham capital and destroyed, with the emperor killed in the battle. The conventional chronicle sequence places the advance in late 1376 and the emperor's death in 1377 near Vijaya, distinguishes Hồ Quý Ly's provisioning role from Đỗ Tử Bình's reported failure to support the campaign, and includes a later tradition that withheld gold tribute helped provoke the conflict. These details should be treated as the Vietnamese court's own explanation, and they are given here as such. The chronicles attribute the defeat to overconfidence, poor reconnaissance, and failures of coordination, and assign blame to particular officials. Because these explanations were written afterward in a court shaped by factional politics, historians accept that a major defeat occurred while treating its detailed causes with caution.

The Ming shi account, in Wade's translation, independently records that in 1377–1378 Champa fought a major war with the Annam king, during which the king died. This is valuable confirmation from a source with no stake in the Vietnamese court's account of its own failures. Wade also notes that the battle was near Vijaya and links it to the narrative of the French historian Georges Maspero. The Chinese record does not supply the operational detail.

The campaign illustrates the demands of a long-distance seaborne invasion. An army sent south needed supplies for weeks, depended on coastal navigation and weather, and had to defeat a defending force that knew the local rivers and passes and could choose the terms of battle. The Cham defenders, according to the Vietnamese accounts, took advantage of these conditions. Later in 1377 Cham forces again attacked the Vietnamese capital, turning a defensive victory into an offensive one.

The Trần emperor's southern expedition of 1376–1377 provides a reverse perspective on the dangers of overconfidence after a previous humiliation. The campaign required movement through constrained coastal corridors toward the region of Vijaya, while the defending Cham forces could exploit knowledge of local routes and the invaders' distance from their own supply base. The Vietnamese chronicle tradition places the emperor's death in a destructive engagement after the advance toward the Cham center. Accounts of warnings, decisions, and battlefield deception may preserve memories of real tactical disputes, but their dramatic details were also well suited to later moral explanations of a ruler's failed judgment.

Two distinctions matter in assigning responsibility. Officials who managed supplies did not necessarily command the same troops as those approaching the decisive engagement, and their withdrawal should not be confused with a different commander's refusal or inability to reinforce the army. The record links Hồ Quý Ly to provisioning and describes Đỗ Tử Bình's failure to support the emperor; these roles need to be treated separately. The aftermath was more than a court succession crisis. The loss of the emperor and elite troops reduced the dynasty's capacity to secure southern districts, created opportunities for further Cham attacks, and made the material burden of war more difficult to sustain.

Section 45.28

Renewed Cham Campaigns, 1378–1389: River Warfare, Territory, and Political Pressure

After 1377 Cham forces attacked the Vietnamese heartland repeatedly. Vietnamese chronicles report raids in 1378 and later years, attacks in Thanh Hóa and Nghệ An, and campaigns along the rivers leading toward the capital. Trần forces won some engagements and lost others, the conventional chronicle sequence credits Hồ Quý Ly with checking Cham advances in some operations in the early 1380s but also records a severe reverse in 1389. The specific movements remain dependent on later court narratives. Control of the contested territories between the Red River delta and the northern Cham plains shifted, and the capital remained under threat.

The Ming shi account gives a Cham-centered view of the same decade. In 1380–1381 Cham envoys reported to the Ming court that Champa had been defeated by Annam in a naval battle after earlier victories, and the Ming emperor responded with an admonition to both sides about the costs of war, comparing them to the snipe and mussel of a Chinese proverb that benefit only the fisherman. In 1383–1387 Cham tribute missions to Nanjing included elephant tusks and, in 1386–1387, a son of the Cham ruler traveling to court. The Ming also recorded in the late 1380s that the Cham ruler took a quarter of Cambodian tribute passing through his waters, which angered the emperor. These entries show a Cham ruler conducting active diplomacy and commerce while waging war, and they confirm that Vietnamese military pressure operated in both directions, with victories and defeats on each side.

The campaigns challenged the established balance. Cham fleets and armies could threaten the capital, the Trần court's fiscal and military problems compounded their effects, and the survival of the monarchy was in doubt. Historians link the instability to the political rise of Hồ Quý Ly, who gained authority as the court's defender and eventually replaced it.

The campaigns following 1377 must not be presented as an uninterrupted sequence of Cham victories. The military balance fluctuated: northern forces sometimes defended river routes and defeated raiding parties, while at other moments Cham formations penetrated frontier districts and threatened major towns. Military actions attributed in Vietnamese chronicles to Hồ Quý Ly and other commanders belong to distinct years and circumstances, and the details should not be smoothed into one dramatic confrontation. Campaign records identify points of movement and the claims of commanders more readily than they document the actual size of armies or the number of civilians displaced.

Repeated operations could have cumulative economic effects even without permanent conquest. Farmers might abandon vulnerable areas, officials might struggle to assess fields, and the movement of grain and labor to support defenses could interrupt local production. Raids against coastal settlements were also attempts to redirect wealth and allegiance, not only to occupy territory. The changing situation in Thanh Hóa, Nghệ An, and Thuận Hóa shows how attacks along rivers could expose the weaknesses of an inland administrative hierarchy. At the same time, Cham crews and soldiers faced the considerable cost of returning northward with prisoners and goods while avoiding hostile positions. This was a prolonged struggle over resources and political authority, not a single thirty-year battle.

Neither side could treat the river landscape as an empty stage for armies. A defended passage could be supplied from adjoining villages; a naval squadron depended on timber, crews, and seasonal movement; and the purchase or seizure of grain transferred part of the cost of campaigning to households outside the royal courts. The contrast between a chronicler's list of victories and these material requirements is central to explaining why raids could succeed while permanent occupation remained difficult. After an attack withdrew, political institutions might be restored, but damaged fields, displaced labor, and contested obligations could continue to affect the region. The chapter should therefore measure military success against the ability to sustain authority, not only the ability to reach a capital.

Section 45.29

The Death of Chế Bồng Nga in 1390 and the Changing Balance of Power

Vietnamese chronicles report that in 1390 Chế Bồng Nga led another campaign against the Vietnamese coast. A Cham defector is said to have identified the king's boat to the Vietnamese commander Trần Khát Chân, whose forces used firearms to kill him. After his death, a Cham general reportedly took the body south, and succession within Champa was contested; some Cham elites reportedly moved to Đại Việt.

The Ming shi account tells a different story. Wade's translation records that the Cham ruler A-da-a-zhe "went astray from the proper ways of a ruler" in the Chinese compilers' words, and that a senior minister named Ge-sheng plotted against him and in the twenty-third year of Hongwu, 1390–1391, killed the king and made himself ruler. The following year Ge-sheng's Grand Steward presented a memorial and tribute, which the Ming emperor refused because he loathed the usurpation, and normal tribute resumed only in 1397–1398. If A-da-a-zhe is indeed Chế Bồng Nga, then Chinese and Vietnamese traditions give incompatible accounts of his death, one a battlefield killing by an enemy, the other an assassination by his own minister. Possibly the two are connected, for example if a Cham general seized power in the confusion after a battlefield death, as the Vietnamese tradition itself suggests, but the sources do not say so, and this chapter does not assert it.

Whatever the manner of the king's death, the consequences were significant. Cham military pressure on the north diminished, Vietnamese courts regained confidence, and Cham politics entered a period of succession struggles that benefited Đại Việt. The Trần dynasty was itself nearly finished. Within a decade Hồ Quý Ly seized the throne and launched a new southern policy (Section 45.30).

The disagreement over Chế Bồng Nga's death is an unusually instructive case of how dynastic histories construct causation. A Vietnamese battlefield narrative recognizes the action of a defender and can make the enemy ruler's death the decisive moment when the balance turned. The Ming shi account, as translated by Wade, instead frames the killing of A-da-a-zhe as the rebellion of a minister, Ge-sheng, and connects it with the Ming court's refusal to recognize the usurper. Neither source was produced as an independent investigation into the fate of a Cham king. The first privileged the Vietnamese commander's success; the second judged kingship through Chinese concepts of loyal service and rightful succession.

The identification of A-da-a-zhe with Chế Bồng Nga is itself tentative, and the dates in different calendar systems should not be treated as interchangeable without explanation. The historian cannot resolve the contradiction by inventing a hidden episode in which both accounts are literally true. What can be established is that the period around 1390 witnessed a major change in the effectiveness of Cham offensives and disputes over political legitimacy. Those changes help explain the subsequent initiative of the Hồ regime, but they should not erase the separate causes of the Trần dynasty's collapse.

Section 45.30

The Hồ Dynasty, Southern Campaigns, and the Frontier Changes of 1402–1403

Hồ Quý Ly, who had risen to dominance at the Trần court, deposed the last Trần rulers and founded the Hồ dynasty in 1400 (Chapter 44 treats the Trần collapse). He had made his reputation in the wars against Champa, and his government turned to the south early. Vietnamese chronicles report campaigns against Champa in 1401 and 1402, after which the Cham ruler, known in Cham sources as Jaya Simhavarman VI and in Vietnamese sources as Ba Đích Lại, is said to have ceded the territories of Chiêm Động and Cổ Lũy, south of the Hải Vân Pass, in 1402 or 1403. The Hồ government reportedly organized these lands into districts, commonly named Thăng, Hoa, Tư, and Nghĩa, and encouraged or compelled northerners to settle there.

The Ming shi account preserves a very different description of the same events. Wade's translation records that in 1403–1404 the Cham king, whom the Chinese name Zhan-ba-di-lai, offered tribute to the newly enthroned Yongle emperor and accused Annam of having invaded Champa. In 1404–1405 a Cham memorial to the Ming court alleged that Annam had attacked with naval forces, had seized the goods conferred on Cham envoys, had given the Cham king a hat, robes, and a seal and thereby treated him as a subject, and had occupied a place the Chinese text calls Sha-li-ya and other parts of Champa; the memorialist said Champa feared it could not survive and asked to be taken within the empire. Wade notes that the location of Sha-li-ya is unknown, though one suggestion is the port of Quy Nhơn.

Zottoli's broader source-critical interpretation of southern coastal politics offers grounds to question whether the Hồ regime exercised sustained control over all territory claimed after 1400; claims in Chinese and Vietnamese narratives do not map straightforwardly onto local administration. The resulting reconstruction remains disputed, and the different types of evidence must be considered separately. The two accounts are not necessarily contradictory, since a cession extracted under military pressure could be described by one side as a gift and by the other as coercion, but they differ sharply in what they say about Cham consent. The distinction between dynastic claim and effective control is also critical. The Hồ regime was short-lived, its administration in the new districts was thin, the districts were far from the capital and populated largely by Cham-speaking people, and they were exposed to Cham counterattack. The episode shows how ambitious expansion could outrun the administrative capacity to sustain it, and why a reported cession in a Vietnamese chronicle cannot by itself establish who actually controlled the ground.

The southern campaigns under the Hồ dynasty belong to a different governmental setting from the wars of the 1370s. Hồ Quý Ly assumed the throne in 1400 after building power within the Trần court, and the new regime attempted to reorganize state resources and strengthen its position against both domestic and external opponents. The reported expansion toward Chiêm Động and Cổ Lũy can therefore be read as a project of securing revenue, manpower, and access to strategic routes as well as a response to remembered Cham offensives. It would be misleading to assume that a proclamation of cession meant immediate rule in the affected valleys.

The location of the proposed new administrative districts also needs precision. A name recorded in Vietnamese may refer to a broad area rather than a securely mapped medieval town. Military occupation, settlement by newcomers, and recognition by existing elites were separate processes that could move at different speeds. The subsequent Ming invasion of Đại Việt disrupted any new administrative framework before it had necessarily become routine. Zottoli's broader argument about coastal and highland networks encourages the historian to keep open the possibility that claims of territorial incorporation outpaced the ability of the Hồ court to enforce them.

Section 45.31

Ming Conquest of Đại Việt and Cham Political Opportunities, 1407–1428

In 1407 the Ming dynasty conquered Đại Việt and ended the Hồ regime, ruling the region directly until the Lê restoration of 1428. The disappearance of an independent Vietnamese monarchy removed the party with which Cham rulers had negotiated, and it created opportunities that Cham leaders used with skill. Wade's translation shows that Ming forces moved against the Hồ in 1406–1407, and that Champa, instructed by the Ming to guard its territory and to seize Annamese fleeing across its borders, attacked and took control of the territory Annam had occupied. The Chinese record states that Cham forces captured two Hồ followers and presented them as prisoners to the emperor, who was pleased by this military assistance and sent a eunuch envoy with silver and silks. Zheng He's fleet visited Champa in 1408–1409 and again in the 1410s, which underlines the importance the Ming court gave to the Cham coast as a maritime waypoint.

The relationship was not simple alliance. When Trần claimants resisted Ming rule, the Ming records show that Champa was ordered in 1415–1416 to assist with troops but did not. A Ming minister reported that the Cham king was divided in loyalty: he had delayed his troops, supplied gold, silk, and war elephants to the Trần claimant Trần Quý Khoáng, who in turn gave the Cham king the daughter of a man the text calls Li Cang, possibly the Hồ ruler Hồ Hán Thương according to Wade's note, and had made a treaty with the claimant's uncle to attack the districts under the Ming prefecture of Sheng-hua, a name that corresponds roughly to the districts of Quảng Nam and Quảng Ngãi. The Ming court, which had just pacified Đại Việt and did not want to commit further troops, merely ordered Champa to return the occupied territory, and the king sent an envoy to admit guilt.

This episode, preserved only in Chinese records, shows several things. Cham rulers were able to shift partners and to act in their own territorial interest, backing different parties in the Vietnamese struggle for independence and seizing districts south of Hải Vân. They also show the limits of Cham opportunity, since the Ming court sought influence over Champa and did not consistently favor Cham claims. The territorial situation in the early 1420s was therefore unsettled, and the claims of the Hồ period had been partly undone.

The Ming occupation of Đại Việt beginning in 1407 changed the frontier by removing the independent northern dynasty as a direct competitor. For Cham rulers, this created possible opportunities to negotiate with Chinese officials, exploit disputes over southern districts, and seek recognition outside Vietnamese court channels. Yet Ming intervention was not simply a restoration of an earlier order: the Chinese government had its own strategic objectives and classified neighboring courts through a tributary hierarchy. Whether a territory was claimed by Ming officials, Cham leaders, or a local intermediary does not necessarily establish the power that any one of them exercised in a distant village.

Wade's translation of the Ming shi reveals that officials could accuse Champa of aiding opponents of the Ming occupation, and that the Cham government sought to protect its relations with the imperial court. Such records should be read in the context of petitions and rival claims. Envoys had incentives to describe a neighbor as aggressive, loyal, or disobedient depending on what they hoped to obtain. The period also reminds us that coastal politics cannot be reduced to an encounter between two perpetual nation-states. The northern political structure changed dramatically, while maritime traders and Cham regional authorities continued pursuing more immediate interests.

Section 45.32

The Restoration of the Lê Monarchy and Relations with Champa, 1428–1445

After Lê Lợi's victory over the Ming in 1427 and his enthronement in 1428, the new Lê court needed to secure recognition from Ming China, consolidate authority at home, and manage frontiers. Relations with Champa combined diplomacy and wariness. Vietnamese records mention Cham missions and Vietnamese expectations of tribute, and disputes arose over the southern districts around Hóa Châu and Thăng Hoa that the Hồ had organized and the Cham had partly retaken. Both sides continued to send envoys to the Ming court, where each could present its case.

The Ming records show how the Ming court viewed this diplomacy. In 1436–1437 the prefect of Qiong on Hainan advised that Champa's annual tribute missions imposed great cost, and the court approved a regulation limiting them to once every three years, in line with practice for Siam and other states. Wade's translation adds a revealing comment: Cham traders gained advantage from trade in China, and although the order was issued, it was not observed. The detail shows that, for the Cham side, tribute missions were also commercial ventures. In 1441–1442 the Cham king died, and his successor, a figure whom the Ming shi calls Mo-he-bi-gai (the Maha Bí Cai of Vietnamese sources), was enfeoffed by a Ming mission. The Ming text itself contains an inconsistency about his relationship to the dead king, which Wade's notes identify as probably a compilers' error, and which illustrates how uncertain Cham royal genealogy remains.

The early Lê rulers faced their own succession tensions, and the court's ability to project force southward was limited until the reign of the young emperor Lê Nhân Tông, whose regency government launched the campaign of 1446.

After the defeat of Ming forces and the establishment of the Lê dynasty, the priorities of the northern court were not exclusively southern. Maintaining authority over recent allies, establishing succession, restoring revenue collection, and dealing with upland political formations were central to the new monarchy. The availability of an experienced military establishment did not itself dictate an immediate conquest of Champa. Relations with the southern coast changed through local incidents, requests for redress, diplomatic missions, and the ability of the court to organize a campaign over a long distance.

On the Cham side, the existence of Ming investiture and continuing tribute missions suggests diplomatic options that did not depend on Vietnamese recognition. The Chinese court's records about quarrels with Annam tell us what envoys chose to report, while the Vietnamese annals preserve the claims used to justify frontier intervention. A historically sound narrative should compare those claims rather than assign all initiative to one side. The later attack of 1446 becomes more intelligible when seen as the culmination of a changing political and military situation, not as the automatic resumption of an ancient campaign of southward expansion.

Section 45.33

The Vietnamese Campaign of 1446 and the Capture of Vijaya

Vietnamese chronicles report that in 1446 the government of Lê Nhân Tông sent an expedition against Champa after reports of Cham raids on the frontier. The army, commanded by officials such as Lê Thụ and Trịnh Khả, captured Vijaya and reportedly seized the Cham ruler, Bí Cai (Maha Bí Cai in the Vietnamese form). The court is said to have installed a relative as a replacement ruler and returned north.

The Ming shi offers both the Vietnamese court's presentation of the origins and the Cham response. Wade's translation records that in 1446–1447 the Ming court sent instructions to the Cham king after the Annam ruler complained that the Cham king, taking advantage of the emperor's youth, had earlier invaded the four districts of Thăng, Hoa, Tư, and Nghĩa and had recently attacked Hóa district repeatedly, carrying off people, livestock, and valuables. The Ming admonished both parties to keep to their own territories. The Cham account came afterward: Wade's translation reports that in the twelfth year of the Zhengtong reign, which corresponds to 1447–1448 on the Chinese calendar, a Cham envoy of the dead king's nephew Mo-he-gui-lai (Maha Quý Lai) told the court that the king, Mo-he-bi-gai, had repeatedly raised troops against Annam, that enemy forces had entered Cổ Lũy and other districts killing and plundering, and that the king had been captured. The nephew explained that he had been the designated heir, had yielded the throne to his uncle because he was young, and now, at the people's request, had begun handling state affairs, awaiting the Ming court's orders. Ming envoys were sent to enfeoff him, and in 1449–1450 the Ming ordered Annam to return the captured king, an order Annam did not obey.

The dates of the Chinese and Vietnamese accounts do not match exactly, which is a reminder of the uncertainty in Cham royal chronology. The capture of a capital in 1446 did not end Cham political resistance or diplomacy: a successor was recognized by Ming envoys, Cham rulers continued to send tribute, and the Vietnamese court had no means of governing Vijaya directly. The episode illustrates a recurring pattern, in which Vietnamese campaigns could seize a Cham center and its ruler, but without administration or settlement they did not eliminate Cham authority.

Whitmore's account of the 1446 expedition describes preparations associated with the Thanh Hóa-centered Lê nobility and the involvement of Trịnh Khả among the principal commanders. An army that had experience in the wars of the Ming occupation and in campaigns nearer the western highlands could now be directed toward the southern coast. Supplies had to be assembled near the traditional frontier, and the forces needed to move across the Hải Vân approaches before striking the port and political center in the Bình Định region. The rapid capture of Vijaya is thus a case study in how logistical preparation and organized royal command made an expedition possible, rather than proof that the target was internally helpless.

The most revealing consequence is what the Lê court did not do. The reports emphasize the capture of the Cham ruler, members of the court, animals, weapons, and other resources; they do not demonstrate an enduring Vietnamese administration of Vijaya after the army withdrew. Ming recognition of subsequent Cham claimants confirms that the political world defeated in the campaign remained capable of reorganization. The event also reveals the difference between the capture of a capital, the removal of a ruler, and the incorporation of territory. Those are three possible outcomes of war, not interchangeable descriptions of a single victory.

The campaign also belongs to the history of the emerging Lê court itself. Commanders who had helped establish the new regime possessed households, retinues, and regional political networks, and the organization of a southern expedition could reinforce bonds between those forces and the monarchy. The court's claim that the campaign was punitive may have coexisted with concern for trade routes, border districts, and the status of a rival claimant. Because the conquest was not followed by permanent occupation of the captured center, the returns were principally prisoners, property, prestige, and temporary leverage in diplomatic negotiations. This makes 1446 an important comparison for the very different administrative consequences of 1471.

Section 45.34

The Crisis of 1470–1471 and Lê Thánh Tông's Campaign against Champa

Lê Thánh Tông, who became emperor in 1460, undertook a program of administrative centralization, legal codification, and military reorganization that gave the Lê state a strength it had lacked. His government's relations with Champa deteriorated in the late 1460s. Wade's translation of the Ming shi preserves the Cham version, relayed by an envoy: in 1469–1470 Annam was extorting rhinoceroses, elephants, and other valuables from Champa and ordering Champa to serve Annam in the same way as it served the Ming court; Champa refused, and Annam sent a major expedition. The Vietnamese version is the edict of 1470, discussed below, which accuses the Cham ruler of repeated attacks on Hóa Châu and of reporting to the Ming in order to disrupt Đại Việt. These two accounts are partisan and neither can serve as independent evidence of who initiated the conflict. A reasonable summary is that the Cham ruler, called Bàn La Trà Toàn in Vietnamese sources and equated by Wade with the Pan-luo-cha-quan of the Chinese text, and the Lê court were in open dispute over the frontier districts and over the Cham ruler's relations with the Ming.

The campaign was a large joint operation. According to Vietnamese chronicles, the emperor personally led forces from the capital by sea and land in the winter of 1470–1471, the army regrouped in the frontier district of Hóa Châu, and it advanced on Vijaya, where the citadel fell in early 1471. The surviving Vietnamese narrative places military preparations and movement through the frontier in the winter of 1470–1471, with the final operations against Vijaya in 1471. Exact march dates, the use of particular weapons in individual engagements, and detailed claims about troop deployment require more secure passage-level evidence than the surviving summary permits, so they are not presented as established operational facts. The scale of mobilization is difficult to assess. The edict itself announces 100,000 men sent first and 150,000 to follow, but these figures are the emperor's rhetoric to his ancestors, not a count of troops, and they should be treated with the skepticism that applies to all premodern military figures. The chronicles report that the Cham ruler was captured and later died in captivity.

The campaign's purposes were political as well as military. Success strengthened the emperor's authority over a court that had experienced factional struggle, rewarded commanders, and provided territory and captives. It also reflected a more systematic policy of settling and administering the south than earlier campaigns had undertaken. Whitmore's studies of the Hồng Đức-era campaigns place the conquest within the emperor's wider efforts to build a centralized state and a professional army, and this chapter follows that interpretation in general terms; students should consult his analysis directly for the operational detail.

The royal edict to the ancestral temple should be analyzed as a political action in its own right. Lê Thánh Tông addressed dynastic ancestors while presenting the enemy as dangerous and morally corrupt; the text attributed broad agreement to the court and people and framed the expedition as an obligatory act of punishment. Those claims were part of the emperor's attempt to justify war in a Confucian political idiom. They cannot establish independently that every Vietnamese subject supported the campaign or that the Cham government committed every offense alleged. Its startlingly large statements about manpower belong to this rhetorical setting and should not be transcribed into a modern troop-strength table without evidence about muster, provisioning, and the composition of the forces.

The Ming shi preserves a different explanation through the words of Cham envoys, who complained of demands imposed by Annam and described their government's refusal to comply. That account, too, sought to influence the response of a more powerful court. The contrast is most valuable when students ask what each actor wanted a third party to believe: the Lê ruler appealed to ancestors and officials in support of action, while the Cham claimant addressed Ming authority in search of recognition or restraint on the invaders. A complete account of 1470–1471 must keep these documents alongside military and administrative evidence rather than allowing one persuasive text to become the narrative's unquestioned voice.

Reconstructing the actual campaign requires attention to chronology rather than collapsing preparations, mobilization, and the fall of the capital into one event. The decision to assemble fleets and land forces had to precede their advance through the coastal corridors; ships depended on suitable seasonal conditions and supplies; and the besieged Cham court could seek to defend approaches or withdraw elites and material resources. Modern reconstructions of routes and weapons help identify tactical possibilities, but the surviving records do not corroborate every proposed maneuver or particular weapon deployment. The general sequence is better established than the dispositions and numbers of the participants.

Section 45.35

The Fall of Vijaya in 1471: Death, Displacement, Conquest, and Historical Interpretation

Vietnamese chronicles report that the capture of Vijaya was accompanied by heavy killing and the capture of many thousands of people, including the Cham ruler and members of his household. Popular summaries of the chronicle quote figures of tens of thousands killed, captured, and executed, but numbers in such records are rarely reliable, they differ among accounts, and none is adopted here. The Ming shi account preserves a Cham account relayed to the Chinese court: Wade's translation records that Annam sacked the country in 1471–1472, captured the king Pan-luo-cha-quan and members of his family totaling over fifty persons, seized his seal and tallies, engaged in massive burning and looting, and occupied the territory. The king's younger brother fled into the mountains and sent an envoy to report the disaster. The Ming Ministry of War recommended that Annam be ordered to return the king and his family, but the emperor, anxious that Annam would disobey, ordered only that castigatory orders be sent with the next Annam tribute mission.

The Ming envoys sent in 1472–1473 to enfeoff the surviving brother arrived at the port of Quy Nhơn, called Xin-zhou in the Chinese text, and found it closed to them. Only then did they learn that the country had been occupied and renamed Jiao-nan prefecture, a Vietnamese administrative name. The Vietnamese court reorganized the conquered territory as a new province, Quảng Nam, and set a boundary at a mountain near the southern limit of Vijaya's region. According to the Ming account, after capturing the fugitive brother the Vietnamese established a grandson of the former king as ruler of the southern border areas, a figure whom Wade's translation calls Zhai-ya-ma-fu-an. Vietnamese chronicles describe a division of the former Cham realm into smaller polities under the Lê court's oversight, though details are debated.

The long-term effects were profound. Cham institutions in the northern and central regions weakened, the population was altered by displacement, captivity, and migration, and Vietnamese settlement increased. Modern scholarship has debated how to characterize the violence and the subsequent suppression of Cham cultural institutions. Some scholars have discussed whether aspects of the events may be described as genocidal; others stress the evidentiary limits and the difficulty of applying modern legal categories to a fifteenth-century campaign. A cautious reading acknowledges that the sources record large-scale killing and captivity, that the numbers are uncertain, and that terminology is contested.

The fall of Vijaya was a major political rupture, not the disappearance of all Cham polities, communities, or cultural traditions.

The destruction of Vijaya should be considered on several different scales. At the political level, it removed an important Cham court and enabled the Lê monarchy to claim authority over a region it had not previously held for long. At the household level, killings, enslavement, forced journeys, and the loss of agricultural resources threatened communities that cannot be reduced to royal dynastic names. At the religious level, changes in patronage could weaken sanctuaries, but the persistence of material sites and later Cham religious communities shows that cultural life was not extinguished. These outcomes were connected without being identical: conquest of a capital did not mean immediate elimination of every local organization in its wider hinterland.

The Ming testimony reported by Wade is valuable for the named persons and diplomatic initiatives it preserves, not because a Chinese bureaucratic compilation was free from political interests. Cham envoys described a devastating assault and sought restitution, while the Ming emperor hesitated to take action against Annam. Vietnamese sources, meanwhile, offered the victorious court's account of punishment and new territorial arrangements. The discrepancy between requests for restoration and the limited response is evidence of the asymmetry confronting Cham petitioners. Numbers of the dead and captured should remain explicitly reported claims. Modern terminology for mass violence can guide ethical analysis, but a sound historical judgment must specify the events and evidence before applying a modern legal classification.

Claims of finality are especially hazardous in the aftermath of a major conquest. Even where Vietnamese administrators established new districts, their control of people, fields, ports, and upland passages would have developed unevenly. The Ming court's continuing dealings with Cham claimants expose how difficult it was for an outside observer to determine who legitimately held the remaining southern territories. A violent rupture in the political organization of the central coast could coexist with local continuity in cultivation, worship, trade, and kinship. Scholarship on the later Cham polities gives independent reason to separate the collapse of Vijaya's court from the far longer history of communities that retained their language and religious traditions.

Section 45.36

Changing Frontiers: Villages, Migration, and the Incorporation of Territory

Territorial claims became administrative practice only through the slow work of registering households, assigning land, collecting taxes, and settling people, and the central coast saw several different attempts to do so. After 1069 the Lý court organized the three northern districts and, according to the Toàn thư, settled people there. After 1306–1307 the Trần court renamed Châu Ô and Châu Rí as Thuận Châu and Hóa Châu and sent officials to register households. The Hồ government in 1402–1403 reportedly organized Chiêm Động and Cổ Lũy into districts called Thăng, Hoa, Tư, and Nghĩa and encouraged or compelled northerners to settle. The Ming, who held Đại Việt from 1407 to 1427, also created a Sheng-hua prefecture whose territory, according to Wade's annotation, extended over what is today Quảng Nam and Quảng Ngãi. After 1471 the Lê court created the province of Quảng Nam. Each of these was a different regime applying its own categories to the same ground, and each depended on the people already living there.

Those people included Cham-speaking villagers, the highland populations of Mon-Khmer and Chamic speakers whom the lowland polities had long dealt with, and local elites. The citadels of Châu Sa and Cổ Lũy on the Trà Khúc river in Quảng Ngãi, which Vickery identifies as major Cham sites, lay in precisely the area that the Hồ and Ming administrations tried to organize, so the new administrations were taking over places that already had fortifications, roads, and political traditions. Some settlers were soldiers or officials; others were farmers, exiles, or people resettled by the state. Cham-speaking villagers who remained were incorporated unevenly, retaining local landholding and religious practice in some places while losing land or status in others.

Evidence for these processes is thin and mostly administrative or chronicle-based, so historians rely on place names, land records from later periods, and archaeology. The incorporation of territory was therefore a long process of negotiation and adjustment that varied by locality, not a single act in a year recorded in a chronicle. The Ming record's report, in 1446–1447, that the Lê court complained of Cham attacks on the four districts the Hồ had organized shows how fragile the early arrangements were, since the same districts had to be defended or recovered repeatedly.

The incorporation of a frontier district was a prolonged social process, not a single administrative event. A court might announce new units and appoint officials, but durable authority depended on knowing which fields produced grain, which villages owed labor, and which local families could organize compliance. The arrival of settlers could change land tenure and the languages used in disputes, while existing residents might continue to cultivate ancestral fields and maintain religious sites under unfamiliar authorities. The evidence is strongest for the court's intent to govern and weakest for the negotiations inside individual villages. Historians should resist using a later predominance of Vietnamese-speaking populations as proof that the earlier transition was rapid or peaceful.

Environmental conditions limited the reach of colonization. The coast consists of separated river plains, and the capacity to irrigate land, maintain embankments, and reach a port varied sharply by district. Control of a military road or citadel need not imply that administrators could collect the same revenues across nearby uplands. Some communities may have adapted by transferring allegiance, some by moving toward less accessible valleys, and others through marriages, trade, or service to the new authorities. Specific outcomes require place-based evidence; the point is to avoid treating a long and geographically uneven process as an undifferentiated advance of one people into another's empty territory.

Section 45.37

War Captives, Enslavement, Dependent Labor, and Population Transfers

Campaigns from 982 onward produced captives. Vietnamese chronicles report that victorious armies returned north with prisoners, including members of royal households, soldiers, artisans, and entertainers. Captives could be settled in villages, assigned to aristocratic households or religious establishments, or put to labor. Later sources refer to communities of Cham descent in northern settlements, a reminder that displacement moved people in both directions. In the 980s, according to the Chinese sources as summarized by Maspéro, many people fled from Champa to Hainan during the turbulent period associated with Lưu Kế Tông, which shows that refugees also moved by sea.

Captivity was not only a Vietnamese practice. Wade's translation of the Ming shi records that in 1407 Cham forces captured two followers of the defeated Hồ regime and sent them to the Ming emperor as prisoners, and that the Ming emperor was angered in 1388–1389 when the Cham king took a quarter of the tribute that Cambodia sent through his waters. The same record documents the largest Cham loss, in 1471, when the Vietnamese captured the king and members of his family totaling over fifty persons and seized the royal seal and tallies. Court-level captivity is relatively well recorded because rulers and their households mattered to chroniclers; the fate of the larger number of ordinary captives is mostly unrecorded, apart from the chronicles' general statements that many thousands were taken.

Captive status was not uniform. Some captives, particularly skilled artisans or court personnel, may have been treated differently from agricultural laborers, and over generations descendants could be absorbed into the surrounding society. Populations left in contested territories faced destruction of property, loss of rulers, and pressure to assimilate. The sources seldom record the experiences of captives directly, so reconstruction depends on administrative references and the later survival of communities.

Captivity is one of the most consistently recorded consequences of medieval warfare in this region, yet dynastic chronicles describe it principally from the perspective of the ruler acquiring people. Categories such as prisoners, servants, soldiers, entertainers, and relocated households may represent very different experiences. A captive skilled in music or construction might enter an elite establishment, while others performed labor in settlements away from their home districts. Their social positions were shaped by legal status, household membership, and dependence on a patron, not simply by whether they continued to speak Cham. The presence of Cham names or performance practices at a Vietnamese court is evidence of transmission under unequal conditions and should not be presented only as voluntary cultural exchange.

Movements in the other direction are harder to document, especially for non-elites. Cham rulers captured people in their raids and could seek to replenish labor or reward followers by relocating them. Warfare might also cause flight without a formal order from either monarchy. In all cases the numbers reported by medieval writers should be separated from the better-established fact that conquest redistributed population and labor. A responsible social history asks who had power to decide where a person lived, who profited from that movement, and how displaced communities might retain social ties or religious practices despite the loss of political security.

Section 45.38

Women, Marriage, Households, and Cross-Cultural Social Relations

Royal marriages such as that of 1306 are the best-recorded relationships between the two societies, but they were exceptional. Royal women appear in other sources too. The Ming shi records that the Ming court conferred silks on a Cham king and his consort in 1436–1437, and that in 1415–1416 a Trần claimant gave the Cham king the daughter of a man the text calls Li Cang, whom Wade's note tentatively identifies with the Hồ ruler Hồ Hán Thương, as part of an arrangement against the Ming, which shows that women were used as pledges in alliances among the same rival claimants who fought each other for the throne of Đại Việt. In 1447–1448 a Cham claimant explained his authority in terms of male descent, nephew and uncle, in the language the Ming court expected; whether that reflected Cham practice or the conventions of petitioning a Chinese court is not clear.

Most women in the frontier zone lived in village households whose organization is poorly documented. Southworth's reading of the Võ Cạnh inscription, which Vickery summarizes, suggests that some early Cham donations followed matrilineal patterns, and modern ethnographic literature describes matrilineal practice among Cham communities, but historians should be cautious about projecting that backward onto the medieval period, and Vietnamese households combined patrilineal ideals with broad practical roles for women in farming and markets that left few written traces.

Cross-cultural relationships, including marriage, concubinage, and captivity, undoubtedly occurred where populations mixed, but the sources rarely mention them. Historians should avoid treating royal marriages as representative of ordinary experience. The chronicles' interest in Huyền Trân tells us more about court politics than about the lives of women in frontier villages.

Royal marriage provides a rare instance in which a woman is visible by name in the political record, but it is a poor substitute for a history of ordinary women's lives. Household labor, inheritance, residence after marriage, and religious patronage may have differed among communities on the coast and within the Vietnamese delta. Available sources make it difficult to describe a single Cham family system, still less to treat it as unchanging for centuries. Later ethnographic descriptions can suggest questions, but they cannot be projected backward without corroborating evidence. The chapter therefore needs to distinguish what inscriptions say about elite donors from what can be inferred about the thousands of less visible women and men who maintained estates, gardens, markets, and shrines.

Cross-cultural households were likely to have experienced the frontier differently from royal advisers. People could learn a neighboring language for commerce, negotiate marriage connections, or continue to identify with local religious and kinship networks after officials altered the name of a district. Such possibilities explain how cultural boundaries could be permeable even when courts were at war. They are not a license to invent individual stories: where no testimony survives, the historian should explain the range of institutions that shaped people's choices and acknowledge that coercion, affection, opportunity, and necessity could coexist in the same community.

Section 45.39

Ports, Maritime Commerce, River Valleys, and Commercial Competition

Commercial geography shaped political competition. Rulers who controlled ports and river mouths collected revenue from trade, and those who commanded forest and upland products had goods to exchange. Vickery stresses that until the twelfth century navigation and maritime trade between Southeast Asia, China, and India were dominated by local groups, among them Austronesian speakers including the Cham, and that Cham polities were ports at the mouths of great rivers. Southworth's argument, as Vickery reports it, links shifts in the prominence of Cham regions to shifts in trade routes. The decline of Guangzhou and of early Tang trade in the mid-eighth century reduced the commercial dominance of the Thu Bồn valley, and as South China Sea trade moved to the Red River ports a new pattern favored the ports of Nha Trang and Phan Rang on the sea-route from Java to northern Vietnam.

The Ming record gives a closer view of what Cham polities exported in the fourteenth to sixteenth centuries. Elephants, elephant tusks, rhinoceros horn, and a fragrant aromatic wood called calambac (jia-nan) were offered as tribute, and the text says that the finest calambac was produced on only one mountain, which the chieftains guarded, cutting off the hands of offenders who collected it. In 1373–1374 a Cham envoy presented seventy thousand jin of sapan wood captured from ships of pirates whom the Cham ruler said he had destroyed. The island of Pulo Condore, which the text calls Kun-lun, was the place where ships bound west waited for the winds, and the Ming record sets the Cham coast at the center of those routes. The Chinese prefect of Hainan complained in 1436–1437 that the tribute missions were costly, and the court limited them to every third year, but Wade's translation adds that Cham traders profited from trade in China and so did not observe the limit.

Competition for these resources and for the profits of exchange gave rulers motives for war and for diplomacy. Control of a port did not guarantee control of the interior, and merchants adjusted their routes to political conditions. Cham ceramics and kiln sites such as Gò Sành in Bình Định testify to local production linked to maritime exchange in a later period. Historians debate how much trade revenue funded Cham military campaigns, as the evidence is incomplete.

The commercial value of a Cham port derived partly from its connections with the interior. Aromatic woods, animal products, and other commodities originated in upland environments, where collection and transport required the participation of communities often absent from dynastic narratives. Merchants near a river mouth could not independently produce those goods, and a ruler's claims to taxes or monopoly access depended on relationships extending beyond a coastal city. The Chinese description of a closely guarded source of exceptionally valuable fragrant wood illustrates the political value attached to control over collection, but its punitive details must be read as a mediated description, not a surveyed legal code applying throughout the coast.

Tributary missions were also commercial expeditions. Wade's translation records repeated complaints that Cham missions imposed expenses on officials in China, while Cham merchants had incentives to return more frequently than formal schedules allowed. This exposes a tension between imperial regulation and the profits of movement across maritime networks. The survival of export goods, ceramics, and port settlements through periods of warfare shows why rulers sometimes sought peace or recognition even when territorial grievances remained unresolved. Trade was never wholly separate from politics: an embassy might open a market, a raid might disrupt shipping, and the loss of a port might alter the economic position of more distant valleys.

Section 45.40

Religion, Architecture, Art, Language, and Cultural Adaptation

Cham religious life varied by region and period, as the inscriptions show. The Thu Bồn valley sanctuary of Mỹ Sơn, with inscriptions from the fifth century to 1263, centered on devotion to Shiva in forms associated with the early king Bhadravarman. Đồng Dương, whose first stele dated 875 praises a Buddhist foundation, represents a Mahayana tradition that was nonetheless compatible with veneration of the old Mỹ Sơn lingam, as Vickery notes. Po Nagar at Nha Trang, a shrine of the south, received royal inscriptions, and Po Klong Garai at Phan Rang is linked to the rulers of the late thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries. Mỹ Sơn is a UNESCO World Heritage site, and the documentation of the site provides a strong architectural reference. Vietnamese religious life combined Buddhism, Confucian institutions, and local cults, and Vietnamese rulers sometimes incorporated Cham elements, including music, into court life. Chinese descriptions are less reliable about religion: the Ming shi account says that the Cham king followed the Buddhist religion, and it includes stories, such as the crocodile ordeal and the bile-drinking at New Year, but Wade's notes show that much of its descriptive passage is borrowed from earlier Chinese travel writings, so historians treat these as secondhand descriptions, not firsthand observation.

Artistic forms moved across the frontier, but historians avoid assuming that a particular form indicates the ethnicity of the artisan. Vickery, citing Jean Boisselier, observes that sculpted monsters at Tháp Mắm in Bình Định show Vietnamese or Chinese influence and, with Southworth, that the site may date to the late fourteenth or fifteenth century, which is an argument for a dating, not proof of who carved them. Dating of Cham architecture is generally tentative, so statements about which court influenced which temple need to be hedged. Place names also show interaction: many central Vietnamese place names derive from Cham words. The fall of Vijaya and earlier campaigns destroyed religious institutions, and the last Cham inscription dates to 1456, but Cham traditions continued in southern temples and communities, and some sacred sites were reinterpreted by new populations.

Religious geography reveals continuity that military chronicles often overlook. A sanctuary such as Mỹ Sơn was not simply a monument built by one ruler and abandoned at his death; successive donors could restore sculptures, re-inscribe authority, and connect contemporary patronage to revered predecessors. Po Nagar and the sanctuaries of Panduranga occupied other regional religious worlds, so a history of temples must resist forcing them into a single chronological sequence corresponding to presumed moves of the royal capital. The changing distribution of dated inscriptions is evidence for shifts in patronage and surviving records, but not a reliable measure of the total number of worshippers or of the disappearance of practices elsewhere.

The relationship between visual form and political power is likewise complex. Sculptures may borrow motifs across Cham, Khmer, Vietnamese, and wider Asian traditions without requiring direct conquest or the migration of every artisan. The rebuilding of a shrine after conflict could express local resilience, the patron's claim to renewed legitimacy, or both. Later Vietnamese communities sometimes incorporated or renamed existing sacred places, creating new meanings layered upon older ones. Those processes require site-specific archaeological and textual study. Present-day conservation should preserve the material record without treating it as a static representation of a single ethnic identity that remained unchanged through the centuries.

The study of material culture also requires an explicit distinction between restoration and authenticity. A temple visible today has often undergone destruction, reconstruction, alteration, and modern conservation; its standing form need not reproduce one particular medieval moment. Inscriptions may establish a historical patron but cannot automatically date every surviving brick course or sculpture. Conversely, a structure lacking a readable inscription can remain valuable evidence for technology, landscape use, and the continuity of ritual practice. The emerging digital Corpus of the Inscriptions of Campā provides a way for students to trace individual texts, places, and editions rather than citing a general story of Cham civilization without identifying the surviving record.

Section 45.41

Champa after Vijaya: Surviving Polities, Panduranga, and Diplomacy

After 1471, Cham political authority continued in the south, especially in Panduranga, and Cham claimants kept using diplomacy to seek recognition and support. Geoffrey Wade's annotated translation of the Ming shi account of Champa, published as an Asia Research Institute working paper in 2003, covers events down to 1544 and includes a part that, as Wade observes, appears in no other historical tradition: the attempts of Cham royal family members, or people hoping to be considered such, to gain recognition after the sack of Vijaya. Through these accounts one follows claimants who traveled into the Lao highlands and then to Hainan Island.

The Ming record, taken as a sequence of identifiable episodes, shows a succession of Cham actors rather than a single exile court. In 1472–1473 the surviving brother of the captured king, Pan-luo-cha-yue, who had fled into the mountains, requested enfeoffment, and the Ming envoys were turned away at Quy Nhơn. A Vietnamese-installed grandson of the former king governed the southern border areas. When Ming envoys went to enfeoff him in 1478–1479, they learned that he had died and that his younger brother, Gu-lai, claimed the throne; the people of Champa told the envoys that the grandson had been murdered by Gu-lai and that Annam had meanwhile enthroned another Cham man, Ti-po-tai, with a false imperial order. The Ming envoys, who the Chinese record says were motivated by private trading interests, enfeoffed Ti-po-tai in exchange for a large bribe, an act for which one of them was later condemned.

In 1481–1482 Gu-lai's envoy asked the Ming to compel the Vietnamese to return the Cham territories, claiming that the original country had comprised twenty-seven regions, with four provinces, one prefecture, and twenty-two districts stretching more than 3,500 li, from Amaravati in the north to the Cambodian border, and that only five regions from Panduranga to Cambodia remained. The Ming court issued an order to the Vietnamese emperor, whom it calls Li Hao, to return the territory. Gu-lai was enfeoffed in 1484–1485 on Hainan, to which he had traveled via Laos, and in 1487–1488 an official, Tu Yong, escorted him back to Champa with 2,000 troops and twenty ships, which the Vietnamese did not oppose. Gu-lai's later requests for a Ming army were refused in 1489–1490, on the ground that the Ming's ancestral injunctions listed Annam and Champa among states not to be attacked, and the Ming court advised the Cham king to strengthen himself rather than rely on Chinese troops.

These claims must be treated critically. The Chinese officials who wrote the record viewed the Cham king as a petitioner, the envoys had interests of their own, and the figure of 3,500 li is a claim made by a ruler seeking support, not a measurement. But the record decisively shows that Cham diplomacy did not cease with the fall of Vijaya, that rival claimants contested legitimacy, that both Cham and Vietnamese actors used Ming recognition for their own purposes, and that the Ming court responded with limited concern. It also shows that the Vietnamese-held Xin-zhou (Quy Nhơn) remained occupied as late as 1499–1500, when an aging Cham king asked that his son inherit the fief. The record continues with later Cham envoys down to 1543–1544. These facts are essential for understanding why 1471 cannot be treated as the end of Cham history.

Post-1471 diplomacy complicates the distinction between a kingdom's defeat and the end of a political tradition. Rival Cham claimants did not merely seek pity from Ming officials; they requested titles, seals, recognition of territories, and sometimes military assistance. The competing accounts about who had murdered or displaced whom were intended to establish legitimate succession before an imperial audience. The Ming court could use those disputes to defend its own hierarchy while avoiding costly confrontation with Đại Việt. Wade's notes also expose failures within the Chinese system, including envoys interested in private trade and an investiture carried out in exchange for a bribe. The resulting record is invaluable precisely because it reveals competing claims rather than a single authoritative succession.

Gu-lai's circuitous journey through Laos and Hainan demonstrates that Cham political networks did not terminate at a coastline occupied by Vietnamese forces. Access to upland routes and islands could provide alternatives to direct travel through contested ports. His return with a Ming escort shows that ceremonial support could have practical consequences, yet the subsequent refusal to provide an army illustrates the limits of that protection. This distinction between symbolic legitimacy, local authority, and effective military control should structure any account of the surviving southern polities. The Ming documents make Cham actors visible, but the everyday experience of Panduranga's communities still requires other evidence.

Section 45.42

Cham Communities, Displacement, Diaspora, and Religious Continuity

Cham communities survived in several regions. In southern Vietnam, Cham villages preserved language, temple traditions, and agricultural practices; some communities adopted Islam over time, while others maintained Hindu-derived traditions, producing the religious diversity of later Cham society. Communities also settled in Cambodia and elsewhere, forming diasporas connected by commerce, kinship, and religion. In the highlands, the Chamic languages Jarai, Rhade, Chru, and Roglai are spoken by communities whose languages Vickery, following Diffloth, describes as developed from Cham spoken by populations of Mon-Khmer origin, which means that the Cham language had spread inland and that its speakers were not only people who retreated south.

On Hainan Island the Chamic-speaking Utsat community is often linked to Cham migration. Wade's notes on the Ming shi account point to the Cham king Gu-lai, who in the 1480s brought his family from Laos to Ya-zhou in southern Hainan, and suggest that the Utsat may derive in part from Chams who came with him, but conclude that it is more likely Gu-lai went there because a Cham population already lived on the island; the date and circumstances remain debated.

Historians distinguish documented migration from broad assumptions about the origins of later Cham populations. Some movements followed specific wars, others occurred gradually, and many cannot be dated. The continuing development of religious and cultural traditions, including manuscripts and rituals, shows that Cham identity was not static but adapted to new circumstances.

Migration after warfare was not a single, uniform exodus. People could move for immediate refuge, join existing trading communities, follow religious teachers, or seek patrons who controlled safer ports. Later Cham communities in Cambodia and the Malay world demonstrate the importance of regional connections, but not every community can be traced to one event in 1471. Claims about the exact number of refugees, their route, or their role in founding a later polity require independent documentation rather than a compelling origin story. The broader history of Cham mobility stretches across many centuries and included exchange, marriage, labor, and religious travel as well as displacement by armies.

Cultural continuity likewise does not mean that everything remained unchanged. Literary traditions, religious institutions, forms of community leadership, and methods of recording genealogy developed as Cham groups adapted to different political circumstances. Some sacred sites remained in use; others were abandoned, appropriated, or reinterpreted. Later Cham manuscripts and oral accounts preserve historical knowledge, but their surviving copies may be far younger than the events described. The proper comparison is therefore not between an allegedly pure medieval culture and a modern remnant. It is between changing practices that communities continued to sustain, revise, and transmit despite changing rulers and borders.

Section 45.43

From the Medieval Frontier to the Early-Modern Nguyễn Period

This section provides a bounded overview; fuller treatment belongs to Chapter 88 and the subsequent Nguyễn-era chapters. Vietnamese sources and later scholarship identify several turning points: the advance of the Nguyễn lords into Phú Yên around 1611, further expansion toward Khánh Hòa around 1653, a campaign against Panduranga in 1692–1693 that altered its status, and the termination of the Panduranga polity's autonomous status in 1832 under the Nguyễn emperor Minh Mạng. These dates are conventional signposts in the later scholarship; the political meaning of each event requires separate study, particularly in the specialist history of Panduranga.

These dates show that Cham political history extended long after 1471. Research on the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries demonstrates that Panduranga negotiated, paid tribute, and resisted, and that its ultimate incorporation was a nineteenth-century development, not a consequence of the fifteenth-century conquest alone. Vickery's observation that Nam tiến, if the term applies at all, applies only from the fifteenth century gains force from this record, since the conquest of the southernmost Cham region took roughly another four centuries after the fall of Vijaya.

The later history of Panduranga makes clear that the political consequences of 1471 were geographically uneven. Vietnamese control expanded into some northern and central coastal areas, but southern Cham rulers continued to negotiate recognition and exercise forms of authority under increasingly restrictive conditions. Nguyễn-era campaigns and settlements were punctuated by agreements, local resistance, and changes in administrative status. The dates traditionally associated with 1611, 1653, and 1692–1693 are useful markers, but they describe different developments and should not be arranged as though they were identical stages in a predetermined territorial program.

The abolition of remaining Panduranga autonomy under Minh Mạng in 1832 belongs to a nineteenth-century state-building context very different from the Lê campaign of 1471. Policies toward taxes, local leadership, and religious institutions had evolved, and Cham communities faced the power of a state whose administrative capacities and political claims differed from those of medieval courts. The chronological distance matters: fuller examination of those changes belongs to Chapter 88 and the later Nguyễn history. Its function here is to prevent a premature ending: Vijaya's fall was decisive, but it was not the final chapter of Cham governance or cultural survival.

Following the later history too far in this chapter would obscure changes in political context. The Nguyễn lords' southern frontier existed amid new patterns of overseas commerce, competing Vietnamese courts, and relationships with Cambodia and the Malay world unlike those of the Lý period. The nineteenth-century imperial state then sought a form of administrative integration that cannot simply be read backward into fifteenth-century conquest. These differences give the later chapters their distinct historical responsibilities. Chapter 45 establishes the survival of Cham political and cultural life and names the turning points; it does not need to compress four additional centuries of social and institutional transformation into a few lines of imagined continuity.

Section 45.44

Champa and Đại Việt in Historical Perspective: Conflict, Coexistence, and the Limits of Nam tiến

The history of Champa and Đại Việt shows two interacting political worlds. Political fragmentation within Champa made decisive conquest difficult but also prevented united resistance. Regional geography, with its string of river-mouth polities separated by passes and headlands, shaped campaigns and administration. Economic resources, including trade in forest and sea products and the revenues of ports, supported military action on both sides. Marriage diplomacy and religious exchange created connections, and settlement transformed the frontier slowly.

The record is also uneven. Cham inscriptions give a detailed view of wars with Angkor and almost none of the wars with Đại Việt; Vietnamese chronicles give narratives, but they are court narratives and disagree among themselves; and Chinese records preserve Cham voices only as petitions to a distant court. Revisionist work by Vickery and others has shown how much of the older story rested on fitting names from different traditions together, and several of its conclusions remain open to challenge.

Territorial transformation was real, often violent, and frequently unequal, but it was neither uninterrupted nor historically predetermined. Cham resurgence in the fourteenth century nearly destroyed the Trần dynasty, and Vietnamese advances in 1402, 1446, and 1471 were separated by years of coexistence. After 1471, Cham claimants used Ming diplomacy for more than a generation, and a Cham polity in Panduranga survived until 1832. The Nam tiến narrative is thus best treated as a later interpretive frame. Cham institutions and communities continued to exercise agency within changing circumstances.

A bilateral history changes the questions historians ask. Instead of treating each Vietnamese advance as one more inevitable step on a map, it asks what northern rulers sought at that particular moment and what rival Cham actors could do in response. It also asks whether a reported victory involved a capital, a port, a ruler, a garrison, or the lasting incorporation of villages. The distinction explains why the battles of 1044, 1446, and 1471 could have very different consequences even when chronicles used similar formulas for victory. Political outcomes depended on military resources, local alliances, administrative capacity, and the choices of communities that neither royal tradition described in full.

Comparison also reveals the strength and limits of the surviving records. Vietnamese chronicles preserve campaigns and the language of royal legitimacy; inscriptions illuminate Cham religious patronage and regional authority; Chinese diplomatic compilations preserve petitions, investitures, and outsiders' assessments; archaeology documents social and cultural activity beyond the moments of war. Each source type makes some lives visible and obscures others. The chapter's conclusion should therefore reject both triumphalist inevitability and the opposite simplification in which a single catastrophe erases centuries of Cham adaptation. The relationship between Champa and Đại Việt was made and remade through coercion, bargaining, exchange, and survival, and the surviving evidence permits a history of contingency rather than destiny.

Chapter Summary

This chapter reconstructed nearly five centuries of relations between Cham polities and Vietnamese states from both sides, using Cham inscriptions, Vietnamese chronicles, Chinese records, and archaeology, and it emphasized that these sources often disagree. Champa was a set of regional polities, associated with Indrapura, Amaravati, Vijaya, Kauthara, and Panduranga, and its unity was episodic rather than fixed.

The Vietnamese states that emerged after 939 inherited a frontier with Cham polities and developed their own interests there. The Early Lê campaign of 982 and the Lý campaigns of 1044, 1069, and 1104 produced captives, prestige, and a contested northern frontier, but they did not bring Cham polities under lasting control. The identification of the Cham king of 1069 remains disputed.

In the twelfth century, Angkor transformed the regional balance. Khmer attacks on Đại Việt and Champa, the traditionally dated Cham attack on Cambodia in 1177, whose supposed capture of Angkor is disputed, and Khmer occupation of parts of Champa kept Cham and Vietnamese courts occupied elsewhere, and they preceded a period of Cham recovery.

The Mongol-Yuan era changed the strategic environment. Cham rulers resisted a Yuan seaborne expedition by combining retreat, negotiation, and guerrilla war, and Trần rulers defeated Yuan invasions. The sources show parallel resistance rather than a formal alliance.

The visit of Trần Nhân Tông in 1301 and the marriage of Huyền Trân and Chế Mân in 1306 were significant diplomatic undertakings. The reported transfer of Châu Ô and Châu Rí led to the formation of Thuận Hóa, but the arrangement was unstable, and it was followed by conflict in 1312 and 1318.

The fourteenth century saw a major reversal. Under Chế Bồng Nga, Cham forces reached Thăng Long in 1371, destroyed a Trần army in 1377, and attacked repeatedly until his death in 1390. This history cannot be told as a continuous series of Vietnamese advances.

In the fifteenth century the Hồ, Ming, and Lê periods saw renewed Vietnamese pressure, culminating in the capture of Vijaya in 1446 and the campaign of 1470–1471, which destroyed the principal Cham center and caused heavy loss of life and displacement. Scholars debate how to characterize this violence, and numerical claims in the chronicles are uncertain.

Beyond war, the chapter examined settlement, captives, women, commerce, religion, and art. After 1471 Cham polities continued in the south, especially Panduranga, until 1832, and Cham communities and traditions survived. The Nam tiến narrative is therefore a later interpretive frame that must be tested against evidence of contingency, reversal, and continuity.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it replaces a one-directional conquest story with a history of interaction. Cham and Vietnamese rulers, merchants, soldiers, captives, and villagers shaped a frontier that was contested, negotiated, and often violent. The chapter shows how the evidence available to historians can distort the past if only one side's records are read.

The chapter also reveals larger processes: how regional polities respond to outside powers such as Angkor, the Yuan, and the Ming; how marriage and religion served diplomacy; and how geography structured both war and trade. The fourteenth-century Cham resurgence and the fifteenth-century Lê campaigns show that power could shift sharply within decades.

The chapter shaped later developments, including the Vietnamese settlement of the central coast and the survival of Panduranga until 1832. Chapter 46 compares the Yuan campaigns across Southeast Asia, and Chapter 88 examines the early-modern expansion into former Cham and Khmer lands. Students should carry forward the habit of asking whose records survive and whose do not.

Selected Historical Sources and Evidence Guide

This chapter compares contemporary or near-contemporary inscriptions, later Vietnamese court chronicles, Chinese diplomatic and dynastic compilations, and modern research. Their differences are treated as historical evidence rather than reconciled by assumption. Dates in contemporary Chinese and Vietnamese sources may reflect different calendars; claims about ancient population or troop totals should be read as reported figures, not audited statistics.
