Chenla, Zhenla, and the Transformation of the Mekong World

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

By the early seventh century CE, Chinese court records were applying a new name, transcribed as Zhenla, to political developments in the Mekong world once dominated in Chinese description by the polity called Funan. Older textbooks told a tidy story: a northern inland kingdom called Chenla rose, conquered Funan, briefly unified the region, and then split into two successor states. Read the actual inscriptions left by the rulers, officials, and religious donors of this period, however, and a far more crowded, regionally varied, and only partially centralized political world comes into view.

This chapter continues directly from the methodological lesson of Chapter 20. There, students learned that "Funan" is a Chinese textual label whose relationship to the archaeological record of the lower Mekong must be investigated rather than assumed. Chapter 21 now applies the same discipline to an even richer, and in some ways even more complicated, body of evidence. Beginning in the late sixth century with an expanding Sanskrit epigraphic record, and by the early seventh century with securely dated Old Khmer inscriptions as well, the Mekong world produced a substantial and growing corpus of inscriptions that allow historians, in the first substantial locally produced textual corpus available for reconstructing this world's political and social institutions in this textbook, to read texts commissioned by local rulers, officials, and religious donors rather than relying solely on outside observers.

This should be good news for historians, and in many ways it is. Inscriptions name real donors, real deities, real officials, and real places; they record land transfers, temple foundations, and genealogies; they allow historians to move, cautiously, beyond the outside observer's vantage point that dominated Chapter 20. But the inscriptions also complicate the tidy Chinese narrative of a single successor kingdom called Chenla. Contemporary inscriptions from this period do not announce that their rulers belong to a country called Chenla. They describe local and regional rulers, competing and cooperating lineages, temple foundations tied to specific places, and networks of religious and political patronage that do not automatically add up to one continuous, centrally administered kingdom.

Consider the interpretive choice this creates. A Chinese court chronicler, writing from a distance and organizing information according to Chinese diplomatic categories, described a polity called Zhenla succeeding, subordinating, or absorbing the polity called Funan. A Khmer-language or Sanskrit inscription, carved into stone at a specific temple by order of a specific ruler or official, describes a specific act of religious patronage, a specific genealogy, a specific grant of land or personnel, tied to a specific place and a specific moment. Neither source was produced to answer the question modern historians want to ask: was there a single, unified Chenla kingdom? Reconciling these two evidentiary streams, without letting either one silently overwrite the other, is the central task of this chapter.

This chapter's title contains a word that deserves emphasis from the outset: transformation. The sixth through eighth centuries in the Mekong world were not simply a story of one kingdom being replaced by another, nor a story of straightforward political decline after an earlier golden age. They were a period of substantial reorganization, in which political authority became visible in new places, temple-building and religious patronage expanded and diversified, and the balance of power among regional centers shifted more than once. Understanding this transformation requires holding several kinds of evidence in view simultaneously: Chinese textual accounts, a growing corpus of local inscriptions, archaeological remains at major sites such as Sambor Prei Kuk, and the broader patterns of continuity connecting this period back to the lower-Mekong world examined in Chapter 20.

Students should approach this chapter expecting a genuinely unresolved historical problem, not a corrected version of Chapter 20's uncertainty. The best current scholarship does not offer a clean, settled account of "the Chenla kingdom" to replace the older, now-revised narrative. It offers, instead, a more historically honest picture: a Mekong world of multiple political centers, shifting alliances, and changing degrees of political concentration and regional autonomy, framed by a foreign textual label, Zhenla, that simplifies this complexity in ways historians must continually work to unpack.

Finally, this chapter maintains the interpretive commitments established across Chapters 19 and 20. Religious and political vocabulary drawn from Sanskrit and from South Asian traditions appears throughout this period's inscriptions, but it was adopted, adapted, and combined with local Khmer institutions, language, and social structures by rulers and communities who were transforming their own political world, not receiving a finished one from outside. The chapter closes, as Chapter 20 did, by handing its central unresolved problem forward: Chapter 33 will examine how the political landscape traced here eventually gave rise to the consolidated order historians associate with Angkor.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish "Zhenla" or "Chenla" as a Chinese textual label from the political communities and centers reconstructed through contemporary Sanskrit and Old Khmer inscriptions.
  2. Explain the older historiographical narrative of a unified Chenla kingdom succeeding Funan, and identify the evidentiary grounds on which that narrative has been substantially revised.
  3. Compare the kinds of historical information that Sanskrit inscriptions and Old Khmer inscriptions typically preserve, and explain why the two languages should not be treated as interchangeable sources.
  4. Describe the political geography of the sixth- through eighth-century Mekong world in terms of regional centers and river corridors rather than fixed modern-style borders.
  5. Analyze the roles of rulers such as Bhavavarman I, Citrasena-Mahendravarman, Īśānavarman I, and Jayavarman I without constructing an artificially unified national king list.
  6. Evaluate Sambor Prei Kuk and its associated inscriptions as evidence for a major political and religious center, while distinguishing this evidence from a claim of permanent, uniform territorial rule.
  7. Explain how temple foundations functioned as political as well as religious institutions, connecting rulers, elites, religious specialists, land, and local communities.
  8. Assess the claim that "Chenla conquered Funan," identifying what the evidence can and cannot establish about this transition.
  9. Evaluate the distinction between "Land Chenla" and "Water Chenla" as a Chinese textual classification, and explain why modern scholarship questions whether it accurately reflects indigenous political organization.
  10. Apply a structured method for analyzing an inscription, including its date, language, findspot, donor, deity, titles, and named places, and identify the limits of what such analysis can establish.
  11. Identify evidence for continuity in agriculture, trade, religion, and population across the transition from Funan-era to Chenla-era political organization.
  12. Contextualize this chapter's unresolved political geography as the necessary foundation for Chapter 33's later treatment of Jayavarman II and the transition to Angkor.

Historical Context

This chapter continues Part V, "The Earliest Documented States," following directly from Chapter 20's examination of Funan and the lower Mekong delta. Chapter 20 established a working method for handling the relationship between a Chinese textual label and the archaeological record it was meant to describe, and it introduced the mandala concept only briefly, deferring full evaluation of that model, along with the related devaraja concept, to Chapter 34's treatment of Angkorian kingship and political order.

The geographic focus shifts in this chapter from the lower Mekong delta, examined in Chapter 20, toward the middle Mekong, the Tonle Sap basin and its approaches, the Stung Sen river valley, and the Dangrek escarpment region, areas associated with the political developments Chinese sources describe under the name Zhenla. Students should recall from Chapter 2 that the Mekong is one of Mainland Southeast Asia's defining river systems, and this chapter examines a stretch of that system somewhat further inland and upstream from the delta zone central to Chapter 20, while remaining attentive to the continued importance of riverine connections linking the two regions.

The evidentiary situation facing historians of this period differs meaningfully from that facing historians of Funan. Chinese textual sources remain available and continue to matter, but this period also provides a substantial and growing corpus of inscriptions produced locally, in Sanskrit and in Old Khmer, an early written form of the Khmer language. This is the first substantial locally produced textual corpus available for reconstructing the Mekong world's political and social institutions in this textbook, rather than solely by outside observers or through founding legends transmitted at a distance. This evidentiary shift is itself historically significant and shapes this chapter's methodological emphasis.

It is essential to state clearly, before the chapter's detailed content begins, what this chapter does not claim. It does not claim that "Chenla" was a single, continuously governed kingdom with a fixed capital and a stable, unbroken succession of rulers. It does not claim that Chenla straightforwardly conquered and replaced Funan in a single decisive event. It does not claim that the later Chinese division of the region into "Land Chenla" and "Water Chenla" accurately reflects how the region's own inhabitants understood their political world. Each of these claims has appeared in older historical writing, and this chapter explains, drawing especially on the historiographical reassessment associated with the historian Michael Vickery and subsequent epigraphic scholarship, why more recent analysis treats them with substantial caution.

Section 21.1

The Problem Chapter 20 Created

Chapter 20 taught students to treat "Funan" as a Chinese textual label whose relationship to the archaeological record of the lower Mekong required careful, independent investigation rather than assumption. That chapter closed by noting that Chinese sources, from the sixth and seventh centuries onward, began applying a different name, transcribed as Zhenla, to political developments in the broader Mekong region. This chapter opens by insisting that the same discipline apply here, with even greater urgency, because the temptation to treat a change in Chinese vocabulary as a simple historical event, "one kingdom replaced another," is strong and has shaped much older historical writing.

The first question this chapter asks is therefore not "what happened when Chenla conquered Funan," a question that assumes its own answer, but rather: did the underlying political landscape of the Mekong world actually change in this period, did Chinese observers' terminology and perception change, or did both occur together in ways that are difficult to fully disentangle? Answering this question responsibly requires moving beyond Chinese textual sources alone, which is why this chapter's central methodological advance, introduced in Section 21.4, is its sustained use of contemporary local inscriptions.

Section 21.2

Zhenla/Chenla as a Textual Label

As with Funan, students must distinguish carefully between "Zhenla," a name recorded in Chinese sources, conventionally rendered in English as "Chenla," and the political communities historians reconstruct from contemporary Khmer-region inscriptions and archaeology. This distinction is, if anything, more important here than in Chapter 20, because it is now possible to compare the Chinese textual label directly against a substantial body of local written evidence, and that comparison reveals a significant mismatch.

Contemporary Sanskrit and Old Khmer inscriptions from the sixth through eighth centuries do not announce that their rulers govern a country called Chenla. They identify rulers, officials, and donors by name and title, describe specific acts of religious patronage at specific temples, and record genealogies connecting rulers to earlier rulers or to prestigious ancestors, but they do not use the term Chenla to describe an overarching political community. This absence is one of the principal reasons that modern historians, following the historiographical reassessment discussed in Section 21.3, have questioned whether "Chenla" ever corresponded to a single, self-recognized political unit in the way older historical narratives assumed.

This chapter therefore treats "Chenla" the way Chapter 20 treated "Funan": as a conventional label, useful for organizing discussion of a particular textual tradition and a particular period, but not as a secure description of a unified indigenous political entity. Where this chapter uses the term, it does so with this qualification understood, and students should read it the same way throughout.

Historical Vocabulary
Zhenla / Chenla

Definition: "Zhenla" is the modern romanization of a name recorded in Chinese dynastic sources from the sixth century CE onward, conventionally rendered in English as "Chenla," for political developments in the Mekong world following the period associated with Funan.

Historical context: No contemporary Sanskrit or Old Khmer inscription from the sixth through eighth centuries uses this name to describe an overarching political community. The name survives only in Chinese textual sources and in modern scholarship built upon them.

Why it matters: Because "Chenla" functions grammatically as a proper name in English-language scholarship, including this textbook, it is easy to mistake it for a secure indigenous political designation. Recognizing it instead as a Chinese textual label is the foundation of this chapter's entire method.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that "Chenla" designates a single, continuously governed kingdom with a fixed capital simply because later Chinese sources further divide it into "Land Chenla" and "Water Chenla." This textbook uses "Chenla" as a conventional label for the textual tradition and period examined in this chapter, not as a claim about a securely reconstructed unified state.

Historical Vocabulary
Epigraphy and Old Khmer

Definition: Epigraphy is the scholarly study of inscriptions carved into durable materials such as stone or metal. Old Khmer is an early written form of the Khmer language, attested in inscriptions from this period alongside Sanskrit.

Historical context: Sanskrit epigraphy in the Mekong world was already well established by the late sixth century CE. By the early seventh century, Old Khmer is securely attested in the Angkor Borei inscription conventionally cited as K.557/600, dated 611 CE; the two catalogue numbers refer to portions of the same inscribed stele rather than to two independent inscriptions. From this period onward, rulers, officials, and religious donors in the Mekong world increasingly produced inscriptions in Sanskrit, Old Khmer, or both, recording religious dedications, land grants, genealogies, and administrative detail.

Why it matters: Epigraphy gives historians their first substantial locally produced textual corpus for the Mekong world examined in this textbook's chronological sequence, produced by the societies under study themselves rather than by outside observers, transforming the kinds of historical questions that can be asked about this period.

Potential misunderstanding: An inscription is not a neutral administrative record. It reflects the interests and self-presentation of the literate elite who commissioned it, and, like any historical source, it must be read critically, cross-referenced against other inscriptions and archaeological evidence, and evaluated for what it does and does not establish, as explained further in Section 21.16.

Section 21.3

The Older Narrative, and Why It Has Been Revised

Before examining the revised scholarly picture, students should understand the older narrative clearly, since it still appears in some general histories and popular accounts. In this older scheme, an inland kingdom called Chenla arose to the north of Funan during the sixth century, gradually grew in strength, eventually conquered or absorbed Funan during the late sixth and seventh centuries, briefly constituted a powerful unified kingdom under strong rulers, and then, in the eighth century, split into two successor states conventionally called "Land Chenla," located further inland, and "Water Chenla," located closer to the lower Mekong and the former Funan heartland.

This narrative has the appeal of a clear, linear story: rise, conquest, unification, division. Its principal weakness is not that earlier scholars ignored inscriptions; epigraphers working in the earlier and middle twentieth century, most notably George Cœdès, undertook extensive and foundational work editing, dating, and translating Sanskrit and Old Khmer inscriptions from this period. The weakness lies instead in how that inscriptional evidence was often interpreted: the older synthesis was structured heavily by the sequence and categories presented in Chinese dynastic histories, and contemporary inscriptions were frequently read within that inherited framework, fitted to a geography and chronology of "Chenla" that the Chinese textual sequence had already supplied. When later historians, most influentially Michael Vickery, reexamined the inscriptional corpus without assuming in advance that the Chinese categories accurately represented indigenous political organization, the older narrative's coherence began to break down. Inscriptions used by earlier scholars to locate and date an early "Chenla" often do not themselves use that name, and the political landscape they reveal, with multiple regional centers, competing and cooperating lineages, and shifting patterns of religious patronage, does not correspond neatly to a single, continuously governed kingdom rising, unifying, and later dividing in two.

This revision does not mean the older narrative was entirely without foundation. Chinese sources genuinely record a change in terminology and genuinely describe increasingly dominant political actors in the Mekong region during this period. It means, rather, that the older narrative imposed a degree of coherence, unity, and linear sequence on the evidence that the contemporary inscriptional record does not straightforwardly support. The remainder of this chapter develops the more historically responsible picture that has replaced it.

Section 21.4

Inscriptions as the New Evidentiary Center

The single most important methodological development in this chapter, compared to Chapter 20, is the availability of a substantial corpus of contemporary inscriptions produced within the Mekong world's Khmer-language political and religious centers. These inscriptions, carved in stone at temples and other sites, and often, though not always, precisely dated, provide historians with evidence generated by the societies under study rather than solely by outside observers or through founding legends transmitted at a distance. The personal names recorded in these inscriptions, including names of Sanskrit, Old Khmer, Austronesian, and other Austroasiatic origin, are themselves a reminder that inscriptional language does not establish a linguistically or ethnically uniform population.

This does not mean inscriptions are free of interpretive difficulty. They were produced for specific purposes, typically to record religious donations, establish or confirm land grants, commemorate genealogies, or assert the piety and status of a ruler or official, and they reflect the perspective and interests of the literate elite who commissioned them, not a neutral administrative census of the whole society. Nonetheless, because inscriptions describe specific, local, dated acts rather than distant diplomatic summaries, they allow historians to check, qualify, and often substantially revise conclusions drawn from Chinese textual sources alone.

The historiographical argument associated with Michael Vickery's reassessment of this period rests centrally on this point: contemporary inscriptions should receive priority for reconstructing seventh-century Cambodian political institutions, rather than being read selectively to fit categories, such as a unified "Chenla kingdom," derived primarily from later or more distant Chinese sources. This chapter adopts that methodological priority throughout, while still using Chinese textual evidence where it offers information the inscriptions do not.

Section 21.5

Sanskrit and Old Khmer: Two Languages, Two Kinds of Evidence

Many inscriptions or inscriptional monuments from this period contain both Sanskrit and Old Khmer sections, although the two portions are not necessarily translations of one another; the two languages typically carry different kinds of historical information. Sanskrit inscriptions frequently contain royal praise poetry, genealogies connecting a ruler to earlier rulers or to divine or legendary ancestors, religious dedications invoking Hindu deities, particularly Śiva and Viṣṇu, and language drawing on transregional South Asian political and literary conventions. This material is invaluable for understanding how rulers wished to present themselves, their lineage, and their relationship to religious authority, but it should be read as elite self-presentation and legitimation rather than as a neutral administrative record.

Old Khmer inscriptions, by contrast, more often record specific, practical information: the names of donated lands, personnel, and goods; lists of temple dependents and their obligations; boundaries of property; and administrative or social relationships expressed in local terms. This material offers historians a much closer view of local economic and social organization than Sanskrit court poetry typically provides, though it too reflects the interests of the literate elite who commissioned the inscriptions rather than a comprehensive record of the whole population.

Students should therefore treat Sanskrit and Old Khmer inscriptions as complementary rather than interchangeable sources. A Sanskrit passage praising a ruler's conquests and divine favor answers different historical questions than an adjoining Old Khmer passage listing the specific fields, water buffalo, and dependents assigned to a temple. Reading both together, as this chapter's Inscription Study feature demonstrates, produces a considerably richer and more balanced historical picture than either language could provide alone.

Section 21.6

Political Geography instead of Modern Borders

Following the method established in Chapter 20's treatment of the lower Mekong, this chapter organizes political geography around river corridors, regional basins, and named centers rather than a solid-colored territorial map. Relevant zones include the middle and lower Mekong itself; the Stung Sen river basin, associated with several important inscriptional sites; the approaches to the Tonle Sap, the great lake whose seasonal expansion and contraction shaped settlement and agriculture across central Cambodia; and the Dangrek escarpment region, bordering territory further north and connected to later Angkorian-period developments examined in Volume III.

Inscriptions from this period cluster around specific places rather than being evenly distributed across a uniform territory, and historians use the geographic distribution of inscriptions, alongside their content, to identify likely regional centers of political and religious authority. This approach necessarily produces a map of nodes and corridors rather than a bounded state, consistent with this chapter's central argument that "Chenla" is better investigated through changing centers, corridors, and relationships than represented as one fixed territorial map.

Section 21.7

Bhavavarman I, Citrasena-Mahendravarman, and Political Transformation

Inscriptions associated with the rulers conventionally named Bhavavarman I and Citrasena-Mahendravarman provide the earliest substantial local textual evidence for the political transformations Chinese sources associate with the rise of Zhenla. These inscriptions, distributed across several sites in the middle Mekong and Tonle Sap regions, record religious dedications, genealogical claims, and titles that indicate rulers of considerable regional importance during the late sixth and early seventh centuries.

Historians should resist constructing an overly neat national king list from this material. Titles and genealogies recorded in these inscriptions may reflect claims to status and legitimacy as much as, or more than, secure, uncontested authority over a defined territory. The same inscriptions that establish a ruler's prestige and religious patronage do not necessarily establish the precise geographic extent of that ruler's effective administration, nor do they guarantee that other regional rulers, attested in other inscriptions from the same period, considered themselves subordinate to the same hierarchy.

What this evidence does establish, with reasonable confidence, is that the late sixth and early seventh centuries saw an intensification of inscriptional activity, temple construction, and religious patronage across multiple centers in the Mekong world, activity that Chinese observers, watching from a distance and organizing their observations according to Chinese diplomatic categories, likely perceived and described, imperfectly, as the rise of a polity called Zhenla.

Section 21.8

Sambor Prei Kuk and Īśānapura

The reign of Īśānavarman I, in the early-to-mid seventh century, is associated with the major urban and temple complex of Sambor Prei Kuk, in present-day central Cambodia, identified by inscriptions and by later tradition with ancient Īśānapura. This site functions in this chapter much as Óc Eo functioned in Chapter 20: as the central archaeological case study through which students can examine, in material detail, what a major political and religious center of this period actually looked like.

Sambor Prei Kuk preserves numerous brick temple structures, organized into distinct architectural groups, along with a substantial inscriptional corpus, including Sanskrit inscriptions from the late sixth and early seventh centuries and Old Khmer inscriptions securely attested in the seventh century. The scale of construction, the sophistication of the site's architectural and artistic program, and the density of associated inscriptions all indicate that Īśānapura functioned as a major center of royal authority and religious patronage during Īśānavarman I's reign.

Historians should nonetheless resist treating Īśānapura simply as "the capital of a unified Chenla Empire" extending uniform administrative control across every place later loosely associated with Chenla in Chinese sources. At the same time, historians should not overcorrect into treating Sambor Prei Kuk as merely a locally important center with no wider reach: inscriptional evidence associated with Īśānavarman I has been read by Michael Vickery and other specialists as indicating that his administrative authority extended into regions well beyond the immediate vicinity of Īśānapura, making his reign a genuine, if geographically uneven, instance of supraregional political integration. The site demonstrates a powerful, well-resourced political and religious center capable of mobilizing considerable labor and artistic skill and, on the best current reading of the evidence, of exercising administrative authority reaching into distant areas; it does not, however, demonstrate that this center administered a uniformly governed territory encompassing every place later loosely associated with Chenla, on a continuous, permanent basis. As with Angkor Borei and Óc Eo in Chapter 20, the appropriate historical description holds both possibilities together: Sambor Prei Kuk was very likely the most prominent political and religious center of its period, whose relationship to other contemporary centers involved some combination of alliance, acknowledged precedence, and, in at least some regions, more direct administrative authority.

Section 21.9

Temples as Political Institutions

A central lesson of this chapter is that temples in this period should be understood as political institutions as much as religious ones. Temple foundations connected rulers, elite families, religious specialists, land, and local communities in ways that reveal considerably more about how power actually operated than a distant Chinese statement that a king ruled a place called Chenla.

When a ruler or elite official founded or endowed a temple, the act typically involved the donation of land, agricultural produce, personnel, and goods necessary to sustain the temple's ongoing religious functions. Inscriptions recording these donations therefore provide historians with concrete evidence of a ruler's or official's resources, networks, and relationships with local communities, evidence considerably more specific than the general claims to authority found in royal praise poetry. A temple's inscriptional record can reveal the names of the officials who administered its properties, the communities obligated to support it, and its connections to other religious foundations, offering a texture of political and economic organization that Chinese diplomatic reports never attempted to capture.

This approach also connects directly to the localization framework introduced in Chapter 19. Religious foundations in this period participated in transregional Śaiva and Vaiṣṇava traditions with deep South Asian roots, while adapting those traditions within local Khmer religious, social, and political settings. The specific form these foundations took, their architectural style, their integration into local landholding and labor systems, and their relationship to Khmer-language administrative practice, reflect a genuinely local synthesis rather than a transplanted South Asian institution operating unchanged in a new setting.

Section 21.10

Ruling Houses, Kinship, and Elite Networks

Rather than assuming a modern bureaucratic state or, at the opposite extreme, a condition of complete political fragmentation, this chapter interprets the political order of this period as emerging from kinship, marriage alliances, religious patronage, military success, local officeholding, and relationships among regional centers. Inscriptional genealogies frequently trace a ruler's descent through both paternal and maternal lines, and marriage alliances between prominent lineages appear repeatedly as a mechanism for consolidating or extending political influence.

This chapter proposes a working distinction that students should apply throughout their reading of this period's evidence: political precedence is not the same as direct administration, and direct administration is not the same as fixed territorial sovereignty. A ruler such as Īśānavarman I or Jayavarman I might be acknowledged, through inscriptions and titles, as the most prominent or precedent-setting figure across a wide area, without that ruler necessarily administering every part of that area continuously through appointed officials, and without that authority translating into a fixed, bounded territorial state in the modern sense. Recognizing these distinct layers allows historians to describe the period's political complexity accurately rather than collapsing it into either "one strong king ruled everything" or "there was no meaningful political order at all."

The rulers named most often in this chapter, Bhavavarman I, Citrasena-Mahendravarman, Īśānavarman I, and Jayavarman I, form a single genealogical chain of uncle, father, and son, and a reader following only that chain could easily reconstruct precisely the kind of continuous national king list this chapter has warned against. Inscriptional evidence from the early eighth century helps complicate that picture, though not in the way a rival lineage would: Jayadevī, daughter of Jayavarman I, is attested as a ruling queen by the inscription K.904, from the Angkor region, dated 713 CE, issuing land grants and exercising royal authority in her own right, using royal titulature comparable to that of the male rulers examined elsewhere in this chapter. Her reign demonstrates that royal authority in this lineage could pass through a female ruler, and it provides important evidence for political continuity and reconfiguration in the years after Jayavarman I, even though the wider structure of authority across the Mekong world in this period, including how far her effective reach extended and how her rule related to other contemporary centers, remains debated among specialists. Her case is therefore most useful not as proof of a rival lineage outside the main line of named kings, but as a reminder that even within a single traceable dynasty, succession, political geography, and the actual configuration of authority were considerably more complicated than a simple list of kings would suggest.

Section 21.11

"Chenla Conquered Funan"? A Cautious Reassessment

Chinese sources present the polity called Zhenla as initially subordinate to Funan and later, by some accounts, dominant over it, and some historians have reconstructed this transition as a north-to-south process of political and possibly military expansion, in which power shifted from the more inland centers associated with early Zhenla toward the lower-Mekong zone previously associated with Funan. This reconstruction is plausible and finds some support in the Chinese textual sequence, but the exact mechanism behind this shift, whether outright military conquest, dynastic absorption through marriage or inheritance, a gradual reorientation of trade and political alliance, or some combination of these processes, is considerably harder to establish than the conventional phrase "Chenla conquered Funan" suggests.

The inscriptional record does not provide a clear, dated account of a decisive military conquest comparable to what the phrase implies. It instead shows overlapping and shifting patterns of political and religious activity across both the lower Mekong and the more inland regions during the sixth and seventh centuries, consistent with several different possible historical processes. Students should therefore treat "Chenla conquered Funan" as a useful shorthand for a genuine historical shift in the balance of regional power, while recognizing that the specific process behind that shift remains substantially reconstructed rather than directly documented.

Section 21.12

Continuity beneath Transformation

This chapter's title emphasizes transformation rather than replacement, and this section makes that emphasis explicit. Agricultural systems developed over the preceding centuries, examined in Chapter 20, did not disappear because Chinese terminology shifted from Funan to Zhenla. Trade networks connecting the Mekong world to South Asia, China, and the wider maritime trading system, examined in Chapter 18, continued to operate, even as the balance of political power within the region changed. Religious traditions, including the elite Sanskrit court culture and the Śaiva and Vaiṣṇava religious practices examined in this chapter, developed continuously from the patterns already visible in Funan-era society rather than beginning anew. The population itself, farming, fishing, and craft-producing communities throughout the Mekong world, experienced political reorganization at the level of ruling elites without necessarily experiencing wholesale social or cultural replacement.

Recognizing this continuity does not minimize the genuine political changes this chapter documents: new centers of authority became prominent, new patterns of temple construction and inscriptional practice emerged, and the balance of power among regional centers shifted more than once across these centuries. But it does mean that this chapter's central process is best understood as a reorganization of an existing, continuously developing Mekong world, not the replacement of one civilization by another arriving from outside or from a previously unconnected region.

Section 21.13

Jayavarman I and the Later Seventh Century

Inscriptions associated with Jayavarman I, dating to the mid-to-late seventh century, are distributed across a notably wide area of the Mekong world, suggesting a period of extensive political influence and inscriptional activity comparable in scale to, and in some respects exceeding, that associated with Īśānavarman I. As with the earlier rulers examined in this chapter, however, this wide inscriptional distribution should be read as evidence of connections, alliances, and acknowledged precedence rather than automatically as evidence of uniform provincial administration extending outward from a single capital.

Scholarship on pre-Angkorian Cambodia has repeatedly cautioned against reading later, better-documented Angkorian institutional models backward into this earlier period. The administrative structures, provincial offices, and bureaucratic mechanisms that later Angkorian inscriptions describe in considerable detail, examined in Volume III, should not be assumed to have existed in comparable form during Jayavarman I's reign simply because his inscriptions appear across a wide geographic area. At the same time, Michael Vickery's reassessment of the inscriptional evidence suggests that Jayavarman I may genuinely have increased central authority relative to his predecessors and possibly succeeded, for at least part of his reign, in achieving a more unified political order than had existed under Īśānavarman I, even if that order fell well short of a modern territorial bureaucracy. The most defensible conclusion is that Jayavarman I's reign represented a further, and by some readings the most substantial, period of concentrated royal prestige and wide-ranging influence in this chapter's timeframe, built on the same combination of kinship, religious patronage, and regional alliance examined throughout this chapter, whose precise administrative reach nonetheless remains difficult to establish with full confidence.

Section 21.14

The Eighth Century: Political Reconfiguration, Not Simple Collapse

Following the periods of wide-ranging royal prestige associated with Īśānavarman I and Jayavarman I, the eighth century presents historians with a genuinely difficult evidentiary problem: securely dated royal inscriptions become notably sparser than in the preceding century, and Chinese sources describe the region using the further-subdivided categories "Land Chenla" and "Water Chenla," examined in Section 21.15. Older historical writing frequently described this development as the "collapse" or "decline" of Chenla, language that this chapter deliberately avoids, since the sparseness of inscriptions is not, by itself, evidence of political breakdown.

The evidence permits more than one responsible interpretation, and this chapter does not adopt either as settled. One possibility is that political authority became more regionalized during this period, distributed among several centers without any single center achieving the kind of wide-ranging prestige associated with Īśānavarman I or Jayavarman I. A second possibility, associated with Michael Vickery's reassessment, is that significant political integration may have survived, or even increased in places, despite the sparser royal epigraphy, since continued investment in temple construction and religious patronage in this period is arguably more consistent with sustained peace and organized society than with widespread disorder. The most defensible conclusion is that the structure of eighth-century political authority in the Mekong world remains difficult to reconstruct with confidence, and that the sparseness of the inscriptional record should be treated as a gap in the evidence rather than as proof of any specific political condition. What can be said with more confidence is that temple construction, religious patronage, and at least some inscriptional activity continued across multiple centers throughout this period, indicating a still-vigorous society whatever its precise political configuration. The language of "collapse" imports an assumption, drawn from a linear rise-and-fall narrative, that the evidence does not require and that this textbook's broader commitment to avoiding teleological narratives, established in earlier chapters, cautions against adopting uncritically.

Section 21.15

"Land Chenla" and "Water Chenla"

Later Chinese textual sources describe the Mekong region as divided into two political units, conventionally translated "Land Chenla," located further inland, and "Water Chenla," located closer to the lower Mekong and the maritime-oriented zone previously associated with Funan. Older historical writing transformed this Chinese division into two coherent, well-defined successor kingdoms, each with its own capital, ruling line, and territory, directly inheriting the supposedly unified Chenla kingdom's territory in two neat halves.

Modern epigraphic scholarship has questioned whether this two-part Chinese classification accurately represented the actual political organization of the region during the eighth century, but it has not replaced that older narrative with an equally confident opposite claim. The historiographical reassessment associated with Michael Vickery specifically rejected the assumption that the eighth century must have been a period of anarchy or extensive political fragmentation; he pointed to continued investment in temple art and architecture as evidence that at least some areas experienced substantial peace and organization during this period, even as securely dated royal inscriptions become sparser. What the inscriptional evidence does establish is that the region's centers of authority do not sort neatly into two coherent blocks corresponding to "land" and "water" zones. Beyond that, the actual degree of integration or fragmentation represented by the eighth-century evidence remains a genuinely open question among specialists, not one this chapter can responsibly resolve in either direction.

This section offers one of the chapter's most transferable historical lessons, applicable well beyond this specific case: a foreign source's division of a region into a small number of named political units does not prove that the region's own inhabitants recognized, or organized their political lives around, exactly those units. Chinese dynastic histories represented the region through the categories "Land Chenla" and "Water Chenla"; whether those categories corresponded closely to indigenous political organization is uncertain, and inscriptional evidence that resists sorting neatly into either category is reason for caution rather than for confidently replacing the Chinese division with a different, equally fixed picture.

Section 21.16

Reading an Inscription: A Method

Having relied throughout this chapter on inscriptional evidence, this section makes explicit the method historians use to move from an inscription's physical and textual features to responsible historical conclusions. This method matters not only for this chapter but for the many later chapters throughout this textbook, particularly across Volume III's treatment of Angkor, that depend on epigraphic evidence.

Method Study

From Stone to History: Reading an Inscription Responsibly

Historians examining an inscription from this period typically consider, in combination:

  • Date: When was the inscription carved, and how securely can that date be established, whether through an explicit date formula, palaeographic comparison, or associated archaeological context?
  • Language: Is the inscription in Sanskrit, Old Khmer, or both, and what does the choice of language suggest about its intended audience and purpose?
  • Findspot: Where was the inscription discovered, and does that location correspond to a known temple, settlement, or other significant site?
  • Donor: Who commissioned or is credited with the inscription, and what titles, genealogical claims, or affiliations does that person assert?
  • Deity or religious dedication: To which deity or religious tradition is the inscription's associated foundation dedicated, and what does this suggest about religious patronage patterns in the region?
  • Titles and genealogy: What titles are claimed, and what ancestral or genealogical connections are asserted, and how do these compare to titles and genealogies in other inscriptions from the same period?
  • Named places and personnel: What place names, personal names, and categories of dependents or officials are recorded, and how might these be cross-referenced against other inscriptions or archaeological evidence?

From this combination of features, historians can responsibly conclude a limited but valuable set of things: that a specific act of religious patronage occurred, at a specific place, involving specific people and resources, framed by specific claims to status and legitimacy. Historians cannot responsibly conclude, from a single inscription alone, the full territorial extent of a ruler's authority, the total population or resources under that ruler's control, or the complete political relationship between that ruler and every other contemporary ruler attested in other inscriptions. Broader conclusions require comparing many inscriptions across sites and periods, cross-referencing them against archaeological evidence, and remaining alert to what the inscriptional record, produced by and for a literate elite, does not and cannot show.

Student analysis questions:

  1. Why is it important to consider an inscription's language, and not only its content, when assessing what it can tell historians?
  2. What kinds of historical claims can a single inscription support on its own, and what kinds of claims require comparison across multiple inscriptions?
  3. Why might historians be cautious about translating every category of temple dependent named in an inscription as "slave," and what would justify or rule out that translation in a specific case?
Inscription Study

Pairing Sanskrit and Old Khmer Evidence from a Temple Foundation

Type of evidence: a paired Sanskrit and Old Khmer inscription associated with a temple foundation of the sort documented at multiple sites in the Mekong world during this period · Context: such inscriptions typically accompany the establishment or endowment of a temple and were carved into stone at the temple site itself.

Paraphrase / Synthesis of a Representative Pattern (not a translation of any single specific inscription):

A representative Sanskrit portion of such an inscription praises a ruler's piety, lineage, and religious merit, invoking Śiva or Viṣṇu and situating the ruler within a genealogy connecting him to earlier, sometimes legendary, ancestors. A representative accompanying Old Khmer portion then lists the specific lands, rice fields, livestock, and named personnel donated to support the temple's ongoing operation, along with the obligations owed by those personnel and the boundaries of the donated property.

Analysis: Read together, the two portions demonstrate how a single act of temple patronage combined transregional religious and political legitimation, expressed in Sanskrit, with concrete local economic and administrative detail, expressed in Old Khmer. Neither portion alone would give historians the full picture: the Sanskrit praise poetry alone would suggest a confident, legitimated ruler without revealing the material foundations of his patronage, while the Old Khmer property list alone would reveal considerable local economic detail without situating it within the ruler's broader claims to authority and religious merit.

Limitations: This kind of inscription cannot establish the total extent of the donor's political authority, nor can it confirm that the genealogical claims made in the Sanskrit portion were universally accepted by other regional rulers. The named personnel and their obligations reflect only the population directly attached to this specific temple, not the broader population of the surrounding region.

Questions for students:

  1. Why might a ruler or official have chosen to record the same religious foundation in two different languages, each emphasizing different kinds of information?
  2. What does the Old Khmer property list reveal about the local economy and social organization that the Sanskrit praise poetry does not?
  3. Why should historians be cautious about treating the Sanskrit genealogical claims in such inscriptions as objective, universally accepted historical fact?
  4. How might comparing many such inscriptions across different sites help historians assess the wider political landscape of this period?

Historiographical Debate

Was Chenla One Kingdom?

The question: Did the political developments Chinese sources describe under the name Zhenla, or Chenla, correspond to a single, continuously unified, territorially uniform kingdom from the sixth through eighth centuries, or does the evidence instead show political integration varying substantially across time and space?

Older unitary-kingdom interpretation: Earlier twentieth-century historical writing, relying heavily on Chinese textual sequence and categories, described Chenla as a single kingdom that rose north of Funan, conquered or absorbed it, briefly unified the Mekong world under strong seventh-century rulers, and then divided into two coherent successor kingdoms, "Land Chenla" and "Water Chenla," continuously governed throughout.
Vickery's epigraphic reassessment: Michael Vickery's detailed reexamination of contemporary Sanskrit and Old Khmer inscriptions rejected the traditional geography and the simple narrative of a continuous, uninterrupted Chenla kingdom conquering Funan in one decisive process. His reconstruction is nonetheless not a picture of continuous fragmentation: he concluded that Īśānavarman I exercised administrative authority reaching into distant regions, that Jayavarman I may have increased central authority and possibly succeeded in creating a more unified state later in the seventh century, and that the eighth century, despite sparser royal epigraphy, was not necessarily a period of political anarchy or fragmentation, since continued investment in temple art and architecture is more consistent with substantial peace and organization in at least some areas.

Evidence: The older interpretation relied primarily on the sequence and categories found in Chinese dynastic histories, compiled at a distance and often well after the events described. Vickery's reassessment gives methodological priority to contemporary local inscriptions, which do not use the term Chenla and instead reveal a landscape of specific, dated, locally situated rulers, foundations, and political communities, some of which point to substantial supraregional authority under particular rulers and others of which suggest more localized, independent activity.

Why it matters: This debate determines whether historians describe this period as the story of one kingdom's rise, unification, and division, or as a more historically accurate story in which political integration was real but uneven, achieved at some times and in some places by particular rulers, and less evident at others. It also serves as a direct test of whether the methodological lessons of Chapter 20 can be extended successfully to a period with a much richer local textual record without simply replacing one overconfident narrative with another.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Funan-Era Networks to Sixth- to Eighth-Century Political Reorganization

What Changed

  • Political prominence shifted from the lower-Mekong centers examined in Chapter 20 toward a wider set of centers along the middle Mekong, Stung Sen basin, and Tonle Sap approaches
  • Chinese textual vocabulary shifted from "Funan" to "Zhenla," and later to the further subdivided categories "Land Chenla" and "Water Chenla"
  • A substantial local corpus of Sanskrit and, from the early seventh century, Old Khmer inscriptions became available, constituting the first substantial locally produced textual corpus available for reconstructing the Mekong world's political and social institutions in this textbook, transforming the kinds of historical questions that can be asked
  • Periods of concentrated royal authority under Īśānavarman I and Jayavarman I were followed by an eighth-century political configuration whose degree of integration or dispersal remains debated

What Stayed the Same

  • Agricultural production and riverine and lacustrine (Tonle Sap-based) economic foundations continued to sustain the region's population and elite institutions
  • Participation in wider South Asian and East Asian religious, commercial, and diplomatic networks continued without interruption
  • Political authority remained organized around multiple centers connected by kinship, patronage, and alliance rather than a single, uniformly administered capital
  • Selective adoption and local transformation of Sanskritic religious and political vocabulary, rather than wholesale replacement of local practice, remained the operative pattern

Why the pattern matters: Describing this period as transformation rather than replacement allows historians to explain both the genuine political reorganization documented in Chinese and inscriptional sources and the deep continuities connecting this period back to the Funan-era Mekong world examined in Chapter 20.

Cause and Effect

Why Political Authority in the Mekong World Reorganized

Long-term causes

Continued agricultural development, expanding inscriptional and religious institutions, and long-standing regional exchange networks across the Mekong world, building on the Funan-era foundations examined in Chapter 20, created the resources and prestige that regional rulers could draw upon.

Short-term causes

Specific rulers, including Bhavavarman I, Citrasena-Mahendravarman, Īśānavarman I, and Jayavarman I, mobilized kinship alliances, religious patronage, and possibly military action to achieve, for a time, wide-ranging regional prestige and influence.

Effect

The balance of prominence within the Mekong world shifted more than once between the sixth and eighth centuries, moving from earlier lower-Mekong centers toward inland and Tonle Sap-region centers, achieving wide-ranging royal prestige under rulers such as Īśānavarman I and Jayavarman I, and later entering a period whose political structure remains difficult to reconstruct with confidence, all while Chinese observers applied simplifying labels, Zhenla, Land Chenla, and Water Chenla, to these shifting and only partly documented realities.

This chapter's response

Consistent use of the working distinctions among political precedence, direct administration, and territorial sovereignty, alongside sustained attention to what contemporary Sanskrit and Old Khmer inscriptions can and cannot establish, independent of Chinese textual categorization.

Historical complexity: No single cause, whether military conquest, religious patronage, kinship alliance, or Chinese diplomatic classification, adequately explains the political reorganization of this period; the evidence instead points to a genuinely multi-causal, regionally uneven process unfolding across some two centuries.

Everyday Life and Social History

Temple Personnel, Farmers, and Local Communities

Because temple inscriptions from this period often list the specific lands, goods, and personnel donated to support a religious foundation, they offer historians one of the richest available windows onto non-elite life in early Southeast Asian history. Named individuals appear as temple dependents obligated to provide labor, agricultural produce, or craft goods, and inscriptions sometimes specify their duties in considerable detail, ranging from agricultural cultivation to the maintenance of ritual objects and temple structures.

Historians must exercise particular caution with the status terminology used in these inscriptions. Old Khmer and Sanskrit categories describing temple dependents do not automatically correspond to modern or even later historical categories such as "slave," "serf," or "free laborer," and translating every dependent category uniformly as "slave" risks importing assumptions not supported by the specific terms and their documented social context. Careful historians examine each category's particular obligations, rights, and social position as described in the inscriptions themselves, and consult specialized scholarship on Khmer social terminology, before assigning a modern equivalent term.

Beyond named temple dependents, the broader population of farmers, fishers, and craft producers who sustained the Mekong world's agricultural and commercial economy remain largely visible only indirectly, through the resources temples and rulers drew upon rather than through direct textual description. Archaeological evidence for settlement patterns, agricultural infrastructure, and craft production around major centers such as Sambor Prei Kuk offers a further, complementary source for reconstructing the lives of this largely undocumented majority, whose labor made possible the religious and political achievements more visible in the inscriptional record.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined the political transformations Chinese sources describe under the name Zhenla, conventionally rendered Chenla, across the sixth through eighth centuries CE in the Mekong world. It began by extending the methodological lesson of Chapter 20 to a new and richer body of evidence, insisting that a shift in Chinese textual vocabulary, from Funan to Zhenla, be investigated rather than treated automatically as a simple historical event in which one kingdom conquered another.

The chapter presented the older historiographical narrative of a unified Chenla kingdom rising, conquering Funan, briefly unifying the Mekong world, and later dividing into "Land Chenla" and "Water Chenla," and explained why this narrative has been substantially revised, especially through the historiographical reassessment associated with Michael Vickery, which gives methodological priority to contemporary Sanskrit and Old Khmer inscriptions over retrospective Chinese categorization. Students learned that contemporary inscriptions from this period do not use the name Chenla for themselves, one of the principal reasons this revision has taken hold.

Sanskrit and Old Khmer inscriptions were examined as complementary rather than interchangeable evidence, with Sanskrit typically carrying royal genealogy, religious legitimation, and transregional political language, and Old Khmer typically recording specific, local, practical information about land, personnel, and obligations. The chapter organized political geography around river corridors and regional centers, including the middle Mekong, the Stung Sen basin, and the Tonle Sap approaches, rather than a fixed territorial map, and it examined the rulers Bhavavarman I, Citrasena-Mahendravarman, Īśānavarman I, and Jayavarman I as evidence of shifting regional prestige and influence rather than as a securely reconstructed national king list.

Sambor Prei Kuk, identified with ancient Īśānapura and associated with Īśānavarman I, served as this chapter's central archaeological case study, demonstrating a major early seventh-century political and religious center whose scale and inscriptional richness support its identification as an important center of authority, without proving uniform administrative control over a large, permanently fixed territory. Temples throughout this chapter were treated as political as well as religious institutions, connecting rulers, elites, religious specialists, land, and local communities in ways that a temple inscription can reveal more precisely than a distant Chinese diplomatic report.

The chapter introduced a working distinction among political precedence, direct administration, and territorial sovereignty, applying it to assess claims about rulers such as Jayavarman I whose inscriptions appear across a wide geographic area. It examined the claim that "Chenla conquered Funan" cautiously, showing that while a genuine shift in regional political power is well supported, the specific mechanism behind that shift is not directly documented. It also emphasized continuity alongside transformation, showing that agricultural systems, trade networks, religious traditions, and population did not disappear or reset when Chinese terminology changed.

The eighth century's sparser inscriptional record was examined as a genuine evidentiary problem rather than as settled evidence of "collapse," with the chapter presenting both the possibility of greater regional dispersal of authority and Michael Vickery's alternative suggestion that substantial integration may have persisted despite thinner epigraphy, evidenced by continued temple construction and patronage. The Chinese categories "Land Chenla" and "Water Chenla" were examined as a further instance of outside simplification, one whose accuracy as a description of indigenous political organization modern scholarship continues to debate rather than having resolved in favor of fragmentation. A dedicated Method Study taught students how historians move from an inscription's date, language, findspot, donor, deity, titles, and named places to responsible historical conclusions, and where those conclusions must stop.

The chapter's recurring argument, reinforced through its Evidence Window, Historiographical Debate, and other features, is that "Chenla" is most useful as a problem to investigate rather than as a political map to assume. This lesson extends and deepens the methodological foundation established in Chapter 20, preparing students for Chapter 33's later treatment of the political consolidation associated with Jayavarman II and for Chapter 22's shift eastward to the distinct historical trajectory of Cham-speaking coastal polities.

The history of historical writing; in this chapter, the study of how interpretations of the Chenla period have changed, especially through the epigraphic reassessment associated with Michael Vickery.

The application of a later or externally derived category to an earlier or different society, a risk this chapter identifies in the Chinese division of the region into "Land Chenla" and "Water Chenla."

A name applied by an outside source to a people, place, or polity, distinguished throughout this chapter and Chapter 20 from the historical realities such labels imperfectly describe.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has extended the methodological lesson of Chapter 20 to a period with a far richer local textual record, and the result is instructive: more evidence did not produce a simpler, more settled historical picture. It produced a more precise understanding of exactly how complicated the sixth- through eighth-century Mekong world actually was, and a clearer sense of where a foreign textual label, Chenla, oversimplified that complexity for its own diplomatic and historiographical purposes.

The central lesson students should carry forward is that the arrival of local written evidence does not eliminate the need for careful, comparative source criticism; it changes the kind of criticism required. Historians must now weigh Chinese textual sequence against contemporary Sanskrit and Old Khmer inscriptions, neither simply accepting the outside observer's tidy narrative nor assuming that inscriptional evidence, produced by and for a literate elite, offers a complete or neutral record of the whole society.

This chapter has also reinforced several commitments established earlier in this textbook: that political authority in early Southeast Asia is often better understood through kinship, patronage, and shifting alliance than through modern bureaucratic or territorial categories; that religious institutions functioned as political as well as spiritual centers; and that ordinary people, visible here chiefly through the temple dependents named in Old Khmer inscriptions, sustained the agricultural and economic foundations on which more visible political and religious achievements rested.

Understanding Chenla in this careful, evidence-conscious way prepares students for Chapter 22's shift to the distinct historical trajectory of Cham-speaking polities, and for the eventual return, in Chapter 33, to the Mekong world examined here, when the political landscape traced across this chapter's many centers begins to move toward the consolidation associated with Jayavarman II and, ultimately, toward Angkor.
