Flores, Luzon, and the Diversity of Archaic Humans

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Chapter 6 described a hominin population that reached Java largely by walking: when Pleistocene sea levels fell, Java became part of a single connected landmass with the Southeast Asian mainland, and Homo erectus could disperse across it without crossing open water. Flores and Luzon present an entirely different problem. Both islands remained separated from the mainland and from one another by channels of deep water even at the lowest Pleistocene sea levels, and yet both islands preserve unmistakable evidence of hominin presence reaching back hundreds of thousands of years. Explaining that evidence, and explaining what kind of hominins produced it, is the task of this chapter.

In 2003, a team of Indonesian and Australian archaeologists excavating in Liang Bua, a limestone cave on the island of Flores in eastern Indonesia, uncovered a small, startlingly complete skeleton of an adult hominin standing barely over a meter tall, with a brain roughly one-third the size of a modern human's. The find, formally described in 2004 and quickly nicknamed the "hobbit" in the popular press, did not fit comfortably into any existing category of human ancestor. It was not a child. It did not closely resemble modern human pygmy populations. Its skull, wrist, and foot bones combined features that some researchers associated with very early members of the genus Homo and others associated with a body plan reduced through long isolation on a small island. The scientific name eventually given to this population, Homo floresiensis, has become one of the most debated hominin discoveries of the past half-century.

Sixteen years later and roughly three thousand kilometers to the northeast, a different but comparably startling discovery emerged from Callao Cave on the island of Luzon in the northern Philippines. There, a small assemblage of teeth, hand and foot bones, and a juvenile femoral shaft, representing at least three individuals, displayed an unusual combination of primitive and derived features that did not match any previously known hominin species. Researchers initially dated the first-recovered foot bone to roughly 67,000 years ago and, on the basis of the expanding assemblage, named this population Homo luzonensis in 2019. A subsequent direct-dating reanalysis published in 2023 revised the minimum age of the fossil material to approximately 134,000 years ago, arguing that the original, younger date reflected a secondary uranium overprint rather than the true age of the bone. Unlike Flores, where a relatively complete skeleton anchors interpretation, the Luzon fossils remain few, fragmentary, and difficult to reconstruct with confidence.

Both discoveries raise the same underlying historical and scientific problem, and it is the problem this chapter is built around: Flores and Luzon are islands that hominins could not have reached simply by walking, even during the coldest and driest phases of the Pleistocene, when sea levels fell far enough to join Sumatra, Java, Borneo, and the Southeast Asian mainland into the single landmass historians and geologists call Sundaland. Reaching Flores or Luzon required crossing water that, even at its narrowest Pleistocene extent, never disappeared entirely. That a hominin population reached each island is now established by abundant archaeological and fossil evidence. Exactly how they did so, using what means, with what degree of intention, and following what evolutionary path once they arrived, remains only partly understood.

This chapter's central argument follows directly from that uncertainty. Pleistocene Southeast Asia did not contain a single, uniform hominin population steadily progressing along one evolutionary line from an ancestral form toward modern humans. It contained multiple, geographically separated populations, each shaped by its own history of isolation, founder effects, and adaptation, and each leaving behind a different, unevenly preserved kind of evidence. Java's Homo erectus record, examined in Chapter 6, is only one strand of this larger story. Flores and Luzon supply two more, and they are stranger, more fragmentary, and in important ways more difficult to interpret responsibly.

Reconstructing this history also means confronting, more directly than Chapter 6 required, the difference between what evidence can establish and what it cannot. A stone tool or a butchered animal bone can demonstrate that a hominin was present at a particular place and time without revealing which hominin species was responsible. A single, well-preserved skeleton can support a detailed anatomical description while still leaving its evolutionary origin genuinely disputed. A handful of teeth and finger bones can justify naming a new species while making that species' appearance, brain size, and way of life almost entirely unknown. Students who have already learned, from Chapter 6, to separate secure observation from inference will need that same discipline here, applied to evidence that is in several respects more provocative and less complete.

This chapter therefore does two things at once. It tells the specific archaeological and paleoanthropological story of Flores and Luzon, from the earliest stone tools on Flores more than a million years old, through the Mata Menge hominin fossils of roughly 700,000 years ago, to Liang Bua and Callao Cave. And it uses that story to establish a broader historical principle that will matter throughout the remainder of Volume I: the human past of island Southeast Asia was not a single corridor but an archipelago of separate, unevenly documented populations, and historians and scientists alike must resist the temptation to flatten that diversity into a tidier narrative than the evidence supports.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why Flores and Luzon posed a fundamentally different geographic problem for hominin dispersal than Java's connection to Sundaland.
  2. Distinguish the deep-water islands of Wallacea and the Philippines from the land-bridged islands of Sundaland discussed in Chapter 6.
  3. Trace the archaeological evidence for hominin activity on Flores, from stone artifacts more than one million years old through the Mata Menge fossils and the Liang Bua skeletal remains.
  4. Describe the discovery, anatomy, and revised chronology of Homo floresiensis at Liang Bua.
  5. Evaluate competing scientific interpretations of Homo floresiensis, including the pathological-modern-human hypothesis and models of descent from early Asian Homo erectus or an earlier Homo lineage.
  6. Distinguish evidence of hominin presence, such as the Kalinga stone tools and butchered rhinoceros bones, from evidence identifying a specific hominin species.
  7. Describe the discovery and limited fossil evidence for Homo luzonensis at Callao Cave, and explain why its physical reconstruction remains far less certain than that of Homo floresiensis.
  8. Analyze the unresolved question of how hominins crossed persistent water barriers to reach Flores and Luzon, and distinguish established fact from speculation on this point.
  9. Assess the concept of island dwarfism as one possible, but not deterministic, explanatory framework for small body size in island hominin populations.
  10. Explain why the discovery of multiple, distinct hominin populations in island Southeast Asia complicates older, linear models of human evolution.
  11. Apply careful distinctions between secure observation, reasonable inference, and unresolved speculation when evaluating paleoanthropological evidence.
  12. Contextualize Flores and Luzon within the wider Pleistocene human landscape of Southeast Asia introduced in Chapter 6 and continued in Chapters 8 and 9.

Historical Context

Chapter 6 established that Homo erectus reached Java within a published range of roughly 1.3 to 1.8 million years ago and that the latest securely dated occurrence of the species at Ngandong falls at approximately 117,000 to 108,000 years ago. That chapter also established the geographic mechanism that made this dispersal possible: during glacial periods, when large volumes of the world's water were locked in continental ice sheets, global sea levels fell low enough to expose large portions of the Sunda Shelf, the shallow continental platform underlying the sea between Sumatra, Java, Borneo, and the Southeast Asian mainland. This exposure joined the region into the single landmass geologists and historians call Sundaland. A hominin population capable of walking across savanna and open woodland, without watercraft or any specialized maritime technology, could in principle reach any point within Sundaland during a sufficiently low sea-level stand.

East and northeast of Sundaland, however, the geography changes sharply. Between Bali and the neighboring island of Lombok, and continuing southeastward through the Lesser Sunda Islands to Flores, Timor, and beyond, lies a marine boundary long recognized by naturalists as unusually significant. In the nineteenth century, the British naturalist Alfred Russel Wallace observed that the animal populations on either side of this boundary differed dramatically, with Asian-affiliated mammals dominant to the west and Australian-affiliated marsupials and other distinctive fauna dominant to the east, despite the narrow physical distances involved. This boundary, later named the Wallace Line in his honor, corresponds to a genuine and persistent deep-water barrier: even at the lowest Pleistocene sea levels, the channels separating Bali from Lombok, and separating the Sunda Shelf generally from Wallacea, the biogeographic zone extending eastward through Sulawesi, the Moluccas, and the Lesser Sunda Islands including Flores, never closed. Any land animal reaching Flores had to cross open water at some point in its history, whatever the exact mechanism.

The Philippine archipelago poses a related but geologically distinct problem. Unlike the Sunda Shelf, most of the Philippines, including Luzon, was never part of a continuous continental shelf connected to the Asian mainland even during the lowest Pleistocene sea-level stands. Deep oceanic trenches and basins separate the archipelago from Borneo, Taiwan, and the mainland. Nineteenth-century naturalists, including Thomas Henry Huxley, recognized that the Philippine fauna, like that of Wallacea, showed evidence of long-term isolation rather than free overland exchange with continental Asia. Reaching Luzon, like reaching Flores, required crossing water that Pleistocene sea-level changes narrowed in places but never eliminated.

Before turning to the evidence itself, students should recall the interpretive discipline introduced in Chapter 6: historians and scientists must distinguish clearly between what evidence directly demonstrates and what it merely permits historians to infer. That discipline becomes considerably more important in this chapter, because the evidence from Flores and Luzon is more anatomically surprising, more incompletely preserved, and more susceptible to premature or overconfident interpretation than the Javan Homo erectus record. A central rule guiding this chapter is that different fossil or archaeological populations existing within the same broad region and period do not automatically represent ancestors and descendants of one another. Evidence of hominin presence at a site, such as stone tools or butchered animal bone, likewise does not automatically identify which hominin species produced that evidence unless fossil remains directly associated with the material allow such identification.

Section 7.1

Beyond Java: A More Complicated Human Past

Chapter 6 left students with a relatively orderly picture: Homo erectus reached Java by walking across a Sundaland exposed by falling Pleistocene sea levels, and the species persisted there, in some form, for well over a million years. That picture, while itself subject to important qualifications about dating and interpretation, at least rests on a coherent geographic mechanism. Flores and Luzon supply no such straightforward mechanism, and this section exists to make that difficulty explicit before the evidence is presented.

Both islands lie beyond the outer edge of Sundaland. Flores sits within Wallacea, the zone of islands separating the Asian and Australian biogeographic realms, where even the lowest Pleistocene sea levels left substantial channels of open water between islands. Luzon sits within the Philippine archipelago, most of which was never joined to continental Asia by a continuous shelf at any point during the Pleistocene. A hominin population capable of the kind of overland walking dispersal described in Chapter 6 would still have been unable to reach either island without crossing water that never disappeared. And yet the archaeological and fossil record on both islands demonstrates, beyond reasonable doubt, that hominins did reach them, and reached them very early: more than a million years ago in the case of the earliest Flores stone tools, and by at least 709,000 years ago in the case of the Kalinga evidence from Luzon.

This chapter's organizing principle follows directly from that combination of facts. Different fossil and archaeological populations existing within the same broad region and period do not automatically represent ancestors and descendants of one another, and evidence of hominin presence at a given site does not automatically identify which hominin species was responsible for it unless directly associated fossil remains permit that identification. Readers should hold both principles in mind throughout the chapter, because the temptation to connect scattered evidence into a single tidy narrative, one continuous lineage marching from Java through Flores to Luzon, is strong and is not, on current evidence, justified.

Instead, this chapter treats Flores and Luzon as separate historical and scientific problems that happen to share a common geographic theme: island isolation beyond the reach of Pleistocene land bridges. Each island's evidence must be evaluated on its own terms, with its own dating, its own fossil record, and its own degree of scientific certainty and uncertainty, before any comparison between them, or between them and Java, can be responsibly attempted.

Section 7.2

Islands Beyond the Land Bridges

Understanding why Flores and Luzon present a different problem than Java requires returning briefly to the physical geography introduced in Chapter 6. Sundaland is the broader geological and biogeographic region comprising the Malay Peninsula, Sumatra, Java, Borneo, and the intervening Sunda Shelf, the shallow continental platform underlying the sea between them. During periods of low Pleistocene sea level, exposure of large portions of the Sunda Shelf connected these present-day landmasses into a much larger continuous dry-land area. This exposed platform has a natural eastern and southeastern boundary. East of Bali, across the strait separating it from Lombok, the seafloor drops away sharply into much deeper water, beyond the edge of the Sunda Shelf itself. This boundary, identified by Alfred Russel Wallace in the nineteenth century on the basis of strikingly different mammal populations on either side of it, marks the limit of the shelf. No plausible combination of Pleistocene sea-level changes closed this channel completely. Animals and, eventually, hominins reaching Flores and the other islands of the Lesser Sunda chain had to cross open water at some point in the journey, however that crossing narrowed at particular times.

The Philippine case differs geologically but produces a similar outcome. Much of the archipelago, including Luzon, rests on its own set of shelves and basins that were never continuous with the Asian mainland shelf, even at the lowest Pleistocene sea-level stands. Deep water separated Luzon from Borneo, from Taiwan, and from the mainland throughout the Pleistocene. Nineteenth-century naturalists including Thomas Henry Huxley recognized, on faunal grounds similar to Wallace's observations further south, that the Philippine islands had long histories of biological isolation.

The historically important inference that follows from this geography is straightforward: hominins reached islands that required water crossings. What is not established by the geography alone, and what the archaeological evidence discussed later in this chapter does not by itself resolve, is exactly how those crossings were accomplished. That distinction, between the secure conclusion that a crossing occurred and the unresolved question of its mechanism, should be kept in view throughout the remainder of this chapter, and is examined directly in Section 7.8.

Historical Vocabulary
Wallacea and the Wallace Line

Definition: Wallacea is the biogeographic zone of islands lying between the Sunda Shelf to the west and the Sahul Shelf, which joined New Guinea and Australia during periods of low sea level, to the east. It includes Sulawesi, the Moluccas, and the Lesser Sunda Islands, among them Flores. The Wallace Line, named for the naturalist Alfred Russel Wallace, marks the western boundary of this zone, running through the narrow strait between Bali and Lombok.

Historical context: Wallace observed in the nineteenth century that despite the short physical distance separating Bali from Lombok, the mammal populations on each side differed dramatically, with Asian-affiliated species dominant to the west and a very different, more limited fauna to the east. This pattern reflects a persistent deep-water barrier that Pleistocene sea-level changes narrowed but never eliminated.

Why it matters: Any land-dwelling hominin population reaching Flores had to cross this barrier, or a related one further east within Wallacea, at some point in its history. The existence of the barrier is what makes hominin presence on Flores scientifically remarkable and historically significant, rather than an unremarkable extension of the Sundaland dispersal pattern described in Chapter 6.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that the Wallace Line represents an absolute, impassable barrier at every point in geological time, or that its existence tells us anything about how hominins crossed it. It establishes that a crossing was necessary; it does not explain how that crossing occurred.

Section 7.3

The Earliest Evidence from Flores

The archaeological record on Flores begins well before any hominin fossil evidence from the island. Stone artifacts recovered from several localities on Flores have been dated to more than one million years ago, making them among the oldest secure evidence of hominin activity anywhere in island Southeast Asia beyond Sundaland itself. These early tools demonstrate that some hominin population reached Flores, and was producing stone artifacts there, over a million years ago, but the tools alone cannot identify which hominin species made them, since no fossil remains have been recovered in direct association with this earliest material.

The first fossil evidence directly bearing on the identity of Flores's early hominins comes from Mata Menge, a site where excavations recovered hominin fossils, including a mandible and teeth, dated to approximately 700,000 years ago. These fossils are important for two related reasons. First, they are directly dated by multiple independent geochronological methods applied to the sedimentary context in which they were found, giving researchers reasonable confidence in their age. Second, they display features consistent with very small body size, comparable in some respects to the far later Liang Bua remains discussed in the following section. This evidence was substantially strengthened by an adult humeral shaft, excavated at the site in 2013 and analyzed in detail in a 2024 study, estimated to be roughly nine to sixteen percent shorter and thinner than the corresponding bone in the LB1 skeleton from Liang Bua. Because a humeral shaft provides a more direct basis for estimating overall body size than dental or jaw fragments alone, this find gave researchers considerably stronger grounds for concluding that very small body size was already established on Flores by roughly 700,000 years ago, and for arguing that the Flores lineage likely derived from an early Asian population resembling Homo erectus.

Students should treat the Mata Menge evidence with appropriate precision. The fossils are genuinely significant, and the 2024 analysis, anchored by the previously excavated humeral shaft, represents some of the strongest currently available evidence bearing on the ancestry of the later Liang Bua population. At the same time, the interval separating Mata Menge from Liang Bua spans roughly 600,000 years, during which very little directly dated fossil evidence survives from Flores. Researchers consider it most likely that a continuous evolutionary lineage connects the two sites, and current evidence supports that conclusion, but it remains a strongly argued inference bridging an enormous gap in the fossil record, not a continuously documented evolutionary sequence. The archaeological record for the intervening centuries remains comparatively sparse, and future discoveries could refine or complicate the currently proposed lineage.

Section 7.4

Liang Bua and the Discovery of Homo floresiensis

Liang Bua is a large limestone cave in the highlands of western Flores, and it has produced the most complete and most thoroughly analyzed evidence of any hominin population discussed in this chapter. The cave was first excavated by the Dutch priest and amateur archaeologist Theodor Verhoeven beginning in 1965, and further excavated by the Indonesian archaeologist Raden Soejono in several seasons from the late 1970s through 1989. A renewed Indonesian-Australian research project began work at the site in 2001 and, in September 2003, recovered a partial skeleton, subsequently designated LB1, along with additional skeletal material from several other individuals uncovered in the following excavation seasons. The discovery and its formal scientific description and announcement in 2004 attracted immediate and intense international attention, both because of the skeleton's unusual anatomy and because of the dramatic dating initially proposed for it.

LB1 stood, by most estimates, little more than a meter tall as an adult, with a cranial capacity roughly one-third that of an average modern human, comparable in absolute terms to some of the smallest-brained members of the genus Homo known from anywhere in the world. The skeleton combined this small body and brain size with a distinctive suite of additional features: a low, sloping cranial vault; a jaw and dentition that some researchers considered primitive relative to modern humans; and wrist, shoulder, and foot bones that some researchers argued retained features more characteristic of early Homo or even earlier hominins than of anatomically modern humans. Popular media quickly labeled the find the "hobbit," a nickname that captured public attention but is not a scientific term, and this textbook uses the species name Homo floresiensis throughout.

The scientific reception of the 2004 announcement was immediately divided, and that division has never been fully resolved, though the balance of evidence has shifted substantially over the past two decades, as Section 7.5 discusses in detail. Equally important to this section is a matter of chronology that the popular narrative surrounding Homo floresiensis long misrepresented. The original dating proposed for the Liang Bua skeletal remains suggested survival of the species until as recently as roughly 12,000 years ago, a claim that received enormous public attention because it implied that a strikingly different hominin species had persisted into a period when modern humans were already present in the region. A major stratigraphic and chronological reanalysis published in 2016, incorporating additional excavation and more refined dating methods, substantially revised this picture. The skeletal remains associated with Homo floresiensis are now dated to approximately 100,000 to 60,000 years ago, while the broader sequence of stone artifacts associated with the site spans roughly 190,000 to 50,000 years ago. Whether the species survived at Liang Bua, or elsewhere on Flores, after roughly 50,000 years ago remains an open question rather than an established fact.

This revision matters for two reasons beyond the specific dates involved. First, it removes a claim, survival until 12,000 years ago, that is no longer supported by the evidence and that students should not repeat as though it remained current. Second, and more broadly instructive, it demonstrates how archaeological chronology is built and rebuilt as excavation, dating technology, and stratigraphic analysis improve. The 2016 revision was not a scandal or a failure of the original research; it was the ordinary, self-correcting process by which archaeological science refines its conclusions as evidence accumulates, and it offers students a concrete example of how historical and scientific knowledge changes over time without becoming arbitrary.

Section 7.5

What Was Homo floresiensis?

No question in this chapter has generated more sustained scientific disagreement than the evolutionary origin and proper classification of Homo floresiensis. Three broad interpretive positions have shaped the debate since 2004, and students should understand each on its own terms before considering how the balance of evidence has shifted.

The first position, prominent especially in the years immediately following the 2004 announcement, proposed that LB1 and the associated remains represented not a distinct species at all, but a pathological modern human, an individual or small population of Homo sapiens affected by a developmental or genetic condition producing unusually small body and brain size. Proponents of this view pointed to several medical conditions capable of producing microcephaly or related skeletal abnormalities in modern humans. The second position held that Homo floresiensis descended from a population of Asian Homo erectus, similar to the population that reached Java, which became isolated on Flores and subsequently evolved reduced body size, and in some proposals reduced brain size, through processes associated with long-term island isolation. The third position, more radical, proposed that the Flores lineage derived not from Homo erectus but from a considerably more primitive and much earlier hominin lineage, possibly more closely related to early African Homo populations or even to pre-Homo hominins, that reached island Southeast Asia far earlier than Homo erectus is otherwise documented to have done.

The pathological-modern-human interpretation has lost much of its earlier scientific support as additional evidence has accumulated. The recovery of skeletal material from several additional individuals at Liang Bua, displaying a broadly consistent small body size and shared anatomical features, made a population-wide, single-instance pathological explanation increasingly difficult to sustain. The subsequent discovery of the much older Mata Menge fossils, dated to roughly 700,000 years ago and displaying comparably small body size, provided further evidence against the pathology hypothesis, since it is far harder to attribute a genetic or developmental disorder to a population documented across such a long span of time and across more than one site. The 2024 Mata Menge study in particular strengthened the case for a long-established, small-bodied lineage descending from an early Asian Homo erectus-like ancestor, giving the second interpretive position considerably more support than it possessed at the time of the original 2004 announcement.

Even so, students should distinguish carefully between what is now strongly supported and what remains genuinely debated. It is strongly supported that Flores possessed a long-lived, small-bodied hominin lineage extending from at least 700,000 years ago to sometime between roughly 100,000 and 50,000 years ago, and that this lineage is not adequately explained as a pathological variant of modern humans. It remains debated exactly which ancestral population gave rise to this lineage, exactly what evolutionary and physiological processes produced its distinctive combination of features, and how closely its anatomy should be compared to that of early Homo erectus as opposed to earlier or more primitive hominin forms. The Historical Debate feature later in this chapter examines these positions and the evidence bearing on them in more detail.

Section 7.6

Kalinga: Hominins on Luzon 709,000 Years Ago

The narrative now shifts from Flores to Luzon, and this section exists specifically to prevent students from moving too quickly to the fossil evidence discussed in Section 7.7. At Kalinga, in the Cagayan Valley of northern Luzon, excavators recovered an assemblage of fifty-seven stone tools alongside the disarticulated and cut-marked bones of a rhinoceros, dated to approximately 709,000 years ago (more precisely, 709 ± 68 thousand years) through a combination of geochronological methods applied to the surrounding sediments. The direct association of the stone tools with cut marks and percussion damage on the rhinoceros bones securely demonstrates deliberate butchery and carcass processing by hominins at this very early date. A subsequent taphonomic study of the site found that the bones and tools had undergone some short-distance transport after the butchery event, meaning the assemblage should not be read as an untouched activity floor preserving the exact spot where the butchery took place, though the evidence for butchery itself remains sound. Wider geochronological work in the Cagayan Valley has also identified evidence of hominin and associated large-mammal presence spanning roughly 796,000 to 273,000 years ago, suggesting that hominins were active in the region both before and after the specific, well-dated Kalinga event, though the continuity and identity of these populations are not established.

The Kalinga evidence establishes, beyond reasonable doubt, that hominins were present and active on Luzon by roughly 709,000 years ago, hundreds of thousands of years before any Luzon hominin fossil currently known to science. This is an extremely important finding, both because it demonstrates that Luzon, like Flores, was reached across persistent water barriers at a remarkably early date, and because it demonstrates the deliberate processing of a large animal carcass using stone tools at that early date. This textbook is deliberately cautious about extending that conclusion further: the evidence securely establishes butchery and carcass processing, but it does not, by itself, establish social coordination, planning depth, or any broader claim about the general sophistication of the hominins responsible.

What the Kalinga evidence does not establish is equally important, and this textbook states the limitation plainly: the stone tools and butchered rhinoceros bones at Kalinga cannot, by themselves, identify which hominin species was responsible for them. No hominin fossil remains have been recovered in direct association with the Kalinga assemblage. It would be a serious overreach to label the Kalinga toolmakers Homo luzonensis, the species discussed in the following section and known only from much later, and much better documented, fossil evidence at Callao Cave. The correct historical statement is narrower and more disciplined: some hominin population, of unknown taxonomic identity, was present on Luzon by roughly 709,000 years ago and deliberately processed a large animal carcass using stone tools. Whether that population was ancestral to the population later represented at Callao Cave, a different and unrelated population, or something else entirely cannot currently be determined from the evidence.

Section 7.7

Callao Cave and Homo luzonensis

Callao Cave, also located in northern Luzon, has produced the fossil evidence that allows scientists to identify a distinct hominin species on the island, even though that evidence remains far more limited than the comparable material from Liang Bua on Flores. The first fossil, a single hominin foot bone, was recovered in 2007 and initially dated to approximately 67,000 years ago. Additional excavation over the following decade recovered further fossil material, ultimately representing at least three individuals and including teeth, additional hand and foot bones, and a juvenile femoral shaft. On the basis of this expanded assemblage, researchers formally described a new species, Homo luzonensis, in 2019. A direct-dating reanalysis published in 2023, using methods applied to the fossil material itself rather than to the surrounding sediment, revised the minimum age of the fossils to approximately 134,000 years ago, arguing that the original, younger estimate reflected a secondary geochemical overprint rather than the bone's true age.

The Callao Cave fossils display a combination of features that researchers found difficult to reconcile with any previously known hominin species. Some dental and skeletal characteristics were considered relatively primitive, resembling features seen in earlier or more basal members of the genus Homo, or in some interpretations resembling features found in the australopithecines that preceded the genus Homo altogether, while other features appeared more derived and comparable to those of later Homo populations. This unusual mosaic of traits, considered alongside the fossils' revised, still relatively late date, made Homo luzonensis immediately significant to debates about the diversity and complexity of the human family tree in the Late Pleistocene.

Students must recognize, however, that the evidentiary basis for Homo luzonensis is considerably thinner than that for Homo floresiensis, and the chapter is deliberately cautious on this point. The Callao Cave assemblage includes no complete skull, no cranium of any substantial completeness, and no skeleton remotely comparable to the LB1 find at Liang Bua. Brain size and overall body proportions for Homo luzonensis cannot currently be reliably estimated from the available material, since the diagnostic elements needed for such estimates, complete or largely complete long bones and cranial remains, have not been recovered. Some proportions of the recovered hand and foot bones have led researchers to consider reduced body size likely for the species, but this remains an inference drawn from indirect skeletal evidence rather than a measurement supported by a complete or substantially complete limb bone, and it should be described with corresponding caution. Ancestry, likewise, remains substantially more speculative for Homo luzonensis than for Homo floresiensis, given the much smaller and more fragmentary fossil sample. This textbook accordingly avoids offering a detailed physical reconstruction, a confident estimated height, or a settled ancestral lineage for Homo luzonensis beyond what the limited fossil evidence actually supports: a distinct, anatomically unusual insular hominin population, probably though not certainly of reduced body size, present on Luzon by at least approximately 134,000 years ago, whose fuller biology and evolutionary history await further discovery.

Section 7.8

Crossing Water: Accident, Intention, or Something Between?

Every population discussed in this chapter shares one basic requirement: at some point in its history, it crossed water that persisted even at the lowest Pleistocene sea levels. The evidence reviewed in Sections 7.3 through 7.7 establishes this requirement was met, repeatedly, across more than a million years and at two geographically separate locations, Flores and Luzon. What the evidence does not establish, and what this section addresses directly, is the mechanism by which these crossings occurred.

Several possibilities have been proposed. Hominins might have used deliberately constructed watercraft, however simple, reflecting planned and repeated voyaging. They might have crossed on natural rafts of vegetation, dislodged from coastlines by storms, river floods, or tsunamis, and carried across channels by currents without any deliberate intention or planning on the part of the hominins involved. They might have made repeated short crossings during periods when channel widths were unusually narrow, gradually island-hopping rather than undertaking a single dramatic voyage. Some combination of these mechanisms, varying by location and period, is also possible, and it is equally possible that the true mechanism was something not yet identified or considered by current research.

This textbook resists framing the question as a simple choice between "they built boats" and "they washed ashore by accident." The archaeological fact of successful colonization does not, by itself, reveal the cognitive planning, technological capability, or intentionality involved in achieving it. A population might have reached an island through a combination of accident and repeated attempts, with only a small number of successful crossings, out of many failed or fatal ones, ultimately producing the populations later evidenced in the fossil and archaeological record. The evidence currently available, stone tools, butchered bones, and hominin skeletal remains on the islands themselves, cannot distinguish among these possibilities, because none of it directly documents the crossing itself.

The appropriate historical conclusion, and the one this chapter asks students to adopt, is narrower than popular accounts often suggest. Deep-water island geography demonstrates that a crossing was required. Successful colonization, documented by tools, butchered animal remains, and fossils, demonstrates that a crossing occurred. Neither fact demonstrates that the hominins involved possessed watercraft, deliberate navigational intent, or any particular level of cognitive sophistication beyond what was minimally necessary to survive the crossing and subsequently establish a viable population. Confident claims about "the first sailors" or "the first mariners" of island Southeast Asia, sometimes encountered in popular writing about Flores and Luzon, considerably overstate what the evidence can currently support.

Section 7.9

Island Evolution and Multiple Human Lineages

Bringing the Flores and Luzon evidence together allows this chapter to state its central historical argument in full. By the middle and later Pleistocene, island Southeast Asia was not occupied by a single, uniform hominin population steadily progressing toward modern humans along one evolutionary line. It contained, at minimum, three geographically distinct populations documented in this and the preceding chapter: Homo erectus on Java, examined in Chapter 6; Homo floresiensis on Flores, most likely descended from an early Asian Homo erectus-like ancestor and displaying a distinctive small-bodied, small-brained anatomy sustained over several hundred thousand years; and Homo luzonensis on Luzon, anatomically distinct from both of the others in ways that current fossil evidence cannot yet fully explain.

These findings help dismantle an older, ladder-like model of human evolution, in which a single ancestral hominin species is imagined to have given way, in tidy sequence, to a single, more advanced successor species, which in turn gave way to the next, ultimately culminating in modern humans. Such a model was already under pressure from fossil discoveries elsewhere in the world well before the Flores and Luzon finds, but those finds provided some of the most vivid and geographically concentrated evidence against it. Instead, the evidence supports a branching, geographically fragmented picture of hominin evolution, in which multiple populations, isolated from one another by water, mountains, or simple distance, followed distinct evolutionary trajectories over long periods, some producing populations markedly different from the ancestral form and from one another.

Island isolation offers one plausible framework for understanding some of these differences. Islands frequently produce distinctive evolutionary outcomes in other species, including notable examples of both dwarfism in large-bodied animals and gigantism in small-bodied animals, driven by factors such as limited food resources, absence of large predators, and small founding population sizes subject to strong genetic drift. Flores itself preserves fossil evidence of dwarfed elephant relatives and of unusually large rodents and reptiles, including Komodo dragons, providing a documented local context for island-driven evolutionary change. It is reasonable to consider whether comparable processes contributed to the reduced body size observed in Homo floresiensis.

Historical Vocabulary
Island Dwarfism

Definition: Island dwarfism refers to an evolutionary pattern in which populations of a normally large-bodied species evolve markedly reduced body size after becoming isolated on an island over many generations. It is often discussed alongside the related pattern of island gigantism, in which small-bodied species evolve increased body size under similar conditions of isolation.

Historical context: Flores provides one of the best-documented settings for this pattern anywhere in the world. The island's fossil record includes dwarfed relatives of large mainland elephants, alongside unusually large rodents and reptiles, including Komodo dragons. Researchers studying Homo floresiensis have proposed that comparable processes, operating over hundreds of thousands of years, contributed to its markedly reduced body size relative to other members of the genus Homo.

Why it matters: Island dwarfism gives historians and scientists a documented, evidence-based process by which a normally larger-bodied lineage could plausibly evolve toward smaller body size on Flores, rather than requiring an appeal to pathology or to an implausibly primitive ancestral form to explain the Liang Bua remains.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not treat island dwarfism as a deterministic law guaranteeing that every isolated population, hominin or otherwise, will shrink in predictable ways, nor as a complete explanation covering every unusual anatomical feature observed at Liang Bua. Reduced body size is only one element of Homo floresiensis's anatomy, and several other features, including aspects of brain structure and limb proportions, require separate lines of evidence and explanation that island dwarfism alone does not supply. The concept should be applied as one well-supported contributing factor among others still under investigation, not as a single formula that resolves the species' full evolutionary history.

At the same time, this textbook cautions against treating island dwarfism as an automatic or fully deterministic explanation, capable by itself of accounting for every unusual feature observed in the Flores or Luzon fossils. Reduced body size is only one element of the anatomical picture at Liang Bua, and several other features, including aspects of brain structure, limb proportions, and skeletal morphology, require separate explanation that current evidence does not fully provide. For Homo luzonensis, the fossil sample is too limited even to establish body size with confidence, meaning that an island-dwarfism explanation cannot yet be meaningfully tested for that species at all. Students should treat island evolution as a genuinely useful explanatory framework, supported by comparative evidence from other Flores fauna, while recognizing that it does not resolve every open question about either species.

Section 7.10

An Archipelago of Humans

This chapter has traced two separate but structurally similar stories: hominin populations that reached deep-water islands well beyond the reach of Sundaland's Pleistocene land bridges, whose early archaeological traces were later followed, at each island, by fossil evidence of anatomically distinctive hominins, without the two bodies of evidence necessarily documenting the same continuous population. On Flores, this record extends from stone artifacts more than a million years old, through the roughly 700,000-year-old Mata Menge fossils, to the skeletal remains of Homo floresiensis at Liang Bua, most recently dated to between approximately 100,000 and 60,000 years ago. On Luzon, the record extends from the roughly 709,000-year-old Kalinga butchery site, whose hominin makers remain unidentified, to the fossil evidence for Homo luzonensis at Callao Cave, now dated to a minimum age of approximately 134,000 years following the 2023 reanalysis; the Kalinga toolmakers cannot be assumed to be ancestral to the much later Callao Cave population.

The central conclusion this chapter asks students to carry forward is straightforward to state but historically significant in its implications: by the later Pleistocene, Southeast Asia was not occupied by one uniform hominin population. Java, Flores, and Luzon each preserve evidence of different populations with different evolutionary histories, some separated from one another by major water barriers for hundreds of thousands of years, and at least one, Homo floresiensis, apparently surviving until surprisingly late in the Pleistocene before its ultimate fate becomes archaeologically unclear. This diversity should not be read as a story of primitiveness or evolutionary failure. It is, instead, a story of remarkable adaptability, of hominin populations reaching and surviving on islands that required crossing persistent water barriers, and of long, largely independent evolutionary trajectories playing out simultaneously across a geographically fragmented region.

This conclusion changes the question that the next chapter must address. Chapter 8 turns to the arrival of anatomically modern humans, Homo sapiens, in Southeast Asia. Given the evidence reviewed in this chapter, that arrival cannot be understood as an entry into an empty or uniform landscape. The relevant historical question, carried forward explicitly into Chapter 8, is not simply when modern humans reached the region, but what kinds of human populations and landscapes they were entering, and what archaic populations had inhabited the region, which may still have survived in some places, and whether overlap or interaction with arriving Homo sapiens can actually be demonstrated by the evidence. Chapter 9 subsequently examines what genetic evidence can and cannot reveal about the deeper population history connecting, or failing to connect, these various hominin populations, including the distinct question of Denisovan ancestry, which this chapter has deliberately left aside in order to keep its focus on the fossil and archaeological evidence for archaic island hominins.

Evidence Window

The Kalinga Rhinoceros Assemblage, Northern Luzon

Location: Kalinga, Cagayan Valley, northern Luzon, the Philippines · Approximate age: c. 709,000 years ago, established through multiple geochronological methods applied to the surrounding sediments · Type of evidence: Archaeological assemblage consisting of fifty-seven stone tools and the disarticulated, cut-marked skeletal remains of a rhinoceros

Context: Excavation at Kalinga recovered a concentration of stone flakes and cores together with the bones of a single rhinoceros individual, several of which display cut marks and percussion damage consistent with deliberate butchery, including disarticulation of limb bones and possible marrow extraction. A later taphonomic study of the site found that the bones and tools had undergone some short-distance transport, likely by a mudflow, after the butchery took place, meaning the assemblage should not be read as an untouched activity floor preserving the exact spot of the butchery event, though the evidence for butchery itself remains sound.

Description of the evidence (not a translated text; this is a physical archaeological assemblage rather than a written document):

The assemblage combines stone artifacts, showing signs of deliberate flaking, with rhinoceros bones bearing cut marks and fracture patterns interpreted as evidence of intentional butchery for meat and marrow. The direct association of tools and modified bone within a securely dated sedimentary layer allows researchers to infer deliberate carcass processing by hominins at or near this location at the stated date.

Analysis: This assemblage demonstrates that hominins present on Luzon by roughly 709,000 years ago deliberately processed a large animal carcass using stone tools for cutting and likely for marrow extraction. This is significant evidence of butchery behavior at a very early date on an island that required crossing persistent water barriers to reach. The evidence securely establishes carcass processing; it does not, by itself, establish social coordination, planning depth, or any broader claim about the general cognitive sophistication of the hominins responsible.

Limitations: No hominin fossil remains were recovered in direct association with the Kalinga assemblage. The evidence therefore cannot identify which hominin species produced it. It also cannot reveal whether the animal was hunted, scavenged, or encountered already dead, though the pattern of cut marks is consistent with active processing of the carcass by hominins. Because the assemblage was subject to some post-depositional transport, it also cannot be treated as a precisely preserved record of the original butchery location.

Perspective represented: The evidence reflects only the physical remains of hominin activity at a single moment or short interval in time; it offers no direct information about the social organization, language, or cultural life of the hominins responsible.

Historical Debate

What Was Homo floresiensis?

The question: What evolutionary origin and taxonomic status best explains the small body size, small brain size, and distinctive skeletal anatomy of the hominin remains recovered from Liang Bua on Flores?

Interpretation A — Pathological modern human: Some researchers, especially in the years immediately following the 2004 announcement, argued that LB1 represented a modern human affected by a developmental or genetic condition producing unusually small body and brain size, rather than a distinct species.
Interpretation B — Descendant of Asian Homo erectus, reduced through island isolation: Many researchers argue that Homo floresiensis descended from a population of Asian Homo erectus, similar to the population documented on Java, which became isolated on Flores and evolved reduced body size over a long period, potentially through processes associated with island evolution.
Interpretation C — Descendant of an earlier, more primitive hominin lineage: Other researchers argue that certain skeletal features are better explained by descent from a considerably earlier and more primitive hominin lineage that reached island Southeast Asia well before Homo erectus is otherwise documented to have done so.

Evidence: All three interpretations draw on the same core evidence: the skeletal anatomy of LB1 and associated individuals from Liang Bua, comparative anatomy of other hominin species, and, more recently, the roughly 700,000-year-old Mata Menge fossils.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns primarily interpretation of anatomical evidence and inferred ancestry rather than disagreement over the age or existence of the remains themselves. The pathological-human interpretation has lost considerable support following the recovery of multiple individuals and the older Mata Menge fossils, both of which are difficult to reconcile with a single-instance developmental disorder. The 2024 Mata Menge study provides some of the strongest currently available support for Interpretation B, though it does not fully resolve every anatomical detail raised in support of Interpretation C.

Why it matters: The resolution of this debate affects how historians and scientists understand the diversity and evolutionary flexibility of the genus Homo, and how far back in time complex, geographically dispersed hominin evolution in island Southeast Asia can be documented.

Student task: Using the evidence summarized above, explain which interpretation currently has the strongest evidentiary support, and identify what kind of future discovery could further strengthen or challenge that interpretation.

Multiple Perspectives

Reading the Same Island Evidence Differently

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives representing different scientific disciplines and methodological priorities. They are not quotations from any single named researcher.

From the perspective of a paleoanthropologist studying skeletal anatomy: The detailed shape of the cranium, jaw, wrist, and foot bones offers the most direct evidence for classifying a hominin population and assessing its likely evolutionary relationships to other known species, even when that evidence is difficult to reconcile fully with any single existing category.
From the perspective of an archaeologist studying stone tools and site formation: Evidence of hominin presence and behavior, such as tool technology and butchery marks, can be documented with confidence even when no fossil remains survive to identify the species responsible, and such evidence should not be over-interpreted as proof of a particular species' presence.
From the perspective of an island biogeographer: Long-term isolation on an island with limited resources and few predators has repeatedly produced dramatic evolutionary changes in body size among other animal species on Flores, providing a plausible, evidence-based framework for interpreting comparable changes in hominins, without guaranteeing that every anatomical feature observed has an island-evolution explanation.
From the perspective of a geochronologist establishing site dating: Confident claims about a hominin population's age or duration depend entirely on the reliability of the dating methods applied to the specific sedimentary layers in which fossils or artifacts are found, and revised dating, such as the 2016 Liang Bua reanalysis, can substantially change scientific and historical conclusions even when the physical fossils themselves remain unchanged.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Reinterpreting Homo floresiensis, 2004 to the Present

What Changed

  • The proposed date range for the Liang Bua skeletal remains shifted substantially, from an original claim of survival to roughly 12,000 years ago to the currently accepted range of roughly 100,000 to 60,000 years ago following the 2016 reanalysis
  • Scientific support for the pathological-modern-human hypothesis declined considerably as additional individuals and the older Mata Menge fossils were recovered
  • Evidence for the species' likely ancestry strengthened substantially with the 2024 Mata Menge study, moving the debate toward, though not fully resolving it in favor of, descent from an early Asian Homo erectus-like population

What Stayed the Same

  • The basic anatomical description of LB1 as small-bodied and small-brained has remained consistent since 2004
  • The scientific classification of the Liang Bua population as a distinct species, Homo floresiensis, separate from modern humans, has persisted despite early challenges
  • Significant uncertainty regarding the precise evolutionary pathway that produced the species has persisted throughout the debate, even as some interpretive positions have gained or lost support

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity in the scientific understanding of Homo floresiensis illustrates how paleoanthropological knowledge develops: not through a single decisive discovery, but through the gradual accumulation and reanalysis of evidence over two decades.

Cause and Effect

Deep Water and the Limits of Inference

Geographic condition

Flores and Luzon remained separated from Sundaland and from each other by persistent deep-water channels throughout the Pleistocene, unlike Java's direct overland connection to the mainland.

Necessary consequence

Any hominin population reaching either island had to cross open water at some point, regardless of how narrow particular channels became during periods of low sea level.

Documented outcome

Archaeological and fossil evidence confirms that hominins did reach both islands, and reached them very early: more than a million years ago on Flores and by at least 709,000 years ago on Luzon.

What remains undetermined

The evidence does not reveal whether these crossings involved deliberate watercraft, accidental rafting, repeated short crossings, or some combination, and this textbook declines to assert a specific mechanism beyond what current evidence supports.

Historical complexity: This is a case in which a very confident conclusion (a crossing occurred) sits alongside a genuinely open question (how it occurred), and responsible historical and scientific writing must keep these two levels of certainty clearly distinguished rather than allowing the confidence of the first to spread, unjustified, onto the second.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living on an Island We Barely Know

The archaeological and fossil evidence from Flores and Luzon allows historians and scientists to reconstruct a genuine, if limited, picture of daily life among these island hominin populations. Stone tool technology at both Liang Bua and Kalinga indicates the deliberate production and use of cutting and possibly processing implements. The Kalinga rhinoceros assemblage and other faunal remains associated with the Liang Bua sequence indicate that these populations hunted or scavenged available animal resources, including, at Liang Bua, evidence associated with locally distinctive island fauna such as dwarfed elephant relatives, giant rodents, and Komodo dragons, the large monitor lizard still found on Flores and a few neighboring islands today. Fossil hominin remains were recovered from cave contexts at both Liang Bua and Callao Cave, though this alone does not establish that either cave functioned as a regular residential shelter, nor does it establish deliberate burial or other mortuary practice; the processes by which the remains came to be deposited and preserved in each cave require separate, site-specific taphonomic analysis rather than a general assumption of habitation or interment.

Beyond this, however, historians and scientists must acknowledge substantial and genuine limits on what can be reconstructed. The stone tool and faunal evidence cannot currently establish whether these populations possessed language in any form comparable to modern human speech, what kind of social organization or group size they maintained, whether they engaged in symbolic or ritual behavior, what kind of shelters or clothing they may have used, or how they organized reproduction, child-rearing, and community life. This textbook resists filling these gaps with plausible-sounding but unsupported speculation. The honest and historically responsible statement is that Flores and Luzon hominins left behind clear evidence of tool use and animal exploitation, and that, in the case of Homo floresiensis, this evidence supports a long-lasting Flores hominin lineage spanning hundreds of thousands of years, while leaving most other dimensions of their daily and social lives outside the reach of current archaeological evidence.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has extended the story of archaic hominins in Southeast Asia, introduced in Chapter 6 through the Javan record of Homo erectus, into the more geographically and scientifically complicated terrain of the deep-water islands of Flores and Luzon. Unlike Java, which hominins could reach largely by walking across the Sunda Shelf once large portions of it were exposed by falling Pleistocene sea levels, joining the landmasses of Sundaland into a much larger continuous dry-land area, Flores and Luzon lay beyond any land bridge. Reaching either island required crossing water that persisted even at the lowest sea-level stands of the Pleistocene, a geographic fact established through the biogeographic observations of nineteenth-century naturalists including Alfred Russel Wallace and Thomas Henry Huxley and confirmed by modern geological reconstruction.

On Flores, the archaeological record begins with stone artifacts more than a million years old, predating any associated hominin fossil evidence. The Mata Menge site, dated to approximately 700,000 years ago, provided the first directly dated fossil evidence for a small-bodied hominin population on the island, and a detailed 2024 study of this material strengthened the case for descent from an early Asian Homo erectus-like ancestor. The much better-known Liang Bua site produced the skeletal remains of Homo floresiensis, including the type specimen LB1, first discovered in 2003 and formally described in 2004. This chapter emphasized the important 2016 revision to the site's chronology, which replaced the widely publicized but now-abandoned claim of survival until roughly 12,000 years ago with a currently accepted date range of approximately 100,000 to 60,000 years ago for the skeletal remains, alongside a longer associated artifact sequence spanning roughly 190,000 to 50,000 years ago.

The chapter examined the ongoing scientific debate over the evolutionary origin of Homo floresiensis, distinguishing among the largely discredited pathological-modern-human hypothesis, the currently best-supported model of descent from an early Asian Homo erectus-like population reduced in size through long-term island isolation, and a more speculative model proposing descent from an earlier and more primitive hominin lineage. Students were asked to distinguish what is now strongly supported, the existence of a long-lived, small-bodied lineage on Flores extending from at least 700,000 years ago, from what remains genuinely debated, the precise ancestral pathway and evolutionary mechanisms that produced it.

On Luzon, the chapter first examined the Kalinga site, where stone tools and a butchered rhinoceros assemblage dated to approximately 709,000 years ago demonstrated hominin presence and deliberate carcass processing on the island long before any associated hominin fossil evidence, while a later taphonomic study cautioned that the assemblage had undergone some post-depositional transport. This chapter deliberately emphasized that the Kalinga evidence cannot identify which hominin species was responsible, and that it establishes butchery rather than any broader claim of social coordination or general behavioral sophistication, a distinction essential to responsible historical and scientific interpretation. The chapter then turned to Callao Cave, where fossil evidence first recovered in 2007 and expanded through subsequent excavation, including teeth, hand and foot bones, and a juvenile femoral shaft, led to the 2019 description of Homo luzonensis, with a 2023 direct-dating reanalysis subsequently revising the species' minimum age to approximately 134,000 years. The chapter emphasized that the fossil evidence for this species remains far more limited than that for Homo floresiensis, lacking any complete skull or substantially complete skeleton, and that confident claims regarding its body size, brain size, or precise ancestry are not currently supported by the available material, though reduced body size is considered likely on the basis of indirect skeletal evidence.

A substantial portion of the chapter addressed the unresolved question of how hominins actually crossed the persistent water barriers separating Sundaland from Flores and Luzon. The chapter concluded that while successful colonization of both islands is well established by archaeological and fossil evidence, the specific mechanism of crossing, whether through deliberate watercraft, accidental rafting, repeated short crossings, or some combination, cannot currently be determined from the evidence, and that confident claims about early hominin seafaring considerably overstate what the evidence supports.

The chapter also introduced island dwarfism as a plausible, evidence-supported framework for understanding some of the anatomical changes observed on Flores, drawing on comparative evidence from other dwarfed and gigantic Flores fauna, while cautioning against treating this framework as an automatic or complete explanation for every unusual feature observed in the hominin fossil record, particularly given the very limited fossil sample currently available for Homo luzonensis.

Taken together, the evidence examined in this chapter demonstrates that Pleistocene Southeast Asia contained multiple, geographically separated hominin populations following distinct evolutionary histories, rather than a single population progressing along one evolutionary line. Java's Homo erectus, Flores's Homo floresiensis, and Luzon's Homo luzonensis together illustrate a branching, fragmented pattern of human evolution in island Southeast Asia that a simple, linear evolutionary narrative cannot accommodate.

This conclusion has direct consequences for how the following chapters must be read. Chapter 8 examines the arrival of anatomically modern humans, Homo sapiens, in Southeast Asia, and this chapter's evidence establishes that any such arrival occurred within a region already inhabited, in some places, by anatomically distinct and long-established archaic hominin populations. Chapter 9 subsequently addresses what genetic evidence, including evidence bearing on Denisovan ancestry, can and cannot reveal about the deeper population history connecting these various hominin populations, a subject this chapter has deliberately left for that later, more specialized treatment.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Sundaland
The broader geological and biogeographic region comprising the Malay Peninsula, Sumatra, Java, Borneo, and the intervening Sunda Shelf; during periods of low Pleistocene sea level, exposure of large portions of the shelf connected these landmasses into a much larger continuous dry-land area. Discussed in Chapter 6 and contrasted with Wallacea in this chapter.
Sunda Shelf
The shallow continental platform underlying the sea between Sumatra, Java, Borneo, and mainland Southeast Asia, large portions of which were exposed during periods of low Pleistocene sea level, connecting Sundaland's present-day landmasses into a much larger continuous dry-land area.
Wallacea
The biogeographic zone of islands, including Sulawesi, the Moluccas, and the Lesser Sunda Islands such as Flores, lying between the Sunda Shelf and the Sahul Shelf and never fully connected to either by Pleistocene land bridges.
Wallace Line
A biogeographic boundary between Bali and Lombok, named for Alfred Russel Wallace, marking a persistent deep-water barrier and a corresponding sharp division in animal populations.
Mata Menge
A site on Flores producing hominin fossils dated to approximately 700,000 years ago, providing early evidence for small body size on the island.
Liang Bua
A limestone cave on Flores where the skeletal remains of Homo floresiensis, including the type specimen LB1, were recovered.
Homo floresiensis
A small-bodied, small-brained hominin species described from Liang Bua, currently dated to approximately 100,000 to 60,000 years ago, whose evolutionary origin remains partly debated.
LB1
The most complete skeleton recovered from Liang Bua and the type specimen used to formally describe Homo floresiensis.
Type specimen
The individual fossil specimen designated as the reference example on which a new species' formal scientific description is based.
Kalinga
A site in northern Luzon producing stone tools and a butchered rhinoceros assemblage dated to approximately 709,000 years ago, demonstrating hominin presence without identifying the species responsible.
Callao Cave
A cave site in northern Luzon producing the fossil evidence, first recovered in 2007, on which the 2019 description of Homo luzonensis was based.
Homo luzonensis
A hominin species described in 2019 from a limited assemblage of teeth, hand and foot bones, and a juvenile femoral shaft from Callao Cave, with a minimum age revised by direct dating in 2023 to approximately 134,000 years, and whose body size and ancestry remain poorly understood due to the limited fossil sample.
Island dwarfism
An evolutionary pattern in which large-bodied species evolve reduced body size on islands, often associated with limited resources and reduced predation, considered as one possible, non-deterministic explanatory framework for small body size in Homo floresiensis.
Insular endemism
The tendency of isolated island populations to evolve distinctive traits not found in mainland relatives, illustrated on Flores by dwarfed elephant relatives, giant rodents, and the Komodo dragon.
Founder effect
The genetic and evolutionary consequences that follow when a new population is established by a small number of individuals, potentially amplifying certain traits over generations.
Stratigraphy
The study of layered sedimentary deposits at an archaeological or fossil site, used to establish the relative and, with dating methods, absolute chronology of finds.
Radiometric dating
A family of scientific dating methods based on the measurable decay of radioactive isotopes, used alongside other geochronological techniques to date the Flores and Luzon sites discussed in this chapter.
Faunal assemblage
The collection of animal remains recovered from an archaeological site, used to reconstruct diet, environment, and, where cut marks are present, evidence of hominin butchery.
Taphonomy
The study of the processes affecting an organism's remains after death, including burial, decay, and modification, used to distinguish natural bone damage from deliberate hominin butchery.
Hominin
A member of the taxonomic grouping including modern humans and their extinct close relatives and ancestors following the split from the ancestors of chimpanzees, used in this chapter to refer collectively to all human and human-related species discussed.
Archaic human
A general term for extinct hominin populations more closely related to modern humans than to earlier hominins, but anatomically distinct from anatomically modern Homo sapiens.
Phylogeny
The evolutionary history and relationships of a group of organisms, a central and contested subject in the study of both Homo floresiensis and Homo luzonensis.
Derived trait
An anatomical feature that differs from the ancestral condition and evolved more recently within a lineage, contrasted with a primitive trait.
Primitive trait
An anatomical feature that resembles the ancestral condition of a lineage and has not changed substantially, contrasted with a derived trait.
Species diagnosis
The set of anatomical features used to distinguish a proposed new species from other known species, central to the scientific description of both Homo floresiensis and Homo luzonensis.
Cranial capacity
The estimated internal volume of the braincase, used as a proxy for brain size and a key diagnostic feature distinguishing Homo floresiensis from modern humans.
Pathology hypothesis
The largely discredited interpretation that the Liang Bua remains represent modern humans affected by a developmental or genetic disorder rather than a distinct species.
Butchery marks
Cut marks, percussion fractures, and related damage on animal bones indicating deliberate processing by hominins using stone tools, central to the interpretation of the Kalinga assemblage.
Deep-water barrier
A persistent channel of open ocean that Pleistocene sea-level changes narrowed but never closed, requiring any land animal, including hominins, to cross water to pass through it.
Land bridge
A temporarily exposed connection of dry land between landmasses, created during periods of low sea level, of the kind that connected Java to the Southeast Asian mainland but never connected Flores or Luzon to it.
Population history
The study of how populations of organisms, including hominins, are related to one another, diverged, migrated, and interacted over time, a subject developed further in Chapter 9.
Anachronism
The error of applying a later concept, identification, or certainty to an earlier body of evidence that cannot support it, as when evidence of hominin presence is mistakenly treated as evidence of a specific species' presence.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to hold two ideas in tension: genuine wonder at what the fossil and archaeological record of Flores and Luzon reveals, and genuine discipline about the limits of that record. Hominins reached islands that required crossing persistent, uncrossable-by-foot barriers of open water, and they did so extraordinarily early, more than a million years ago in the case of Flores and by at least 709,000 years ago in the case of Luzon. Once established, at least one of these populations, on Flores, persisted for hundreds of thousands of years, developing a distinctive small-bodied anatomy that forced scientists to reconsider assumptions about the flexibility and diversity of the genus Homo. These are remarkable historical and scientific facts, and they deserve to be presented as such.

At the same time, this chapter has repeatedly insisted that remarkable evidence does not license unsupported conclusions. Evidence of hominin presence is not evidence of a hominin species' identity unless fossils permit that identification, as the Kalinga case makes clear. A single well-preserved skeleton can support a detailed anatomical description while leaving its evolutionary origin genuinely disputed, as the ongoing Homo floresiensis debate demonstrates. A handful of teeth and small bones can justify naming a new species while leaving its appearance and way of life almost entirely unknown, as the case of Homo luzonensis shows. And the successful colonization of an island separated by open water does not, by itself, reveal whether that colonization involved deliberate boats, chance rafting, or some combination of the two.

This discipline matters beyond this single chapter. The methods of evaluating evidence introduced here, distinguishing presence from identity, distinguishing strongly supported conclusions from genuinely contested interpretations, and distinguishing revised scientific understanding from earlier, now-abandoned claims, will recur throughout this textbook whenever evidence is incomplete, contested, or subject to ongoing revision, which is to say, throughout nearly the entire work.

Chapter 7 also completes an important structural transition within Part II of this volume. Chapter 6 introduced a single, relatively well-documented hominin lineage on Java. This chapter has shown that the same broad period and region in fact contained several distinct, geographically separated hominin populations, each with its own history, evidence, and unresolved questions. That expanded, more fragmented picture is the one students should carry into Chapter 8, which turns to the arrival of anatomically modern humans in a Southeast Asia that was, on the evidence reviewed here, considerably more populated by diverse archaic hominins than older, simpler narratives once assumed.
