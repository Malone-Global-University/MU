India, China, and the Early Maritime Trade

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Somewhere on the narrow neck of the Thai-Malay Peninsula, in the centuries before the Common Era, artisans sat before small anvils and grinding stones, shaping beads of carnelian and agate whose raw stone was likely sourced from South Asia and whose finished forms were sized, shaped, and strung to suit Southeast Asian tastes. They were not passive recipients of a finished foreign product. They were participants in a growing, many-stranded system of exchange that was beginning to draw the South China Sea, the Bay of Bengal, the Indian Ocean, and, at its farthest and thinnest edges, the Mediterranean-connected world into a single, if loosely and unevenly joined, commercial fabric.

Chapter 17 traced how communities across island and peninsular Southeast Asia built dense regional networks of exchange, moving nephrite ornaments, pottery, and other goods along routes connecting Taiwan, the northern Philippines, coastal Vietnam, Borneo, the Thai-Malay Peninsula, and eastern Indonesia centuries before any of the region's best-documented states appeared in the historical record. That regional achievement is the essential precondition for this chapter. Southeast Asia did not receive long-distance trade with South Asia and China as an empty stage awaiting foreign visitors. It received that trade as a region already organized, already connected internally by sea, and already accustomed to moving goods, styles, and technologies across considerable distances.

This chapter asks a different question from Chapter 17, though a closely related one: how did those already-existing regional networks become linked to expanding systems of exchange reaching South Asia, China, the wider Indian Ocean, and, more distantly and more rarely, the Mediterranean-connected world? The temptation, when telling this story, is to imagine a simple line running from a civilizational center to a receiving periphery: Indian merchants sailing east with luxury goods, Chinese officials sending emissaries south, and Southeast Asian communities absorbing what arrived. The archaeological and textual evidence examined in this chapter does not support that picture. It instead describes a far more interesting and more historically accurate process, in which Southeast Asian ports, craftspeople, and intermediaries selected which commodities to produce and exchange, organized the routes and settlements through which goods moved, and often manufactured, locally, the very objects that a careless reading of the evidence might mistake for straightforward imports.

The site of Khao Sam Kaeo, on the upper Thai-Malay Peninsula, anchors much of this chapter's argument. Excavations there have revealed a settlement occupied from approximately the fourth century BCE into the first century CE, with particularly intensive cosmopolitan production and exchange documented during the fourth through second centuries BCE, where South Asian-style ceramics and craft techniques, Southeast Asian ornament traditions, and metallurgical practices related to Western Han Chinese traditions all appear together. Crucially, the evidence does not show a warehouse of finished imports. It shows raw materials, production debris, unfinished pieces, and tools, all pointing toward local manufacture using imported materials and, in some cases, imported or transferred techniques. Khao Sam Kaeo was a place where people, materials, technologies, and local demand interacted, not merely a place where foreign goods arrived and were unpacked.

This chapter also asks students to think carefully about geography as an active historical force rather than a passive backdrop. The narrow Kra Isthmus, where the Thai-Malay Peninsula pinches to its narrowest width, allowed goods and travelers to move between the Bay of Bengal and Andaman Sea on one side and the Gulf of Thailand and South China Sea on the other without necessarily sailing the long way around the peninsula's southern tip. This geography helps explain why relatively small port settlements on the upper peninsula, rather than only the great classical kingdoms examined in later chapters, became unusually cosmopolitan places where objects and techniques from South Asia, mainland and island Southeast Asia, and southern China converged.

A further theme running throughout this chapter concerns the limits of archaeological evidence itself. Glass, stone, ceramic, and metal objects survive in the ground far better than the aromatic resins, timbers, spices, textiles, and foodstuffs that very likely moved in the opposite direction, from Southeast Asian forests, coasts, and workshops toward South Asian, Chinese, and more distant markets. An archaeological record dominated by imported beads and metal objects can therefore create a misleading impression that exchange flowed mostly in one direction, toward Southeast Asia, when a fuller accounting of Southeast Asian exports, most of them archaeologically invisible, would likely show a far more balanced and reciprocal system.

Finally, this chapter draws a firm boundary around what it does not attempt to explain. The circulation of Sanskrit, Buddhist and Hindu religious practice, Indian-derived political concepts, and courtly artistic conventions, the processes historians often discuss under the older label "Indianization," belongs to Chapter 19, which examines directly how Southeast Asian societies selected and transformed those cultural materials. This chapter's task is narrower and, in some ways, more foundational: to show how commercial networks intensified and interconnected before, and independently of, any particular cultural or religious transformation, and to insist that commerce itself does not automatically produce cultural conversion. The two processes were related, but they were not the same process, and conflating them would misrepresent both.

By the chapter's end, students should be able to explain how Southeast Asia's position between South Asia and China, so often described in older writing as a passive "crossroads," was in fact actively constructed and maintained by Southeast Asian communities who connected, redirected, and profited from the exchange systems developing around them, rather than one that had trade routes as innocent bystanders through which someone else's commerce simply passed.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how regional Southeast Asian maritime networks, established by the period covered in Chapter 17, became linked to expanding South Asian, Chinese, Indian Ocean, and Mediterranean-connected systems of exchange.
  2. Trace the geographic factors, including the Bay of Bengal, the Kra Isthmus, the Gulf of Thailand, and the South China Sea, that shaped early long-distance trade routes.
  3. Distinguish among imported finished objects, imported raw materials worked locally, and locally invented forms produced using transferred foreign techniques.
  4. Analyze the archaeological evidence from Khao Sam Kaeo and related peninsular sites as evidence for craft mobility, technological transfer, and local production for local consumers.
  5. Evaluate the significance of carnelian, agate, and glass bead production as evidence for the structure of early long-distance exchange.
  6. Assess the evidentiary problem posed by the archaeological invisibility of many likely Southeast Asian exports, including aromatic woods, resins, and other organic commodities.
  7. Explain the growing maritime linkage between southern China, especially the Hepu region, and South and Southeast Asian exchange systems during the Han period.
  8. Describe the concept of relay trade and distinguish it from the assumption of direct long-distance voyages by any single merchant or ship.
  9. Evaluate the evidence for Mediterranean-connected objects in Southeast Asia while avoiding the overstatement of direct Mediterranean-Southeast Asian contact.
  10. Identify the role of Southeast Asian merchants, intermediaries, and port communities in connecting foreign traders with inland producers and regional consumers.
  11. Distinguish the commercial and technological exchange examined in this chapter from the cultural and religious transformations, including the spread of Sanskrit and Indian-derived religious and political models, reserved for Chapter 19.
  12. Contextualize the growth of trans-Asian maritime exchange as a foundation for, but not a determinant of, the earliest documented Southeast Asian states examined beginning in Chapter 20.

Historical Context

This chapter builds directly on the argument developed in Chapter 17, which examined the archaeological evidence for Sa Huynh, Kalanay, and related maritime exchange traditions connecting coastal Vietnam, the Philippines, Borneo, and eastern Indonesia in the centuries before the region's earliest well-documented states. That chapter established two points essential to understanding the present one. First, Southeast Asian communities had already constructed dense, functioning networks of maritime exchange, moving worked nephrite, pottery, and other goods across considerable distances, well before intensive contact with South Asia and China. Second, historians studying these networks must exercise caution in interpreting artifact distributions, since the presence of a shared object type at distant sites does not by itself establish direct contact, a single point of origin, or a single ethnic or linguistic community responsible for its spread.

By the middle of the first millennium BCE, comparable local maritime networks were also developing along parts of the South Asian coastline, particularly around the Bay of Bengal. Archaeological research increasingly indicates that these two zones of network development, one centered on Southeast Asia and one centered on South Asia, developed cross-Bay contacts that are archaeologically visible as early as the fourth through second centuries BCE, and that by the second and first centuries BCE these separate regional networks had become integrated into a larger, more regularized Bay of Bengal sailing circuit, producing an expanding and increasingly systematic zone of interaction rather than a single dramatic moment of first contact. This chapter examines how that connection developed, what evidence documents it, and what commercial, technological, and social changes it produced within Southeast Asia itself.

At the same time, developments were unfolding along the southern coast of China. Han dynasty expansion and consolidation, especially from the second century BCE onward, extended Chinese administrative and commercial reach toward the far south, including the region around present-day Guangxi and northern Vietnam. Ports such as Hepu became nodes in a maritime system linking southern China with South and Southeast Asian trading circuits. Southeast Asia therefore found itself, by the last centuries BCE and the early centuries CE, positioned between two expanding zones of exchange, one reaching from the west through the Bay of Bengal and one reaching from the north along the South China Sea coast, in addition to its own long-established internal networks examined in Chapter 17.

Students should resist two tempting but historically inaccurate simplifications as they read this chapter. The first simplification treats this period as a story of "India and China discovering Southeast Asia," which inverts the more accurate picture of Southeast Asian communities extending their own networks outward to meet expanding South Asian and Chinese systems partway. The second simplification treats archaeological evidence for imported materials, techniques, or objects as proof of large-scale foreign settlement or direct colonization, when the evidence, examined carefully, more often supports a picture of selective technological transfer, small numbers of mobile specialists, and vigorous local production for local markets. This chapter follows the evidence toward the second, more complex picture throughout.

Section 18.1

From Regional Networks to Trans-Asian Exchange

Chapter 17 closed with a map of Southeast Asian exchange stretching from Taiwan and the northern Philippines through coastal Vietnam, Borneo, the Thai-Malay Peninsula, and eastern Indonesia, connected by shared traditions of worked nephrite ornament, pottery styles, and other goods moving along maritime routes that Southeast Asian communities themselves had built and maintained over many centuries. This chapter enlarges that map considerably. It now includes China to the north, the Bay of Bengal and South Asia to the west, the wider Indian Ocean beyond that, and, at the outer and thinnest edge of the system, goods and influences connected to the Mediterranean world.

It is important, however, not to imagine this enlarged map as a single, seamless "trade route" comparable to a modern shipping lane. The evidence instead points toward a set of overlapping circuits, each with its own history, its own participants, and its own rhythms, joined to one another chiefly through intermediary ports and intermediary communities rather than through direct, continuous voyages spanning the entire distance from one end of the system to the other. A pot, a bead, or a mirror recovered from a Southeast Asian archaeological site may have passed through the hands of several different communities, in several different regions, before reaching its final resting place, and no single merchant or vessel necessarily traveled the whole distance the object itself traveled.

This distinction matters for how students should picture the historical process examined in this chapter. Rather than a story in which South Asian or Chinese merchants "discovered" Southeast Asia and began trading directly with it, the more historically accurate picture describes multiple, already-developing regional systems, one centered on the Southeast Asian maritime world examined in Chapter 17, one developing along the Bay of Bengal coast of South Asia, and one extending from the Chinese mainland toward its southern coasts, gradually growing large enough, and dense enough, to intersect with one another. Southeast Asian communities did not wait passively for these larger systems to arrive; they extended their own networks to meet them, and in doing so, shaped the resulting trans-Asian system as active participants rather than as its passive object.

Section 18.2

The Geography of Maritime Connection

Understanding why particular places, rather than others, became centers of trans-Asian exchange requires attention to physical geography. The Bay of Bengal and the Andaman Sea lie to the west of the Thai-Malay Peninsula, connecting it, across open water, to the eastern coast of South Asia and to Sri Lanka. The Gulf of Thailand and the South China Sea lie to the east, connecting the peninsula to mainland Southeast Asia's coasts, to the Mekong Delta, and, farther north, to the southern coast of China. Between these two maritime worlds lies the Malay Peninsula itself, a long, narrow landmass extending south from the mainland.

At its narrowest point, in the area historians and geographers call the Kra Isthmus, the peninsula's width narrows dramatically. This geographic feature carried real historical significance. A ship, or more likely a combination of ships and porters or riverine transport, could move goods from the Bay of Bengal side of the peninsula to the Gulf of Thailand side, and vice versa, by a combination of short sea voyages and overland or river transport across the isthmus, rather than by sailing the entire length of the peninsula and around its southern tip near the Strait of Malacca. This trans-peninsular option did not replace the longer maritime route around the peninsula, which remained important throughout the period this textbook examines and grew in later centuries, but it provided an additional, and in some periods highly significant, corridor linking the Bay of Bengal and South China Sea worlds.

This geography helps explain a pattern that might otherwise seem puzzling: why relatively modest port settlements on the upper Thai-Malay Peninsula, rather than exclusively the larger and better-documented classical states examined in later chapters, became unusually cosmopolitan places in the centuries before the Common Era. A settlement positioned to take advantage of trans-peninsular routes could draw materials, techniques, and visitors from both the Bay of Bengal and South China Sea worlds, producing the kind of technologically and materially diverse archaeological record this chapter examines in detail at Khao Sam Kaeo and related sites.

Section 18.3

How Do We Know Trade Occurred?

Before examining specific commodities, routes, and sites, students must understand the kinds of evidence historians and archaeologists use to reconstruct early trans-Asian exchange, and the interpretive care that evidence demands. As in Chapter 17, the guiding methodological principle is that the presence of a foreign-looking object at a Southeast Asian site does not, by itself, prove that a foreign merchant carried it there, and the presence of a foreign raw material does not, by itself, prove that the finished object made from it was imported rather than made locally.

The relevant categories of evidence include imported ceramics and their local imitations; the chemical composition of glass, which can sometimes indicate a broad region of manufacture even when the finished object's origin cannot be pinpointed exactly; the technology used to work carnelian and agate, which can reveal whether a particular drilling or heating technique matches South Asian, Southeast Asian, or hybrid practices; metallurgical traditions and alloys, including distinctive bronze compositions; coins and inscribed or blank stone seals, which occur even at early cosmopolitan port sites such as Khao Sam Kaeo, though inscriptions of all kinds become more abundant, and increasingly tied to religious and courtly institutions, only in the later centuries examined in Volume II's later chapters; botanical remains, which can indicate the presence of particular plants or foodstuffs; and the physical layout of settlements, including workshop areas, production debris, and evidence for specialized craft zones.

Two interpretive distinctions recur throughout this chapter and deserve emphasis here. The first is the difference between a foreign-looking object and a foreign merchant: an object made in a foreign style can be produced locally by artisans trained in that style, whether those artisans were themselves foreign-born, locally trained, or some combination developed over time. The second is the difference between a foreign raw material and a finished import: raw glass, unworked carnelian, or metal ore can travel very long distances and then be shaped into a finished object entirely within Southeast Asia, according to Southeast Asian tastes and for Southeast Asian consumers. Applying these distinctions carefully is what allows Khao Sam Kaeo, examined in detail below, to be understood correctly as a site of local production using imported inputs, rather than as a simple depot for finished South Asian and Chinese goods.

Section 18.4

South Asia and the Bay of Bengal

The westward side of this chapter's story begins not with a single unified "India" but with multiple, historically distinct commercial systems developing along South Asia's own coastlines. The eastern coast of South Asia, portions of southern India, Sri Lanka, and the Bengal region each developed local maritime and riverine exchange networks during the middle centuries of the first millennium BCE, in a pattern broadly comparable to, though historically independent from, the Southeast Asian regional networks examined in Chapter 17. Treating "India" as a single undifferentiated trading partner would obscure this internal complexity and would misrepresent how exchange with Southeast Asia actually developed, since different South Asian coastal communities participated in Bay of Bengal exchange to different degrees and in different ways.

Cross-Bay connections are archaeologically visible as early as the fourth through second centuries BCE, documented at sites such as Khao Sam Kaeo and Ban Don Ta Phet; by approximately the second and first centuries BCE, these connections had intensified into a larger and more regularized Bay of Bengal sailing circuit linking South Asian coastal networks with Southeast Asian ones. Among the clearest markers of this connection are carnelian and agate, semi-precious stones whose sources lie predominantly in South Asia and whose associated working techniques, including particular methods of heat-treatment, shaping, and drilling, are closely associated with South Asian craft traditions; certain classes of ceramics, including fine wares whose forms and decoration parallel South Asian types; and, somewhat later, raw and worked glass, along with the technological knowledge required to shape it. High-tin bronze objects, a category of metalwork associated with particular South and Southeast Asian production traditions, provide a further tangible link documented in the archaeological record of this period.

Students should resist projecting the courtly, religious, and political connections examined in Chapter 19, including the spread of Sanskrit and Indian-derived religious models, backward onto this earlier commercial phase. The evidence discussed in this section documents material and technological exchange: stones, glass, metal, and the skills required to work them. Although short commercial inscriptions on stone seals, discussed further in Section 18.5, already appear at early cosmopolitan ports such as Khao Sam Kaeo, the evidence discussed in this section does not, by itself, document the circulation of religious texts, courtly titles, or the more elaborate epigraphic and literary use of Sanskrit, which becomes archaeologically and textually visible only in later centuries and which this textbook treats as a related but analytically distinct development.

Section 18.5

Khao Sam Kaeo: A Cosmopolitan Port before the Classical States

The port settlement of Khao Sam Kaeo, located on the upper Thai-Malay Peninsula in present-day Chumphon Province, Thailand, provides this chapter's central case study, and for good reason: the site's archaeological record demonstrates, more clearly than perhaps any other in the region for this period, the interactive and localized character of early trans-Asian exchange. Occupied from approximately the fourth century BCE into the first century CE, with its most intensively documented cosmopolitan phase falling within the fourth through second centuries BCE, well before the emergence of the region's best-documented early states examined in Chapters 20 through 27, Khao Sam Kaeo has yielded South Asian-style ceramics and associated craft traditions, Southeast Asian ornament styles, and metallurgical evidence connected to Western Han Chinese traditions, all recovered from within a single settlement complex.

Among the site's most striking finds are more than twenty inscribed or blank stone seals, several bearing short texts in Prakrit written in the Brāhmī script, one of which displays letter forms that appear archaic even by comparison with third-century-BCE Aśokan-period inscriptions in South Asia itself. These seals should not be mistaken for evidence of the later, more elaborate Sanskrit epigraphic and courtly traditions examined in Chapter 19; they instead illustrate a narrower and earlier phenomenon, the practical use of South Asian writing in a commercial and cosmopolitan port setting, most plausibly by or among merchants and craftspeople who required seals for marking, ownership, or exchange. This distinction, between early commercial writing at a trading settlement and the later Southeast Asian adoption and transformation of Sanskrit religious and political epigraphy, preserves the boundary this chapter maintains with Chapter 19 while still allowing the seals to serve as valuable evidence for the mobility of literate individuals connected to South Asian commercial networks.

The crucial interpretive point is that Khao Sam Kaeo was not simply a place where finished South Asian and Chinese goods arrived and were stored or redistributed. Excavated evidence includes raw materials, unfinished ornaments, production debris, and tools consistent with on-site manufacture, indicating that South Asian-style objects found at the settlement were, in substantial part, made there, using techniques either brought by mobile craftspeople or transmitted through some other form of technological transfer, and shaped, at least in part, according to preferences that served Southeast Asian consumers and local political or social display needs.

This evidence supports a historical interpretation considerably more interesting than a simple narrative of foreign goods entering a receiving society. Khao Sam Kaeo appears to have functioned as a genuinely cosmopolitan production center, where materials, technologies, and people connected to South Asia, mainland and island Southeast Asia, and southern China converged, and where local elites and consumers exercised real influence over what was produced and how. The metallurgical evidence is particularly striking in this regard: analysis of bronze-working traditions at the site has identified affinities extending across a vast geographic range, described by some archaeometallurgical researchers as an interlocking sphere of technological connection spanning several thousand kilometers, from South Asia through Southeast Asia to Western Han China.

A single governing sentence can summarize the significance of this site for the chapter's larger argument: Khao Sam Kaeo was not merely a place where foreign goods arrived; it was a place where people, materials, technologies, and local demand interacted, producing new hybrid forms of craft production that belonged to no single one of the traditions that fed into them.

Section 18.6

Craftspeople on the Move

If Khao Sam Kaeo's ornaments were made locally using South Asian techniques, an important question follows: how did those techniques reach the Thai-Malay Peninsula? The archaeological evidence for stone-bead production offers the clearest window onto this problem. Carnelian and agate working, as noted above, involved specialized techniques, including particular methods of heat-treatment to alter stone color and particular drilling methods, that are closely associated with South Asian craft traditions. The discovery of production debris, unfinished pieces, and associated tools at Khao Sam Kaeo demonstrates that at least some of this specialized working occurred on-site, within Southeast Asia, rather than exclusively in South Asia before export.

This evidence supports several possible, and not necessarily mutually exclusive, historical scenarios: South Asian craftspeople may have traveled to and resided, temporarily or for longer periods, at Khao Sam Kaeo and comparable settlements; Southeast Asian artisans may have received intensive training in South Asian techniques, whether through direct instruction from visiting specialists or through some other channel of technological transmission; or some combination of both processes may have occurred over time, as an initial period of foreign craft presence gave way to increasingly localized production carried out by artisans who had absorbed and adapted the relevant skills. The archaeological record, as currently understood, cannot determine with certainty which of these scenarios, or which combination, best describes the historical reality at any given site or period, and responsible historians acknowledge this uncertainty rather than asserting a single confident narrative.

What the evidence can establish more confidently is that the objects being produced, whatever the origin of the techniques and hands that made them, were shaped to conform to Southeast Asian tastes, sizes, and modes of display. This point is historically significant because it breaks decisively with a simplistic model in which finished Indian objects traveled unchanged to a passively receiving Southeast Asian audience. In its place, the evidence supports a considerably more dynamic model: skills, artisans, and materials combined with local elite and consumer demand to produce new systems of craft production that served Southeast Asian social and political purposes from the outset, rather than merely imitating a foreign original.

Section 18.7

Glass, Carnelian, Agate, and the Archaeology of Commerce

Beads deserve sustained attention as a category of evidence in their own right. Chapter 17 introduced beads and related ornaments as objects circulating within Southeast Asia's own regional networks. This chapter now asks a further question: where did some of these beads, and the materials and skills used to make them, ultimately originate, and how did local production change over time as trans-Asian connections intensified?

Current archaeological research on Southeast Asian bead production describes a picture considerably more complex than a single, simple model of Indian export to a Southeast Asian market. Instead, scholars have identified multiple phases of production and, quite possibly, multiple production centers operating at different times and in different places across the region, some using imported raw material, some using imported technique applied to more locally available material, and some combining both foreign and indigenous elements into genuinely new production traditions. This picture developed through decades of accumulating archaeological research and continues to be refined as new sites are excavated and existing collections are re-analyzed using improved scientific techniques.

For historical analysis, it is useful to distinguish among several possible categories that a single bead or ornament might fall into: an imported finished bead, carried to Southeast Asia complete and ready for use; imported raw glass or unworked stone, later shaped into a finished object within Southeast Asia; local manufacture using foreign material but locally invented forms; local manufacture using technology transferred from abroad but applied to locally sourced material; and, finally, locally designed ornaments made by foreign-trained or foreign-born specialists working within a Southeast Asian social and economic context. Each of these categories implies a different historical relationship between Southeast Asia and the wider trading world, and careful archaeological analysis, including provenance studies of glass composition and close examination of stone-working technique, is required to distinguish among them in any particular case.

Evidence Window

Carnelian, Agate, and Glass Provenance

What the evidence is: Archaeologists studying carnelian, agate, and glass ornaments from Southeast Asian sites apply several complementary techniques. Chemical and isotopic analysis of glass can sometimes indicate a broad region of raw-material origin, even when it cannot identify a precise workshop. Close microscopic examination of drilling marks, heat-treatment evidence, and finishing techniques on carnelian and agate can indicate whether a piece was made using methods closely associated with South Asian craft traditions, methods more typical of Southeast Asian workshops, or some hybrid combination. The presence of unfinished pieces, production waste, and tools at a settlement provides direct evidence for on-site manufacture, as distinct from the mere presence of finished ornaments, which could equally well have arrived through exchange.

What the evidence shows: At Khao Sam Kaeo and related peninsular sites, this combination of evidence points toward substantial local manufacture using both imported raw material and imported or transferred technique, alongside continuing local stylistic preferences that shaped the finished forms. This pattern, documented across multiple phases of research, has led specialists to speak of a genuinely interactive production system rather than a simple one-directional export relationship.

What the evidence cannot show: Provenance and technological analysis generally cannot identify individual craftspeople, cannot by itself determine whether an artisan was foreign-born or locally trained in a foreign technique, and cannot establish the precise mechanism, whether migration, apprenticeship, intermarriage, or another channel, by which a particular technology moved from one region to another. Historians must therefore describe several plausible scenarios rather than asserting a single certain account.

Questions for students:

  1. Why is the presence of production debris, rather than only finished ornaments, especially important evidence for local manufacture?
  2. What are the limits of using stylistic similarity alone, without technological or compositional analysis, to establish an object's origin?
  3. Why might historians prefer to describe several plausible scenarios for craft mobility rather than asserting a single confident narrative?
  4. How does this evidence change the way students should think about the phrase "Indian beads found in Southeast Asia"?
Section 18.8

Ports, Port-Settlements, and Trans-Peninsular Routes

Moving from artifacts to institutions, this section examines the settlements themselves. Alongside Khao Sam Kaeo, archaeologists have identified several related sites on the upper Thai-Malay Peninsula, including Khao Sek, Maliwan, and Phu Khao Thong, sometimes also called Bang Kluai Nok. Together these sites reveal an interconnected Kra Isthmus exchange landscape, though their specific political and economic relationships to one another were not identical or uniform. The evidence for Khao Sam Kaeo and Khao Sek is particularly strong for a closely related, ranked, and complementary port relationship, with some archaeologists interpreting the two as belonging to a single confederated system: Khao Sek, on the Bay of Bengal side of the peninsula, appears connected to Khao Sam Kaeo, nearer the Gulf of Thailand side, through trans-peninsular routes and specialized, possibly complementary functions related to particular stages of production, storage, or exchange. Maliwan, by contrast, appears to have functioned as its own contemporaneous west-coast port-city rather than as a satellite of Khao Sam Kaeo, while Phu Khao Thong is more closely associated with the neighboring settlement of Bang Kluai Nok as a related but distinct complex. Students should therefore picture the Kra Isthmus region as a landscape of several interconnected but not identical port systems, rather than as a single administratively unified network.

Recent archaeological work on the Kra Isthmus more broadly has moved away from an older tendency to treat these coastal settlements as loosely organized trading beaches where ships happened to stop. Instead, current interpretation increasingly favors a picture of specialized communities embedded within an organized regional exchange system, including populations sometimes associated with maritime and riverine mobility, who together maintained the routes, transshipment points, and production centers that linked the Bay of Bengal and South China Sea worlds. This more organized picture is consistent with the evidence, discussed above, for specialized craft production requiring sustained technical knowledge and infrastructure rather than occasional, opportunistic exchange.

Historians borrow several terms from later, better-documented commercial history to describe these settlements, while remaining cautious about projecting later institutional arrangements backward onto this earlier period. A port-of-trade, in the sense used by economic historians, generally refers to a settlement whose primary function is facilitating exchange among different trading communities, often under some form of political protection or regulation. An entrepôt refers more specifically to a port whose economic importance derives chiefly from its role in re-exporting or transshipping goods rather than from a large local agricultural hinterland. Transshipment describes the process of unloading goods from one mode or route of transport and reloading them onto another, precisely the kind of activity the Kra Isthmus's trans-peninsular routes would have required. These terms are useful analytical tools for describing the functions Khao Sam Kaeo and related settlements appear to have performed, though students should remember that the specific political and legal arrangements associated with later, textually documented ports-of-trade cannot be assumed to have existed in identical form during this earlier, largely pre-literate period.

Section 18.9

Southeast Asian Commodities

A history of early trans-Asian trade that focused only on objects arriving in Southeast Asia would badly distort the historical picture, since it would describe only half, and probably less than half, of the exchange actually taking place. This section asks directly: what did outsiders want from Southeast Asia, and what did Southeast Asian communities offer in exchange for the carnelian, glass, and metal goods examined above?

Based on later, better-documented patterns of Southeast Asian export, and on scattered evidence from this earlier period, historians identify several broad categories of likely Southeast Asian commodities: aromatic woods and resins, prized in South Asian, Chinese, and eventually Mediterranean-connected markets for use in religious ritual, medicine, and luxury consumption; forest products more generally, including various plant and animal materials valued for specific uses; spices, though the precise varieties and scale of spice exchange during this particular period remain less well documented than for later centuries; marine products, including items derived from shellfish, fish, and other sea life; metals, including tin, found in commercially significant deposits on the Thai-Malay Peninsula and in parts of the archipelago; gold, associated with several island and mainland sources; and, in some contexts, textiles and locally manufactured craft goods, where the evidence permits such an inference.

A crucial methodological point governs this entire discussion: most of the commodities listed above are organic, perishable, or otherwise poorly suited to archaeological survival. Aromatic resins, timber, spices, and textiles decompose; they leave little or no direct trace in most tropical and subtropical soil conditions, in sharp contrast to glass, stone, ceramic, and metal objects, which survive far better and therefore dominate the surviving archaeological record. This creates a serious evidentiary asymmetry: a Southeast Asian archaeological site might yield dozens of imported carnelian beads while providing no direct physical trace whatsoever of the aromatic wood, resin, or foodstuff that Southeast Asian communities likely exchanged for them. Historians must therefore be especially careful not to mistake an archaeologically visible import for evidence that exchange flowed mainly, or only, from South Asia and China toward Southeast Asia. The likelier reality, consistent with later, better-documented periods in which Southeast Asian forest and marine products commanded high value across Asian and eventually global markets, is a considerably more reciprocal system whose Southeast Asian half is simply harder to see archaeologically.

Section 18.10

China and the Southern Maritime Route

Turning to the northern side of this chapter's map, students should avoid imagining a sudden, large-scale Chinese commercial or settler presence appearing throughout Southeast Asian ports during this period. The evidence instead supports a picture of growing, but still relatively limited and unevenly documented, linkage between southern China and the maritime systems already developing across Southeast Asia and the Bay of Bengal.

The Hepu region, on the coast of present-day Guangxi in southern China, provides an especially useful anchor point for this discussion. Archaeological excavation of Han-period tombs in the Hepu area has revealed imported luxury materials, including glass and other exotic goods, whose presence is best explained by Hepu's role within a maritime trading system connecting southern China with South and Southeast Asian commercial networks, a system some scholars associate with the broader concept of a maritime Silk Road, though that term, like many retrospective labels, should be used with awareness of its later coinage and its potential to imply more organizational coherence than the actual historical system possessed.

Evidence for southern Chinese connections also appears farther south, within Southeast Asia itself. The metallurgical analysis of bronze-working traditions at Khao Sam Kaeo, discussed above, has identified affinities with Western Han Chinese metallurgical practices, suggesting that technological and material connections extended along the South China Sea coast and into the Southeast Asian mainland and peninsula during the same general period that Bay of Bengal connections were also intensifying. Taken together, the Hepu evidence and the Khao Sam Kaeo evidence allow historians to describe Southeast Asia during this period as a genuine middle zone, linked by sea to expanding commercial systems developing on both its western and northern sides, rather than as a region that experienced these connections in isolation from one another.

Section 18.11

A Trans-Asian Network, Not Simple Transit

An older historiographical habit pictures early long-distance trade through Southeast Asia as a straightforward transit corridor: goods moved from India to China, or from China to India, and Southeast Asia's ports functioned essentially as waypoints along a route whose true origin and destination lay elsewhere. The evidence examined in this chapter supports a considerably more complex, and more historically accurate, alternative picture.

Rather than a single route running in one direction between two endpoints, the evidence points toward multiple, overlapping, and multi-directional circuits operating simultaneously. Bay of Bengal exchange connected the Kra Isthmus region to South Asia. Separately, though overlappingly, South China Sea and Gulf of Thailand exchange connected the same peninsular region, along with the Mekong Delta and other mainland and island locations, to southern China. At the same time, Southeast Asia's own internal regional networks, examined at length in Chapter 17, continued to operate, moving goods, people, and technologies among Southeast Asian communities independently of, though increasingly intersecting with, the longer-distance connections to South Asia and China.

Khao Sam Kaeo again provides the clearest illustration of this multi-directional pattern, since its archaeological record documents South Asian, Southeast Asian, and Western Han Chinese-related technological traditions converging within a single settlement. Some archaeometallurgical researchers studying this evidence have described the resulting picture as a segmented but interlocking technological sphere extending across a vast geographic distance, reportedly more than five thousand kilometers when measured from South Asian sources through Southeast Asia to Han Chinese centers. The key historical conclusion to draw from this evidence is that Southeast Asia did not merely lie between other people's trade routes; it actively connected routes that would otherwise have remained separate, and its own communities, ports, and craftspeople were essential to making those connections function.

Section 18.12

Mediterranean-Connected Goods: How Far Did Distant Exchange Reach?

This section requires particular interpretive caution, since it addresses one of the areas most vulnerable to historical overstatement. Scattered Southeast Asian archaeological sites have yielded objects connected, directly or indirectly, to the Mediterranean world, including glass items, engraved gemstones known as intaglios, and gold-glass beads of types associated with Mediterranean or Mediterranean-influenced production. It is tempting, upon encountering such finds, to conclude that Roman or other Mediterranean merchants sailed directly to Southeast Asia. The evidence does not support this conclusion, and careful scholarship explicitly warns against it.

Several considerations counsel caution. First, actual Mediterranean-manufactured objects recovered from Southeast Asian sites remain comparatively scarce, particularly when set against the far more numerous South and East Asian materials examined earlier in this chapter. Second, objects that imitate Mediterranean forms, produced by artisans elsewhere in Asia who had encountered Mediterranean styles indirectly, can be mistaken for genuine imports unless subjected to careful compositional and stylistic analysis, meaning that some apparent "Roman" finds may in fact be locally or regionally produced imitations rather than objects that ever touched Mediterranean shores. Third, and most fundamentally, even genuine Mediterranean-manufactured objects reaching Southeast Asia most plausibly arrived through long intermediary chains involving multiple regional exchanges, rather than through any direct voyage connecting the Mediterranean world to Southeast Asian shores.

The burial complex of Pangkung Paruk in Bali offers a striking illustration of both the reality and the limits of this distant connection. The site consists of multiple sarcophagus burials, several formally excavated and containing glass, gold, carnelian, copper-alloy, and shell objects, with radiocarbon dating placing the burials principally in the second and third centuries CE. Roman-related gold-glass beads and bronze mirrors stylistically associated with Han China both appear among the site's mortuary assemblages, a combination that demonstrates island Southeast Asia's position within exchange systems extending across much of Afro-Eurasia by the early first millennium CE. Students should note, however, that the surviving excavation and reporting record does not securely establish that particular beads and a particular mirror were deposited together within one single, individually documented burial; the association is a site-level pattern across the mortuary complex rather than a single, precisely recorded grave assemblage. At the same time, this remarkable evidence does not establish that a Roman ship, or any Mediterranean merchant personally, ever reached Bali. It establishes that objects connected to distant Mediterranean production could, through a sequence of intermediary exchanges spanning thousands of kilometers, eventually reach a Southeast Asian community, where they were deposited alongside equally distant Chinese-related objects in a manner that reflected specifically Balinese mortuary practice and local valuation of exotic goods, whatever their ultimate origin.

Section 18.13

Intermediaries and the Relay Structure of Trade

The Pangkung Paruk evidence introduces a concept essential to correctly understanding early trans-Asian exchange: the distinction between a direct voyage and a relay system of trade. A direct voyage would involve a single ship, crew, or merchant traveling the entire distance between two distant points, for example between the Mediterranean world and island Southeast Asia. A relay system, by contrast, involves a sequence of shorter exchanges, each conducted by different participants operating within their own regional or sub-regional networks, through which an object can travel an enormous cumulative distance without any single person or vessel ever covering more than a fraction of that distance.

Applying the relay model to the evidence examined in this chapter, a hypothetical Mediterranean-connected object might have moved, over time and through multiple exchanges, from the Mediterranean world into the western Indian Ocean, from there to South Asian coastal markets, across the Bay of Bengal to the Thai-Malay Peninsula, through trans-peninsular or maritime routes to the Gulf of Thailand and South China Sea, and finally into island Southeast Asia, with each leg of the journey conducted by different merchants and communities who may have known little or nothing about the object's ultimate origin or eventual destination. This relay structure allows historians to make sense of genuinely long-distance connections, such as that documented at Pangkung Paruk, without needing to imagine direct, continuous voyages that the broader evidence does not support.

The relay model also carries an important lesson for how students should think about ancient economic connection more generally. It permits a form of very long-distance linkage, sometimes described informally as an early instance of interregional or "globalizing" exchange, while avoiding the anachronistic assumption that ancient participants possessed anything resembling a modern integrated global market, coordinated shipping networks, or awareness of the full extent of the systems in which they were, in fact, embedded. A Southeast Asian bead-maker at Khao Sam Kaeo very likely had no direct knowledge of Mediterranean glass sources, just as a Mediterranean glassmaker almost certainly had no direct knowledge of Southeast Asian consumers; both were nonetheless linked, indirectly and through many intermediary steps, within a single, if loosely and unevenly connected, system of exchange.

Section 18.14

Southeast Asian Merchants and Intermediaries

The Authoritative Chapter Outline specifically requires sustained attention to Southeast Asian merchants, ports, and intermediaries, and this section addresses that requirement directly. Even where the archaeological and limited textual record of this period does not preserve the names or personal histories of individual Southeast Asian traders, the structure of the exchange system described throughout this chapter requires the participation of local actors possessing specific and valuable knowledge.

Such knowledge would necessarily have included familiarity with local waterways, tides, and coastal hazards; understanding of monsoon wind timing, which governed when voyages across the Bay of Bengal and South China Sea could safely and efficiently be undertaken; command of regional languages sufficient to negotiate with both inland producers and visiting foreign traders; awareness of market preferences on multiple sides of an exchange, allowing an intermediary to judge what materials, techniques, or finished goods particular consumers, whether local elites, foreign visitors, or other Southeast Asian communities, would value; connections to inland supply routes, since materials such as forest products, tin, and gold generally originated away from the coast and had to be brought to port through some form of organized collection and transport; and, quite plausibly, sensitivity to local political conditions, including the relationships among coastal communities and any inland or interior polities capable of affecting trade.

Historians describe this pattern using necessarily careful language, acknowledging the limits of the evidence while still insisting on its historical significance: the identities of most individual Southeast Asian merchants and intermediaries active during this period are usually absent from the surviving record, since neither Southeast Asian communities nor their foreign trading partners left extensive written accounts naming specific traders at this early date. Their economic function, however, is clearly visible in the structure of the network itself. A settlement such as Khao Sam Kaeo could not have organized specialized craft production, maintained trans-peninsular routes, and drawn materials and techniques from multiple distant regions without local actors performing precisely the intermediary functions described here. The absence of names in the record should not be mistaken for an absence of historical agency.

Section 18.15

Foreign Communities without Colonization

Having established both the likelihood of mobile foreign craftspeople, discussed in Section 18.6, and the central importance of local Southeast Asian intermediaries, discussed in Section 18.14, this section addresses directly how these two categories of historical actor coexisted, and cautions against conflating their presence with anything resembling colonization or political conquest.

The evidence from Khao Sam Kaeo and related sites is consistent with several possible forms of foreign presence, each involving different degrees of permanence and different social implications: small, mobile communities of craft specialists, who may have traveled seasonally or periodically rather than settling permanently; resident merchants or artisans, who established longer-term residence at a port while remaining a minority within the broader local population; intermarriage between visiting or resident foreigners and local communities, a pattern well documented in many later, better-recorded periods of Southeast Asian port history and plausible, though harder to demonstrate archaeologically, for this earlier period; and temporary settlement tied to specific seasons of the monsoon-governed sailing calendar, after which visiting traders and craftspeople might return to their places of origin.

What the evidence does not support is any model involving mass migration, political takeover, or colonial administration imposed by South Asian, Chinese, or other outside powers during this period. The archaeological record at Khao Sam Kaeo indicates that Southeast Asian elites and consumers retained substantial control over what was produced, how it was styled, and for what purposes it was used, even as they drew on foreign materials and, quite possibly, foreign specialists to accomplish this production. This distinction, between welcoming and incorporating mobile foreign specialists on terms that served local interests, and being subjected to foreign political or military domination, is essential to maintaining the chapter's larger argument about Southeast Asian historical agency, and it directly anticipates the more explicitly cultural and religious version of the same argument developed in Chapter 19.

Section 18.16

Trade before "Indianization"

By this point in the chapter, students have encountered substantial evidence for South Asian materials, South Asian-derived craft technologies, and, quite plausibly, South Asian craftspeople present within Southeast Asian commercial settlements. It is natural, having absorbed this evidence, to ask a further question: did this intensifying commercial and technological contact lead automatically to the adoption of South Asian religion, political concepts, or writing systems?

The answer, based on careful attention to chronology and to the specific character of the evidence, is no, not automatically and not immediately. The commercial and technological connections examined throughout this chapter, centered on stone and glass ornament production, metallurgy, ceramics, the organization of ports and routes, and even the short commercial inscriptions found on seals at Khao Sam Kaeo and Khao Sek, are documented earlier, and rest on a different kind of evidence, than the clearer archaeological and epigraphic signs of sustained Sanskrit literacy, Buddhist and Hindu religious institutions, and Indian-derived courtly political models that become visible in the historical record only in later centuries and that form the specific subject of Chapter 19's examination of the historiographical debate over "Indianization" and localization.

This chapter closes, therefore, by drawing a firm and deliberate boundary rather than by anticipating that later discussion in detail. Intensifying trans-Asian commerce created the conditions, including wealth, cosmopolitan port populations, and habitual contact with South Asian visitors and materials, within which the later circulation of scripts, religious specialists, Sanskrit, and Indian-derived political and artistic conventions became possible. But creating the conditions for a later development is not the same as causing that development directly, and the mechanisms, motivations, and selective processes by which Southeast Asian societies eventually adopted and transformed specific religious and political ideas require their own dedicated historical treatment, undertaken fully in the next chapter rather than here.

Section 18.17

Toward Funan and the Earliest Documented States

This chapter's final task is to point forward, briefly and without anticipating the fuller treatment reserved for Part V, toward the earliest well-documented Southeast Asian states. The commercial developments examined throughout this chapter, expanding trans-Asian exchange, intensifying craft specialization, the growth of cosmopolitan port settlements, and the concentration of wealth and long-distance connections at particular locations, provided part of the historical context within which more complex and more clearly documented political formations later emerged.

The Mekong Delta deserves particular mention here as one of several zones increasingly drawn into the expanding networks this chapter has examined, since it lay within reach of both South China Sea and, through the Gulf of Thailand and peninsular routes, more distant Bay of Bengal connections. However, this chapter deliberately stops short of examining the archaeological site of Óc Eo or the historical and archaeological problem of "Funan" in any detail, since the Authoritative Chapter Outline assigns that specific subject, including the Chinese textual accounts, the archaeological evidence, and the historiographical debate over the relationship between the two, to Chapter 20.

Students should resist a tempting but historically unsupportable shortcut: the claim that trade caused states, as though increased commerce automatically and mechanically produced centralized political authority. The relationship between commercial intensification and political complexity is considerably more indirect and more historically contingent than such a formula suggests, involving specific local political choices, social structures, and historical circumstances that varied considerably from one Southeast Asian location to another. This chapter's contribution is to establish the commercial and technological foundation; Chapter 20 examines, with appropriate care for the evidence and its limits, how political complexity actually developed at particular places within that broader commercial context.

Historical Vocabulary
Entrepôt, Transshipment, and Relay Trade

Definition: An entrepôt is a commercial port whose economic importance derives chiefly from its function as a hub of exchange, storage, and re-export, rather than from control over an extensive agricultural hinterland. Transshipment refers to the process of moving goods from one mode or route of transport to another, for example from a ship on the Bay of Bengal side of the Kra Isthmus to porters or river transport crossing to the Gulf of Thailand side. Relay trade describes a system of exchange in which goods travel very long total distances through a sequence of shorter transactions conducted by different participants, rather than through any single continuous voyage from origin to final destination.

Historical context: These terms, though originally developed by economic historians studying later, better-documented periods, provide useful analytical tools for describing the functions performed by settlements such as Khao Sam Kaeo and its related sites on the Thai-Malay Peninsula during the centuries examined in this chapter.

Why it matters: Without these concepts, students might mistakenly assume that long-distance objects, such as the Mediterranean-connected beads found in Bali, imply direct voyages between distant endpoints. Understanding relay trade allows for a historically accurate picture of extensive indirect connection without anachronistic assumptions about ancient shipping capability or awareness.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that the specific legal, administrative, or institutional arrangements associated with entrepôts and ports-of-trade in later, textually documented periods, examined in Volumes IV and V, necessarily existed in identical form during this earlier, largely pre-literate period. The terms describe economic function, not a fixed, unchanging institutional package.

Historical Vocabulary
Intermediary and Port-of-Trade

Definition: An intermediary is any individual or community whose economic role consists of connecting separate parties in an exchange, for example linking inland producers of tin or forest products with visiting foreign traders, or linking one regional network to another. A port-of-trade, in the sense economic historians sometimes use the term, refers to a settlement whose primary function is facilitating exchange among different trading communities, often operating under some degree of local political oversight or protection.

Historical context: The archaeological evidence examined throughout this chapter, including specialized craft production requiring organized supply of raw materials and coordinated access to multiple distant networks, implies the presence of skilled intermediaries at Southeast Asian ports, even though the specific individuals involved are rarely, if ever, named in surviving evidence from this period.

Why it matters: Recognizing the necessary role of Southeast Asian intermediaries prevents the historically inaccurate impression that early trans-Asian trade was organized and controlled exclusively by South Asian or Chinese merchants, with Southeast Asian communities playing only a passive, receiving role.

Object Study

A Han-Related Bronze Mirror from Pangkung Paruk, Bali

Object type: Bronze mirror, stylistically and compositionally consistent with Han Chinese production traditions, associated with a sarcophagus burial reported by the landowner at the Pangkung Paruk mortuary complex, Bali · Date: the wider burial complex is radiocarbon-dated principally to the second and third centuries CE (calibrated dates ranging roughly from the early second to the fourth century CE) · Type of evidence: Archaeological artifact studied through excavation, stylistic comparison, and archaeometric analysis, not a written document

Context: Pangkung Paruk is a multi-sarcophagus mortuary complex; several sarcophagi were formally excavated and found to contain glass, gold, carnelian, copper-alloy, and shell objects, while two mirrors, including the one discussed here, were associated with a sarcophagus reported to archaeologists by the landowner rather than recovered under fully controlled excavation conditions. Roman-related gold-glass beads occur within the site's broader mortuary assemblages. The site as a whole demonstrates that the community responsible for these burials had access to, and chose to include, objects connected to both East Asian and Mediterranean-connected production traditions, but the surviving documentation does not allow historians to state with confidence that this specific mirror and specific Roman-related beads were deposited together in one individually recorded grave.

Description (based on archaeological and archaeometric reporting, not a written text):

Archaeological reports describe the mirror as stylistically consistent with Han Chinese mirror types. Compositional analysis found its metal consistent with expectations for Han-period bronze. However, lead-isotope analysis of the mirror produced a signature that does not match the isotopic patterns expected for known, securely provenanced Han mirrors, leading the researchers who studied it to describe its precise workshop of manufacture as unresolved. The mirror therefore illustrates both the value and the limits of archaeometric analysis: it can test and sometimes challenge a stylistic attribution, but it cannot always resolve the question it raises.

Analysis: This object is valuable to students of this chapter because it demonstrates, in a single concrete case, the reality of very long-distance indirect connection discussed throughout Sections 18.12 and 18.13, while also illustrating why archaeologists must treat even a strong stylistic attribution as provisional until compositional and isotopic evidence has been checked against it. Because the object carries no inscription and its precise findspot and associations were not fully documented under controlled excavation, historians must rely on the relay-trade model to explain its presence in Bali, rather than asserting a specific, traceable journey or a securely reconstructed single burial assemblage.

Limitations: The evidence cannot establish who transported the mirror, how many intermediary exchanges were involved, how long the journey took, or precisely which other objects, if any, were deposited alongside it in a single grave. It cannot resolve the mirror's exact workshop of manufacture, given the unexpected lead-isotope result. It also cannot establish the identity, status, or beliefs of the person or people buried at the site, beyond what can be inferred from the presence of unusually exotic grave goods across the mortuary complex as a whole.

Questions for students:

  1. What can this object establish about Bali's connections to distant exchange systems, and what can it not establish?
  2. Why is the relay-trade model, rather than a direct-voyage model, the more responsible way to interpret this evidence?
  3. Why does an unexpected lead-isotope result matter for how confidently historians can attribute an object to a specific production tradition, even when its style looks familiar?
  4. Why does the difference between a formally excavated sarcophagus and one reported by a landowner matter for how much weight historians can place on claims about grave-good associations?
Historical Debate

Direct Trade or Relay Exchange?

The question: When archaeologists recover objects connected to very distant regions, such as Mediterranean-related glass in island Southeast Asia, how much of the intervening journey should historians attribute to direct long-distance voyages by identifiable groups of merchants, and how much should they attribute to a longer sequence of shorter, indirect exchanges among many different intermediary communities?

Interpretation A — emphasis on documented direct maritime capability: Some historians emphasize that ancient Indian Ocean and South China Sea mariners possessed considerable navigational skill and that certain well-documented later periods do show evidence for substantial direct voyages across large stretches of open water, suggesting that at least some long-distance connections examined in this chapter could have involved more direct contact between distant regions than a strict relay model assumes.
Interpretation B — emphasis on relay and intermediary structures: Other historians emphasize that the specific evidence available for this earlier period, including the comparative scarcity of confirmed Mediterranean-manufactured objects, the concentration of evidence at intermediary ports such as Khao Sam Kaeo and Hepu, and the absence of textual documentation for direct voyages spanning the entire Mediterranean-to-Southeast-Asia distance, supports a relay-based model as the more cautious and better-supported interpretation for most of the exchange examined in this chapter.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of archaeological finds, compositional and technological analysis, and comparison with later, better-documented periods, but differ in how much weight to give to demonstrated maritime capability elsewhere in the ancient world relative to the specific, more limited evidence available for direct long-distance voyages connecting the Mediterranean world to Southeast Asia during this particular period.

Why it matters: The choice between these interpretations affects how historians describe the character of ancient trans-Asian connection: as a small number of direct, if rare, long-distance voyages, or as a considerably larger number of shorter, indirect exchanges producing an equivalent cumulative effect. This textbook favors the relay-based interpretation as the better-supported default for the evidence discussed in Section 18.12, while acknowledging that documented direct voyages became more significant in some contexts during later centuries examined in subsequent chapters.

Student task: Using the Pangkung Paruk evidence and the general pattern of archaeological finds discussed in this chapter, construct an argument for which interpretation better fits the available evidence, and identify what kind of additional evidence, if discovered, would strengthen the case for direct long-distance voyages.

Multiple Perspectives

Life at a Cosmopolitan Port

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified from archaeological and comparative evidence. They are not quotations from any specific named historical individual.

From the perspective of a local Southeast Asian port intermediary: Success depends on knowing which inland communities can supply tin, forest products, or other valued goods, understanding which visiting traders want which materials, and judging when the winds favor a safe crossing of the bay, knowledge that gives an intermediary real economic power even without owning any of the exotic materials passing through the port.
From the perspective of a mobile craft specialist working in carnelian and agate: Whether trained abroad or trained locally in techniques that arrived from elsewhere, a specialist's livelihood depends on producing ornaments that satisfy the tastes of the people who will actually wear or display them, meaning technical skill must be paired with sensitivity to local preference rather than simple reproduction of a foreign model.
From the perspective of a local elite consumer at a Southeast Asian port settlement: Possessing ornaments made from rare imported stone, or objects associated with distant and prestigious places, communicates status and connection to a wider world, but the specific forms, sizes, and combinations that carry the most social meaning are governed by local custom rather than by the preferences of the community from which the raw material originally came.
From the perspective of a modern archaeologist analyzing the resulting evidence: Distinguishing an imported object from a locally made object using imported material or technique requires painstaking compositional and technological analysis, and even after such analysis, some questions, particularly about the specific identity and mobility of individual craftspeople, may remain permanently unanswerable from the physical evidence alone.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Regional Networks into Trans-Asian Networks

What Changed

  • The geographic scale of exchange expanded from the predominantly intra-Southeast Asian networks examined in Chapter 17 to systems reaching South Asia, China, and, indirectly, the Mediterranean-connected world
  • New materials, including carnelian, agate, and glass, and new production technologies became incorporated into Southeast Asian craft traditions
  • Certain port settlements, particularly on the upper Thai-Malay Peninsula, developed increasingly specialized, cosmopolitan production functions
  • Southern China, through ports such as Hepu, became increasingly linked to the same broad maritime system reaching Southeast Asia and South Asia

What Stayed the Same

  • Southeast Asian communities continued to exercise selection and control over what was produced, how it was styled, and for what purposes it was used
  • Local intermediaries remained essential to connecting foreign materials and techniques with inland producers and regional consumers
  • The internal Southeast Asian regional networks examined in Chapter 17 continued to operate alongside, rather than being replaced by, the newer trans-Asian connections
  • Monsoon-governed sailing seasons continued to structure the timing of maritime exchange, as they had for the regional networks of the earlier period

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents the error of describing this period as a sudden rupture in which Southeast Asia's own regional history was superseded by outside contact, when the evidence instead shows outside contact being absorbed into, and extending, patterns of exchange and local agency already well established by the time this chapter's story begins.

Cause and Effect

Why Long-Distance Exchange Intensified

Long-term causes

The prior development of independent regional maritime networks within Southeast Asia, examined in Chapter 17, and comparable independent network development along the South Asian coast, created the underlying infrastructure of skill, settlement, and route knowledge that later trans-Asian connections could build upon.

Short-term causes

Growing South Asian coastal demand for particular Southeast Asian commodities, Han Chinese dynastic expansion and consolidation extending administrative and commercial reach toward the southern coast, and the geographic advantage of trans-peninsular routes across the Kra Isthmus together created specific, more immediate pressures and opportunities for intensified exchange during the last centuries BCE and first centuries CE.

Effect

Cosmopolitan port settlements such as Khao Sam Kaeo developed specialized craft production combining South Asian, Southeast Asian, and Chinese-related materials and technologies, while southern Chinese ports such as Hepu and, indirectly, communities as distant as Bali became linked within an increasingly extensive, if unevenly connected, system of exchange.

This chapter's response

Consistent attention throughout to Southeast Asian selection, production, and intermediary agency, careful distinction between imported objects and locally made objects using imported inputs, and explicit separation of this commercial and technological history from the cultural and religious transformations reserved for Chapter 19.

Historical complexity: The intensification of trans-Asian exchange cannot be reduced to a single cause, such as Han dynastic expansion alone or South Asian commercial initiative alone; it resulted from the near-simultaneous, and not entirely coordinated, growth of several separate regional systems that happened to intersect most productively at particular geographic points, above all the Thai-Malay Peninsula.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Cosmopolitan Port Community

Behind the archaeological evidence for beads, metallurgy, and ceramics examined throughout this chapter lay an actual working community of people whose daily lives this evidence only partially illuminates. At a settlement such as Khao Sam Kaeo, daily life would have included farmers and fishers supplying food to a population engaged substantially in craft production and exchange rather than direct subsistence agriculture; artisans working carnelian, agate, glass, and metal, whose specialized skills required years to develop and whose workshops would have structured much of the settlement's physical space; boat operators and porters managing the trans-peninsular and maritime routes connecting the settlement to both the Bay of Bengal and Gulf of Thailand sides of the peninsula; local elites who commissioned and displayed the finished ornaments and metal goods produced at the settlement, using them to express status and social position within their own communities; and, quite plausibly, small numbers of visiting or resident foreign specialists and traders, whose presence, while real, should not be exaggerated into a large foreign population dominating the settlement's social life.

Because most surviving evidence for this period consists of durable material remains rather than written records, historians must reconstruct this social world indirectly, through spatial analysis of settlement layout, workshop organization, burial evidence where available, and careful comparison with better-documented later periods of Southeast Asian port history. This indirect reconstruction inevitably leaves many aspects of daily life, including specific social relationships, family structures, and individual experiences, beyond secure historical recovery, a limitation students should keep in mind when engaging with confident-sounding descriptions of "life" at sites such as Khao Sam Kaeo.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced how the regional maritime networks Southeast Asian communities had already built by the period examined in Chapter 17 became linked to expanding systems of exchange reaching South Asia, China, the wider Indian Ocean, and, at the outer edge of the system, the Mediterranean-connected world. The chapter's central argument rejects a simple picture of India and China discovering and trading with a passively receiving Southeast Asia, replacing it with a more historically accurate account in which Southeast Asian ports, craftspeople, and intermediaries actively selected commodities, organized routes and settlements, and, in many documented cases, manufactured locally the very objects that a careless reading of the archaeological evidence might mistake for straightforward foreign imports.

Geography played an essential role in shaping where this trans-Asian connection concentrated. The Kra Isthmus, the narrowest portion of the Thai-Malay Peninsula, allowed goods and travelers to move between the Bay of Bengal and Andaman Sea on one side and the Gulf of Thailand and South China Sea on the other, explaining why relatively modest port settlements on the upper peninsula, rather than only the region's largest and best-documented later states, became unusually cosmopolitan centers of exchange during the centuries examined in this chapter.

The archaeological site of Khao Sam Kaeo anchored much of the chapter's evidence and argument. Its remains, spanning occupation from approximately the fourth century BCE into the first century CE and most intensively documented for its cosmopolitan fourth-through-second-century-BCE phase, combine South Asian-style ceramics and craft techniques, Southeast Asian ornament traditions, and metallurgical evidence related to Western Han Chinese practices within a single settlement complex, alongside clear evidence for local manufacture using imported materials and, quite plausibly, imported or transferred technique. This evidence supports a picture of genuine interaction among people, materials, technologies, and local demand, rather than the simple arrival of finished foreign goods.

The chapter examined carnelian, agate, and glass ornament production as a particularly clear evidentiary case, distinguishing among imported finished objects, imported raw materials worked locally, and locally invented forms produced using transferred technique. It also addressed directly the archaeological problem posed by Southeast Asia's likely exports, aromatic woods, resins, forest products, spices, and other largely organic and perishable commodities, which survive far less well in the archaeological record than the glass, stone, and metal objects imported from South Asia and China, creating a serious risk of overstating the one-directional character of early trans-Asian exchange.

Turning northward, the chapter examined evidence for growing linkage between southern China and the maritime systems already developing across Southeast Asia and the Bay of Bengal, anchored by the Han-period tombs of the Hepu region on the Guangxi coast and by metallurgical evidence at Khao Sam Kaeo pointing toward connections with Western Han Chinese traditions. This evidence allowed the chapter to describe Southeast Asia as a genuine middle zone linking expanding commercial systems developing on both its western and northern sides.

The chapter also addressed, with particular caution, the evidence for Mediterranean-connected objects reaching Southeast Asia, exemplified by the Roman-related gold-glass beads and Han-related bronze mirrors documented within the multi-sarcophagus mortuary complex of Pangkung Paruk in Bali, while noting that the precise association of specific objects within a single documented grave, and the exact workshop origin of at least one mirror, remain matters of ongoing archaeological uncertainty. Rather than concluding that Mediterranean merchants sailed directly to Southeast Asian shores, the chapter introduced the concept of relay trade, in which objects travel very long cumulative distances through a sequence of shorter, indirect exchanges conducted by different intermediary communities, allowing for genuine long-distance connection without anachronistic assumptions about ancient shipping capability or awareness.

Throughout, the chapter emphasized the necessary but largely undocumented role of Southeast Asian merchants and intermediaries, whose local knowledge of waterways, monsoon timing, regional languages, market preferences, and inland supply routes made the entire system of exchange function, even though their individual names and personal histories rarely survive in the record. It also addressed the likely presence of mobile foreign craft specialists at Southeast Asian ports, while carefully distinguishing such presence, whether temporary, seasonal, or longer-term, from any form of colonization or foreign political domination.

Finally, the chapter drew a deliberate boundary around its own subject, distinguishing the commercial and technological exchange examined here from the cultural and religious transformations, including the spread of Sanskrit, Buddhist and Hindu religious institutions, and Indian-derived political and artistic models, that form the specific subject of Chapter 19's historiographical debate over "Indianization" and localization. Intensifying commerce created conditions within which later cultural transformation became possible, but commerce alone did not automatically produce that transformation, and the two processes require separate historical explanation.

The chapter closed by gesturing briefly toward the earliest well-documented Southeast Asian states, particularly those associated with the Mekong Delta, while explicitly reserving the detailed archaeological and textual examination of Funan and Óc Eo for Chapter 20. The chapter's governing conclusion, restated here, is that Southeast Asia was not merely located between India and China; Southeast Asian communities actively connected, redirected, transformed, and profited from the exchange systems developing around them, a conclusion that positions Southeast Asian societies as historical actors rather than passive intermediaries in their own history.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Entrepôt
A commercial port whose economic importance derives chiefly from its role as a hub of exchange, storage, and re-export rather than from an extensive agricultural hinterland.
Transshipment
The process of moving goods from one mode or route of transport to another, such as from sea transport to overland or river transport across the Kra Isthmus.
Relay trade
A system of exchange in which goods travel long cumulative distances through a sequence of shorter transactions among different intermediary participants, rather than through a single continuous voyage.
Port-of-trade
A settlement whose primary function is facilitating exchange among different trading communities, often under some degree of local political oversight.
Intermediary
An individual or community whose economic role consists of connecting separate parties in an exchange, such as inland producers and visiting foreign traders.
Bay of Bengal exchange
The developing system of maritime exchange connecting the eastern coast of South Asia with the Thai-Malay Peninsula and other parts of Southeast Asia, archaeologically visible from the fourth through second centuries BCE and increasingly integrated into a larger regional sailing circuit by the second and first centuries BCE.
Kra Isthmus
The narrowest portion of the Thai-Malay Peninsula, historically important as a corridor linking the Bay of Bengal and Andaman Sea with the Gulf of Thailand and South China Sea.
Trans-peninsular route
A route combining short sea voyages with overland or river transport across the Kra Isthmus, allowing goods to move between the Bay of Bengal and South China Sea sides of the peninsula without sailing around its southern tip.
Carnelian
A semi-precious reddish stone, sourced predominantly from South Asia, worked into beads and ornaments using techniques including heat treatment and specialized drilling.
Agate
A banded semi-precious stone, worked alongside carnelian using related techniques into beads and ornaments found at Southeast Asian archaeological sites.
Raw glass
Unworked glass material, as distinct from finished glass objects, whose chemical composition can sometimes indicate a broad region of origin through provenance analysis.
Craft mobility
The movement of skilled artisans, whether temporarily or permanently, between regions, inferred archaeologically from the combination of transferred technique and evidence for local production.
Technological transfer
The spread of specialized production knowledge, such as stone-working or metallurgical techniques, from one region or community to another, through mechanisms that are not always directly recoverable from the archaeological record.
High-tin bronze
A category of bronze metalwork with a distinctively high tin content, associated with particular South and Southeast Asian production traditions and used as archaeological evidence for cross-regional connection.
Maritime Silk Road
A retrospective term sometimes used by historians to describe maritime exchange systems linking China, Southeast Asia, South Asia, and more distant regions; a useful but modern label that should not be assumed to describe a single organized route in antiquity.
Hepu tombs
Han-period burial sites in the Hepu region of present-day Guangxi, southern China, whose imported luxury materials provide evidence for a maritime trading system linking southern China with South and Southeast Asian networks.
Mediterranean-connected exchange
Trade and material connections linking Southeast Asia, indirectly and through intermediary regions, to the Mediterranean world, evidenced by scattered finds of Mediterranean-related glass and related objects.
Archaeological invisibility
The tendency of organic, perishable commodities, such as aromatic resins, textiles, and foodstuffs, to leave little or no direct trace in the archaeological record, in contrast to durable materials such as glass, stone, and metal.
Prestige goods
Rare or exotic objects valued chiefly for the social status their possession communicates, rather than for their practical utility alone.
Diaspora artisan
A craftsperson who has relocated, temporarily or permanently, away from their region of training or origin, a category historians consider as one possible explanation for evidence of transferred craft technique.
Provenance analysis
Scientific and technological study, including chemical and isotopic analysis, used to determine or narrow down the geographic origin of a material such as glass or stone.
Localization
The process by which imported materials, technologies, or ideas are adapted and transformed to serve local needs and preferences, examined here in a commercial and technological context and examined further, in a religious and political context, in Chapter 19.
Cultural reception
The process by which a society encounters, evaluates, and selectively incorporates elements originating from another society, a concept relevant to both this chapter's commercial focus and Chapter 19's cultural focus.
Khao Sam Kaeo
A port settlement on the upper Thai-Malay Peninsula occupied from approximately the fourth century BCE into the first century CE, whose archaeological remains, most intensively documented for the fourth through second centuries BCE, provide this chapter's central evidence for cosmopolitan, locally organized trans-Asian exchange.
Trans-Asian exchange
This chapter's term for the expanding, multi-directional system of commercial and technological connection linking Southeast Asia, South Asia, China, and, indirectly, the Mediterranean-connected world.
Composite technological tradition
A craft production tradition combining elements derived from more than one regional source, as documented in the metallurgical and ornament evidence from Khao Sam Kaeo.
Mekong Delta zone
The lower Mekong River region, introduced in this chapter as one of several areas increasingly drawn into expanding trans-Asian exchange, and examined in full detail in Chapter 20 in connection with Funan and Óc Eo.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it establishes, on firm archaeological ground, one of this textbook's most important recurring arguments: that Southeast Asian societies were active participants in, rather than passive recipients of, the wider historical processes connecting them to South Asia, China, and the more distant Indian Ocean and Mediterranean-connected worlds. The evidence from Khao Sam Kaeo, in particular, shows this argument operating at the level of concrete material practice, in the choices artisans and consumers made about what to produce, how to style it, and for whom, centuries before any of the region's classical states left the kinds of inscriptions and monuments examined in later chapters.

The larger processes this chapter reveals extend well beyond the specific commodities and sites discussed here. The pattern of Southeast Asian communities selecting, adapting, and locally reproducing foreign materials and technologies, rather than simply receiving finished foreign products, previews a pattern this textbook will encounter repeatedly in later centuries, including in the reception of Buddhism, Hinduism, and Islam, examined in Chapters 19 and 62 through 66, and in the adaptation of European commercial and administrative institutions during the early modern and colonial periods examined in later volumes. Understanding this chapter's commercial and technological evidence carefully therefore equips students with an interpretive habit of mind applicable throughout the rest of this textbook.

This chapter also connects different regions and periods in a way that should reshape how students think about ancient connectivity more generally. The relay-trade model introduced in Section 18.13 allows for genuine, historically significant long-distance connection, exemplified by the Pangkung Paruk find linking Bali to both Han China and the Mediterranean-connected world, without requiring anachronistic assumptions about direct, continuous ancient voyages spanning thousands of kilometers. This nuanced picture of indirect but real connectivity offers a valuable corrective to popular narratives that either exaggerate ancient globalization or dismiss ancient long-distance contact as negligible.

Finally, this chapter's deliberate restraint, its refusal to explain the spread of Sanskrit, religion, and Indian-derived political models in detail, and its refusal to explain the archaeology of Funan and Óc Eo, models an important historical discipline: recognizing the proper boundaries of one's own chapter and trusting the larger structure of the textbook to address related questions in their appropriate place. Chapter 19 will now take up directly the cultural and religious questions this chapter has deliberately set aside, building on the commercial and technological foundation established here, while Chapter 20 will examine how some of the developments touched on in this chapter's final section contributed to the archaeology and historiography of the region's earliest documented states.
