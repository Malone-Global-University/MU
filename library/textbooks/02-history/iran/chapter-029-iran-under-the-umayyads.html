Iran under the Umayyads: Rule, Resistance, and New Social Orders

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

Conquest ended Sasanian sovereignty in a matter of decades. Governing the conquered land took much longer, and it never produced a single, uniform system. Across Iran in the later seventh and early eighth centuries, Arab governors ruled through Iranian tax collectors, Muslim armies settled unevenly across radically different regions, and a growing population of converts blurred a boundary between conqueror and conquered that the conquest itself had drawn with deceptive clarity.

Imagine a plausible district in the Zagros foothills in the 680s: at the top of its tax roll stood, in all likelihood, the name of a local dehqān, an Iranian landholder whose family had collected revenue there under the Sasanian kings and who now collected it, on much the same terms, for a Muslim governor seated many days' journey away. The governor answered to Damascus; the dehqān answered to the governor; the peasants who actually worked the land answered, as they long had, to the dehqān. Had a traveler asked any of these people whether the Sasanian Empire had fallen, the honest answer would have been yes. Had the same traveler asked whether daily life in the district had been transformed, the honest answer, for most people in most places, would have been: not yet, and not entirely, and not evenly. This particular register and town are illustrative rather than documented, but the underlying pattern of continued dehqān-administered tax collection is well supported by the evidence examined throughout this chapter.

This is the central paradox that Chapter 29 explores. The Umayyad dynasty, which took definitive shape under Muʿawiya ibn Abi Sufyan after 661, inherited from the conquest generation an enormous, overwhelmingly non-Arab and overwhelmingly non-Muslim population spread across a plateau whose regions differed sharply from one another in geography, economy, and political tradition. Damascus could claim sovereignty over this population. It could not, in the space of a single generation, replace the institutions through which that population had long been governed, taxed, and organized. Umayyad rule in Iran therefore rested, to a degree that popular narratives of sudden Arab-Islamic transformation often understate, on the continued operation of inherited Iranian administrative practice, adapted rather than abolished, negotiated rather than simply imposed.

At the same time, this was not merely Sasanian government under new management. Arab soldiers and their families settled permanently in parts of Iran, in numbers and patterns that varied enormously by region. A caliphal state gradually reorganized taxation, reformed the coinage, and extended a more distinctly Islamic administrative vocabulary across its eastern provinces. A growing number of Iranians entered the Muslim community as converts, becoming mawālī, clients of Arab tribes, and in doing so raised difficult questions about status, obligation, and revenue that Umayyad governors never fully resolved. Resistance to Umayyad rule came from many directions and for many different reasons, seldom reducible to a single story of Persian nationhood rising against Arab occupation.

Nowhere did these tensions gather more consequentially than in Khurasan, the vast northeastern region introduced in Chapter 1 and already significant in Chapter 28's account of the conquest. Khurasan received Arab settlers on a scale unmatched elsewhere in Iran, including, according to later tradition, tens of thousands of soldiers and family members transplanted from the garrison cities of Iraq. It also remained a zone of continuing warfare against Transoxiana and Tokharistan, a region where Iranian dehqāns and regional rulers retained real authority, and a place where Arab tribal rivalries imported from Iraq combined with local grievances over taxation and status to produce an unusually combustible political environment. The conditions this chapter describes in Khurasan will not resolve themselves within Umayyad rule. They will instead become, as Chapter 30 explains, the foundation of the revolutionary movement that destroyed the Umayyad dynasty altogether.

This chapter does not attempt to narrate that revolution, nor does it attempt the fuller account of Islamization that Chapter 32 provides, nor the cultural and linguistic transformations examined in Chapter 33. Its task is narrower and, in some ways, more foundational: to explain how a conquered, overwhelmingly non-Muslim Iranian population came under sustained Umayyad government, and how taxation, Arab settlement, surviving Iranian elites, religious conversion, and repeated episodes of resistance combined to produce social arrangements that were no longer simply Sasanian and not yet the mature Persianate Islamic civilization of the Abbasid age.

Students should read this chapter, then, not as a chronicle of caliphs and governors for its own sake, but as an inquiry into how conquest becomes government, how empires absorb populations they did not create, and how the unresolved tensions of one generation can become the revolutionary material of the next.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Muʿawiya's Umayyad regime reconsolidated Muslim authority over Iran after the disruptions of the First Fitna.
  2. Describe the continuing role of Iranian dehqāns and regional rulers in local administration and tax collection under Umayyad governors.
  3. Analyze the gradual and regionally uneven development of tribute, land tax, and poll tax arrangements in conquered Iran.
  4. Compare patterns of Arab settlement across Iraq, western and central Iran, Fars, Sistan, and Khurasan.
  5. Define the historical meaning of mawlā and mawālī and explain the social and fiscal consequences of conversion.
  6. Assess the administrative and monetary reforms associated with Abd al-Malik and al-Hajjaj, including the reformed Islamic dirham.
  7. Distinguish among the varied causes of resistance and revolt under Umayyad rule, including taxation, tribal conflict, religious-political movements, and local autonomy.
  8. Explain why Khurasan developed an unusually complex and volatile Arab-Iranian frontier society under the Umayyads.
  9. Identify the overlapping social groups present in Iran on the eve of the Abbasid Revolution.
  10. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of whether Umayyad social divisions in Iran were primarily ethnic, religious, fiscal, or political.
  11. Avoid simplified or anachronistic narratives that treat Umayyad Iran as uniformly governed, wholly Arabized, or animated chiefly by Persian ethnic nationalism.
  12. Connect the administrative, fiscal, and social conditions described in this chapter to the revolutionary movement examined in Chapter 30.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did the Umayyad state convert military conquest into sustained government over a largely non-Muslim, non-Arab population?
  2. Why did Umayyad governors rely so heavily on surviving Iranian local elites rather than replacing them with an entirely new administrative class?
  3. How did taxation arrangements inherited from the conquest gradually change, and why did that change proceed unevenly across different regions?
  4. Why did Arab settlement take such different forms in Iraq, western Iran, Fars, Sistan, and Khurasan?
  5. What tensions did religious conversion create between Iranian converts, the Arab tribes to whom they became attached, and a fiscal system built around the taxation of non-Muslims?
  6. How did Abd al-Malik's and al-Hajjaj's reforms change the character of the Islamic state without immediately erasing earlier Iranian administrative practice?
  7. When Iranians or Arab settlers resisted Umayyad authority, what specifically were they resisting, and how did their grievances differ by region and social position?
  8. Why did Khurasan, more than any other Iranian region, become a place where the boundary between Arab conqueror and Iranian subject grew especially complicated?
  9. What social and fiscal conditions had accumulated in Iran, and especially in Khurasan, by the early eighth century, and why would those conditions prove revolutionary?

Historical Context

Chapter 28 traced the Arab-Muslim conquest of Iran from the early campaigns of the 630s through the death of the last Sasanian king, Yazdegerd III, and the subsequent disruptions of the First Fitna, the civil war among Muslims that followed the murder of the caliph Uthman in 656. That chapter established two points essential to understanding the present one. First, Sasanian imperial sovereignty ended relatively quickly at the level of the royal court and central administration, even as organized and sometimes prolonged resistance continued in particular regions, Fars, the Caspian littoral, and parts of Khurasan among them, for years afterward. Second, and just as importantly, the conquest did not sweep away Iranian society itself. Local landholders, administrative practices, tax structures, and religious communities survived the fall of the dynasty that had once governed them, because the conquering armies were far too few in number, and far too occupied with continuing warfare and internal Muslim politics, to administer the plateau directly.

The First Fitna (656 to 661) interrupted whatever consolidation the early conquest governors had achieved. Tribute payments lapsed in some districts; local rulers who had submitted to Muslim authority found room to reassert autonomy; and on the eastern frontier, particularly in Khurasan and the regions beyond it, Muslim control weakened considerably. When Muʿawiya ibn Abi Sufyan emerged from that civil war as caliph and founded what historians call the Umayyad dynasty, ruling from Damascus, he inherited not a settled province but a reconquest problem: much of Iran needed to be resubdued, or at least have its obligations to the Muslim state reaffirmed, before anything resembling stable government could begin.

This chapter accordingly opens not with the initial conquest, already examined in Chapter 28, but with the harder and less dramatic work of turning military supremacy into durable rule. That work unfolded across roughly eight decades, from Muʿawiya's accession in 661 through the final years of Umayyad authority in the east in the 740s, examined in the opening of Chapter 30. Across those decades, the Umayyad state faced a structural problem common to many conquest regimes: a small governing and military elite needed to extract revenue from, maintain order among, and eventually begin to religiously and culturally incorporate a vastly larger conquered population, without either possessing the administrative capacity to govern that population directly or wishing to destroy the productive capacity on which its own revenue depended.

The solution the Umayyads arrived at, unevenly, provisionally, and never as a single deliberate policy, was to govern largely through existing Iranian structures while gradually building a parallel Arab-Islamic administrative and military framework alongside them. Understanding how that arrangement worked, where it succeeded, where it broke down, and what new social categories it produced, is the task of this chapter.

Section 29.1

From Conquest to Government: Muʿawiya and the Reassertion of Umayyad Authority

When Muʿawiya ibn Abi Sufyan secured recognition as caliph in 661, bringing the First Fitna to a close, he inherited an Iran whose relationship to Muslim authority had grown uneven and, in places, openly uncertain. Chapter 28 has already shown that the civil war among Muslims diverted resources and attention away from the eastern provinces just as initial conquest-era arrangements needed reinforcement. Regions that had agreed to pay tribute in the 640s and early 650s had, in some cases, stopped paying it. Local rulers who had submitted to Muslim garrison commanders found room, amid the distraction of the Fitna, to reassert a fuller measure of independence. On the eastern frontier, in Khurasan and the lands beyond it, Muslim control had never been more than partial, and it weakened further during these years.

Muʿawiya's government therefore faced, from its first years, a task better described as reconquest and reconsolidation than as ordinary administration of an already pacified province, though the task fell unevenly across different Iranian regions. Fars and Kerman had already experienced serious tax revolt during the First Fitna and had been forcibly pacified by Ziyad ibn Abihi under the caliph Ali in 659, so these two provinces entered the Umayyad period already substantially, if tensely, subdued. The heavier burden of renewed reconquest after 661 therefore fell on Sistan and Khurasan. Muslim forces retook Zaranj and other positions in Sistan, restored tribute obligations across parts of Khurasan, and renewed pressure on regional rulers farther east. Resistance did not disappear; western Tokharistan, at the eastern edge of the Khurasani sphere, continued to resist effective Muslim control even as Khurasan itself was re-subdued and reorganized under Muʿawiya's governors.

It is worth pausing on the administrative shape this reconquest took, because it established patterns that would persist for decades. Muʿawiya did not attempt to govern Iran directly from Damascus through an extensive imperial bureaucracy dispatched from the Umayyad capital. Instead, authority flowed through a comparatively small number of governors, frequently appointed to Iraq first and Iran and the eastern provinces as an extension of that Iraqi command. Basra and Kufa, the great Arab garrison cities of lower Iraq, functioned as the practical administrative and military gateway to Iran, though their respective spheres were not identical. In the earlier division of conquest-era administration, Kufa was especially connected with Jibal and Azerbaijan to the northwest, while Basra served as the principal base for Khuzistan, Fars, Kerman, Sistan, and Khurasan. From Muʿawiya's reign onward there was an increasing tendency to consolidate responsibility for the Iranian and eastern provinces under a single powerful governor of Iraq, who in turn appointed subordinate provincial governors to coordinate military campaigns, tribute collection, and local administration across this vast eastern zone.

This arrangement reflected both a practical necessity and a deeper structural reality that recurs throughout the chapter. The Umayyad state, for all its military success, remained a comparatively small governing apparatus superimposed atop a much larger and more populous conquered society. Effective rule required delegation, first to regional Arab governors and military commanders, and beneath them, as the next section explains, to the Iranian administrative structures that had survived the conquest largely intact. The question this chapter now turns to, having established how reconquest restored nominal Umayyad authority across Iran by the 660s and 670s, is how that authority was actually translated into day-to-day government.

Section 29.2

Governing through the Existing Iranian Order

A small population of Arab governors, garrison soldiers, and administrators could not, by itself, collect taxes from, maintain order among, and adjudicate disputes for the many millions of people living across the Iranian plateau and its surrounding regions. Nor did the Umayyad state possess, in its first decades, a fully developed Arabic-language bureaucratic apparatus capable of replacing the administrative machinery the Sasanian state had built up over centuries. The practical consequence, visible across nearly every region examined in this chapter, was that Umayyad governors ruled substantially through the continued operation of pre-existing Iranian local administration, adapted to new political circumstances rather than replaced by them.

At the center of this continuity stood the dehqāns, a class of Iranian landholding notables whose local authority long predated the conquest and who had functioned under the Sasanian state as district and village administrators, tax assessors, and intermediaries between central government and local populations. Under Umayyad rule, dehqāns in many regions continued performing substantially the same functions, now directed toward Muslim governors rather than Sasanian officials. A dehqān's household typically retained its landholdings, its local social standing, and its practical knowledge of who owed what, to whom, and on what basis, knowledge that no incoming Arab administrator could easily replicate without years of local experience the conquest generation simply did not have time to acquire.

This pattern extended beyond individual dehqāns to entire regional political structures in some areas. In Khurasan particularly, local Iranian rulers, sometimes holding titles and authority reaching back into the late Sasanian or even earlier period, often remained in place under Umayyad suzerainty, retaining internal authority over their territories in exchange for tribute payments and political submission to Muslim governors. This was not a uniform policy so much as a recurring practical accommodation: where local rulers could guarantee tribute and order, Umayyad governors frequently found it more efficient to confirm their authority than to attempt direct replacement.

Students should draw from this section a distinction that recurs throughout the chapter and that corrects a common oversimplification of the conquest era: political sovereignty changed considerably more quickly, and more completely, than local administration did. The Sasanian dynasty had fallen; a Muslim caliph in Damascus now claimed ultimate authority over the Iranian plateau; but in a great many villages and districts, the person actually assessing and collecting taxes, adjudicating minor disputes, and representing the community to higher authority was the same kind of figure, often quite literally a member of the same family, who had performed those functions under Sasanian rule. This continuity should not be mistaken for the absence of change. It should instead be understood as the mechanism through which change, when it did come, was gradually transmitted into Iranian society rather than imposed upon it all at once from outside.

It is equally important not to overstate this continuity into an image of undisturbed Iranian self-government. Dehqāns and regional rulers operated within a political framework now defined by Muslim governors who could remove them, reassign their obligations, or bypass them when convenient. Their authority was confirmed rather than guaranteed, and, as Section 29.7 will show, the relationship between local Iranian elites and Muslim governors was frequently a source of friction as well as cooperation, particularly as fiscal demands increased or as governors sought to extend more direct control into regions long accustomed to substantial local autonomy.

Section 29.3

Tribute, Land Tax, Poll Tax, and the Problem of Who Paid What

Popular summaries of early Islamic fiscal history sometimes present a tidy formula: Muslims paid a religious alms tax, non-Muslims paid a poll tax called the jizya, and land everywhere was subject to a land tax called the kharāj. This formula describes, in broad outline, a fiscal system that eventually took shape across the Islamic world. It does not accurately describe the situation in Iran during the decades this chapter examines. The terminology itself was still developing, its application varied considerably by region and by the specific terms individual conquest-era treaties had established, and the practical mechanics of assessment and collection depended heavily on the very Iranian administrative structures discussed in Section 29.2.

In the immediate aftermath of conquest, many Iranian districts had submitted to Muslim forces under negotiated agreements specifying a fixed tribute payment, owed collectively by the community or its ruler rather than assessed individually according to a standardized formula. These early tribute arrangements reflected practical military and political bargaining as much as any developed fiscal theory: a besieged town or a threatened regional ruler agreed to pay a specified amount, in exchange for protection of life, property, and, frequently, continued local religious practice. Responsibility for actually collecting the agreed sum, and for apportioning it among individual households or landholdings, typically remained with local dehqāns and regional administrators, exactly the continuity Section 29.2 describes.

Over the decades that followed, Umayyad administration moved gradually, and unevenly across different provinces, toward more standardized taxation, distinguishing more systematically between a tax on land, owed regardless of the cultivator's religion, and a tax specifically associated with non-Muslim status. This movement toward standardization was neither instantaneous nor uniform. Local variation in terminology, assessment method, and the balance between collective and individual responsibility for payment persisted well beyond the period this chapter covers, and historians working from surviving administrative and literary evidence continue to debate exactly how, and how quickly, the more familiar classical distinction between land tax and poll tax emerged from this earlier, more improvised set of arrangements.

One problem, however, emerged clearly and repeatedly across the evidence for this period, and it deserves particular emphasis: what happened to state revenue when a taxpayer converted to the religion of the ruling power? If a portion of a district's fiscal obligation to the Muslim state rested on the number of non-Muslim taxpayers it contained, then conversion to Islam threatened to reduce that district's, and by extension the state's, revenue, even as it fulfilled what might otherwise be regarded as a desirable religious outcome from the perspective of Muslim authorities. This tension, between encouraging or at least accepting conversion on religious grounds and protecting a fiscal system partly built around the taxation of non-Muslims, did not resolve itself quickly or consistently. Different governors, in different periods and different provinces, responded differently, sometimes exempting converts from taxes associated with non-Muslim status as a matter of principle, sometimes continuing to demand those payments from converts on the grounds that the underlying tax attached to land or to prior fiscal community rather than to individual religious status, and sometimes vacillating between these positions under pressure from competing constituencies. Section 29.5 returns to this problem directly in connection with the growing population of Iranian converts, and Section 29.8 shows how acutely it was felt in Khurasan specifically.

Section 29.4

Arab Settlement in Iran: Not One Pattern

Geography Study

Patterns of Arab Settlement across Iraq and Iran

Using a map of Iraq, the Iranian plateau, and Khurasan, students should locate and compare the following zones of Arab military and civilian settlement:

  • Iraq: the massive, concentrated garrison cities of Kufa and Basra, the administrative and demographic base for eastward expansion
  • Western and central Iran: comparatively dispersed and limited Arab settlement, concentrated mainly around garrison points and administrative centers
  • Fars, Kerman, and Sistan: continued limited Arab presence alongside persistence of older Sasanian-style coinage and administrative forms well after reform elsewhere
  • Khurasan and Merv: large-scale, state-supported settlement of Arab soldiers and, increasingly, entire families, beginning from at least the 660s and traditionally associated with a major transfer of population from Kufa and Basra around 671
  • Tokharistan and the approaches to Transoxiana: a continuing military frontier zone rather than an area of dense permanent settlement during most of this period

Geography Analysis Questions:

  1. Why might the Umayyad state have encouraged large-scale, family-based Arab settlement in Khurasan specifically, rather than distributing settlers evenly across all conquered Iranian territory?
  2. How might the comparatively limited Arab presence in Fars, Kerman, and Sistan help explain the persistence of older coinage types in those regions, discussed further in this chapter's Object Study?
  3. What administrative and military advantages did concentrating Arab settlement in garrison cities such as Kufa, Basra, and Merv offer compared to dispersing settlers across the countryside?
  4. Why does treating "Arab settlement in Iran" as a single uniform phenomenon risk misrepresenting the social history examined in this chapter?

It is a serious oversimplification to imagine that Arab conquest meant the Iranian plateau was simply filled, region by region, with Arab colonists replacing an Iranian population. The actual pattern of Arab settlement varied dramatically by region, reflecting military strategy, administrative convenience, geography, and the differing character of continuing warfare on different frontiers.

In Iraq, immediately adjacent to Iran and functioning as the administrative gateway described in Section 29.1, Arab settlement took its most concentrated and transformative form. The garrison cities of Kufa and Basra, founded in the earliest years of the conquests, grew into major urban centers housing large Arab military populations organized along tribal lines, alongside growing numbers of non-Arab residents, including Iranians, drawn into their orbit through trade, service, clientage, and eventually conversion. These cities functioned as the staging ground and demographic reservoir for further Arab movement eastward into Iran and Khurasan.

Across much of western and central Iran, by contrast, Arab settlement remained comparatively limited and dispersed. Governors, garrison detachments, and administrative personnel established themselves in key towns and provincial centers, but large-scale transplantation of Arab civilian population into the Iranian countryside on the Iraqi model did not generally occur. In these regions, the continuity of Iranian local administration described in Section 29.2 operated with relatively little direct daily interaction between ordinary Iranian cultivators and a resident Arab population, at least in the earlier Umayyad decades.

Fars, Kerman, and Sistan present a related but distinct pattern. These southern and southeastern regions saw Arab governors, garrisons, and administrative oversight, but again without the scale of permanent civilian Arab settlement found further east. These provinces also continued striking Sasanian-style coinage for some years after the reformed Islamic dirham had replaced it elsewhere, a fact this chapter's Object Study examines; the persistence of the older coinage is best read as evidence of continued monetary and administrative practice, and in some cases of ongoing political disturbance, in these provinces, rather than as direct proof of how extensively Arabs had or had not settled there.

Khurasan stands apart from every other Iranian region in the scale and character of Arab settlement it received. Beginning from at least the 660s, and associated in later Arabic historical tradition with a substantial transfer of population from Kufa and Basra around 671, Khurasan became host to large numbers of Arab soldiers who brought, over time, their families with them, establishing a permanent and demographically significant Arab presence concentrated particularly around Merv but extending across the wider Khurasani region. This was not, however, a simple transplantation of an isolated garrison population. Over the following decades, these Arab settlers in Khurasan increasingly took on civilian as well as military roles, becoming merchants, landholders, artisans, and scholars as well as soldiers, and became progressively embedded within the surrounding Iranian society rather than remaining segregated from it. This distinctive Khurasani pattern, examined further in Section 29.8, would prove decisive for the region's later political trajectory.

Section 29.5

Mawālī, Conversion, and an Emerging Muslim Iranian Population

This section introduces a subject that Chapter 32 will examine in full: the Islamization of Iran's population. Chapter 29's task is narrower and more specific. It considers the social and fiscal consequences of conversion during the Umayyad period, when converts remained a growing but still comparatively modest portion of Iran's overall population, and when the institutional mechanisms for incorporating them into the Muslim community were themselves still developing.

Historical Vocabulary
Mawlā / Mawālī

Definition: Mawlā (plural mawālī) was a broad Arabic term whose range of meanings included client, patron, ally, and freedman. In the early Islamic conquest society examined in this chapter, it was frequently used for non-Arab Muslims who entered a relationship of walāʾ with an individual Arab patron and thereby acquired affiliation with that patron's tribal group. Many Iranian converts entered Muslim society through such relationships, but the term should not be treated as a simple synonym for "non-Arab Muslim" or for all Iranians generally; its precise meaning and social weight varied by context, period, and individual circumstance.

Historical context: Because the early Muslim community's social and military organization was substantially structured around Arab tribal affiliation, a convert without such affiliation needed some recognized place within that structure. Attachment to an Arab tribal patron provided one. A growing, though through most of the Umayyad period still comparatively limited, number of Iranian converts who became mawālī served in Muslim armies, worked in administration, engaged in trade, and, in later generations, became prominent in Islamic scholarship, while their status as clients, rather than as full tribal members, could carry real social and sometimes fiscal distinctions from that of Arab Muslims.

Why it matters: The growing mawālī population directly complicated the fiscal categories discussed in Section 29.3. It also created a body of Muslims with grievances distinct from those of non-Muslim Iranians and distinct from those of Arab settlers, grievances concerning unequal military pay, unequal social standing despite shared religious membership, and continued fiscal obligations some governors attempted to impose on converts. These grievances would matter considerably to the revolutionary coalition examined in Chapter 30.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not treat "mawālī" as a simple synonym for "all Iranian Muslims" or assume that mawālī status was uniformly degrading or uniformly resented across every period and region. Experience varied by individual circumstance, patron tribe, region, and period, and mawālī individuals achieved wealth, learning, and influence even as social and institutional distinctions could nevertheless persist.

Conversion to Islam among Iranians during the Umayyad period proceeded gradually and unevenly, driven by a range of motives that varied by individual and circumstance, including religious conviction, social and economic advantage, family and community pressure, and the practical benefits of closer association with the ruling power. Converts increasingly entered Muslim armies, administrative service, commerce, and, over time, religious and legal scholarship, becoming an integral part of the Muslim community's growth within Iran even as their numbers remained, throughout most of the Umayyad period, a minority of Iran's overall population.

Yet sharing Muslim religious status with Arab Muslims did not automatically translate into equal social, legal, or fiscal treatment in practice. Arab Muslims, particularly those of established tribal standing, often retained social and material advantages over mawālī converts, including differences in military pay and status. More consequentially for the fiscal history examined in Section 29.3, some governors, under pressure to maintain state revenue, continued to demand from converts taxes historically associated with non-Muslim status, reasoning that the obligation attached to the land or to the taxpayer's prior fiscal community rather than disappearing automatically upon conversion. This practice, documented especially in Khurasan, generated grievances among many affected converts, who had expected that accepting Islam would end such payments.

Khurasan again provides the clearest documented instances of this tension. Disputes over whether converts should continue paying taxes associated with non-Muslim status arose repeatedly in the region, and different governors responded differently: some attempted genuine fiscal relief for converts as a matter of religious principle or political conciliation, while others, facing revenue pressure or skepticism about the sincerity of some conversions, reimposed or maintained the disputed payments. This oscillation between reform and renewed fiscal pressure, rather than any single consistent Umayyad policy, characterizes the historical record for this problem across the period this chapter examines.

Students should resist a simplified narrative in which Arab rulers uniformly oppressed Persian mawālī as a matter of ethnic prejudice. The historical reality, as the evidence for this period shows, involved an intersecting set of factors, including tribal affiliation, class and wealth, military rank, regional administrative practice, and individual governors' fiscal priorities, that shaped the mawālī experience in ways too varied to reduce to a single formula. Ethnic distinction mattered, and Arab tribal identity carried real social privilege throughout this period; but it operated alongside, and was often entangled with, these other dimensions of status and obligation.

Section 29.6

Abd al-Malik, al-Hajjaj, and the Consolidation of an Islamic Imperial State

The Second Fitna (680 to 692), a further and even more destabilizing episode of civil war among Muslims, once again interrupted whatever administrative consolidation the Umayyad state had achieved since Muʿawiya's accession. When Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan emerged from this conflict as caliph and restored central authority, his reign (685 to 705) initiated a period of significant administrative strengthening across the Umayyad state, with consequences that reached directly into Iran and Khurasan.

Much of this consolidation in the east was carried out through the forceful governorship of al-Hajjaj ibn Yusuf, who held authority over Iraq, and through it over Iran and the eastern provinces, from 694 to 714. Al-Hajjaj is remembered, in both contemporary and later sources, as an unusually vigorous and often harshly effective administrator, who tightened fiscal and military supervision over the eastern provinces, suppressed rebellion with considerable severity, and pressed Khurasani governors toward more systematic and more demanding tribute collection. His governorship illustrates, in concentrated form, a broader pattern this chapter traces: the gradual tightening of caliphal administrative reach into regions that earlier Umayyad governors had, of practical necessity, governed more loosely and through greater reliance on local Iranian intermediaries.

Alongside this administrative tightening, Abd al-Malik's reign saw significant reform of Umayyad coinage, extending gradually across the state's eastern provinces. Early Muslim rulers in the conquered Sasanian territories had, as Chapter 28 explained, initially continued striking coins in Sasanian style, sometimes modified with Arabic inscriptions or Islamic religious phrases, but retaining familiar Sasanian royal imagery. Beginning under Abd al-Malik, this practice gave way to a new, aniconic coinage bearing Arabic inscriptions rather than royal portraiture, a change that took full effect at most Umayyad mints by approximately 698 to 699, though, as this chapter's Object Study explains, Sasanian-type issues persisted somewhat longer at some mints in Fars, Kerman, and Sistan, a persistence associated in part with continued political disturbance in those provinces rather than with any single, clearly established cause.

This monetary reform carried significance well beyond its practical fiscal function. It marked a visible, state-wide assertion of a distinctly Islamic imperial identity, moving deliberately away from symbols inherited from the defeated Sasanian monarchy. At the same time, the reform's uneven regional pace, full and rapid at most mints, delayed at others, itself illustrates the chapter's broader argument: Umayyad government did not transform Iran uniformly or all at once, but through a gradual, regionally variable process.

Administrative Arabicization proceeded on its own, even more gradual and regionally uneven, timetable, and students should be careful not to extend this section's discussion of monetary reform into premature claims about the wholesale or simultaneous replacement of Iranian administrative language and personnel with Arabic and Arab officials. In Iraq, the fiscal register, or dīwān, is reported to have been changed from Persian into Arabic under al-Hajjaj's governorship around 697. In Khurasan, by contrast, Persian reportedly remained the language of fiscal administration for several further decades, until roughly 739, close to the very end of Umayyad rule in the east. The fuller linguistic and cultural transformation this difference points toward belongs to the history Chapter 33 examines, and even there proceeded gradually across the early Islamic centuries rather than being accomplished within the Umayyad period alone.

Section 29.7

Resistance, Revolt, and the Limits of Umayyad Rule

Umayyad authority over Iran, reconstructed after the First Fitna and further consolidated under Abd al-Malik and al-Hajjaj, remained throughout this period contested rather than fully secure. Resistance to that authority took many forms and arose from many different sources, and this chapter's evidence cautions strongly against collapsing that variety into a single narrative of Persian national resistance to Arab domination.

Some resistance arose directly from fiscal grievance, as communities or their dehqān representatives objected to the level, method, or perceived unfairness of tribute and tax demands, particularly as governors like al-Hajjaj pressed for stricter and more systematic collection. Other resistance originated with regional Iranian rulers seeking to preserve or reclaim a degree of political autonomy that earlier, looser Umayyad oversight had permitted them, and that tighter administrative control now threatened. On the Khurasani frontier specifically, resistance also took the form of continued military opposition by Tokharistani and other frontier polities not yet brought fully under Muslim control, a dimension of resistance closer to ongoing conquest warfare than to internal rebellion against established rule.

A further significant strand of resistance came from Kharijite religious-political movements, whose radical positions on legitimate leadership within the Muslim community led adherents into repeated armed conflict with Umayyad authority across various provinces, including parts of Iran, quite apart from any specifically Iranian grievance; Kharijite resistance reflected internal Muslim religious-political controversy as much as or more than any ethnic or regional dynamic. Still other episodes of unrest originated in tribal conflict among Arab settlers themselves, as rival Arab tribal groupings, whose rivalries often had roots in earlier Arabian and Iraqi politics, competed for governorships, military command, and access to revenue within Iran and especially within Khurasan, a dynamic examined further in Section 29.8. Finally, straightforward military mutiny and opposition to specific unpopular governors, whether on grounds of perceived injustice, excessive severity, or simple political miscalculation, produced repeated local crises throughout the period.

A significant body of evidence indicates that economic grievance, and taxation disputes specifically, drove a substantial share of the revolts and disturbances recorded across Khurasan and other Iranian regions during this period, rather than ethnic hostility toward Arabs as such providing the primary motive force. This point deserves particular emphasis because it corrects a durable popular misconception. When historical actors in this period resisted Umayyad government, they were, in the great majority of documented cases, resisting something more specific than "Arab rule" in the abstract: a particular tax demand, a particular governor's policy, a particular tribal rival's advantage, a particular religious-political claim about legitimate authority, or a particular threat to local autonomy. These specific grievances could, and in Khurasan eventually would, combine into something larger and more coordinated, but that convergence, examined in Chapter 30, was itself a historical development requiring explanation rather than an inevitable expression of latent Persian national resistance waiting to be released.

Recognizing this complexity matters for understanding both this chapter's period and the revolutionary movement that follows it. A coalition capable of overthrowing the Umayyad dynasty would need to draw together grievances as varied as those just described, uniting Arab settlers, mawālī converts, and non-Muslim Iranians around shared objections to specific policies and specific governors, rather than around a single unifying ethnic identity that, as this chapter has shown throughout, did not map cleanly onto the actual social divisions of Umayyad Iran.

Section 29.8

Khurasan: Frontier Society and a New Arab-Iranian Political World

The preceding sections have repeatedly pointed toward Khurasan as an exceptional case within the broader pattern of Umayyad rule over Iran, and this section draws those threads together, without yet narrating the revolutionary events that Chapter 30 examines in full. Khurasan's exceptional character rested on a specific combination of circumstances that, taken together, made it unlike any other Iranian province under Umayyad administration.

First, as Section 29.4 explained, Khurasan received Arab settlement on a scale and of a character unmatched elsewhere in Iran: large numbers of soldiers and, increasingly, their families, concentrated particularly around Merv but extending across the wider region, settled permanently rather than merely garrisoning temporarily. This settlement was dispersed enough, and sufficiently integrated into local economic life over time, that Arab settlers in Khurasan did not remain a segregated military caste; they became landholders, merchants, and neighbors of the surrounding Iranian population in ways that increasingly blurred the sharp initial distinction between conqueror and conquered.

Second, as Section 29.2 explained, Khurasan's Iranian dehqāns and regional rulers retained substantial local authority under Umayyad suzerainty, continuing to manage land, labor, and local administration even as Arab settlers arrived in growing numbers around and among them. This meant that Khurasani society, by the early eighth century, contained not two separated populations, Arab rulers and Iranian subjects, but an increasingly interwoven set of communities whose members shared economic interests, local loyalties, and, for a growing number of Iranians, religious identity as converts, even as formal distinctions of tribal affiliation, fiscal status, and military standing persisted among them.

Third, Khurasan remained, throughout this period, an active military frontier, with continuing campaigns directed toward Tokharistan and Transoxiana. This ongoing warfare sustained a large, continuously active military establishment in the region, gave Khurasani Arab settlers and their mawālī comrades-in-arms shared battlefield experience and shared grievances over pay and treatment, and kept Khurasan's governors and garrisons more militarily significant, and more politically consequential, than those of most other Iranian provinces.

Fourth, and critically, the Arab settler population in Khurasan carried with it the tribal rivalries and factional divisions that had developed within the garrison cities of Kufa and Basra before ever reaching Khurasan. These rivalries, rooted in pre-Islamic Arabian tribal genealogy and sharpened by decades of competition for governorships, military command, and stipends within Iraq, transplanted themselves into Khurasani politics and frequently proved as disruptive to Umayyad governors in the region as any tension between Arab settlers and the surrounding Iranian population.

The result, by the early decades of the eighth century, was a genuine paradox. Khurasan functioned simultaneously as the furthest active frontier of Umayyad military expansion and as the Iranian region in which the social boundary between Arab conqueror and Iranian subject had become least clear and most contested, precisely because of the depth of settlement, economic integration, shared military service, and growing conversion that distinguished it from every other province this chapter has examined. Large Arab communities in Khurasan increasingly shared practical interests with their Iranian neighbors, even as internal Arab tribal rivalry remained a persistent and destabilizing force within the settler population itself, and even as disputes over taxation, discussed in Section 29.5, continued to strain relations between converts and the fiscal administration.

This chapter does not narrate how these accumulated conditions produced revolution. That is Chapter 30's task. What this section has established is the raw material from which that revolution would eventually be built: a militarized, economically integrated, socially complex Khurasani society containing Arab settler factions divided against one another, a growing mawālī population among whom grievances over taxation, pay, and status became increasingly politically consequential, surviving Iranian elites with their own interests to protect, and a governing administration whose fiscal and military demands repeatedly strained relationships across all of these groups.

Section 29.9

New Social Orders on the Eve of Revolution

By the early decades of the eighth century, Iran had been transformed by roughly seventy years of Umayyad rule, without having been transformed into any single, settled new order. It was no longer, in any meaningful sense, Sasanian Iran under temporary foreign military occupation; too much had changed in administration, in religious composition, in the permanent settlement of new populations, and in the symbolic and institutional character of the state, for that description to hold. Yet it was equally not yet the mature Persianate Islamic civilization that later chapters of this textbook, particularly those of Volume III's remaining chapters, will describe, a civilization in which Iranian administrative expertise, New Persian literary culture, and Islamic religious and legal institutions would combine into new, more settled syntheses.

What existed instead, across Iran generally and in Khurasan with particular intensity, was a society composed of multiple overlapping and only partially reconciled groups. Arab military-settler families, some now several generations removed from their Iraqi or Arabian origins, held land, status, and military obligation within a framework still substantially organized around tribal affiliation. Newer Arab migrants continued to arrive, particularly into Khurasan, bringing fresh manpower and fresh tribal rivalries. A growing population of Iranian Muslim converts, the mawālī discussed in Section 29.5, shared Muslim religious affiliation with Arab Muslims but often occupied a differently situated and sometimes socially subordinate position within the conquest-era order, carrying grievances over pay, status, and continued fiscal demands. A still-larger population of non-Muslim Iranian taxpayers, Zoroastrian, Christian, Jewish, and adherents of other religious traditions, continued to bear the fiscal burdens examined in Section 29.3, administered still substantially through the dehqāns and regional rulers discussed in Section 29.2. Muslim administrators, increasingly drawing on both Arab and Iranian personnel, staffed a state apparatus that had grown more systematic under Abd al-Malik and al-Hajjaj but remained, in its reach into ordinary local life, heavily dependent on inherited Iranian structures.

These overlapping groups did not exist in simple harmony, nor did they exist in simple, uniform opposition to one another along a single line of division. Tax status, conversion, tribal rivalry, regional autonomy, the degree of Arab-Iranian social integration, and accumulated resentment of particular governors and particular policies, all cut across one another in ways that made Umayyad Iran, and Khurasan above all, a genuinely complex and unstable social landscape by the 730s and early 740s. Governors such as Nasr ibn Sayyar, appointed to Khurasan in this later period, attempted fiscal and administrative reforms aimed at addressing some of these accumulated grievances, particularly the disputed taxation of converts, but these efforts came too late, and addressed too narrow a portion of the underlying tensions, to prevent the crisis that followed.

This chapter closes, then, not with resolution but with accumulation: the accumulated tensions of tax status, conversion, tribal rivalry, regional autonomy, Arab-Iranian integration, and resentment toward specific governors and policies, concentrated with particular intensity in a militarily significant, economically integrated, and socially complex Khurasan. Chapter 30 examines how those accumulated conditions became, in the hands of a determined revolutionary movement organized initially around clandestine religious-political appeal and eventually around open military rebellion, the foundation for the destruction of the Umayyad dynasty itself and the establishment of a new Abbasid caliphate in which Iranian elites, examined in Chapter 31, would occupy a substantially different and more central position.

Historical Vocabulary
Jizya, Kharāj, and Tribute

Definition: In the fully developed classical Islamic fiscal system, jizya refers to a poll tax owed by non-Muslim subjects and kharāj to a tax on agricultural land. During the Umayyad period examined in this chapter, however, these terms and the distinction between them were still developing, and much of the earliest fiscal relationship between conquered Iranian communities and the Muslim state took the form of negotiated, often collectively assessed, tribute rather than a standardized individual tax applied uniformly across every province.

Historical context: Building directly on Chapter 28's account of conquest-era treaties, this chapter has shown that early Persian submission agreements frequently specified a fixed tribute sum, with local dehqāns remaining responsible for collecting and apportioning it. Only gradually, and unevenly by region, did Umayyad administration move toward more standardized land taxation and more consistent poll-tax practice, a process that continued well beyond the period this chapter examines.

Why it matters: Understanding this gradual, regionally variable development prevents students from reading the mature classical jizya-kharāj system backward into the conquest and early Umayyad periods, and helps explain why conversion, discussed in Section 29.5, created such genuine and unresolved fiscal difficulty: the categories themselves were still being worked out even as growing numbers of Iranians moved between them.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that every non-Muslim in every Iranian province paid an identical, clearly defined jizya from the moment of conquest onward, nor that kharāj had one single stable meaning and rate throughout the Umayyad period. Both terms, and the practices they described, remained subject to significant regional and chronological variation.

By the mid-680s, in the politically fragmented years before Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan's eventual victory and full coinage reform, mints across Fars and other Iranian provinces, under both Umayyad and rival authorities, continued striking coins that retained the familiar Sasanian royal bust and fire-altar design while adding a short Arabic religious phrase identifying the issuing authority with Islam. This coin, struck at Bishapur under the Zubayrid governor Abd al-Malik ibn Abd Allah, is a well-documented and securely attributed example of that broader, politically contested practice.

Description of the artifact: The coin's obverse and reverse preserve the Sasanian-style royal portrait, crown, and fire-altar-with-attendants design essentially unchanged from pre-conquest issues, together with a Pahlavi mint and date formula. Added in the obverse margin is a brief Arabic inscription, bism Allāh, Muhammad rasūl Allāh ("in the name of God, Muhammad is the messenger of God"), placed alongside, rather than replacing, the inherited Sasanian visual and textual elements.

Analysis: This coin captures, in a single dated and attributed physical object, precisely the layered transitional condition this chapter describes, while also illustrating that the experimentation this chapter examines was not confined to Umayyad administrators alone: an administration, in this case a Zubayrid one contesting Umayyad authority during the Second Fitna, still relying on inherited Sasanian techniques of royal representation and Pahlavi administrative formulae, onto which a distinctly Islamic religious claim, rendered in Arabic, has been grafted without yet replacing the older visual language altogether. Competing authorities, Umayyad and Zubayrid alike, were independently experimenting with adding Islamic inscriptions to inherited Sasanian coin types during these contested years. It is direct, contemporary material evidence that the broader transition from Sasanian to standardized Islamic coinage, completed under Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan at most mints by 79 AH / 698–699 CE once Umayyad authority had been reasserted, proceeded through a politically fragmented and experimental intermediate stage rather than a single abrupt change imposed uniformly from Damascus.

Limitations: A coin legend is extremely brief and can establish only what it states; it cannot by itself tell historians why Abd al-Malik ibn Abd Allah's Zubayrid administration chose to add this particular phrase, how ordinary users of the coin understood or reacted to it, or how widely coins of this type circulated relative to older, unmodified Sasanian-style issues still in use elsewhere or to contemporary Umayyad-issued coins carrying similar inscriptions. Surviving transitional coins also vary somewhat in wording, script, and placement across different mints, years, and issuing authorities, cautioning against treating any single specimen as representative of a single, centrally mandated empire-wide policy.

Perspective: The coin represents the perspective of the issuing authority, the Zubayrid governor Abd al-Malik ibn Abd Allah and the mint officials acting under his authority, rather than that of the population using the coin in daily transactions; it says nothing directly about how Iranian merchants, taxpayers, or officials themselves regarded the religious phrase newly appearing on their currency, nor about how such users distinguished, if at all, between coins issued by rival Zubayrid and Umayyad authorities during this contested period.

Historical Debate

Were Umayyad Social Divisions in Iran Primarily Ethnic, Religious, Fiscal, or Political?

The question: Historians examining Umayyad Iran, and Khurasan in particular, disagree over which analytical lens best explains the social tensions and eventual instability this chapter has documented. Should the central dividing line be understood chiefly as ethnic, Arab versus Iranian; chiefly as religious, Muslim versus non-Muslim; chiefly as fiscal, taxed versus exempt, or more precisely, who owed what to whom; or chiefly as political, tied to tribal faction, governor, and access to power, regardless of ethnicity or religion?

Interpretation A — Ethnic and status-based division was fundamental: Some historians emphasize that Arab identity carried real, persistent social and material privilege throughout the Umayyad period, that mawālī converts faced genuine discrimination despite sharing Muslim religious status with Arab Muslims, and that this underlying ethnic hierarchy shaped how fiscal and political conflicts were experienced and remembered, even when their immediate triggers were nominally about taxation or governance.
Interpretation B — Fiscal and political factors better explain the specific conflicts: Other historians argue that the documented revolts and disputes examined in Section 29.7 correlate more closely with specific taxation grievances, tribal rivalries among Arabs themselves, and conflicts over particular governors' policies than with any generalized ethnic animus, and that treating "Arab versus Iranian" as the primary axis of conflict risks obscuring the more precise and better-evidenced causes at work in most individual episodes.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same broad body of evidence: administrative and numismatic sources, later Arabic historical and geographical writing, and the documented pattern of revolts, tribal conflicts, and fiscal disputes traced throughout this chapter. They differ chiefly in which factor they treat as explanatorily primary and which as secondary or derivative.

Why it matters: The interpretation a historian adopts shapes how the revolutionary movement examined in Chapter 30 is understood, whether primarily as an Iranian national uprising against Arab rule, as a fiscal and political rebellion that happened to draw on both Arab and Iranian participants, or as some combination that varied by participant and by moment.

Student task: Drawing on the evidence presented across Sections 29.2 through 29.8, construct a brief argument for which interpretation the evidence in this chapter better supports, while acknowledging what the other interpretation captures that yours may understate.

Multiple Perspectives

Living under the New Order: Perspectives on Umayyad Iran

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across administrative, numismatic, and later literary evidence. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor.

From the perspective of an Iranian dehqān administering a district: Sovereignty has changed hands, but daily responsibility has not: assessing land, apportioning tribute among households, and answering to a distant governor's demands remain the defining tasks of local authority, now directed toward a Muslim rather than a Sasanian superior.
From the perspective of an Arab settler family in Merv: Two or three generations after first arriving as soldiers, the family now holds land, trades in the local market, and counts Iranian neighbors among its daily associates, even as tribal affiliation and rivalry with other Arab settler groups continue to shape political loyalty and military obligation.
From the perspective of a recent Iranian convert serving as a mawlā: Conversion has brought shared membership in the Muslim community and new opportunities in military and administrative service, but it has not erased every distinction from Arab Muslims, and uncertainty over continued tax obligations makes the material benefits of conversion feel less secure than its religious significance.
From the perspective of a non-Muslim Iranian taxpayer: Religious practice continues largely as before, mediated through a local community structure the conquest did not destroy, but as neighbors convert, those who remain non-Muslim can continue to carry poll-tax obligations even as land and tribute liabilities around them are reorganized or reassessed.
From the perspective of an Umayyad governor concerned with revenue: Balancing the religious principle that sincere converts should be incorporated fully as Muslims against the fiscal necessity of maintaining state revenue creates a genuine administrative dilemma, one that different predecessors and successors have resolved in different and sometimes contradictory ways.
From the perspective of an Arab soldier whose tribal rivals have also settled in Khurasan: Old rivalries from Kufa and Basra have not disappeared simply because the setting has changed; competition for governorships, command, and stipends among rival Arab tribal groupings remains as consequential to daily political life as any relationship with the surrounding Iranian population.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Early Conquest Order to Late Umayyad Iran

What Stayed the Same

  • Dehqāns and regional Iranian rulers continued to administer land, labor, and local taxation in many districts
  • Agricultural production, local markets, and everyday economic life continued largely along established lines
  • A religiously plural population, including Zoroastrian, Christian, Jewish, and other communities, persisted throughout the period
  • Iraq's garrison cities remained the administrative and demographic gateway to Iran and Khurasan

  • Permanent Arab settlement, especially in Khurasan, introduced a new demographic and social presence absent under Sasanian rule
  • The Muslim population grew steadily through conversion, producing a significant mawālī class with its own distinct grievances
  • Caliphal administration grew stronger and more systematic under Abd al-Malik and al-Hajjaj, tightening oversight that earlier governors had exercised more loosely
  • Coinage and other symbols of state authority shifted from Sasanian-derived forms toward a distinctly Islamic administrative and religious vocabulary
  • New categories of social and fiscal status, Arab settler, mawlā, non-Muslim taxpayer, emerged and increasingly overlapped in complex ways, particularly in Khurasan

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both continuity and change prevents two opposite errors: assuming Umayyad rule changed nothing beneath a change of sovereign, or assuming it swept away Iranian society and replaced it wholesale with an Arab-Islamic order within a single generation.

Cause and Effect

Taxation, Conversion, and Accumulating Instability

Long-term causes

A small Arab-Muslim governing and military elite superimposed atop a vastly larger, religiously plural Iranian population, combined with a fiscal system partly dependent on taxing non-Muslims specifically.

Short-term causes

Growing rates of conversion in Khurasan, inconsistent governor policy toward converts' tax status, and tightening fiscal demands under governors such as al-Hajjaj and his successors.

Effect

Documented grievances among many converts and mawālī, particularly in Khurasan, over unfulfilled fiscal expectations, compounding existing grievances over military pay and social status, and feeding into the broader accumulation of tension described in Section 29.9.

This textbook's response

Chapter 30 traces how this specific grievance, combined with tribal rivalry among Arab settlers and continuing dissatisfaction among non-Muslim taxpayers and Iranian elites, fed into a revolutionary coalition capable of overturning Umayyad rule.

Historical complexity: No single cause, taxation, conversion, tribal rivalry, or ethnic tension alone, adequately explains the instability this chapter documents; each interacted with and intensified the others in ways that varied by region, governor, and moment.

Object Study

The Reformed Islamic Dirham

What it is: A silver coin introduced across most Umayyad mints beginning around 698 to 699 under Abd al-Malik, bearing Arabic inscriptions, including religious phrases, rather than the royal portraiture characteristic of Sasanian coinage and of the earlier Arab-Sasanian transitional coins discussed in Chapter 28.

Continuity with Chapter 28: Chapter 28 examined an early Arab-Sasanian coin that retained a Sasanian-style royal image while adding Arabic or Islamic phrases, material evidence of a conquering power initially governing through inherited symbols rather than replacing them outright. The reformed dirham examined here represents the next stage in that same object's history: the same basic monetary instrument, the silver dirham, now stripped of royal portraiture entirely and reconstituted around a distinctly Islamic visual and textual vocabulary.

What it shows: The reform's uneven regional timing, effective at most mints by about 698 but delayed somewhat longer in Fars, Kerman, and Sistan, offers tangible evidence for this chapter's broader argument about regionally variable administrative transformation. A coin struck in a Khurasani or Iraqi mint in 700 might already carry the new aniconic Islamic design, while a coin struck in the same year in parts of Fars might still follow older Sasanian-derived conventions.

Why it matters: Coinage functioned, in this period as in most premodern states, as one of the most visible and widely encountered expressions of political authority available to ordinary people. The shift from Sasanian-derived to distinctly Islamic coin design therefore offers historians a rare, datable, and geographically traceable form of evidence for the pace and unevenness of Umayyad administrative and symbolic consolidation across Iran, complementing the administrative and literary evidence examined throughout this chapter.

Everyday Life and Social History

What Umayyad Rule Actually Changed for an Ordinary Household

For most Iranian households across most of the period this chapter examines, the most immediate and consequential change brought by Umayyad rule was not a change of religion, language, or daily custom, but a change in the ultimate destination of tax obligations that had, in most respects, already existed under Sasanian government. The person collecting those obligations locally was, in a great many districts, the same kind of figure, and sometimes the very same family, who had collected them before the conquest. What had changed was the distant authority to whom that revenue ultimately flowed, and, gradually, the terms on which it was calculated.

In regions of significant Arab settlement, particularly Khurasan, ordinary life changed more substantially and more directly. New neighbors, new languages heard in the market, new military obligations for those who took up arms or served in garrisons, and, increasingly, the real possibility of conversion and its attendant social and fiscal consequences, became part of daily experience in a way they simply were not for households in regions of lighter Arab presence, such as much of Fars or Sistan. A household in Merv by the early eighth century inhabited a genuinely different social world than a household in a Zagros foothill village, even though both nominally lived under the same Umayyad caliphate.

Historians reconstructing this everyday experience must rely heavily on indirect evidence, administrative and fiscal records, numismatic evidence such as the coinage examined in this chapter's Object Study, and later historical and geographical compilations, because ordinary cultivators, artisans, and household members of this period left few if any records of their own. Reading such sources carefully for what they imply about daily life, rather than only for what they state explicitly about governors and campaigns, remains essential to recovering the social history this chapter has attempted to present.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced how the Umayyad dynasty, established under Muʿawiya ibn Abi Sufyan after the First Fitna of 656 to 661, converted the military supremacy achieved by the conquest generation, examined in Chapter 28, into sustained, though never fully uniform or uncontested, government over Iran. That process began with reconquest and reconsolidation, falling unevenly across different regions: Fars and Kerman had already been forcibly pacified in 659, before the Umayyad period proper, while Muʿawiya's governors faced the heavier task of restoring tribute and Muslim authority in Sistan and Khurasan, regions whose relationship to Muslim rule had weakened considerably amid the internal Muslim conflict of the preceding years.

At the center of this government lay a structural reality this chapter has emphasized throughout: a comparatively small Arab-Muslim governing and military elite could not administer a vastly larger, religiously plural Iranian population without relying heavily on surviving Iranian institutions. Dehqāns and regional Iranian rulers continued to manage local administration and tax collection across much of Iran, meaning that political sovereignty changed hands considerably more quickly and completely than local administrative practice did.

Taxation itself evolved gradually from negotiated, often collectively assessed conquest-era tribute toward more standardized, though still regionally variable, distinctions between land tax and a tax associated with non-Muslim status. This fiscal system created a genuine and recurring administrative dilemma once significant numbers of Iranians began converting to Islam, since conversion threatened to erode the very revenue base on which the state depended, a tension different governors resolved differently and inconsistently across the period.

Arab settlement in Iran followed no single pattern. Iraq's garrison cities of Kufa and Basra housed large, concentrated Arab military populations and functioned as the administrative gateway to Iran. Western and central Iran, along with Fars, Kerman, and Sistan, saw comparatively limited and dispersed Arab settlement. Khurasan, by contrast, received large-scale, family-based Arab settlement beginning from at least the 660s, producing a demographically significant and increasingly economically integrated Arab population concentrated particularly around Merv.

Growing numbers of Iranian converts entered the Muslim community as mawālī, clients of Arab tribes, sharing Muslim religious status with Arab Muslims but frequently facing continued social and fiscal distinctions from them. Disputes over whether converts should continue paying taxes historically associated with non-Muslim status recurred repeatedly, especially in Khurasan, generating documented grievances that would feed into later revolutionary politics.

Under Abd al-Malik and his forceful governor al-Hajjaj, the Umayyad state achieved significant administrative and monetary consolidation, including the introduction of a reformed, aniconic Islamic coinage beginning around 698 to 699, though this reform, like the broader and still more gradual Arabicization of local administration, proceeded unevenly across different provinces, persisting in older Sasanian-derived coin forms longer at some mints in Fars, Kerman, and Sistan, and persisting in Persian-language fiscal administration in Khurasan for several further decades after Iraq had shifted to Arabic.

Resistance to Umayyad rule arose from many distinct sources throughout this period, including fiscal grievance, regional Iranian rulers' desire for autonomy, continuing frontier warfare in Tokharistan, Kharijite religious-political dissent, and tribal rivalry among Arab settlers themselves. The evidence examined in this chapter cautions strongly against reducing this varied resistance to a single narrative of Persian ethnic nationalism confronting Arab domination; most documented episodes reflect more specific grievances over taxation, governance, and political access.

Khurasan emerged across this chapter as an exceptional case within Umayyad Iran, combining large-scale Arab settlement, continued Iranian elite authority, active frontier warfare, and transplanted Arab tribal rivalry into an unusually complex and economically integrated frontier society, one in which the boundary between Arab conqueror and Iranian subject grew progressively less clear even as internal tensions among Arab settler factions, mawālī converts, and non-Muslim taxpayers continued to accumulate.

By the early decades of the eighth century, Iran contained overlapping social groups, Arab military-settler families, newer Arab migrants, Iranian Muslim converts, non-Muslim Iranian taxpayers, dehqāns, regional rulers, and a more systematic but still Iranian-dependent Muslim administration, whose accumulated tensions over taxation, conversion, tribal rivalry, and regional autonomy set the stage for the crisis of the 730s and 740s. Governor Nasr ibn Sayyar's late fiscal reforms addressed some of these grievances but arrived too late and too narrowly to prevent the emergence of the revolutionary conditions Chapter 30 will examine in full.

This chapter's central argument, that Umayyad rule in Iran rested on adaptation of inherited Sasanian institutions rather than their wholesale replacement, and that the social order this produced was neither simply Sasanian nor yet Abbasid-Persianate, provides the essential bridge between the conquest examined in Chapter 28 and the revolutionary transformation examined in Chapter 30.

Key Terms

Umayyad

Dehqān
An Iranian landholding local notable who continued to administer district and village affairs and collect taxes under Umayyad governors, providing crucial administrative continuity from the Sasanian period.
Mawlā / Mawālī
A term covering clients, patrons, allies, and freedmen; in this context, typically a non-Arab convert affiliated with an Arab tribe through walāʾ, sharing Muslim religious affiliation with Arab Muslims while often occupying a differently situated, sometimes subordinate, social position.
Walāʾ
The institution of clientage through which a mawlā became formally attached to an Arab tribal patron.
Jizya
A tax historically associated with non-Muslim status, whose application and terminology were still developing during the period this chapter examines.
Kharāj
A tax on agricultural land, gradually distinguished from jizya as Umayyad fiscal administration became more standardized.
Tribute
A negotiated, often collectively assessed payment characteristic of early conquest-era fiscal arrangements, prior to more standardized taxation.
Collective assessment
A fiscal method in which a community or district owed a total sum, apportioned internally by local elites such as dehqāns, rather than a tax calculated individually according to a standardized formula.
Garrison city
A concentrated urban settlement of Arab soldiers and their dependents, exemplified by Kufa and Basra in Iraq and functioning as bases for further eastward expansion and administration.
Tribal affiliation
Membership in an Arab tribal group, a central organizing category for military, social, and political life among Arab settlers throughout this period.
Khurasan
The historical northeastern Iranian region, introduced in Chapter 1 and central to this chapter, distinguished by unusually large-scale Arab settlement and complex Arab-Iranian social integration.
Merv
The principal city of Khurasan and the focal point of large-scale Arab settlement beginning in the 660s and traditionally intensified around 671.
Tokharistan
A region at the eastern edge of the Khurasani sphere that continued to resist Muslim control even as Khurasan itself was reorganized under Umayyad rule.
Transoxiana
The Central Asian region beyond the Oxus river, the target of continuing Umayyad military campaigns launched from Khurasan.
Kufa and Basra
The major Arab garrison cities of lower Iraq, source of much of the Arab military and settler population that moved into Iran and Khurasan.
Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan
Umayyad caliph (685 to 705) whose reign brought significant administrative strengthening and monetary reform, though Arabicization of local fiscal administration proceeded at very different speeds in different provinces, reaching Khurasan only decades later than Iraq.
Al-Hajjaj ibn Yusuf
Powerful governor of Iraq and the eastern provinces (694 to 714), associated with forceful fiscal and administrative consolidation and with documented grievances and opposition among particular constituencies of both Arab and Iranian subjects.
Reformed dirham
The aniconic Islamic silver coin introduced at most Umayyad mints beginning around 698 to 699, replacing Sasanian-style coinage and marking a visible assertion of distinct Islamic political and religious identity.
Kharijite movements
Radical religious-political movements whose disputes over legitimate leadership within the Muslim community produced repeated armed conflict with Umayyad authority, including in parts of Iran.
Nasr ibn Sayyar
Late Umayyad governor of Khurasan whose fiscal reform efforts illustrate the accumulated problems examined in this chapter without resolving the crisis that followed.
Dīwān
A register or bureau, particularly for military pay and fiscal administration, increasingly important to Umayyad state organization as governance grew more systematic.
Conversion
The process by which Iranians adopted Islam, examined in this chapter chiefly for its social and fiscal consequences rather than its religious or cultural dimensions, which Chapter 32 treats fully.
Regional ruler
An Iranian political authority, particularly in Khurasan, who retained internal governance over a territory under Umayyad suzerainty in exchange for tribute and submission.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has examined one of history's recurring and difficult problems: how a conquering power, numerically small relative to the population it has subdued, converts military supremacy into durable government. The Umayyad answer in Iran, reliance on surviving Iranian administrative structures, gradual and regionally uneven fiscal reform, uneven patterns of settlement, and slowly developing institutions for incorporating converts, was neither a coherent master plan nor a simple continuation of Sasanian rule under new management. It was, instead, a genuinely new and unstable social order, assembled piece by piece across seven decades of negotiation, accommodation, reform, and conflict.

Understanding this chapter matters for the volume that follows it because the social order it describes did not resolve itself peacefully into the Persianate Islamic civilization later chapters will examine. It fractured, in Khurasan above all, under the weight of accumulated grievances over taxation, conversion, tribal rivalry, and governance that this chapter has traced in detail. Chapter 30 will show how that fracture became revolution, and Chapter 31 will show how Iranian elites, no longer simply local administrators serving a distant Arab caliphate but increasingly central participants in a new imperial system, reshaped the Abbasid state that emerged from the crisis.

Students should carry forward from this chapter a specific caution against overly simple narratives, whether of total continuity, in which nothing really changed beneath a change of ruler, or of total rupture, in which Arab-Islamic conquest instantly and completely remade Iranian society. The actual history, as this chapter has shown through dehqāns and governors, tribute and reformed coinage, mawālī converts and Arab settler families, Kharijite rebels and Khurasani frontier society, was considerably more complicated, more regionally varied, and, ultimately, more historically interesting than either simple narrative allows.

The story this textbook tells continues directly from here. The conditions this chapter has assembled, an overtaxed and unevenly incorporated mawālī population, a fractious Arab settler community divided by tribal rivalry, surviving Iranian elites with their own interests to protect, and a Khurasan uniquely positioned at the intersection of all these pressures, are not the end of the Umayyad story in Iran. They are its volatile final chapter, and the opening conditions of the revolutionary transformation examined next.
