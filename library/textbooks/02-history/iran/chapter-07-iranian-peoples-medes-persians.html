Iranian Peoples on the Plateau: Medes, Persians, and Related Groups

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

In the ninth century BCE, Assyrian scribes recording the campaigns of their kings began writing down names that historians now recognize as Iranian: Parsua, Madai, and a scattering of local rulers whose territories the annals name but rarely locate with precision. These names mark a genuine turning point. They do not, however, hand historians a finished ethnic map of the Iranian plateau.

Chapter 6 followed a difficult and largely silent trail: the reconstructed movements of Indo-Iranian and early Iranian-speaking communities before any outside observer wrote their names down. That chapter depended on comparative linguistics, archaeological inference, and cautious argument about population movement, precisely because no contemporary source described these communities in their own words or named them as a distinct people. This chapter begins at the moment that silence partially breaks. Beginning in the ninth century BCE, the imperial record keepers of Assyria, campaigning and extracting tribute across the Zagros and the western Iranian highlands, started to write down place names, ruler names, and ethnonyms, that is, names for lands, rulers, and peoples, that modern scholars can connect, with varying degrees of confidence, to Iranian-speaking populations. As this chapter will stress throughout, these labels are not a uniform category: some clearly name a land or territory, others name an individual ruler, and only some may reasonably be treated as naming a people in an ethnic sense, and deciding which is which is itself part of the historian's task.

It would be a serious mistake, however, to treat this change in the evidence as a change from ignorance to certainty. Assyrian scribes were not ethnographers. They wrote annals to celebrate royal conquest, record tribute, and assert imperial claims to distant territory, not to classify the languages, self-understandings, or cultural boundaries of the peoples they encountered. A name recorded in a triumphal inscription tells us that an Assyrian army, or an Assyrian informant, associated a place, a ruler, or a group with a particular label at a particular moment. It does not automatically tell us what language that group spoke, how its members understood their own identity, how stable its political organization was, or how its name related to names recorded a generation earlier or later. Historians who forget this distinction risk turning a genuinely fragmentary and difficult body of evidence into an artificially tidy story of tribes marching neatly across a map toward their historical destinies.

This chapter asks a question that follows directly from that caution: once Iranian-speaking peoples become visible in the written and archaeological record, who, in fact, can historians identify with reasonable confidence, and by what evidence? The chapter's two central case studies are the land the Assyrians called Parsua or Parsuwash, whose name scholars connect, cautiously, to the later Persians, and the land or peoples the Assyrians called Madai, connected to the later Medes. Both cases reward careful attention precisely because they resist simple narration. The land of Parsua, attested in the Zagros in 843 BCE and often localized south or southwest of Lake Urmia, though its precise geography remains debated, cannot simply be equated with the later Persians of Fars, even though the names appear related. The Medai of the ninth century BCE are not a single unified Median kingdom but, so far as Assyrian sources allow historians to judge, a shifting collection of local rulers and communities across a broad stretch of western and central Iran.

This chapter also insists on a second, equally important point: the ninth- and eighth-century Iranian plateau was not simply becoming uniformly Iranian as Median and Persian names appear in the sources. Elamites, Manneans, Urartians, the peoples of Ellipi, and other communities whose languages remain imperfectly understood continued to inhabit, govern, and shape large portions of the same landscape. Iranian-speaking peoples moved into a plateau that was already populated, already politically organized in places, and already linguistically diverse. Understanding the Medes and Persians therefore requires understanding their neighbors, not treating those neighbors as background scenery for an Iranian story that was, in the ninth century BCE, only one thread among several.

Finally, this chapter draws a firm boundary around its own scope. It is not, and does not attempt to be, a political history of Median expansion, Median-Assyrian warfare, or the eventual Median contribution to the fall of Assyria. Those developments, along with the complex relationship between Media, Babylonia, and the wider seventh- and sixth-century Near East, belong to Chapter 8. This chapter stops well short of political narrative. Its task is prior to that narrative: to establish, carefully and with appropriate caution, who the Medes, Persians, and other Iranian-speaking peoples were as they first become visible to outside observers, what kind of evidence supports that visibility, and what that evidence cannot yet tell historians.

By the chapter's end, students should understand that the transition from Chapter 6's reconstructed speech communities to Chapter 7's named peoples is a transition in the kind of evidence available, not a transition from uncertainty to certainty. A name in an Assyrian tribute list is a beginning for historical inquiry, not its conclusion. Learning to treat such names with exactly this mixture of appreciation and caution is the central skill this chapter aims to build, a skill that will remain essential when Chapter 8 turns to the political history that these named peoples went on to make.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the appearance of Iranian-connected ethnonyms, land names, and ruler names in Assyrian sources changes, without resolving, the historical problem introduced in Chapter 6.
  2. Describe the character of Neo-Assyrian royal inscriptions as historical evidence, including their purposes and their limitations for ethnic and geographic reconstruction.
  3. Identify the major non-Iranian-speaking peoples and polities of the ninth- and eighth-century Iranian plateau, including Elamites, Manneans, Urartians, and the peoples of Ellipi.
  4. Trace the appearance of *Parsua*/*Parsuwash* in the Assyrian record and evaluate the evidence connecting this name to the later Persians of Fars.
  5. Trace the appearance of *Madai* in the Assyrian record and explain why early Median political organization appears fragmented rather than unified.
  6. Distinguish among Medes as a people, Median as a language, and Media as a geographic or political category.
  7. Evaluate the strengths and limitations of archaeological evidence, such as that from Nush-i Jan and Godin Tepe, for identifying "Median" material culture.
  8. Explain the significance of Anshan and the Elamite world for understanding early Persian identity in Fars.
  9. Identify other Iranian-speaking peoples attested in this period or slightly later, including Saka/Scythians, Bactrians, Sogdians, Parthians, and others, and locate them approximately.
  10. Distinguish ethnicity, language, and political identity as separate analytical categories when interpreting ancient evidence.
  11. Assess common errors in popular treatments of early Median and Persian history, including migration narratives presented with false certainty.
  12. Explain how this chapter's methodological caution prepares students for the political history of Media and Assyria examined in Chapter 8.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How can historians identify Medes, Persians, and other early Iranian-speaking peoples in the written and archaeological record without assuming that every name, culture, or population encountered was ethnically Iranian?
  2. What can Assyrian royal inscriptions establish about the peoples they name, and what do they leave uncertain?
  3. Why does the ninth-century Iranian plateau resist description as a simply "Iranian" landscape?
  4. What is the evidentiary basis for connecting the land of *Parsua*, attested in the Zagros and often localized south or southwest of Lake Urmia, with the later Persians of Fars, and what are the limits of that evidence?
  5. Why do Assyrian sources describe multiple Median rulers rather than a single Median king in the ninth and eighth centuries BCE?
  6. Can archaeological sites be confidently labeled "Median," and on what basis might historians resist or accept such labels?
  7. How did the Elamite world shape the environment into which Persian-speaking populations moved in southwestern Iran?
  8. Why is it misleading to treat "Iranian" in this period as equivalent only to "Median and Persian"?
  9. What is the difference between a linguistic identity, a political identity, and an externally applied geographic label, and why does that difference matter for interpreting ancient names?

Historical Context

Chapter 6 established that Indo-Iranian and, later, distinctly Iranian-speaking populations moved onto and across the Iranian plateau over an extended period beginning approximately in the second millennium BCE, a process reconstructed primarily through comparative linguistics and archaeological inference rather than through any contemporary written testimony from the migrating populations themselves. That chapter also cautioned against treating archaeological cultures as reliable markers of language or ethnicity, since material culture, burial practice, and settlement pattern do not by themselves reveal what language a community spoke or how its members understood their own collective identity.

This chapter picks up the story at the point where outside observers begin to leave written records naming peoples and places on and around the plateau. That point is not uniform across the plateau; it arrives earliest and most fully in the west, where the expanding Neo-Assyrian Empire, centered in northern Mesopotamia, repeatedly campaigned across the Zagros Mountains from the ninth century BCE onward in pursuit of tribute, resources, and strategic control over the routes linking Mesopotamia to the Iranian interior and beyond. Assyrian kings commissioned inscriptions commemorating these campaigns, and it is within these inscriptions, alongside occasional administrative and letter texts, that names connected to the Medes and Persians first appear.

It is essential to recall the political geography already introduced in earlier chapters. Elam remained a significant power in southwestern Iran and lowland Susiana well into this period, and Elamite political and cultural traditions remained influential in highland Fars; the regional and dynastic name Anshan retained importance even though the archaeological city at Tal-e Malyan had greatly declined, a distinction examined further in Section 7.9. To the northwest, the kingdom of Urartu, centered around Lake Van in eastern Anatolia, contested influence over the northwestern Zagros and the Lake Urmia basin with Assyria. Mannea, a kingdom in the region around Lake Urmia, and Ellipi, a polity in the central Zagros, appear repeatedly in Assyrian sources as significant regional powers whose populations are not securely identified as Iranian speaking. This dense, competitive, and linguistically mixed political landscape is the setting into which the earliest Assyrian land names, ruler names, and people-designations later connected with Iranian-speaking communities enter the historical record.

Historians studying this period must also bear in mind the specific character of Neo-Assyrian source material, a subject this chapter's next section develops in detail. Unlike later Achaemenid royal inscriptions, examined in Chapter 12, which present a court's own official self-description, Neo-Assyrian annals describe foreign peoples from an external, frequently hostile, and often geographically approximate perspective. Names recorded in the course of a military campaign or tribute demand reflect Assyrian administrative and military concerns first, and only secondarily, and imperfectly, the self-understanding of the peoples being described.

Section 7.1

From Reconstructed Speakers to Named Peoples

Chapter 6 closed with a population historians can describe only indirectly: communities whose language belonged to the Iranian branch of the Indo-Iranian family, whose presence on the plateau is inferred from comparative linguistics and cautious archaeological argument, but who left no contemporary text naming themselves or describing their own political organization. This chapter opens at the point where that indirect reconstruction meets a different, and in some ways more concrete, kind of evidence: names written down by outside observers.

Beginning in the ninth century BCE, Neo-Assyrian royal inscriptions begin to record land names, ruler names, and, in some cases, names that may reasonably be read as ethnonyms, across the Zagros and western Iranian highlands. Among these, two names have drawn particular attention from historians of Iran: Parsua, or Parsuwash, presented in Assyrian sources as the name of a land, first attested in 843 BCE in the Zagros region, and Madai, attested from the 830s BCE, associated with numerous local rulers across a broader stretch of western and central Iran. Both names are widely, though not universally or uncritically, connected by historians to the later Persians and Medes respectively; whether either label corresponds to a coherent ethnic community, as opposed to an administrative or geographic designation later associated with one, is precisely the question this chapter examines.

It is tempting, faced with such names, to conclude that the historical fog has finally lifted: that historians can now say with confidence who lived where, what language they spoke, and how their society was organized. This chapter resists that temptation. A name recorded by an Assyrian scribe is a different kind of evidence from a modern census record, an ancient inscription commissioned by the people it describes, or an extensive body of administrative documents produced by the community in question. It is, at best, a single point of external observation, filtered through Assyrian military and administrative concerns, recorded for Assyrian purposes, and preserved because it survived in Assyrian royal archives rather than because ancient scribes anticipated that later historians would need it for ethnic reconstruction.

This chapter's central methodological claim, building directly on Chapter 6's caution about archaeology and language, is this: a recorded name does not automatically supply historians with a stable ethnicity, a fixed territory, an identifiable archaeological culture, or a coherent political community. Each of these things must be argued for separately, using appropriate evidence, and each argument carries its own uncertainties. The sections that follow work through this problem systematically, first by examining how Assyrian evidence functions as a historical source, then by surveying the plateau's broader population, and finally by following the specific and genuinely difficult cases of Parsua and Madai.

Section 7.2

Reading Assyrian Evidence for Early Iran

Neo-Assyrian royal inscriptions, the primary written source for the peoples examined in this chapter, were composed for specific and limited purposes. Assyrian kings commissioned annals, summary inscriptions, and building inscriptions chiefly to commemorate military campaigns, record the tribute extracted from subjected or intimidated populations, and assert the king's claim to have brought distant lands under Assyrian influence or control. These texts are royal and administrative documents, not ethnographic surveys, geographic treatises, or neutral historical narratives.

This purpose shapes what the inscriptions can and cannot tell historians. They are relatively reliable for establishing that an Assyrian army reached a particular region in a particular year, connected to a securely dated royal reign, and they often preserve names, whether of places, rulers, or peoples, that would otherwise be entirely lost to history. In this sense, Assyrian annals are indispensable: without them, historians would have no ninth-century attestation of Parsua or Madai at all. At the same time, the inscriptions typically describe geography only approximately, since their authors were concerned with claiming conquest and tribute rather than producing a precise itinerary or map. A campaign described as reaching "the land of Parsua" does not necessarily allow modern historians to draw a confident boundary around that land, and successive campaigns sometimes describe the same or similar names in ways that are difficult to reconcile into a single consistent geography.

A further complication concerns the Assyrian scribes' own working categories. Assyrian administrators organized distant, often loosely governed regions into named "lands," each frequently associated with a local ruler who might submit, rebel, be replaced, or be recorded inconsistently across different inscriptions and different reigns. These categories reflect Assyrian administrative convenience as much as, or more than, any political or ethnic self-organization on the part of the people described. When an inscription lists the "land of Madai" alongside dozens of comparably named lands and rulers, it is describing the world through an Assyrian administrative lens, not necessarily through a lens that Median communities themselves would have recognized or used.

None of this means that Assyrian evidence should be dismissed. It means that historians must read it carefully, asking in each case what kind of claim a given inscription actually supports. An inscription can reliably establish that an Assyrian king claimed tribute from a named ruler in a named land in a given year. It cannot, by itself, establish that this land had fixed and stable borders across decades, that the population within it spoke a single language, or that the name recorded corresponds precisely to a later, better documented people bearing what appears to be a similar name.

Historical Method

When Does a Name Identify a People?

Historians working with ancient ethnonyms must ask a sequence of questions before treating a recorded name as evidence for a stable historical people. First, who recorded the name, and for what purpose? A name recorded in a triumphal inscription serves a different function than a name recorded in an administrative letter or a later geographic treatise, and each type of source carries different risks of distortion.

Second, does the name recur consistently across multiple, independent sources, or does it appear once and then vanish, only for a similar-sounding name to reappear later in a different context? Consistency across independent sources strengthens confidence that a name designates a real, continuing community; a single attestation, or an attestation separated by a long gap from a similar later name, requires much greater caution.

Third, does the name correspond to any independent linguistic, archaeological, or later self-descriptive evidence? A name alone, without corroborating evidence of language, material culture, or later self-identification, supports only a limited historical claim: that an outside observer, at one moment, associated a label with a place, ruler, or group.

Fourth, historians must ask what the name does not tell us. A name typically does not establish precise territorial boundaries, internal political organization, the full linguistic character of a population, or the relationship between the named group and its neighbors, unless additional evidence addresses these questions directly.

Applying the method: When this chapter discusses Parsua and Madai, it applies exactly this sequence of questions, treating each name as a starting point for cautious historical argument rather than as a settled ethnic fact.

Section 7.3

A Plateau of Many Peoples

Before turning to the Medes and Persians specifically, this chapter must establish a point that recurs throughout this textbook's treatment of Iranian history: the ninth- and eighth-century Iranian plateau was not becoming uniformly "Iranian" as Median and Persian names entered the written record. It remained, as it long had been and would long continue to be, a mosaic of distinct peoples, languages, and political systems, among which Iranian-speaking communities were significant but far from exclusive.

To the southwest, Elam, examined in Chapter 5, remained a substantial political and cultural presence throughout the Neo-Elamite period, its language and administrative traditions continuing to shape lowland Susiana and highland Fars alike. To the northwest, around Lake Urmia, the kingdom of Mannea exercised significant regional power, contested by both Assyria and the kingdom of Urartu centered around Lake Van, itself a major power whose language, Urartian, belonged to a different family entirely from the Iranian languages under discussion in this chapter. In the central Zagros, the polity of Ellipi appears repeatedly in Assyrian sources as a significant local power whose linguistic affiliation remains uncertain to modern scholars.

Iranian-speaking populations, wherever and whenever they arrived in particular districts, therefore entered a landscape already occupied by established polities, existing agricultural and pastoral populations, and functioning political and religious institutions. The historical process this chapter describes was not the replacement of one population by another across an empty landscape, but a prolonged period of coexistence, interaction, and gradual change in the relative prominence of different linguistic and political communities. Evidence from the Zagros region documents Iranian-speaking groups existing alongside several non-Iranian linguistic traditions, a pattern of genuine mixture rather than straightforward succession.

This point directly extends the lesson of Chapter 5: Elam does not disappear from Iranian history merely because Iranian-speaking peoples begin to appear in the sources. Nor do Mannea, Urartu, or Ellipi vanish to make room for a supposedly inevitable Iranian future. Each of these polities pursued its own political interests, sometimes allying with, sometimes resisting, and sometimes remaining largely independent of both Assyrian imperial pressure and the emerging Iranian-speaking communities examined in the sections that follow. Students should carry this plural, mixed picture forward through the remainder of this chapter, resisting any temptation to read the ninth-century plateau as a simplified prelude to a unified Iranian nation.

Section 7.4

Parsua/Parsuwash: The First Persian Problem

The earliest securely dated attestation connected, with appropriate caution, to the later Persians appears in Assyrian sources from 843 BCE, describing a land called Parsua, or in some renderings Parsuwash, located in the Zagros region, often localized south or southwest of Lake Urmia, though Assyrian geographic reporting is approximate enough that the precise territory remains debated among specialists. This date and general location together constitute what this chapter calls the first Persian problem: a land-name that resembles, both phonetically and in later scholarly reconstruction, the name of the Persians who would eventually establish the Achaemenid Empire from a base far to the southeast, in Fars. It is worth emphasizing that Assyrian sources present *Parsua* as the name of a land or country, not explicitly as the name of an ethnic group; whether, and to what extent, an ethnic or linguistic community corresponded to this administrative land-name is itself part of the historical problem this section examines, not a fact the label supplies automatically.

The temptation to narrate this evidence as a simple story, in which "the Persians" originally lived near Lake Urmia and subsequently migrated across several centuries to Fars, should be resisted, or at least presented with considerably more caution than popular accounts often supply. What the evidence actually establishes is more limited: a land-name resembling "Parsua" appears in the Zagros region in the ninth century BCE, and a related or similar name, *Parsumash*, discussed further in Section 7.8, appears in seventh-century Assyrian sources in connection with a ruler later identifiable, through a debated identification, with a king associated with the title "king of Anshan." The relationship between these attestations, whether they represent the same population migrating across an enormous distance, two related but distinct groups sharing a common name or linguistic root, or some more complex process of political and cultural transmission that need not involve the wholesale movement of a population, remains a genuinely unresolved and actively debated historical problem.

Several considerations complicate a simple migration narrative. First, the geographic distance between the Zagros region associated with Parsua and Fars is very great, and Assyrian sources provide no continuous documentation of a population moving across that distance; the evidence consists of separated attestations rather than a traceable route. Second, names can spread, be adopted, or recur independently for reasons other than population movement, including political emulation, dynastic claims, or simple linguistic coincidence involving a common root word. Third, the political and cultural environment of Fars, as Section 7.9 will show, was deeply shaped by Elamite tradition, raising the further question of how a possibly migrating Iranian-speaking population interacted with, was absorbed into, or absorbed elements of, the established Elamite legacy associated with Anshan.

This chapter therefore presents the *Parsua*-to-*Parsumash*-to-*Pārsa* sequence as a documented pattern of related names appearing at different times in different places, worthy of serious historical attention and cautious inference, but not as a settled migration narrative with a known route, timeline, or mechanism. This chapter's Historical Debate feature returns to this problem directly, presenting the range of scholarly positions without declaring a single winner.

Section 7.5

The Medes Enter the Written Record

Assyrian sources begin referring to a people or land called Madai, or in some renderings Matai, from the 830s BCE, with the precise year cited differently across scholarly treatments depending on how particular inscriptions are dated and interpreted. Rather than treat the difference between, for example, 836, 835, or 834 BCE as historically significant, this chapter treats the broader decade as the relevant marker: by some point in the 830s BCE, Assyrian scribes were recording a name connected with the later Medes.

What the earliest evidence shows is considerably more fragmented than a unified "Median kingdom." Assyrian inscriptions describe numerous local rulers and settlements scattered across a broad stretch of western and central Iran, each individually named, each individually subject to Assyrian tribute demands or military pressure, without any indication of a single overarching Median political authority. This pattern persists for a considerable time; Assyrian evidence continues to describe multiple, independent Median rulers well into the seventh century BCE, long after the name Madai first appears in the record.

This fragmentation matters for how students should understand the term "Medes" throughout this chapter and the two that follow. When this chapter, and Chapter 8 after it, refer to "the Medes" in the ninth and eighth centuries BCE, the term designates a linguistically and perhaps culturally related set of communities and rulers across a broad region, identified as such primarily through the Assyrian ethnonym applied to them, rather than a centralized state with a single ruler, capital, or unified policy. The process by which this fragmented collection of communities eventually developed into the more centralized political power discussed in Chapter 8, and the extent to which Assyrian and Babylonian sources allow historians to trace that process with confidence, is a central question for that later chapter, not this one.

Students should also resist the temptation to read later, far more centralized Achaemenid administrative categories backward onto this earlier period. The neat lists of subject peoples that Achaemenid royal inscriptions would eventually present, examined in Chapter 12, describe a mature imperial system many generations removed from the fragmented, locally organized communities that Assyrian scribes encountered in the ninth and eighth centuries BCE.

Section 7.6

What Was "Media"?

The term "Media," and its associated adjective "Median," can refer to at least three related but analytically distinct things, and careful historical writing should distinguish among them rather than allowing one sense to blur silently into another. Failing to make this distinction is one of the most common sources of confusion in popular treatments of early Iranian history.

First, "Medes" can refer to a people: the communities and individuals whom Assyrian and later sources describe using this label, understood as a population connected by some combination of language, geography, and, eventually, shared political organization. Second, "Median" can refer to a language: specifically, a Northwestern Iranian language that specialists reconstruct primarily through indirect evidence, since no substantial contemporary text written in Median itself survives. Scholars reconstruct Median chiefly from personal names, place names, loanwords absorbed into other languages, particularly Old Persian, and comparative linguistic analysis, rather than from an independent Median textual corpus.

Third, "Media" can refer to a geographic or political region: the territory, understood differently at different periods, associated with Median rule or Median population. This geographic sense expands, contracts, and shifts considerably across the period covered by this chapter and Chapter 8, from the scattered local territories of the ninth century BCE to the more consolidated political entity examined in the next chapter.

Keeping these three senses separate prevents a specific and recurring error: assuming that because a site, an artifact, or a place name can be associated with the geographic region called Media, it must therefore have been created or used by ethnically or linguistically Median people, speaking the Median language, as part of a unified Median political system. Each of these connections must be independently established. A pot found within the general territory later associated with Media does not, by that fact alone, become a "Median pot" in any meaningful ethnic or linguistic sense, a point developed further in the next section's treatment of archaeological evidence.

Historical Vocabulary
Ethnonyms, Land Names, and Ruler Names

Definition: An ethnonym is a name used to designate a people or ethnic group, which may be used by a group to describe itself, an "autonym" or "endonym," or applied to a group by outsiders, an "exonym." Assyrian sources, however, do not always clearly distinguish a people's name from the name of the land it inhabited or the ruler who governed it. *Madai* functions in Assyrian usage as a label plausibly connected to both a land and a related population, while *Parsua* is presented explicitly as the name of a land or country; whether an ethnic community corresponded neatly to that land-name is a matter for historical argument, not something the label supplies automatically.

Historical context: Because most of the names discussed in this chapter come from Assyrian royal inscriptions, historians cannot always be certain whether a recorded name reflects the self-designation of the population described, an Assyrian administrative label for a territory, a ruler's name extended to his land, or some combination of these. This uncertainty does not make the names useless, but it does require caution in interpreting exactly what kind of identity, if any, a given name describes.

Why it matters: Recognizing the exonym/autonym distinction, and the distinction between land names, ruler names, and ethnonyms proper, helps students avoid assuming that an Assyrian-recorded name necessarily matches how the people so named understood or described themselves. It also helps explain why related but not identical forms of a name, such as *Parsua*, *Parsuwash*, and *Parsumash*, might appear across different sources and periods without necessarily indicating a single, continuously self-identifying population.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not conclude that a land name or ruler name recorded by outsiders is therefore worthless as evidence, nor should they assume that every such name straightforwardly identifies an ethnic group. Land names, ruler names, and genuine ethnonyms are different categories requiring separate, source-critical treatment, exactly the distinction this chapter applies throughout.

Section 7.7

Can Archaeology Identify the Medes?

Iron Age archaeological sites in western Iran, including Nush-i Jan, Godin Tepe, and Baba Jan, provide valuable evidence for settlement, architecture, and material culture during roughly the same centuries in which Assyrian sources attest Medes and other named peoples in the region. It is tempting, and older scholarship frequently succumbed to this temptation, to label the distinctive architectural and artifactual assemblages found at such sites simply "Median," treating the archaeological evidence as a direct confirmation and elaboration of the written sources' ethnic claims.

More recent and methodologically careful scholarship treats this labeling with considerably greater caution, for reasons directly connected to Chapter 6's warnings about archaeology and language. Pottery styles, building techniques, fortification designs, and burial practices do not, by themselves, reveal the language spoken by the people who made or used them, nor do they reveal how those people understood their own ethnic or political identity. A distinctive architectural tradition found across a broad region of western Iran during the Iron Age could reflect a shared Median political or cultural sphere, but it could equally reflect regional building practices shared across multiple linguistic communities, the spread of a particular elite architectural fashion independent of ethnicity, or simply the constraints and opportunities of local geography and available materials.

Even the category "Median art," as a term used to describe a coherent stylistic tradition attributable specifically to Median populations, has proven notoriously difficult for specialists to define with precision, given the absence of texts that would allow archaeologists to connect specific sites or artifacts securely to a Median political or ethnic identity, as opposed to a broader regional Iron Age culture of western Iran that may have included Medes, other Iranian speakers, and non-Iranian populations alike.

This does not mean that sites such as Nush-i Jan and Godin Tepe are unimportant for understanding the period this chapter examines. They illuminate, in valuable and otherwise unavailable detail, the settlement patterns, architectural traditions, and material life of Iron Age western Iran during precisely the period in which Assyrian sources attest Medes. What they do not automatically do is confirm that any specific building, artifact assemblage, or settlement was created, used, or governed by people who identified themselves, or would have been identified by contemporaries, as Medes in any secure ethnic or political sense. The correct formulation, and the one this textbook adopts throughout, treats such sites as evidence for the archaeological context of the Median period, not as self-evidently "Median sites."

Historical Method

Is This Site Median?

Consider a hypothetical fortified building excavated in western Iran, dated by its pottery and construction technique to approximately the eighth century BCE, located within the broad region that Assyrian sources associate with Madai. A textbook, museum label, or popular article might describe this as "a Median fortress." What would justify, and what would undermine, that description?

Evidence that might support an ethnic or political attribution: Inscriptions found at the site naming a ruler or population using a term connected to Media; a securely dated Assyrian text describing a specific named settlement matching the site's location; distinctive material culture found consistently and exclusively across a well-defined region matching other, independently established evidence for Median territory.

Evidence that would complicate or undermine such an attribution: An absence of any inscriptional evidence naming the site's population or rulers; architectural or artifactual styles shared broadly across a wider region inhabited by multiple, linguistically distinct populations; the site's location in a contested border zone claimed at different times by Mannea, Urartu, Assyria, or Median rulers; the general absence, across the entire region, of texts written in the Median language itself.

Conclusion for students: In most actual cases from this period, the second category of evidence considerably outweighs the first. Historians and archaeologists should therefore generally describe such sites in terms of their date, location, and material characteristics, reserving the label "Median" for cases where independent written evidence genuinely supports that specific ethnic or political attribution.

Section 7.8

Persian Movement toward Southwestern Iran

Following the appearance of *Parsua* in the Zagros in 843 BCE, the historical trail connected to the later Persians grows considerably thinner for roughly a century and a half. When it resumes with somewhat greater clarity, in the seventh century BCE, the evidence is more complicated than a simple relocation of the same name to a new place. In 691 BCE, inscriptions of the Assyrian king Sennacherib list *Parsumash* alongside Anzan, that is, Anshan, as separate names among the allies mustered by Elam against Assyria at the battle of Halule. The two names appear side by side in this source, not as a single identical designation, which by itself cautions against assuming that "Parsumash" simply meant "Anshan" to Assyrian scribes of this period.

Later in the seventh century, inscriptions of Ashurbanipal refer to a ruler named Kuraš, rendered in English as Cyrus, described as king of *Parsumash*, in connection with events following Assyria's defeat of Elam. This is contemporary seventh-century evidence. Separately, Cyrus II's Babylonian Cyrus Cylinder, an inscription dated to 539 BCE, retrospectively identifies his grandfather, an ancestor named Cyrus I, with the royal title "great king, king of Anshan." Many historians have identified Ashurbanipal's Kuraš of Parsumash with this same Cyrus I of Anshan, on the grounds that the name, approximate period, and royal status align plausibly. This identification, if accepted, would provide a genuine link between the seventh-century Parsumash evidence and the Anshan title recorded more than a century later. It is, however, an identification and an inference, not a fact stated outright by any single ancient source, and the geographic implications that follow from it, including exactly where a "king of Parsumash" or a "king of Anshan" resided and ruled, remain actively debated among specialists.

The traditional scholarly reconstruction connects the ninth-century Parsua evidence, the seventh-century Parsumash evidence, and the Anshan title into a single narrative: an Iranian-speaking population, or a ruling group bearing a related name, moving over the course of the eighth and seventh centuries BCE from the Zagros toward the southwestern region that would eventually bear the name Pārsa, or Persis in its later Greek rendering, examined already in Chapter 1's discussion of the terms "Persia" and "Iran." This reconstruction is plausible, draws on genuine linguistic connections between the recorded names, and has been influential in scholarly and popular accounts alike. It is not, however, a documented itinerary, and each link in the chain, from Parsua to Parsumash, and from the historical Kuraš of Parsumash to the genealogical Cyrus I "of Anshan," depends on inference rather than direct, continuous textual attestation.

Alternative explanations, discussed more fully in this chapter's Historical Debate feature, remain worth taking seriously: the name could have spread through political emulation or dynastic claims rather than population movement; two related but historically distinct groups could have independently borne similar names; or a smaller elite or ruling group could have moved and established itself among an already resident population in southwestern Iran without this movement constituting a wholesale migration of an entire people. Whatever the precise mechanism, by the later seventh century BCE, Persian rulers in the Achaemenid genealogical tradition bore the title "king of Anshan," situating the emergence of historically documented Persian political leadership within, rather than apart from, the long-established Elamite political and cultural world of southwestern Iran, a connection the next section examines directly and which will prove essential for understanding the specific character of Achaemenid state formation examined beginning in Chapter 9.

Section 7.9

Persians and the Elamite World of Anshan

The association of Persian rulers with the title "king of Anshan" carries significance well beyond geography, but it also requires a careful distinction that this section introduces explicitly, along with a careful separation of contemporary from retrospective evidence. Anshan, introduced in Chapter 5 as the highland component of Elamite civilization, had a long history as an Elamite political and cultural center, and the archaeological city long identified with Anshan, the site now known as Tal-e Malyan in Fars, was indeed a major Elamite urban center for much of the second and early first millennia BCE. However, the archaeological evidence indicates that Tal-e Malyan itself appears to have been largely abandoned as a major settlement by around 1000 BCE, with no clear evidence of large-scale reoccupation until the Achaemenid period considerably later. This means that when Cyrus II's 539 BCE Cyrus Cylinder retrospectively titles his grandfather Cyrus I "great king, king of Anshan," historians cannot simply assume that this earlier ruler resided at, or governed from, the physical city of Tal-e Malyan as a functioning urban capital. "Anshan" by this period had evidently become, at least in part, a regional and dynastic name and royal title carrying prestige and legitimacy inherited from Elam's long history, rather than necessarily designating an occupied administrative center at the old city site itself. It is worth stressing again that Ashurbanipal's contemporary seventh-century naming of Kuraš as king of Parsumash is a different, and independently attested, piece of evidence from the later, retrospective Anshan title; the connection between the two rests on scholarly identification of the two Cyruses rather than on a single seventh-century source stating both facts together.

This distinction does not weaken the larger point this section makes. Persian political emergence in Fars did not occur on an empty stage awaiting an Iranian-speaking population, but within a region deeply shaped by Elamite administrative practice, religious tradition, and political memory, a legacy that a title such as "king of Anshan" deliberately invoked, whatever the precise physical location of Persian rule at the time.

This context matters directly for how students should understand later Achaemenid history, examined beginning in Chapter 9 and developed further in Chapter 12's treatment of Achaemenid society and administration. Early Persian rulers associated with Anshan operated within, and to some degree inherited, an administrative and cultural environment with deep Elamite roots. Some early Achaemenid rulers are associated in later tradition and in some contemporary evidence with Elamite royal titulature, and Elamite continued to serve as one of the major administrative languages of the Achaemenid Empire long after its establishment, alongside Old Persian and Aramaic, a point examined further in Chapter 12.

This chapter does not attempt to resolve the complex and specialized scholarly questions surrounding the precise nature of the Persian-Elamite relationship in Fars during the late seventh and sixth centuries BCE, questions that depend on difficult and sometimes fragmentary textual and archaeological evidence. What students should retain from this section is the general principle: Persian identity, as it becomes historically visible in Fars, formed in dialogue with, rather than in isolation from, an existing and sophisticated Elamite political and cultural world. This is a direct extension of Chapter 5's insistence that Elam does not simply vanish to make way for a new Iranian order, and it anticipates Chapter 12's fuller treatment of the multilingual, multicultural character of the mature Achaemenid state.

Section 7.10

Beyond Medes and Persians: Other Iranian-Speaking Peoples

A recurring error in popular treatments of early Iranian history treats "Iranian" as effectively synonymous with "Median and Persian," as though these were the only two Iranian-speaking peoples of historical significance. This section briefly broadens the picture, previewing, without narrating in detail, several other Iranian-speaking peoples who become historically visible in this period or somewhat later, chiefly through later Achaemenid-era evidence that preserves a considerably fuller picture of Iranian peoples across an enormous geographic range than the fragmentary ninth- and eighth-century Assyrian sources allow for western Iran alone.

To the northeast, in Central Asia, sources eventually attest Bactrians, associated with the region of Bactria in what is now northern Afghanistan and adjoining territory, and Sogdians, associated with Sogdiana, the region around Samarkand and Bukhara in what is now Uzbekistan. To the east, sources attest peoples associated with Arachosia, in what is now southern Afghanistan and adjoining Pakistan, and with Drangiana, or Zarangiana, corresponding to the historical region of Sistan introduced in Chapter 1. In the northeastern lands later organized as historical Khurasan, also introduced in Chapter 1, Iranian-speaking populations that would eventually give rise to the Parthians, examined much later in this textbook beginning in Chapter 17, are attested by later sources, though their earlier history remains obscure. Achaemenid royal inscriptions and Greek sources also mention peoples such as the Sagartians, whose precise location remains debated among specialists and should not be treated as securely fixed.

This chapter deliberately keeps this survey brief. Each of these peoples has its own, separately documented history that later chapters of this textbook will examine in appropriate chronological and geographic context: Bactria, Sogdiana, and the wider Central Asian Iranian world recur throughout the textbook's treatment of the Achaemenid Empire, the Silk Road, and the medieval Persianate world; the Parthians receive sustained treatment in Volume II. The purpose here is simply to establish, clearly and early, that the Iranian-speaking world of antiquity was considerably larger and more diverse than the Medes and Persians alone, a point that guards against a narrow, anachronistically Persia-centered reading of early Iranian history.

Section 7.11

Scythians, Saka, and the Steppe Connection

This section connects the steppe-focused discussion of Indo-Iranian origins in Chapter 6 to the documented, if still historically complex, world of the first millennium BCE. "Scythian" and "Saka" are broad historical labels, used respectively by Greek and Achaemenid Persian sources, for multiple distinct Iranian-speaking populations inhabiting the Eurasian steppe from the Black Sea region eastward across Central Asia. These labels should not be understood as naming a single, uniform nation or political entity; rather, they designate a wide range of mobile pastoral populations sharing certain linguistic, cultural, and economic characteristics while remaining politically and locally distinct from one another.

Assyrian and Near Eastern sources of the eighth and seventh centuries BCE also mention groups conventionally called Cimmerians, who appear alongside and sometimes in conflict with populations more confidently identified as Iranian speaking. Students should exercise particular caution here: the linguistic identity of the Cimmerians is considerably less secure than that of the Scythians or Saka, and this chapter does not classify the Cimmerians as an Iranian-speaking people without qualification, in keeping with the methodological caution this chapter has emphasized throughout.

The steppe world's Iranian-speaking populations would become considerably more visible to historians in the Achaemenid period, when Persian royal inscriptions and Greek historical writing alike describe interactions, including conflict, diplomacy, and imperial campaigning, between the Achaemenid state and various Saka groups along the empire's northern and eastern frontiers, developments examined in later chapters of this volume. For the period covered by this chapter, the steppe connection should be understood chiefly as a reminder that the Iranian-speaking world extended geographically far beyond the Zagros and the Iranian plateau proper, into Central Asia and the western steppe, even as the plateau itself remained the primary focus of the Assyrian sources this chapter has examined in detail.

Section 7.12

Ethnicity, Language, and Political Identity

This section synthesizes the chapter's recurring methodological concern into an explicit analytical framework. Historians studying the ancient Iranian plateau must keep at least three distinct categories separate: language, meaning the speech community to which an individual or group belonged; ethnicity or self-identification, meaning how a group understood and described its own collective identity, insofar as this can be recovered at all from ancient evidence; and political identity, meaning the polity, ruler, or administrative category under which a group lived or was classified by outside powers such as Assyria.

These three categories did not necessarily align in the ninth, eighth, or seventh centuries BCE, and historians should not assume they did. An individual could speak an Iranian language while living under a non-Iranian ruler, such as an Elamite governor or a Mannean king. A community could participate in a genuinely mixed population, incorporating both Iranian and non-Iranian speakers, without any of its members necessarily experiencing this mixture as a contradiction or crisis of identity. A population could adopt imported material culture, whether Assyrian, Elamite, Urartian, or otherwise, without this adoption implying any change in the language its members spoke or the political loyalties they held. And an entire community could be described by Assyrian scribes using a single geographic or administrative label, such as "Madai" or "Parsua," without that label corresponding precisely to any internally coherent ethnic or political community as its own members might have understood it.

This chapter's synthesis carries a further caution, one that connects directly back to Chapter 1's broader editorial commitments: later imperial categories, particularly the detailed and administratively sophisticated lists of subject peoples that Achaemenid royal inscriptions would eventually present, examined in Chapter 12, should not be projected backward onto the considerably more fragmented and locally organized ninth-century plateau this chapter has examined. The mature Achaemenid imperial system organized an enormous and genuinely diverse population into administrative categories for its own specific purposes, generations after the events this chapter describes. Reading those later categories backward risks imposing a false clarity and coherence onto a period whose defining historical characteristic, so far as the evidence allows historians to judge, was precisely its fragmentation, its linguistic mixture, and its resistance to simple ethnic classification.

Section 7.13

From Peoples to Politics

By the eighth and seventh centuries BCE, the fragmented world of local Median rulers, scattered Persian-connected communities, and neighboring non-Iranian polities that this chapter has surveyed increasingly found itself drawn into the orbit of Assyrian imperial ambition. Repeated Assyrian campaigns, tribute demands, and, at times, direct military confrontation progressively integrated western Iranian communities into a wider system of imperial warfare, alliance, and resistance that would, over the following two centuries, transform the political landscape of the plateau.

Assyrian sources continue to describe multiple, independent Median chiefs and local rulers rather than a single centralized Median king as late as approximately 672 BCE, confirming that political fragmentation among Median communities persisted well into the seventh century BCE. These Assyrian sources do not, however, by themselves establish when or how a broader Median polity subsequently emerged. By the closing decades of the seventh century, Babylonian evidence attests Cyaxares and Median forces as major political actors in the events that would bring down the Assyrian Empire; the nature, extent, and internal organization of the resulting Median power remain genuinely debated among historians and are examined directly in Chapter 8. Persian rulers bearing the title "king of Anshan," similarly, remain, at this stage, a regional power operating within an Elamite-influenced political environment rather than an independent imperial force.

This chapter has deliberately stopped short of narrating how these fragmented communities became the centralized political powers that would eventually contest, ally with, and ultimately outlast the Assyrian Empire itself. That narrative, encompassing the complex relationship between Media, Assyria, and Babylonia, and the political consolidation of Median power under the pressure of imperial warfare and diplomacy, is the specific task of Chapter 8. The question this chapter has asked, "who were these peoples?", now gives way to a different question: how did political power among the Medes and their western Iranian neighbors change under the pressure of Assyrian and Babylonian imperial politics? Readers equipped with this chapter's caution about names, evidence, and identity are now prepared to follow that political narrative without mistaking Assyrian administrative convenience, later imperial categories, or attractive migration stories for settled historical fact.

Perspective: The text represents the perspective of the Assyrian royal court and its scribal establishment, concerned with celebrating royal military conquest and the king's imperial reach; it says nothing directly about how the inhabitants or rulers of the land Parsua understood their own community or their relationship to Assyria.

Historical Debate

Were Parsua, Parsumash, and the Later Persians the Same Migrating Population?

The question: Does the appearance of related names across two centuries, *Parsua* in the Zagros in 843 BCE, *Parsumash* linked to a king called Kuraš in the mid-to-late seventh century, and the Achaemenid genealogical title "king of Anshan" applied to a Cyrus I, document a single population and a single ruling line that migrated across the plateau, or does it reflect a more complex process involving related but distinct groups, an uncertain identification between two men named Cyrus, or independent use of similar names?

Interpretation A — A continuous population and dynasty: Many historians accept the identification of Ashurbanipal's Kuraš, king of Parsumash, with the Cyrus I later titled "king of Anshan" in Achaemenid genealogical tradition, and treat this as part of a continuous, if incompletely documented, history connecting the ninth-century Parsua evidence to the historical Achaemenid line that would produce Cyrus the Great. On this view, related names across the centuries reflect one lineage and one broadly continuous population moving and consolidating power in the direction of Fars.
Interpretation B — Distinct or uncertain links: Other historians note that Sennacherib's 691 BCE inscriptions list Parsumash and Anshan as separate names, that the identification of Ashurbanipal's Kuraš with the genealogical Cyrus I rests on inference from name and approximate period rather than an explicit ancient statement, and that names could spread through political emulation, dynastic claims, or coincidence rather than continuous population movement or a single unbroken dynasty. On this view, each link in the traditional chain, from Parsua to Parsumash, and from Parsumash to Anshan, requires independent scrutiny rather than being assumed from name resemblance alone.

Evidence: Both interpretations rely on the same limited body of evidence: the Assyrian attestations of Parsua and Parsumash, the separate listing of Parsumash and Anzan/Anshan in 691 BCE, Ashurbanipal's naming of Kuraš of Parsumash, and the later Achaemenid genealogical title "king of Anshan." The interpretations differ in how much inferential weight they are willing to place on name and period resemblance, absent an ancient source that states the connections outright.

Why it matters: The choice between these interpretations affects how historians describe the origins of the Achaemenid dynasty and the broader question of Persian identity in Fars, examined beginning in Chapter 9. It also serves as a broader case study in the limits of onomastic evidence, meaning evidence based on names alone, for reconstructing ancient population movements and royal genealogies.

Student task: Based on the evidence and reasoning presented above, evaluate both interpretations and explain what kind of additional evidence, if it existed, would most strengthen or weaken each position.

Multiple Perspectives

Living Among Named and Unnamed Peoples

From the perspective of an Assyrian scribe recording tribute: The task at hand is to record which lands submitted, what tribute they provided, and which rulers can be relied upon or must be watched; the finer distinctions of language and self-identification among the peoples of the Zagros matter far less than their political and material relationship to Assyrian power.

From the perspective of a Mannean or Elamite official observing Iranian-speaking newcomers or neighbors: Established political and administrative traditions, and existing relationships with Assyria and other powers, continue to structure daily governance, regardless of the linguistic background of neighboring or resident communities whose presence is neither entirely new nor cause for alarm.
From the perspective of a modern historian working with Assyrian inscriptions: Each name, each tribute list, and each campaign narrative must be weighed carefully against its own limitations as evidence, resisting the temptation to fill gaps in the record with confident migration stories or clean ethnic maps that the sources themselves do not support.

Cause and Effect

Why Assyria Made Iranian Peoples Visible

Long-term causes

The prior Iranian-speaking migrations onto the plateau, examined in Chapter 6, created the demographic and linguistic foundation that later Assyrian scribes would eventually encounter and record.

Short-term causes

The specific expansion of Neo-Assyrian military campaigns into the Zagros and western Iranian highlands from the ninth century BCE, driven by Assyrian interests in tribute, resources, and control of strategic routes.

Effect

The recording, in Assyrian royal inscriptions, of specific ethnonyms and place names, including *Parsua* and *Madai*, that historians can connect, with appropriate caution, to the later Persians and Medes.

This chapter's response

Careful, source-critical treatment of these names that neither dismisses their value as the earliest historical attestation of Iranian-speaking peoples nor treats them as settled proof of fixed ethnicity, territory, or political unity.

Historical complexity: Assyrian visibility was not a neutral process of discovery; it reflected specifically Assyrian military and administrative interests, meaning that the very existence and content of the evidence historians rely upon was shaped by an outside power's priorities rather than by any effort on the part of Median or Persian communities to record their own history for posterity.

Everyday Life and Social History

Life Beneath the Named Rulers

The Assyrian inscriptions this chapter has examined name rulers, lands, and tribute, but they say almost nothing directly about the daily lives of the pastoralists, farmers, and artisans who made up the great majority of the populations described by names such as Parsua and Madai. Historians must infer this social history indirectly, from archaeological evidence at sites such as Nush-i Jan, Godin Tepe, and Baba Jan, from later Iranian textual traditions that may preserve, in altered form, older social patterns, and from careful comparison with better-documented pastoral and agricultural societies elsewhere in the ancient Near East.

Archaeological evidence and Assyrian tribute records indicate combinations of agriculture and animal husbandry in many communities of western Iran during this period, shaped by the same mountain and valley geography examined in Chapter 1. In some upland zones, seasonal movement of herds between summer and winter pasture was probably important, though the fragmentary and poorly bounded nature of the Assyrian evidence does not permit a uniform reconstruction of everyday economic life across all territories labeled Parsua or Madai, and historians should resist assuming that a single economic pattern applied evenly across so large and varied a region. Local rulers, the same figures named individually in Assyrian tribute lists, likely depended on some combination of this agricultural and pastoral base for the tribute, in animals and goods, that Assyrian sources describe them providing, whether willingly or under military pressure. Because the surviving written sources were produced by an external, elite, and administratively focused Assyrian scribal establishment, historians must read them with particular care for indirect evidence about the ordinary populations who left no records of their own, a caution that will recur throughout this textbook's treatment of ancient and medieval societies alike.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the transition from the reconstructed, unnamed Iranian-speaking communities examined in Chapter 6 to the first historically visible, named peoples of the Iranian plateau: the Medes and Persians, alongside their many neighbors. It began by establishing a central methodological caution that governs the entire chapter: a name recorded by outside observers, however valuable, does not by itself establish a stable ethnicity, a fixed territory, an identifiable archaeological culture, or a coherent political community.

The chapter examined the character of Neo-Assyrian royal inscriptions as historical evidence, explaining that these texts, composed to commemorate conquest and record tribute, provide relatively reliable chronology but often only approximate geography, and reflect Assyrian administrative and military priorities rather than the self-understanding of the peoples they describe. It then established that the ninth- and eighth-century Iranian plateau remained a genuinely mixed landscape, in which Elamites, Manneans, Urartians, and the peoples of Ellipi continued to play significant roles alongside emerging Iranian-speaking communities.

Two specific cases received sustained attention. The name *Parsua* or *Parsuwash*, a land-name attested in the Zagros region in 843 BCE, and the later, separately attested name *Parsumash*, connected through a debated identification of Ashurbanipal's Kuraš of Parsumash with the Cyrus I later titled "king of Anshan" in Achaemenid genealogical tradition, present what this chapter called the first Persian problem: genuine evidence of related names appearing at different times and places, connected by a plausible but ultimately unproven chain of inference that this chapter's Historical Debate feature examined without declaring a definitive resolution. The name *Madai*, attested from the 830s BCE, described not a unified Median kingdom but numerous local rulers scattered across western and central Iran, a fragmentation still attested as late as approximately 672 BCE.

The chapter distinguished carefully among Medes as a people, Median as a reconstructed language surviving no independent textual corpus, and Media as a shifting geographic and political category, warning against collapsing these three senses together. It examined the limits of archaeological evidence from sites such as Nush-i Jan, Godin Tepe, and Baba Jan for identifying "Median" material culture, concluding that such sites illuminate the archaeological context of the Median period without automatically confirming a specific ethnic or political attribution.

The chapter then followed the Persian-name problem southeastward, examining the separate seventh-century attestations of Parsumash and Anzan/Anshan, the debated identification connecting Ashurbanipal's Kuraš of Parsumash to the Achaemenid genealogical "king of Anshan," and the important distinction between Anshan as a regional and dynastic title and Tal-e Malyan as an archaeological city apparently abandoned around 1000 BCE. It explained why the "king of Anshan" title nonetheless situates early Persian political emergence within, rather than apart from, the long-established Elamite legacy introduced in Chapter 5. It broadened the picture further by briefly surveying other Iranian-speaking peoples, including Bactrians, Sogdians, and the peoples later organized as Parthians, Arachosians, and Drangians, as well as the steppe world of Scythians and Saka, cautioning specifically against classifying the Cimmerians as securely Iranian speaking.

A synthesizing section distinguished language, ethnicity, and political identity as separate analytical categories, warning against projecting the far more centralized and administratively sophisticated categories of the later Achaemenid Empire, examined in Chapter 12, backward onto the fragmented ninth-century plateau this chapter has described. The chapter closed by establishing the transition to Chapter 8: the fragmented communities of local Median rulers and Anshan-connected Persian rulers this chapter has surveyed increasingly found themselves drawn into the orbit of Assyrian imperial politics, setting the stage for the political history of Media, Assyria, and Babylonia that follows.

Throughout, this chapter has extended Chapter 6's central methodological lesson: if archaeology alone cannot identify a language, a recorded name alone cannot identify a stable ethnicity, territory, or political community. Learning to hold this caution alongside genuine appreciation for the value of the Assyrian evidence is the chapter's central intellectual achievement, and it prepares students to read the political narrative of the chapters that follow with appropriate historical care.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Ethnonym
A name used to designate a people or ethnic group, whether applied by the group itself (an autonym) or by outsiders (an exonym); distinct from a land name or ruler name, though Assyrian sources do not always keep these categories clearly separate.
Neo-Assyrian annals
Royal inscriptions composed for Assyrian kings to commemorate military campaigns and record tribute, providing relatively reliable chronology but often only approximate geography.
Parsua / Parsuwash
An Assyrian land-name attested from 843 BCE, associated with a region in the Zagros often localized south or southwest of Lake Urmia, though the precise geography remains debated; cautiously connected by many historians to the later Persian name.
Parsumash
A land/ruler name attested in seventh-century Assyrian sources, notably as the title of a king called Kuraš (Cyrus) under Ashurbanipal; listed separately from Anzan/Anshan in Sennacherib's 691 BCE inscriptions, but connected to the Anshan title through a debated later identification.
Madai / Matai
The Assyrian name connected with the Medes, attested from the 830s BCE, initially describing numerous local rulers rather than a unified kingdom.
Mannea
A kingdom centered around Lake Urmia, a significant regional power not securely identified as Iranian speaking.
Urartu
A kingdom centered around Lake Van in eastern Anatolia, contesting influence over the northwestern Zagros with Assyria and Mannea.
Ellipi
A polity in the central Zagros attested in Assyrian sources, whose linguistic affiliation remains uncertain.
Anshan / Tal-e Malyan
"Anshan" is a regional and dynastic name, later a royal title, associated with early historical Persian rulers; Tal-e Malyan is the archaeological Elamite city of that name, apparently abandoned by around 1000 BCE, well before the period of the Persian title's attestation.
Median language
A reconstructed Northwestern Iranian language for which no independent textual corpus survives; known chiefly through names, loanwords, and traces in Old Persian.
Nush-i Jan
An Iron Age site in western Iran often discussed in relation to the Median period, though its precise ethnic attribution remains debated.
Godin Tepe
A Zagros site with a long occupation sequence, including Iron Age levels relevant to the archaeology of the Median period.
Baba Jan
An Iron Age Zagros site providing evidence for regional settlement and material culture in this period.
Median art
A scholarly category describing a supposed coherent stylistic tradition attributable to Median populations, notoriously difficult to define precisely given the absence of confirming texts.
Saka
A broad label, used in Achaemenid Persian sources, for multiple Iranian-speaking steppe populations, not the name of a single uniform nation.
Scythians
A broad Greek-language label for Iranian-speaking steppe populations, overlapping with but not identical to the Persian term Saka.
Cimmerians
A population mentioned in Near Eastern sources alongside Iranian-speaking groups, whose own linguistic identity is considerably less secure and should not be assumed Iranian.
Bactria
A historical region in what is now northern Afghanistan and adjoining territory, home to Iranian-speaking Bactrians.
Sogdiana
A historical region around Samarkand and Bukhara in Central Asia, home to Iranian-speaking Sogdians.
Arachosia
A historical region in what is now southern Afghanistan and adjoining Pakistan, associated with an Iranian-speaking population.
Drangiana / Zarangiana
The historical region corresponding to Sistan, associated with an Iranian-speaking population.
Sagartians
A people attested in Achaemenid royal inscriptions and Greek sources whose precise location remains debated among specialists.
Onomastic evidence
Evidence based on names, such as personal names, place names, and ethnonyms, used cautiously by historians to reconstruct population history and linguistic distribution.
Autonym / Exonym
An autonym is a name a group uses for itself; an exonym is a name applied to a group by outsiders. Most Assyrian-recorded ethnonyms discussed in this chapter are likely exonyms.
Political fragmentation
The condition, attested for the Medes in the ninth through seventh centuries BCE, of multiple independent local rulers rather than a single centralized political authority.
Elamite legacy
The Elamite political, administrative, linguistic, and cultural traditions present in southwestern Iran before and during the emergence of historically visible Persian rulers there, invoked in part through the later royal title "king of Anshan."

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has occupied an unusually delicate position in the textbook's larger narrative. It stands at the threshold between the reconstructed, unnamed communities of Chapter 6 and the political history of Media, Assyria, and Babylonia that Chapter 8 will narrate in full, and it has deliberately resisted the temptation to rush across that threshold before establishing the evidentiary caution the subject demands. The Medes and Persians who will dominate so much of this textbook's remaining volumes first enter the historical record not as fully formed nations with clear borders and secure identities, but as names in Assyrian tribute lists, attached to scattered rulers and uncertain territories.

This caution matters for reasons that extend well beyond the ninth century BCE. The habits of mind this chapter has cultivated, distinguishing a name from a stable identity, distinguishing archaeological evidence from ethnic or linguistic proof, and resisting the pull of a tidy migration story when the evidence supports only a more fragmented and uncertain picture, will recur throughout this textbook whenever the evidence for a period or a people is genuinely difficult. Historians of any period, not only ancient Iran, must learn to ask what a source can establish and what it cannot, and this chapter has aimed to model that discipline concretely rather than merely asserting it as an abstract principle.

At the same time, this chapter has not treated uncertainty as an excuse for silence. The Medes and Persians, however imperfectly documented in this early period, were real historical communities whose eventual political consolidation would reshape the ancient Near East, and the chapter has traced what can responsibly be said about their earliest visibility: their fragmentation, their coexistence with Elamites, Manneans, Urartians, and others, and the specific, genuinely debated evidence connecting early Zagros attestations to the later Persians of Fars.

Students who carry forward this chapter's central lesson, that a named people is not the same thing as a fully known people, are well prepared for Chapter 8's political narrative, in which the fragmented Median rulers surveyed here begin to consolidate power under the pressure of Assyrian and Babylonian imperial politics, and for Chapter 9's account of how one Persian kingdom, emerging from the Elamite-influenced world of Anshan, would eventually build an empire stretching from the Aegean to the Indus. That story, like this chapter's, deserves to be told with the same careful attention to evidence, uncertainty, and historical complexity that this chapter has modeled throughout.
