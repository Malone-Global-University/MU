Volume I, Part III - The Achaemenid Empire
10

Cambyses II, Darius I, and the Construction of an Imperial State

Chapter Opening

An empire built by one man's victories had to survive his death, an expedition to the Nile, and a dispute over who was truly king.

Between 530 and 521 BCE the Persian Empire passed through its most dangerous test. Cyrus's death left a functioning multinational empire that had never yet undergone a royal succession. Cambyses II added Egypt, yet his reign is known largely through hostile Greek tradition that Egyptian sources challenge.

In 522 BCE, a succession crisis produced two rival accounts of a single event, and the principal surviving narrative was written by the man who won. Darius's Behistun inscription is at once a precious historical text and a political argument. This chapter follows the events but also asks how historians know them.

The chapter draws on inscriptions, Babylonian and Egyptian documents, and Greek historians, and it treats their limits as part of the story. It is the bridge from the founding empire of Chapter 9 to the administrative machinery of Chapter 11.

Section 10.1

From Founder to Successor: The Problem of Imperial Succession

Chapter 9 ended with an empire that already functioned: Cyrus had joined Media, Lydia, Babylonia and other lands under one king, and had left governors, scribes, armies and local elites in place. What he had not tested was succession. Cyrus had inherited the kingship of Anshan/Persia from his father Cambyses I, but the multinational empire he left in 530 BCE had been assembled largely through his own conquests. No Persian king had yet inherited that entire imperial realm from a predecessor. This chapter asks whether the empire could outlive its founder. Cambyses did not inherit an administrative vacuum; he inherited working arrangements, and the question was whether they would hold under a new king.

Section 10.2

Cambyses II and the Empire Cyrus Left

Cambyses II (r. 530-522 BCE), Cyrus's son, is securely attested as king in contemporary Babylonian documents, and the arrangements of his father continued. He had a brother, Bardiya, whose fate becomes the chapter's central problem. Much of the vivid literary portrait of Cambyses, including stories of cruelty, sacrilege, and madness, comes from later Greek authors, especially Herodotus. No surviving evidence demonstrates that these authors had direct access to Persian royal archives, so their narratives must be checked against contemporary Babylonian and Egyptian evidence. This chapter therefore separates what contemporary evidence attests from what later tradition added. Egypt was the largest major independent Near Eastern kingdom still outside Persian rule and became the principal target of Cambyses's expansion.

Section 10.3

Why Egypt?

Egypt, ruled by the Twenty-Sixth (Saite) Dynasty, was wealthy, agriculturally productive, and tied to the eastern Mediterranean and Red Sea worlds. It was among the few great powers still outside Persian control. Control of Egypt could provide enormous agricultural and fiscal resources, strengthen Persian power in the eastern Mediterranean, and open routes toward Libya and Nubia. These are reconstructed strategic advantages, not a documented statement of Cambyses's personal intentions. Greek tradition offers personal explanations, but historians should not assume that Egypt was simply Cyrus's unfinished plan; strategic logic explains the attack without requiring an inherited blueprint.

Section 10.4

525 BCE: Pelusium, Memphis, and the Conquest of Egypt

In 525 BCE Cambyses invaded. Persian forces defeated the Egyptian army near Pelusium, on the eastern edge of the Nile delta, then advanced to Memphis and took control of the country; the last Saite king, Psamtik III, was removed. Herodotus supplies much of the colorful detail of the campaign, including stories of desert crossing and treachery, which cannot be verified independently. What is secure is the outcome: by 525 Egypt was a Persian-ruled land, and Cambyses took Egyptian royal titles.

Section 10.5

Cambyses as Pharaoh: Conqueror, Sacrilegious Tyrant, or Egyptian King?

Greek writers portray Cambyses as mocking Egyptian religion, desecrating temples and killing the sacred Apis bull. Contemporary Egyptian evidence complicates this. Cambyses adopted Egyptian royal forms and participated in cultic practice, while an Apis bull received an official burial in a sarcophagus under his authority. This evidence complicates the broader Greek portrait of Cambyses as uniformly hostile to Egyptian religion, although the Apis burial alone cannot establish whether the separate story that he wounded another Apis bull is historical. This does not make him a tolerant hero: Persian occupation could be coercive, and Egyptian memory of the reign was not uniformly favorable. A much later Egyptian source, the Demotic Chronicle of the early second century BCE, claims that Cambyses sharply reduced revenues previously assigned to most temples. Because that text emerged from a later anti-Persian priestly environment, the claim is relevant to Egyptian memory of Cambyses but must be distinguished from contemporary sixth-century evidence. The method lesson is that a hostile literary tradition is not automatically false, but must be checked against contemporary evidence produced by the people concerned.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: The Lie (drauga)

Definition: Old Persian drauga means "lie" or "falsehood." Darius gives the term strong political significance by using it to characterize rival claims to kingship and the rebellions against his rule.

Historical context: Behistun repeatedly attributes rebellion and rival kingship to "the Lie."

Why it matters: Darius converts political opposition into a moral and ideological category.

Potential misunderstanding: Drauga does not literally mean "rebellion," and historians should not adopt Darius's characterization of his opponents as a neutral description.

Section 10.6

Beyond Egypt: Libya, Nubia, and the Limits of Expansion

Ancient tradition ties Cambyses to further ambitions toward Nubia (Kush), the Libyan desert oasis of Ammon, and Carthage. Evidence varies greatly. Persian influence south of Egypt is plausible and some movement into Nubia is reported, while dramatic tales of armies lost in the desert come mainly through Herodotus. Not all expeditions were equally successful or equally documented, and this section should be read as a lesson in the limits of evidence rather than as a campaign narrative.

Section 10.7

Bardiya, Gaumata, and the Crisis of 522 BCE

The story now shifts from expansion to succession. According to Darius's later Behistun inscription, Cambyses had secretly killed his brother Bardiya; while Cambyses was away, a Magus named Gaumata impersonated Bardiya and seized royal authority. Babylonian documents show that for several months a ruler was officially recognized under the name Bardiya (Barziya). They cannot tell us whether that king was Cyrus's son or the impostor Darius describes. It is therefore accurate to say that Darius later claimed that Gaumata impersonated Bardiya, and inaccurate to state it as established fact. Likewise the murder of Bardiya by Cambyses is Darius's claim.

Section 10.8

Cambyses Dies: What Actually Happened?

Cambyses died in 522 BCE while returning from Egypt. Darius says he died "by his own death," a phrase whose meaning is disputed. Herodotus tells of an accidental self-inflicted wound, and other traditions differ; none should be taken as established, and no mental diagnosis of Cambyses is warranted. The political consequence is clear: Cambyses died without regaining the throne from whoever ruled as Bardiya.

Section 10.9

Darius Takes the Throne

Darius, son of Hystaspes, was neither Cyrus's son nor Cambyses's brother. By his own account he and six Persian nobles killed Gaumata on 29 September 522 BCE, after which Darius became king. Because he lacked direct descent, his accession needed a far more elaborate argument for legitimacy than Cyrus or Cambyses had needed. Darius claimed descent from Achaemenes through a collateral Persian royal line, but that genealogy is itself part of the legitimating argument presented in Behistun and related inscriptions, not independent proof of his right to rule. Historians should call him neither simply a legitimate successor nor simply a usurper: those labels depend on the disputed events of 522.

Section 10.10

Behistun: Darius Tells the Story of His Own Accession

The Behistun inscription, carved on a cliff in western Iran, is the flagship source for this crisis. Written in Old Persian, Elamite and Babylonian, it narrates Darius's genealogy, the alleged Gaumata imposture, his seizure of power, the rebellions, and his defeat of rival kings. It is the only Achaemenid royal inscription with such an extended historical narrative, and it is both history and self-justification. See the Primary Source Window below for analysis.

Primary Source Window

Primary Source Window: Darius's Behistun Inscription

Context: Commissioned by Darius after 522-521 BCE; trilingual royal inscription. Source type: Royal monumental inscription.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): Darius traces his royal family, says Cambyses secretly killed his brother Bardiya, says the Magus Gaumata impersonated Bardiya and seized the kingdom, describes killing Gaumata with the help of Ahura Mazda, and presents later opponents as liars.

Analysis: It shows the arguments Darius used to defend his kingship: genealogy, divine support, and the labeling of enemies.

Limitations: It is contemporary but not independent; it cannot by itself show whether Gaumata existed as described.

Perspective: The victor; the losing side is absent.

Section 10.11

Revolt across the Empire, 522-521 BCE

Darius's accession did not end the crisis. Revolts broke out in regions including Babylonia, Media, Elam, Persia, Margiana and Armenia. Several rebels claimed royal identities: pretenders in Babylon claimed descent from Nabonidus, and in Persia Vahyazdata also claimed to be Bardiya. Behistun labels opponents followers of "the Lie," Darius's ideological category rather than an objective description. The revolts had varied causes, leaders and claims and should not be reduced to national independence movements. Darius did not simply inherit an empire and begin reform; he first had to reconquer much of it politically and militarily.

Everyday Life and Social History

Repeated mobilization probably burdened soldiers, farmers, and temples in rebel regions, and local governors had to choose sides; specific experiences are poorly documented and should not be overstated. A much later Demotic tradition claims that Cambyses reduced the revenues of many Egyptian temples; contemporary evidence documents continuity as well as disruption, so the scale and immediate effects should not be overstated.

Section 10.12

Was Gaumata Really an Impostor?

See the Historical Debate below. The evidence does not permit certainty, and this chapter does not choose a winner.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Gaumata or Bardiya: Who Did Darius Kill?

The question: Was the man Darius killed an impostor?

Interpretation A: Darius broadly tells the truth: Cambyses killed Bardiya secretly, Gaumata impersonated him, and Darius restored legitimate rule.
Interpretation B: The man killed may have been the real Bardiya, and the impostor story was built to legitimize Darius.

Evidence: Behistun, Babylonian documents recognizing a king Bardiya, and Herodotus.

Points of disagreement: Interpretation of interested sources.

Why it matters: A detailed royal inscription is still an interested source.

Student task: What new evidence could settle it?

Historical Method

Can a King Be the Historian of His Own Coup?

Behistun is contemporary with the accession but not independent of Darius's interests. Babylonian tablets are contemporary and administrative but silent on identity. Herodotus is later and distant. Students should distinguish contemporary evidence from independent evidence.

Section 10.13

From Crisis to Imperial State

Darius inherited governing structures from Cyrus and Cambyses and did not invent the satrapal principle; those kings had already adapted structures from earlier kingdoms. After the revolts he increasingly regularized and documented imperial government, and around 519 BCE major fiscal reorganization and more systematic tribute assessment are associated with his reign. The detailed machinery belongs to Chapter 11. Cyrus built the empire; Cambyses expanded it and tested its survival of succession; Darius survived a legitimacy crisis and restored political control. After political control had been restored, Darius could pursue further standardization, regularization, and documentation of the administrative structures already inherited from Cyrus, Cambyses, and predecessor kingdoms. The detailed machinery of that system is the subject of Chapter 11, Satrapies, Roads, Royal Power, and Achaemenid Administration.

Multiple Perspectives

Multiple Perspectives: The Crisis of 522 BCE

Darius and the six nobles: Restorers of order against falsehood, in their own account.
Babylonian scribes and temples: Recorded kings by regnal name; documents show recognition of Bardiya.
Egyptian priests: Under Persian rule; their evidence complicates Greek hostility to Cambyses.
Later Greek authors: Distant, and shaped by their own concerns.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change Over Time: Cyrus, Cambyses, Darius

Continuity: inherited provincial structures, local elites and scribes, multinational kingship. Change: Egypt incorporated; Cyrus's immediate royal line ceased to occupy the throne, while Darius asserted a collateral Achaemenid genealogy and constructed a new explicit argument for dynastic legitimacy; Darius articulates explicit genealogy and legitimacy; government becomes more regularized. Change was abrupt in 522 and gradual afterward; court and provincial elites experienced the political rupture most directly; evidence for how rapidly these changes affected rural communities is much thinner.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: The Survival of the Empire

Long-term causes

Rapid imperial expansion; a succession system not yet tested at the scale of Cyrus's empire; diverse regional centers and elite networks whose relationships to the new dynasty varied.

Short-term causes

Succession uncertainty while Cambyses was absent; the appearance of a ruler claiming to be Bardiya; Cambyses's death; and Darius's subsequently contested accession.

Effect

Revolts, then reconquest and consolidation under Darius.

Complexity

No single cause; rebel motives varied.

Chapter Summary

Chapter 10 shows how the empire Cyrus built survived his death. Cambyses expanded it into Egypt in 525 BCE, while later Greek portraits of him must be tested against Egyptian evidence. In 522 a succession crisis, known mainly through Darius's Behistun inscription, threatened the empire. Darius took the throne, faced widespread revolt, and reconquered much of the realm. He then began regularizing government, the subject of Chapter 11.
