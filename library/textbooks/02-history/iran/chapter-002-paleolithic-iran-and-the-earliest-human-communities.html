Paleolithic Iran and the Earliest Human Communities

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

Long before there was an Iran, a Persia, or even a settled village on the plateau, small bands of humans and earlier hominin populations moved through the same mountains, valleys, and river corridors that later shaped the political history examined throughout this textbook. Their story is recovered not from royal inscriptions or chronicles but from stone tools, cave sediments, animal bones, and the patient, comparative work of archaeologists.

High in the limestone folds of the central Zagros Mountains, a scatter of caves and rock shelters, places now known to archaeologists by names such as Kobeh, Warwasi, Bisitun, Wezmeh, and Yafteh, has preserved thin, dark bands of ash, broken bone, and worked stone that record tens of thousands of years of human presence. None of these places was a city, a shrine, or a palace in any sense a later chapter of this textbook would recognize, and not all of them represent the same kind of deposit. Some, including Warwasi and Yafteh, preserve clear evidence of repeated occupation, temporary camps, hunting stations, and shelters used across many episodes of a long, seasonal round of movement across the highlands and the lowlands beyond them. Others, including Wezmeh, preserve a very different kind of record, a carnivore-dominated paleontological deposit within which a single, isolated human tooth was found, evidence of a human presence at or near the cave rather than proof of sustained residential use. Reconstructing what happened in and around these shelters requires a different kind of historical method than the one this textbook will use once written sources become available, and it requires distinguishing carefully among different kinds of sites rather than treating every cave as though it documented the same kind of human activity. There are no names, no dates recorded by the people themselves, and no accounts of decisions, rivalries, or beliefs. What survives is material: flaked stone, burned earth, the bones of wild goat and other animals, and, in a small number of cases, human remains themselves.

This chapter asks what can honestly be said about the earliest chapters of human presence on the Iranian plateau, a landscape whose geography, mountains, deserts, and scarce water, Chapter 1 has already described in detail. It examines a very long span of time, conventionally called the Paleolithic, extending from the first appearance of hominin populations in the region, evidence that reaches deep into the Pleistocene and probably spans hundreds of thousands of years, although the chronology of Iran's earliest occupation remains poorly constrained, through the emergence and spread of anatomically modern humans, and finally to the last hunting and gathering societies whose ways of life the agricultural transition described in Chapter 3 would eventually transform.

Readers should approach this chapter with different expectations than those Chapter 1 addressed. There is no political history to narrate here, no dynasty to trace, and no state to describe. The central historical problem of this chapter is instead evidentiary: how do archaeologists know what they claim to know about people who left no writing, and what, precisely, can and cannot be concluded from stone tools, cave deposits, animal bones, and the handful of human skeletal remains recovered from Iranian Paleolithic sites? Much of what once seemed settled in the study of Iran's earliest human history has been revised by new excavations, new dating techniques, and new genetic evidence over the past several decades, and specialists continue to debate basic questions, including when hominins first reached the plateau, how Neanderthal and modern human populations related to one another in the region, and how quickly new stone tool traditions actually spread.

This chapter also asks students to hold two ideas together that can feel, at first, to be in tension. On one hand, the Iranian plateau examined in this chapter was inhabited by human and hominin communities for a span of time vastly longer than the entire political history the remaining one hundred and six chapters of this textbook will cover; the events of Chapters 9 through 108 together span roughly two and a half millennia, while this chapter's subject probably spans several hundred thousand years, with the earliest, poorly dated evidence potentially extending considerably further back. On the other hand, the evidentiary record for this immense span of time is extremely thin compared to what later chapters can draw upon, consisting of a limited number of excavated sites, a small number of human remains, and stone tool assemblages that must be interpreted with considerable caution. Historians and archaeologists must therefore resist two temptations at once: the temptation to treat sparse evidence as though it revealed a complete picture, and the temptation to dismiss the deep past as unknowable simply because it lacks the documentary richness of later periods.

Finally, this chapter establishes vocabulary and interpretive habits that later chapters addressing prehistory, Chapter 3 on the Neolithic transition, Chapter 4 on the rise of complex societies, and Chapter 5 on Elam, will depend upon. Students who understand how archaeologists date sites, distinguish stone tool traditions, and interpret faunal and environmental evidence in this chapter will be far better equipped to evaluate the claims those later chapters make about the origins of agriculture, urbanism, and the first complex societies of the plateau.

The story that follows is therefore a story assembled piece by piece from an incomplete archive written in stone and sediment rather than ink. It begins, as far as the current evidence allows historians to say, with the very first traces of hominin presence on the Iranian plateau.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Define the Paleolithic period and distinguish its Lower, Middle, and Upper subdivisions as archaeologists use them.
  2. Explain what kinds of evidence, stone tools, cave stratigraphy, faunal remains, and skeletal material, allow archaeologists to reconstruct Paleolithic life on the Iranian plateau.
  3. Describe the current, actively debated evidence for the earliest hominin presence on the plateau and explain why claims about this period remain provisional.
  4. Identify major Zagros cave and rock shelter sites associated with Middle and Upper Paleolithic occupation, including their approximate chronology and significance.
  5. Analyze the evidence bearing on the presence of Neanderthal populations in Iran and on their relationship to early modern human populations.
  6. Explain the significance of the Upper Paleolithic transition and the evidence associated with early modern human presence at sites such as Yafteh Cave.
  7. Describe the technological changes across Lower, Middle, and Upper Paleolithic stone tool traditions and relate these changes cautiously to broader behavioral questions.
  8. Assess how Pleistocene climate and environmental change shaped settlement, mobility, and resource use on the plateau.
  9. Evaluate what can and cannot be concluded about the social organization of Paleolithic communities from the available archaeological evidence.
  10. Explain regional variation in Paleolithic evidence across different parts of the plateau, including the Zagros, the Caspian region, and the central and eastern plateau.
  11. Trace changing Epipaleolithic settlement, mobility, and resource-use patterns and explain how they formed part of the background to the Neolithic developments examined in Chapter 3.
  12. Apply careful, source-critical historical reasoning when evaluating claims about prehistoric peoples, avoiding both overconfidence and unwarranted skepticism.

Historical Context

Chapter 1 established the physical geography of the Iranian plateau: its mountain systems, desert basins, scarce water, and connective corridors linking it to Mesopotamia, the Caucasus, Central Asia, South Asia, and the Persian Gulf. This chapter examines a span of hominin occupation extending deep into the Pleistocene and probably across hundreds of thousands of years, although the chronology of Iran's earliest occupation remains poorly constrained, long before any of the political, religious, or linguistic categories examined in later chapters had come into existence.

The broader region surrounding the Iranian plateau, sometimes referred to by archaeologists as part of Southwest Asia or the wider Middle Eastern corridor, has produced some of the most significant Paleolithic evidence anywhere in Eurasia, including well known sites in the Levant, Iraq, and the Caucasus. Iran's own Paleolithic record, concentrated heavily though not exclusively in the folded limestone terrain of the Zagros Mountains, forms an important but still incompletely explored part of this wider picture. Compared to some neighboring regions, systematic Paleolithic archaeology in Iran developed later and has, for practical and political reasons at various periods, proceeded more slowly, meaning that the current picture remains provisional and subject to revision as new fieldwork continues.

Understanding Paleolithic Iran requires two kinds of context established more fully in Chapter 1: geography and climate. The Zagros Mountains, with their caves, rock shelters, and mixed woodland-steppe environments, have produced the overwhelming majority of documented Iranian Paleolithic sites, in part because limestone cave environments preserve archaeological deposits unusually well and in part because decades of fieldwork have concentrated there. This does not mean that other regions of the plateau, the Caspian littoral, the central plateau, or the northeast, were unoccupied; it means the evidentiary record from those regions remains comparatively thin, a point this chapter returns to in its discussion of regional variation.

Climate context matters equally. The period this chapter examines falls almost entirely within the Pleistocene epoch, a span of repeated glacial and interglacial cycles that altered temperature, rainfall, vegetation, and the distribution of animal populations across Southwest Asia many times over. These fluctuations, reconstructed by climate scientists and archaeologists working with pollen records, sediment cores, and faunal remains, shaped where Paleolithic communities could live, what resources were available to them, and how populations expanded, contracted, or shifted location over time. This chapter treats climate not as a deterministic force dictating human behavior but as a changing set of conditions and constraints within which Paleolithic communities made choices about mobility, subsistence, and shelter.

Section 2.1

The Paleolithic Framework: Concepts and Chronology

The word Paleolithic, from Greek roots meaning "old stone," designates the immensely long period of human and hominin history characterized by the use of chipped, or flaked, stone tools, before the development of agriculture, pottery, and settled village life examined in Chapter 3. Archaeologists conventionally divide the Paleolithic into three broad phases, Lower, Middle, and Upper, based primarily on changes in stone tool technology, supplemented, where possible, by fossil and genetic evidence concerning the hominin populations responsible for producing those tools.

Students should understand this three-part framework as an analytical tool rather than a set of natural, sharply bounded categories. The transitions between Lower, Middle, and Upper Paleolithic technologies did not occur everywhere at the same time, did not proceed at the same pace in every region, and did not necessarily correspond to a single hominin population replacing another in any simple or uniform way. Archaeologists apply the framework because it allows comparison across sites and regions, not because Paleolithic peoples themselves recognized these categories or experienced them as decisive breaks.

The Lower Paleolithic refers to the earliest conventional phase, associated in much of Africa and Eurasia with early hominin populations and including both core-and-flake technologies and, as Section 2.2 discusses, Acheulean bifacial traditions such as handaxes and cleavers. The Middle Paleolithic, the best documented phase in Iran's archaeological record, is associated across much of Southwest Asia and Europe with prepared-core flake tool industries, most notably the Mousterian tradition examined in Section 2.4, and with Neanderthal populations, though, as this chapter discusses, the relationship between tool traditions and specific hominin populations is not always straightforward. The Upper Paleolithic is associated with blade-based stone tool technologies, greater use of bone and antler, evidence of symbolic behavior in some regions, and, across Southwest Asia generally, the spread of anatomically modern humans.

Chronology within this framework depends heavily on scientific dating methods, examined further in Section 2.7, each of which carries its own limitations. Students encountering specific dates in this chapter should understand them as estimates derived from specific dating techniques applied to specific archaeological contexts, not as fixed, universally agreed figures. Where Iranian Paleolithic chronology remains especially uncertain, principally for the Lower Paleolithic and for the precise timing of the Middle to Upper Paleolithic transition, this chapter states that uncertainty explicitly rather than presenting a false precision.

Historical Vocabulary
Paleolithic Periodization

Definition: The division of the Paleolithic into Lower, Middle, and Upper phases, and, at its end, the Epipaleolithic, based primarily on changes in stone tool technology and supplemented, where available, by fossil and genetic evidence for the hominin populations involved.

Historical context: Archaeologists developed this framework by comparing stone tool assemblages across many sites and regions in Africa, Europe, and Asia, associating a sequence of different, though not inherently more advanced, technological approaches, core-and-flake and Acheulean bifacial traditions, prepared-core flake technologies such as the Mousterian, blade-based Upper Paleolithic industries, and finally small Epipaleolithic microliths, with successive, though not perfectly bounded, periods of human and hominin history.

Why it matters: This framework allows comparison of Iranian evidence with Paleolithic sequences documented elsewhere in Southwest Asia, the Caucasus, and Europe, situating the plateau's own record within a much larger, comparative scientific picture rather than treating it in isolation.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that the transition from one phase to the next occurred everywhere at the same time, at the same pace, or as a result of a single, uniform cause. As this chapter has emphasized regarding the Middle to Upper Paleolithic transition specifically, the timing and character of these shifts varied by region, and in Iran's case reveal a genuine mosaic of local traditions rather than a single, tidy sequence.

Historical Vocabulary
Hominin and Homo sapiens

Definition: "Hominin" is a broad scientific term referring to modern humans and their close fossil relatives and ancestors, used when discussing populations, including Neanderthals and other now-extinct groups, that are closely related to but not identical with modern humans. "Homo sapiens," or anatomically modern humans, refers specifically to the single species to which every living human being belongs.

Historical context: Paleolithic archaeology in Iran, as elsewhere, must distinguish among different hominin populations using skeletal and, increasingly, genetic evidence, since stone tool technology alone frequently cannot reliably identify which population produced a given assemblage. This chapter has discussed direct skeletal evidence bearing on this question from Wezmeh Cave, assessed as Neanderthal, from Bisitun Cave, and from Eshkaft-e Gavi and Warwasi, both of which have yielded molars identified as anatomically modern human.

Why it matters: Using "hominin" rather than assuming every prehistoric find automatically represents modern humans allows this chapter to discuss Neanderthal evidence, such as the Wezmeh premolar, accurately and without anachronism, while reserving "Homo sapiens" for contexts where the evidence genuinely supports that specific identification.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that sophisticated technology, ornamentation, or other material culture, such as the evidence from Yafteh Cave discussed in Section 2.6, automatically identifies its makers as Homo sapiens rather than another hominin population. Material culture and skeletal or genetic identification are separate lines of evidence that must be evaluated independently and, wherever possible, together.

Section 2.2

The Lower Paleolithic on the Plateau

Evidence for the earliest phase of human presence on the Iranian plateau remains real, but its chronology is poorly constrained, and, in important respects, genuinely contested among specialists. Unlike some neighboring regions of Southwest Asia and the Caucasus, where well stratified Lower Paleolithic sites have produced securely dated hominin fossils and associated stone tools, Iran's Lower Paleolithic record consists overwhelmingly of surface finds, together with a small number of excavated contexts whose dating and cultural attribution remain debated. A comprehensive scholarly review of the Iranian Paleolithic has specifically identified considerable chronological and archaeological gaps in this earliest phase of the record.

The technological content of this evidence, however, is more substantial than a description limited to simple core and flake tools would suggest. Iranian Lower Paleolithic assemblages include genuine Acheulean technology, a tradition characterized across Africa and much of Eurasia by carefully shaped bifacial tools. Surface localities in the western foothills near Jiroft in southern Iran, for example, have produced one of the most substantial known Lower Paleolithic assemblages in this part of the plateau, including large cores, bifaces and handaxes, cleavers, picks, and heavy-duty scrapers made on large flakes. This evidence demonstrates that Lower Paleolithic populations on or near the plateau practiced a recognizably Acheulean technological tradition, not merely an undifferentiated core-and-flake technology.

This technological richness, however, does not resolve the chronological problem that defines this section. Because the Jiroft-area material and most other Iranian Acheulean-type finds derive from surface contexts rather than securely stratified and dated excavated deposits, archaeologists cannot yet assign them confident absolute ages, and the same evidentiary caution introduced in this chapter's opening applies with particular force here: a well documented technological tradition is not the same thing as a well dated one. This scarcity of secure dating should not be read as evidence of absence for the tradition itself, however. The Iranian plateau lies geographically between regions with substantial, better dated Lower Paleolithic and Acheulean records, the Caucasus to the northwest and parts of the Levant and Central and South Asia beyond, making it unsurprising that early hominin populations produced comparable technology while moving through or across the plateau. Until further systematic excavation produces securely dated, well stratified Lower Paleolithic contexts on the plateau itself, however, claims about the precise timing, extent, and character of the earliest hominin presence in Iran must remain provisional.

Several factors help explain the relative scarcity of confirmed Iranian Lower Paleolithic evidence compared to some neighboring regions. Systematic archaeological survey and excavation in Iran developed later than in some neighboring countries and has, at various periods, faced practical and political constraints on sustained fieldwork. Additionally, many potential Lower Paleolithic contexts on the open plateau surface lack the kind of protective cave or rock shelter stratigraphy that has preserved later Paleolithic evidence so well in the Zagros, making early open-air sites more vulnerable to erosion, disturbance, and difficulty of secure dating.

Students should draw a specific methodological lesson from this section: the absence of abundant evidence for a particular period or region is frequently a product of the history of archaeological research itself, including where fieldwork has and has not been concentrated, rather than a reliable indicator of where ancient human activity did or did not occur. This lesson recurs at several points later in this chapter and should inform how students evaluate claims throughout the remainder of this textbook whenever archaeological evidence is central to a chapter's argument.

Section 2.3

The Middle Paleolithic and the Question of Neanderthals

The Middle Paleolithic provides the best documented and most extensively studied phase of Iran's Paleolithic record, concentrated overwhelmingly in the cave and rock shelter sites of the Zagros Mountains. In wider Southwest Asian archaeological schemes, the Middle Paleolithic extends back more than two hundred thousand years. Iran's own early Middle Paleolithic, however, remains poorly dated for much the same reasons discussed in Section 2.2 regarding the Lower Paleolithic: many relevant contexts lack the secure stratigraphy and chronometric dating needed to place them confidently within this long span. The later Middle Paleolithic, by contrast, roughly the period after about fifty thousand years ago, is substantially better documented, and it is this later portion that has produced the great majority of the Zagros Mousterian evidence this chapter discusses. Across this better documented span, Zagros sites have produced substantial stone tool assemblages associated with the Mousterian tradition, a flake-based technology widely documented across Southwest Asia, the Caucasus, and Europe during this period.

Across much of this wider Mousterian-associated world, the technology is closely linked to Neanderthal populations, and a body of fossil and genetic evidence from sites in the Levant, the Caucasus, and Europe has considerably advanced scientific understanding of Neanderthal biology, behavior, and eventual disappearance as a distinct population. Iran's own evidence for Neanderthal presence is real but more limited than in some neighboring regions, and it rests on a small number of specific specimens rather than an extensive fossil record. The single most important such specimen is Wezmeh 1, an unerupted upper premolar recovered from Wezmeh Cave in the Zagros, belonging to a child of approximately eight years of age. A detailed micro-computed tomography reassessment of this tooth's internal structure, published in 2019, found its morphology closely aligned with Neanderthals and described it as the first direct evidence of Neanderthal presence on the western Iranian plateau. Wezmeh Cave's surrounding Late Pleistocene deposits are themselves dominated by the remains of carnivores rather than by evidence of sustained human occupation, meaning this single tooth documents a Neanderthal presence at the site without necessarily indicating that the cave functioned as a regular human dwelling in the way better documented occupation sites such as Warwasi appear to have done.

A second, independent line of direct skeletal evidence for Middle Paleolithic hominins in Iran comes from Bisitun Cave, examined further in Section 2.4, where excavations recovered a fragmentary human right radius, a forearm bone, from within the cave's Middle Paleolithic sequence. This specimen, published in detail by specialists in 2006, provides a second, geographically separate point of direct human skeletal evidence for the Iranian Zagros Middle Paleolithic, complementing the Wezmeh premolar and strengthening, though not resolving, the wider regional picture of hominin presence across the Zagros during this period.

Students should approach claims about Neanderthal presence in Iran with appropriate caution regarding both what the evidence supports and what remains uncertain. The identification of specific skeletal or dental remains as Neanderthal rather than early modern human, or as belonging to some other population, depends on careful comparative anatomical and, increasingly, genetic analysis, and such identifications are sometimes revised as analytical techniques improve or as additional remains are recovered. Furthermore, the presence of Mousterian-type stone tools at a site does not, by itself, prove Neanderthal presence, since Middle Paleolithic-type technologies were, in some regions of Southwest Asia, also produced by early modern human populations. This chapter therefore distinguishes carefully between what the stone tool evidence alone can establish and what additional skeletal or genetic evidence would be needed to confirm which hominin population, or populations, actually occupied a given Zagros site during the Middle Paleolithic.

The Zagros Mousterian record also matters for a broader reason connected to this textbook's larger themes. Chapter 1 introduced the Zagros as both a barrier and a corridor connecting the plateau to Mesopotamia and, more broadly, to the wider Southwest Asian world. The Middle Paleolithic evidence confirms that this connective function operated across an extraordinarily long span of time, situating the Zagros caves within the same wider zone of Neanderthal and early human distribution that includes well known sites in the Levant, Iraq, and the Caucasus, long before any of the later political or cultural boundaries examined in this textbook existed.

Section 2.4

Zagros Cave Sites and the Archaeology of the Middle Paleolithic

Several specific Zagros sites illustrate how archaeologists reconstruct Middle Paleolithic life from material remains. Kobeh Cave, in the central Zagros, has produced Mousterian stone tool assemblages alongside substantial faunal remains dominated by caprines, chiefly wild goat, with sheep also represented, and smaller numbers of gazelle and other taxa. Detailed taphonomic analysis of the Kobeh faunal assemblage, examining specific patterns of bone breakage, cut marks, and carnivore damage, has specifically challenged older assumptions that Middle Paleolithic hominins in this region relied heavily on scavenging carcasses left by other predators; the Kobeh evidence instead supports a pattern more consistent with active hunting of caprines by the cave's occupants. This finding illustrates why archaeologists should not treat hunting and scavenging as equally likely, undifferentiated possibilities for a given Middle Paleolithic site; the specific taphonomic evidence at each site must be examined on its own terms.

Warwasi Rock Shelter offers a different kind of evidentiary value: a long, stratified sequence of deposits spanning the Middle Paleolithic through the Upper Paleolithic and into the Epipaleolithic, examined further in Sections 2.5 and 2.11. Because Warwasi preserves multiple superimposed occupation layers across this long span, it functions as an important stratigraphic and typological sequence, allowing archaeologists to compare technological change across successive layers at a single location. Students should understand this value carefully, however: Warwasi's sequence lacks extensive absolute chronometric dating of the kind radiocarbon or other scientific methods can provide, and the degree of true stratigraphic continuity across its Middle to Upper Paleolithic transition remains a subject of ongoing scholarly discussion rather than a settled matter. Warwasi is therefore best understood as a vital comparative reference sequence rather than as a secure, independently dated chronological anchor for the wider Zagros Paleolithic record.

Bisitun Cave, located near the site later associated with the famous Achaemenid royal inscription examined in Chapter 11, has also produced Middle Paleolithic occupation evidence, including, within its Level F+ deposits, a fragmentary human right radius published by specialists in 2006, one of the more significant direct skeletal finds from the Iranian Middle Paleolithic record and a useful complement to the Wezmeh premolar discussed in Section 2.3. A separate specimen once thought to be a human tooth from the same general context was subsequently reidentified as belonging to a bovid, a caution worth noting as an example of how skeletal identifications in Paleolithic archaeology are sometimes revised upon closer analysis. Students should take particular care to distinguish the Paleolithic archaeological deposits at Bisitun, dating to many tens of thousands of years before the present, from the Achaemenid era inscription at the same general location, which dates to the sixth century BCE. The two are separated by an immense span of time and belong to entirely unrelated historical contexts; their geographic proximity is a coincidence of a limestone cliff face favorable to both ancient cave occupation and, many tens of thousands of years later, royal monumental inscription, not evidence of any historical connection between the two.

Taken together, the Zagros cave sites illustrate the value of cave and rock shelter environments for Paleolithic archaeology generally. Caves and rock shelters tend to accumulate and preserve sediment, ash, bone, and stone tools in ways that open-air sites on the plateau surface, exposed to erosion and disturbance, generally do not. This preservation advantage helps explain why the documented Iranian Middle Paleolithic record concentrates so heavily in the Zagros rather than reflecting an assumption that Middle Paleolithic populations avoided other regions of the plateau entirely.

Archaeological Source Window

Reading a Middle Paleolithic Cave Deposit: Kobeh Cave

Type of evidence: Excavated stratified cave deposit, including stone tool assemblage and faunal remains · Location: Central Zagros Mountains · Period: Middle Paleolithic, Mousterian-associated

Context: Unlike the written primary sources students will encounter beginning in later chapters, Paleolithic archaeology relies on material evidence recovered through careful excavation. At Kobeh Cave, archaeologists have documented layers of sediment containing flaked stone tools alongside the bones of hunted caprines and other animals, evidence that must be interpreted through controlled excavation and taphonomic methods rather than read as a direct narrative account.

Summary of the evidence: Excavated layers at Kobeh Cave contain Mousterian-type flake tools together with faunal remains dominated by caprines, chiefly wild goat, with sheep also represented and gazelle and other taxa present in smaller numbers, distributed across multiple occupation horizons that suggest repeated, though not necessarily continuous, use of the cave over an extended period. Detailed taphonomic study of bone breakage and surface damage at Kobeh has supported active hunting of caprines as the primary means by which this fauna reached the site, rather than scavenging of carcasses killed by other predators.

Analysis: The association between particular stone tool types and particular faunal remains within the same stratigraphic layer allows archaeologists to propose, with reasonable confidence in this specific case, that hunting rather than scavenging was the primary subsistence strategy practiced by the cave's Middle Paleolithic occupants. The presence of multiple, distinguishable occupation layers additionally suggests that the cave was used more than once, potentially across a considerable span of time, rather than during a single brief episode.

Limitations: Faunal and lithic evidence of this kind cannot establish the size of the group that used the cave, its social organization, its language, or its beliefs. It also cannot, by itself, definitively identify which hominin population was responsible for a given occupation layer without additional skeletal or genetic evidence, since Mousterian-type tools were produced by more than one hominin population across different parts of Southwest Asia.

Perspective: This kind of evidence reflects a functional, subsistence-oriented record of activity at the site; it says relatively little directly about social relationships, though careful comparative analysis across multiple sites can support broader, more cautious inferences.

Section 2.5

The Upper Paleolithic Transition and Early Modern Humans

The transition from the Middle to the Upper Paleolithic in the Zagros region marks one of the most significant technological shifts documented in the Iranian Paleolithic record. Bayesian statistical modeling of radiocarbon evidence from several Zagros sequences, including Yafteh Cave, Kobeh Cave, and comparable sites in the wider region, places the onset of this transition at approximately forty-five thousand to forty thousand years ago, with the precise timing varying somewhat by site and assemblage rather than occurring as a single, region-wide event on a fixed date. Upper Paleolithic stone tool assemblages are characterized generally by an increased emphasis on blade-based technology, in which long, parallel-sided stone blades are struck from a carefully prepared core, in contrast to the flake-based, though technologically sophisticated, Levallois-type core preparation that characterizes Middle Paleolithic Mousterian assemblages.

Across Southwest Asia more broadly, the Upper Paleolithic transition is often discussed in connection with the spread of anatomically modern humans, though the precise relationship between this population spread and the appearance of new stone tool technologies remains an active subject of scholarly discussion rather than a settled matter. Some archaeologists emphasize population movement, in which modern human populations moving into a region introduced new technologies along with themselves. Others emphasize the possibility of technological transmission, cultural adaptation, demographic change within existing populations, or more complex patterns of interaction and regional continuity. The Iranian evidence, discussed further in Section 2.6 through the specific case of Yafteh Cave, contributes to this wider regional discussion without resolving it definitively.

What the Zagros evidence does establish with reasonable confidence is that, by roughly forty-five to forty thousand years ago, blade-based Upper Paleolithic technology had become established at several Zagros cave sites, continuing a pattern of occupation at locations, including Warwasi, that had already seen Middle Paleolithic use. In the central Zagros specifically, this technology is closely associated with what archaeologists term the Baradostian industry, documented particularly well at Yafteh Cave and examined further in Section 2.6. Students should be careful, however, not to treat "Baradostian" as though it described Upper Paleolithic technology across the entire Iranian plateau uniformly. More recent research, discussed further in Section 2.10, indicates that the Zagros and adjoining regions of the plateau supported a genuine mosaic of distinct Upper Paleolithic technological traditions rather than a single, homogeneous industry, with southern Zagros assemblages in particular showing sufficient distinctiveness that some specialists have proposed a separate southern tradition, sometimes called the Rostamian. The continuity of occupation at locations such as Warwasi across this transition, even where the associated technology and, quite possibly, the associated hominin population changed, illustrates a recurring theme in Paleolithic archaeology: specific caves and rock shelters offered advantages, shelter, proximity to water, access to hunting grounds, that made them attractive to successive occupants across long spans of time, independent of which particular population or technological tradition occupied them at a given moment.

Section 2.6

Yafteh Cave and the Baradostian/Zagros Aurignacian Debate

Yafteh Cave, located in the central Zagros near the modern city of Khorramabad, stands as one of the most important documented Upper Paleolithic sites on the Iranian plateau. Excavations at Yafteh have produced substantial blade-based stone tool assemblages, worked bone tools, and personal ornaments, including perforated deer canines, pierced marine shells transported from considerable distances, and pigments such as hematite, associated with layers dated to the Upper Paleolithic period.

This rich material culture is conventionally associated with Homo sapiens across the wider Upper Paleolithic record of Eurasia, and archaeologists generally treat blade-based technology, worked bone, and personal ornamentation of this kind as characteristic of behavior documented among modern human populations elsewhere in Southwest Asia and Europe. Students should understand this association carefully, however: it is comparative and archaeological, not based on diagnostic Homo sapiens fossils recovered at Yafteh Cave itself. No skeletal remains securely identified as Homo sapiens have been recovered from Yafteh's Upper Paleolithic layers, and the site's importance rests on its material culture rather than on direct fossil evidence. Yafteh's ornaments demonstrate complex symbolic or social signaling within its Upper Paleolithic communities; they are not, by themselves, taxonomically diagnostic, and students should resist treating ornamentation as a simple, unambiguous marker automatically distinguishing one hominin population from another. The relationship between symbolic material culture and questions of cognitive or social complexity remains an area of active research and, at points, genuine scholarly disagreement.

Direct osteological evidence for Homo sapiens presence in Iran comes chiefly from two Zagros sites. At Eshkaft-e Gavi, a southern Zagros cave, excavations recovered ten fragmentary hominin specimens, including a molar from the base of the site's Upper Paleolithic sequence that specialists have identified as diagnostically modern human on the basis of its dental morphology. Other hominin remains from Eshkaft-e Gavi likely derive from later Upper Paleolithic or Epipaleolithic contexts, and some of the recovered bones carry cut marks and evidence of burning, findings that have prompted careful discussion among specialists regarding the circumstances under which these remains came to be deposited at the site. Warwasi Rock Shelter, discussed already in Section 2.4 for its long stratigraphic sequence, has independently yielded a second isolated molar, identified as belonging to a fossil modern human, from its Upper Paleolithic, or "Lower Baradostian," levels, adding another rare piece of direct osteological evidence to the regional picture. Eshkaft-e Gavi and Warwasi together therefore provide the kind of direct fossil evidence for Homo sapiens presence in Iran that the Yafteh material culture, however rich, does not by itself supply, and the three sites together illustrate why archaeologists must draw on multiple independent lines of evidence, material culture and skeletal remains alike, when addressing questions of hominin identity.

Some specialists have connected the Zagros Upper Paleolithic material, including evidence from Yafteh Cave, to the broader Aurignacian technological tradition well documented in Europe and parts of the Levant, describing aspects of the Zagros sequence as a "Zagros Aurignacian." Others emphasize the distinctiveness of the local Baradostian sequence as a regionally specific development rather than a simple extension of a single, unified Aurignacian tradition spreading from elsewhere, and the precise relationship among the Baradostian, a proposed Zagros Aurignacian, Levantine Upper Paleolithic traditions, the European Protoaurignacian, and the classic European Aurignacian remains genuinely unresolved among specialists. This disagreement illustrates a pattern that recurs throughout Paleolithic archaeology: broad technological similarities across a wide geographic area can reflect population movement, cultural transmission and imitation across neighboring but distinct groups, independent innovation responding to similar functional needs, or some combination of these processes, and archaeologists must weigh the available evidence carefully rather than assuming any single explanation automatically applies.

Taken together, Yafteh Cave, Eshkaft-e Gavi, and Warwasi bear on the wider question of when anatomically modern humans became established in the Zagros region specifically, and, by extension, on the Iranian plateau more broadly. Specialists continue to refine the precise chronology and taxonomic picture through ongoing dating work and renewed analysis of existing specimens, and this chapter presents the combined evidence from these sites as important, well documented data points within a still developing regional picture rather than as a final, settled answer to when modern humans first reached the plateau.

Section 2.7

Technology, Tools, and Subsistence across the Paleolithic

Stone tool technology provides the primary evidentiary backbone for Paleolithic archaeology generally, including in Iran, because stone survives in the archaeological record far better than most organic materials. Understanding how archaeologists classify and interpret stone tools therefore matters for evaluating the claims made throughout this chapter.

Archaeologists classify stone tool assemblages, collections of tools recovered together from a given archaeological context, according to features such as the method used to strike flakes or blades from a stone core, the shape and retouching of finished tools, and the overall composition of tool types present. The Mousterian tradition associated with the Middle Paleolithic, discussed in Section 2.3, is generally characterized by flake tools produced using carefully prepared cores, a technique sometimes called the Levallois method after the French site where it was first systematically described, though the technique itself was practiced across a wide geographic area including the Zagros. The Baradostian tradition associated with the Upper Paleolithic, discussed in Sections 2.5 and 2.6, is instead characterized by blade production, yielding longer, more standardized tool blanks that could be further shaped into a wider variety of specialized implements, including scrapers, burins used for working bone and antler, and points potentially used for hunting.

Faunal remains, the bones of animals recovered from archaeological deposits, provide the second major category of evidence for Paleolithic subsistence. Analysis of which species are present, how bones were butchered, and what age and sex profiles are represented allows archaeologists to make inferences, always subject to appropriate caution, about hunting strategies, seasonal site use, and the character of the surrounding environment at the time of occupation. Rather than pointing to a single, uniform "Zagros diet," the faunal evidence from different sites instead demonstrates considerable regional and site-specific variation in hunting strategy and prey selection. Kobeh Cave's assemblage, discussed in Section 2.4, is heavily dominated by caprines, particularly wild goat, while other Zagros sites document different balances of prey species depending on local topography, elevation, and the surrounding animal populations available to a given group. This variation is itself an important lesson: rather than assuming a single, textbook-style Paleolithic diet applied uniformly across the Zagros, students should expect, and look for, meaningful differences in subsistence strategy from one site and one period to another, supplemented, though less visibly in the archaeological record due to poorer preservation, by plant foods that Paleolithic groups certainly also gathered and consumed.

Chronometric dating methods, chiefly radiocarbon dating for more recent Paleolithic contexts and other techniques, including optically stimulated luminescence and uranium-series dating, for older material beyond the reliable range of radiocarbon methods, allow archaeologists to place these technological and subsistence sequences into an approximate chronological framework. Each method carries characteristic margins of error and specific ranges within which it functions reliably, and archaeologists frequently cross-check results using multiple methods where possible. Students should understand that Paleolithic dates presented in this chapter, and throughout Paleolithic archaeology generally, are best understood as estimated ranges derived from specific dating techniques rather than as precise, unquestionable figures.

Section 2.8

Climate, Environment, and the Pleistocene Plateau

Nearly the entire span of this chapter falls within the Pleistocene epoch, a geological period lasting from roughly two and a half million years ago until approximately 11,700 years ago, characterized by repeated cycles of glacial, colder and often drier, and interglacial, warmer and often wetter, climate conditions across much of the globe, including Southwest Asia. These climate cycles, reconstructed by scientists through evidence including ice cores, deep-sea sediment records, cave mineral deposits, and pollen preserved in lake and marsh sediments, produced substantial and repeated changes in vegetation, water availability, and the distribution of animal populations across the Iranian plateau and its surrounding regions.

During colder, drier glacial phases, including the especially pronounced Last Glacial Maximum of roughly twenty-six to nineteen thousand years ago, much of the plateau's interior likely became still more arid than during warmer interglacial phases, potentially concentrating human and animal populations into more favorable refuge zones, including well watered valleys and foothill areas of the Zagros and, plausibly, other mountain systems. During warmer, wetter interglacial phases, a broader range of the plateau's territory may have supported vegetation and animal populations capable of sustaining human groups, potentially allowing wider dispersal across the landscape.

This chapter treats these climate fluctuations as a genuinely important shaping condition for Paleolithic settlement and mobility without treating climate as a deterministic force that mechanically dictated human behavior. Paleolithic communities responded to changing environmental conditions through a range of strategies, including altering patterns of seasonal movement, shifting the relative importance of different food resources, and, where necessary, relocating to more favorable areas, strategies that reflect active human adaptation and decision-making within changing environmental constraints rather than passive environmental determinism.

Reconstructing Pleistocene environmental conditions specifically for the Iranian plateau remains an active area of ongoing scientific research, and the regional environmental record, particularly for the plateau's interior away from the better studied Zagros zone, remains less completely documented than for some neighboring regions. This chapter presents the general pattern of Pleistocene climate fluctuation as well established while acknowledging that its specific regional effects on plateau environments and human settlement in different parts of Iran require continued refinement through ongoing paleoenvironmental research.

Section 2.9

Social Life, Mobility, and Early Human Communities

Reconstructing the social organization of Paleolithic communities on the Iranian plateau requires considerable interpretive caution, since the surviving archaeological evidence, chiefly stone tools, faunal remains, cave stratigraphy, and a small number of human skeletal remains, speaks only indirectly to questions of group size, kinship, leadership, language, or belief. This section presents what can be reasonably inferred from comparative archaeological and, where relevant, ethnographic evidence concerning hunter-gatherer societies generally, while clearly distinguishing such inference from direct documentation.

Paleolithic communities on the plateau were almost certainly organized as small, mobile groups practicing some form of hunting and gathering, moving across the landscape on a seasonal or otherwise periodic basis in response to the availability of game, edible plants, and water, patterns broadly consistent with hunter-gatherer societies documented archaeologically and, in more recent historical periods, ethnographically across many regions of the world. The repeated use of specific caves and rock shelters, documented archaeologically at sites such as Warwasi and Yafteh across long spans of time, suggests that certain locations offered recurring practical advantages, shelter from weather, proximity to water and hunting grounds, that made them attractive stopping points within a broader pattern of seasonal or periodic movement, rather than necessarily indicating permanent, year-round occupation by any single group.

Direct evidence for social relationships, cooperation, division of labor, or belief systems among Iranian Paleolithic communities remains extremely limited. The presence of personal ornaments at Yafteh Cave, discussed in Section 2.6, offers one of the few forms of evidence potentially bearing on questions of symbolic behavior and, by extension, social communication or identity expression, though, as that section discussed, the interpretation of such evidence requires care and remains subject to ongoing scholarly discussion. No burial sites with clear evidence of deliberate mortuary treatment have been definitively documented and widely accepted from the Iranian Paleolithic record in the way that such evidence exists at some Middle Paleolithic sites elsewhere in Southwest Asia, and students should not assume that practices documented at better known sites outside Iran necessarily applied equally to Iranian Paleolithic populations without direct local evidence.

This section's central methodological lesson deserves explicit statement: historians and archaeologists must resist filling evidentiary gaps with confident, specific claims about Paleolithic social life that the material evidence does not actually support. Cautious, comparative inference grounded in the available evidence is appropriate and valuable; confident narrative reconstruction of specific beliefs, relationships, or events for which no direct evidence survives is not.

Section 2.10

Regional Variation across the Plateau

The overwhelming concentration of documented Iranian Paleolithic evidence in the Zagros Mountains, discussed throughout this chapter, should not be mistaken for evidence that Paleolithic populations were confined to this single region of the plateau. Chapter 1 described a plateau of considerable environmental diversity, including the humid Caspian littoral, the arid interior basins, and the historical regions extending toward Khurasan, Sistan, and the Persian Gulf littoral, and it is reasonable to expect that Paleolithic populations, to varying degrees and at various periods, made use of a wider range of this environmental diversity than the current excavated evidence alone documents.

Some surface finds of Paleolithic-type stone tools have been reported from regions of the plateau outside the Zagros, including parts of the central plateau and areas toward the northeast, though such surface finds generally lack the secure stratigraphic context and reliable dating that excavated cave and rock shelter sites provide, limiting how much can be confidently concluded from them. The Caspian littoral and adjoining Alborz range, with their markedly different ecology from the arid interior, as Chapter 1 emphasized, have historically presented a case for future research, since their environmental character differs so substantially from the Zagros zone where most excavation has concentrated.

Recent fieldwork has begun to fill part of this gap. Excavations at Sorheh Rockshelter, on the southern flank of the Alborz Mountains, have produced a stratified Initial Upper Paleolithic assemblage dated to a broad window of roughly fifty thousand to thirty-five thousand years ago. Detailed techno-typological analysis of the Sorheh material shows technological affinities with Initial Upper Paleolithic traditions documented farther west and farther east across Southwest and Central Asia, while also displaying distinctive local characteristics that do not simply replicate the Zagros Baradostian sequence discussed in Sections 2.5 and 2.6. This evidence is significant precisely because it comes from outside the Zagros, demonstrating that the northern Iranian plateau supported its own, at least partly distinct, Upper Paleolithic technological trajectory rather than functioning merely as an extension of Zagros developments. Sorheh therefore strengthens the broader picture, introduced in Section 2.5, of the Iranian Upper Paleolithic as a genuine mosaic of regional traditions, and it illustrates concretely how continued fieldwork outside the best studied Zagros zone can meaningfully revise the regional picture this chapter presents.

This section reinforces a methodological point introduced earlier in the chapter: archaeological visibility, meaning how much evidence has actually been recovered and documented for a given region, does not necessarily track ancient human presence in any straightforward way. It instead reflects, in significant part, where systematic fieldwork has been concentrated, which depends on factors including research history, geological conditions favorable or unfavorable to preservation, and practical considerations affecting where archaeologists have been able to conduct sustained excavation. Students should therefore treat the current regional distribution of documented Iranian Paleolithic sites as a reflection of the current state of archaeological knowledge rather than as a complete and final map of where Paleolithic populations actually lived across the plateau.

Section 2.11

The Epipaleolithic (Zarzian) and Late Hunter-Gatherer Adaptations

The final phase of hunter-gatherer adaptation on the Iranian plateau before the agricultural transition examined in Chapter 3 is generally termed the Epipaleolithic, a period emerging during the closing stages of the Last Glacial Maximum discussed in Section 2.8 and continuing afterward, beginning approximately twenty to eighteen thousand years ago and continuing until roughly twelve to ten thousand years ago, dates that, as elsewhere in this chapter, remain approximate and subject to regional variation. In the Zagros region specifically, this Epipaleolithic technological tradition is generally referred to as the Zarzian, named for a site outside Iran's own borders but applied broadly across the Zagros arc to describe the characteristic microlithic industries of this period.

Zarzian and other Epipaleolithic stone tool assemblages across the Zagros and wider Southwest Asian region are generally characterized by an increased proportion of very small, standardized stone tools, often referred to as microliths, which were frequently hafted in combination, set into wood or bone handles to create composite tools, a technological approach that allowed more efficient use of available stone material and, plausibly, supported an increasingly diverse range of specific tool functions related to hunting, plant processing, and other tasks.

Students should approach the Zarzian with the same chronological caution this chapter has applied to earlier periods, and in this case with particular force. Relatively few Zagros Epipaleolithic sites have produced secure absolute dates, and many assignments of surface or poorly stratified lithic material to the Zarzian rest on typological similarity to better documented assemblages rather than on independent chronometric confirmation. Even the relationship between the latest Zarzian material and the earliest Neolithic-related traditions discussed in Chapter 3, including material sometimes labeled Mlefatian, lacks secure stratigraphic continuity at most sites, meaning the transition from Epipaleolithic to Neolithic technology in this region remains considerably less well documented, chronologically, than the broad narrative of gradual change might suggest.

Alongside this technological shift, archaeological evidence from the broader Southwest Asian region, including sites in the Zagros and neighboring areas examined more fully in Chapter 3, suggests that some Epipaleolithic groups began exploiting a wider range of plant and animal resources with increasing intensity, in some cases returning repeatedly to particular favorable locations across multiple seasons rather than maintaining the more thoroughly mobile settlement pattern associated with earlier Paleolithic phases. This pattern, sometimes described by archaeologists as increasing residential intensification, is a genuine and documented development, but students should resist reading it as evidence of a smooth, linear, or inevitable march toward full sedentism and agriculture. The pace, extent, and even the direction of these changes appear to have varied considerably from one Epipaleolithic group and locality to another, and the eventual Neolithic transition examined in Chapter 3, while clearly connected to these Epipaleolithic developments, should not be treated as their predetermined outcome.

This chapter deliberately stops short of narrating the agricultural transition itself, reserving that subject for full treatment in Chapter 3. Its purpose here is limited to establishing that the shift toward agriculture and settled village life did not occur as a sudden, unprecedented event but instead built upon several thousand years of gradual, uneven, and incompletely dated behavioral change among Zarzian and other Epipaleolithic hunter-gatherer populations, change that is itself documented, however incompletely, in the archaeological record this chapter has surveyed.

Section 2.12

From Paleolithic Iran to the Neolithic Revolution

This concluding section synthesizes the chapter's material and looks forward, briefly and without anticipating Chapter 3's full narrative, toward the transformation that follows. Across the vast span of time this chapter has surveyed, from the still uncertain earliest hominin presence discussed in Section 2.2 through the Middle Paleolithic Zagros cave record discussed in Sections 2.3 and 2.4, the Upper Paleolithic transition and the evidence from Yafteh Cave discussed in Sections 2.5 and 2.6, and the Epipaleolithic developments discussed in Section 2.11, the Iranian plateau supported successive, and in some periods possibly overlapping, hominin and human populations adapting to a demanding and environmentally varied landscape through hunting, gathering, and changing and increasingly specialized tool technologies, and, in some contexts, symbolic behavior.

None of these populations lived within anything resembling a state, a settled agricultural village, or a fixed territorial boundary of the kind later chapters of this textbook will examine. Their movements were shaped by the seasonal availability of game and plant foods, by the specific advantages of particular caves and rock shelters, and by the broader climatic rhythms of the Pleistocene epoch described in Section 2.8. Yet their long presence on the plateau, extending across a period vastly longer than the entire political history this textbook otherwise covers, establishes an essential truth that later chapters will build upon: the Iranian plateau examined in Chapter 1 as a distinctive geographic and environmental space was already, probably for hundreds of thousands of years before any city, any writing system, or any state existed, a lived and inhabited landscape.

The Epipaleolithic developments discussed in Section 2.11, increasing use of microlithic technology and increasingly intensive exploitation of specific favorable locations, set the immediate context for the profound transformation Chapter 3 examines: the shift toward plant and animal domestication, settled village life, and the beginnings of the social and economic patterns that would eventually make possible the complex societies examined in Chapter 4 and the civilization of Elam examined in Chapter 5. That transformation did not erase the deep Paleolithic past this chapter has surveyed; it built upon many thousands of years of accumulated human knowledge about the plateau's plants, animals, seasons, and terrain, knowledge developed and transmitted, without writing, across an immense span of human generations.

Historical Debate

When Did Modern Humans Reach the Iranian Plateau?

The question: When did anatomically modern humans first become established in the Zagros region and more broadly across the Iranian plateau, and how does this timing relate to the disappearance of Neanderthal populations from the same region?

Interpretation A — A relatively early and technologically driven transition: Some specialists, emphasizing the Bayesian-modeled radiocarbon chronology placing the Zagros transition at approximately forty-five to forty thousand years ago and the blade-based technology and personal ornaments documented at Yafteh Cave, argue that anatomically modern humans became established in the Zagros relatively early within the broader Southwest Asian Upper Paleolithic sequence, with technological change closely tracking the spread of new populations into the region.
Interpretation B — A more gradual, regionally complex transition: Other specialists caution against assuming a simple, rapid, one-directional replacement, noting that stone tool technology and even ornamentation alone cannot definitively identify which hominin population produced them, that the diagnostically modern human molars from Eshkaft-e Gavi and Warwasi and the Sorheh Rockshelter sequence together suggest a more regionally varied picture than a single Zagros-centered narrative allows, and that the relationship between technological change and population change across Southwest Asia more broadly appears regionally variable rather than uniform.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of evidence: stratified stone tool assemblages, Bayesian-modeled radiocarbon dating results from sites including Yafteh Cave and Kobeh Cave, the diagnostic molars from Eshkaft-e Gavi and Warwasi, the Sorheh Rockshelter sequence on the southern Alborz, and comparative evidence from Neanderthal- and modern-human-associated sites elsewhere in Southwest Asia and the Caucasus. They differ chiefly in how confidently that evidence supports a specific model of population change and in how much weight to give the technological record as opposed to the more limited, though genuine, skeletal evidence available for Iran specifically.

Why it matters: How this debate is resolved affects broader understanding of how modern humans spread across Southwest Asia and interacted, or did not interact, with Neanderthal populations in this specific region, a question connected to wider, ongoing scientific research into human origins and dispersal across Eurasia.

Student task: Considering the evidence and reasoning described above, explain what kind of additional evidence, skeletal remains, ancient DNA, or further securely dated stratigraphic sequences, would most help resolve this debate, and why.

Multiple Perspectives

Reading the Same Evidence: Perspectives in Paleolithic Archaeology

Note: the perspectives below represent different scholarly and analytical approaches to the same body of evidence, not quotations from named individual researchers.

The lithic (stone tool) specialist: Emphasizes careful classification of stone tool types, manufacturing techniques, and assemblage composition as the most reliable and widely available evidence for tracing technological and, cautiously, cultural change across the Paleolithic sequence.
The zooarchaeologist (specialist in animal remains): Emphasizes faunal assemblages as evidence for subsistence strategies, seasonal site use, and environmental conditions, cautioning that stone tools alone cannot reconstruct the full pattern of Paleolithic diet and resource use.
The paleoanthropologist (specialist in human and hominin skeletal remains): Emphasizes the comparatively limited skeletal evidence from Iranian sites as the most direct, though frustratingly scarce, form of evidence for identifying which hominin populations were actually present, and cautions against relying on stone tool typology alone to identify hominin populations.
The paleoclimatologist (specialist in ancient climate): Emphasizes reconstructed climate and environmental data as essential context for interpreting settlement patterns, arguing that Paleolithic archaeology cannot be fully understood without accounting for the repeated glacial and interglacial cycles of the Pleistocene.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Paleolithic Iran across a Vast Span of Time

What Changed

  • Tool-production strategies changed from core-and-flake and, in the Middle Paleolithic, technologically sophisticated Levallois core-preparation approaches, toward greater emphasis on blade and bladelet production in the Upper Paleolithic and, later, small, specialized Epipaleolithic microliths
  • The hominin populations present on the plateau likely changed over time, from earlier hominin groups possibly present during the Lower Paleolithic, to Neanderthal-associated populations during much of the Middle Paleolithic, to anatomically modern humans by the Upper Paleolithic
  • Settlement and mobility patterns shifted gradually toward more intensive, repeated use of specific favorable locations by the Epipaleolithic period
  • Climate conditions across the Pleistocene fluctuated repeatedly between colder, drier glacial phases and warmer, wetter interglacial phases, altering the resources available to human populations

What Stayed the Same

  • Human and hominin communities across this entire span depended fundamentally on hunting, gathering, and mobility rather than agriculture or permanent settlement
  • The Zagros Mountains continued to attract repeated occupation across multiple Paleolithic phases due to their caves, water sources, and proximity to game
  • Stone remained the primary durable material for tool production throughout the entire Paleolithic sequence
  • The plateau's role as a corridor connecting Southwest Asia, the Caucasus, and Central and South Asia persisted across this entire span, just as it would persist, in different form, throughout the political history examined in later chapters

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity across the Paleolithic prevents students from imagining this period as an undifferentiated, static "prehistoric" backdrop; it was instead a dynamic span of human history in its own right, marked by genuine technological, demographic, and environmental change across an immense span of time.

Cause and Effect

Factors in the Middle-to-Upper Paleolithic Transition

Candidate long-term factors

Broader patterns of population movement, cultural transmission, and technological change across Africa and Southwest Asia over tens of thousands of years, none of which current evidence isolates as a single determining cause of the Zagros transition.

Candidate short-term factors

Specific population movements, environmental changes documented in the Pleistocene climate record, and localized technological innovations occurring within Southwest Asia and the Caucasus in the millennia immediately preceding the documented Zagros transition around forty-five to forty thousand years ago.

Documented outcome

The appearance of blade-based Baradostian and related technologies, worked bone tools, and personal ornaments at Zagros sites including Yafteh Cave, alongside a distinct Initial Upper Paleolithic industry at Sorheh Rockshelter, and direct fossil evidence for Homo sapiens presence at Eshkaft-e Gavi and Warwasi.

This chapter's response

Careful attention to what the Zagros and wider plateau evidence can and cannot establish about the relationship between technological change and population change, avoiding a simple, uncomplicated narrative of population replacement, or of straightforward cognitive advance, that the current evidence does not fully support.

Historical complexity: Current archaeological research does not establish a simple cognitive or behavioral "revolution" as the cause of this transition. Specialists instead debate the relative roles of population movement, demographic change, cultural transmission, technological innovation, regional continuity, and environmental change, and historians should resist reducing this complex, still incompletely understood process to a single cause or a single, tidy narrative.

Everyday Life and Social History

Ordinary Life in Paleolithic Iran

Although this chapter cannot reconstruct specific individuals, families, or events from the Iranian Paleolithic record, the archaeological evidence surveyed throughout this chapter does allow cautious, general statements about the texture of daily life for Paleolithic communities on the plateau. A small band moving seasonally between lowland and highland zones of the Zagros, following the availability of wild goat, gazelle, and other game, would have relied on carefully made stone tools, knowledge of the local terrain and its water sources, and, most likely, close cooperation among group members for successful hunting, food processing, and shelter.

The repeated use of specific caves and rock shelters across many thousands of years, documented at sites such as Warwasi, suggests that Paleolithic groups accumulated and likely transmitted, without writing, detailed practical knowledge of the landscape, including which locations offered reliable shelter, water, and access to game across different seasons. Such knowledge, though invisible in the archaeological record in any direct sense, must be inferred as a necessary condition for the successful, repeated occupation the material evidence documents. This chapter's emphasis on caution regarding specific claims about Paleolithic social life should not obscure the basic point that these were capable, resourceful human communities meeting the demands of a challenging environment across an immense span of time, using tools, knowledge, and cooperation that later chapters of this textbook, in transformed and vastly more complex forms, will trace forward into the agricultural, urban, and political history of Elam, the Achaemenids, and the many societies that followed.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has surveyed the Paleolithic archaeology of the Iranian plateau, an immense span of time extending from the still uncertain earliest hominin presence in the region through the last hunting and gathering societies whose ways of life the Neolithic transition examined in Chapter 3 would eventually transform. It began by establishing the analytical framework archaeologists use to organize this long period, the Lower, Middle, and Upper Paleolithic, together with the Epipaleolithic phase that followed, while cautioning students that this framework is an interpretive tool rather than a set of natural, universally applicable categories.

The chapter examined the Lower Paleolithic evidence on the plateau, including genuine Acheulean bifacial technology documented at surface localities such as those near Jiroft, while emphasizing that this technological richness does not resolve a genuine chronological problem: most Iranian Lower Paleolithic evidence lacks secure dating, and the scarcity of confirmed, well dated contexts likely reflects, in significant part, the history of archaeological research rather than definitive proof of an absence of early hominin activity. It then turned to the far better documented, though still unevenly dated, Middle Paleolithic record concentrated in Zagros cave and rock shelter sites, including Kobeh Cave, Warwasi Rock Shelter, Bisitun Cave, and Wezmeh Cave, examining the Mousterian stone tool tradition and specific direct skeletal evidence, the Wezmeh 1 Neanderthal premolar and the Bisitun Cave human radius, bearing on hominin presence in the Zagros.

The chapter then examined the Middle to Upper Paleolithic transition, dated approximately to between forty-five and forty thousand years ago and varying by site, and its association with blade-based technology, including the Baradostian in the central Zagros and a distinct Initial Upper Paleolithic industry at Sorheh Rockshelter on the southern Alborz. It examined Yafteh Cave's rich material culture, worked bone tools and personal ornaments conventionally, though not fossil-diagnostically, associated with Homo sapiens, alongside the more direct osteological evidence for modern human presence supplied by diagnostic molars from Eshkaft-e Gavi and Warwasi. It presented this transition as a genuine but still actively debated turning point rather than a fully settled historical fact, and it emphasized that the Iranian Upper Paleolithic record reveals a mosaic of regional technological traditions rather than a single, uniform sequence.

Subsequent sections examined the methods archaeologists use to classify stone tools and interpret faunal remains, emphasizing genuine site-to-site variation in subsistence strategy rather than a single uniform "Zagros diet," the role of Pleistocene climate fluctuation in shaping settlement and mobility across the plateau, and the considerable interpretive caution required when reconstructing the social organization of Paleolithic communities from indirect material evidence. The chapter also addressed regional variation across the plateau, noting both that the concentration of documented evidence in the Zagros reflects the history of archaeological fieldwork and that recent work at Sorheh Rockshelter has begun to reveal a distinct northern plateau trajectory, and it examined the Epipaleolithic, or Zarzian, period as a transitional phase whose increasingly intensive resource use, while genuinely connected to later developments, should not be read as a smooth or predetermined march toward the agricultural transformation examined in the next chapter.

Throughout, this chapter has emphasized a methodological lesson with implications extending well beyond the Paleolithic period specifically: historians and archaeologists must distinguish carefully between what available evidence can support and what remains uncertain, provisional, or actively debated, resisting both unwarranted confidence and unwarranted skepticism when interpreting a sparse and unevenly distributed archaeological record. This lesson will remain relevant as later chapters address the Neolithic transition, the rise of complex societies, and the archaeology of Elam, each of which continues to depend, in varying degrees, on the same kinds of material evidence and the same interpretive caution this chapter has introduced.

The Iranian plateau examined in this chapter was, probably for hundreds of thousands of years, a landscape shaped and inhabited by successive hominin and human populations long before any of the political, religious, linguistic, or cultural categories examined throughout the remainder of this textbook came into existence. Understanding this deep history is not merely a preliminary curiosity; it establishes that the plateau's long historical significance as a distinctive, connective landscape, introduced geographically in Chapter 1, extends back across an almost unimaginably long span of human presence, setting the stage for the transformative developments Chapter 3 examines next.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Paleolithic
The "old stone age," the long period of human and hominin history characterized by chipped stone tool technology, preceding agriculture and settled village life.
Lower Paleolithic
The earliest Paleolithic phase, including Acheulean bifacial technology in Iran; its chronology on the Iranian plateau remains poorly constrained, since most evidence derives from undated surface contexts.
Acheulean
A Lower Paleolithic technological tradition characterized by carefully shaped bifacial tools, including handaxes and cleavers, documented at surface localities in Iran such as those near Jiroft, though lacking secure absolute dating.
Middle Paleolithic
A Paleolithic phase associated across much of Southwest Asia with flake-based Mousterian tool technology and, in various regions, Neanderthal populations; in wider regional schemes it extends beyond 200,000 years ago, though Iran's own well documented record concentrates after roughly 50,000 years ago.
Upper Paleolithic
A Paleolithic phase associated with blade-based tool technology, worked bone and antler tools, and, across much of Southwest Asia, the spread of anatomically modern humans; in Iran it comprises a mosaic of regional traditions rather than a single uniform industry.
Epipaleolithic (Zarzian)
The final hunter-gatherer phase preceding the Neolithic transition, associated in the Zagros with the Zarzian microlithic tradition; its absolute chronology remains poorly established at most sites.
Hominin
A term referring to modern humans and their close fossil relatives and ancestors, used to discuss populations that may not belong to the species Homo sapiens.
Homo neanderthalensis (Neanderthals)
A hominin population widely distributed across Ice Age Europe and Southwest Asia; the Wezmeh 1 premolar from Wezmeh Cave provides the first direct evidence of Neanderthal presence on the western Iranian plateau.
Homo sapiens (anatomically modern humans)
The species to which all living humans belong; on the Iranian plateau it is directly documented by diagnostic molars from Eshkaft-e Gavi and Warwasi, while Upper Paleolithic material culture at sites such as Yafteh Cave is conventionally, though not fossil-diagnostically, associated with this species.
Mousterian
A Middle Paleolithic flake-based stone tool tradition documented widely across Southwest Asia, the Caucasus, and Europe, including at several Zagros cave sites.
Baradostian
An Upper Paleolithic blade-based stone tool tradition especially well documented at Zagros sites including Yafteh Cave, understood as one strand within a broader mosaic of regional Upper Paleolithic traditions rather than a single Zagros-wide industry.
Rostamian
A proposed distinct southern Zagros Upper Paleolithic tradition, identified through assemblages such as those from Ghār-e Boof, illustrating regional diversity within the Iranian Upper Paleolithic.
Levallois technique
A method of carefully preparing a stone core to produce standardized flakes, a technologically sophisticated approach associated with Mousterian and related Middle Paleolithic industries across a wide geographic area.
Microlith
A very small, standardized stone tool, often hafted in combination with others, characteristic of Epipaleolithic (Zarzian) technology.
Lithic assemblage
A collection of stone tools recovered together from a given archaeological context, analyzed to identify technological traditions and behavior.
Faunal assemblage
A collection of animal bones recovered from an archaeological context, analyzed to reconstruct subsistence practices and environmental conditions.
Stratigraphy
The layered sequence of sediment and archaeological deposits at a site, used to establish relative chronology, with lower layers generally, though not always straightforwardly, older than upper layers.
Radiocarbon dating
A chronometric dating method measuring the decay of a radioactive carbon isotope in organic material, reliable within a specific range and subject to margins of error.
Chronometric dating
Scientific dating techniques, including radiocarbon and other methods, used to establish approximate absolute ages for archaeological deposits.
Pleistocene epoch
The geological epoch lasting from roughly 2.6 million to about 11,700 years ago, characterized by repeated glacial and interglacial climate cycles, within which nearly this entire chapter's subject falls.
Holocene epoch
The current geological epoch, beginning approximately 11,700 years ago, whose warmer, more stable climate provides the immediate backdrop for the Neolithic transition examined in Chapter 3.
Last Glacial Maximum
A period of especially cold, arid climate conditions roughly twenty-six to nineteen thousand years ago, likely constraining Paleolithic settlement across much of Southwest Asia.
Rock shelter
A shallow cave or overhang used for shelter, a common and archaeologically important type of Paleolithic site in the Zagros Mountains.
Hunter-gatherer
A society or population subsisting through hunting wild animals and gathering wild plant foods rather than through agriculture.
Seasonal mobility
A pattern of movement across the landscape tied to seasonal changes in the availability of game, plants, and water, characteristic of many Paleolithic and Epipaleolithic communities.
Southwest Asia
A broad regional term encompassing the Levant, Mesopotamia, the Iranian plateau, and adjoining areas, used by archaeologists to situate the Iranian Paleolithic record within its wider comparative context.
Personal ornament (in Paleolithic archaeology)
A worked object, such as a perforated shell, deer canine, or pigment, interpreted as evidence of symbolic or social signaling, documented at Yafteh Cave; not by itself a reliable marker of which hominin species produced it.

Major Sites and Evidence

Identification & Significance

Kobeh Cave
Central Zagros Middle Paleolithic site with Mousterian tools and a caprine-dominated faunal assemblage; taphonomic analysis supports active hunting rather than scavenging as the primary means of prey acquisition.
Warwasi Rock Shelter
Long, stratified Zagros sequence spanning Middle Paleolithic through Epipaleolithic occupation and the source of an isolated fossil modern human molar from its Upper Paleolithic levels; an important comparative typological sequence, though it lacks extensive absolute dating and its continuity across the Middle-to-Upper Paleolithic transition remains debated.
Bisitun Cave
Zagros cave with Middle Paleolithic occupation, including a fragmentary human right radius from its Level F+ deposits, located near, but historically unrelated to, the later Achaemenid inscription examined in Chapter 11.
Wezmeh Cave
Zagros cave whose carnivore-dominated Late Pleistocene deposits yielded Wezmeh 1, an isolated human premolar assessed as Neanderthal, the first direct evidence of Neanderthal presence on the western Iranian plateau.
Eshkaft-e Gavi
Southern Zagros cave that yielded ten fragmentary hominin specimens, including a diagnostically modern human molar from the base of its Upper Paleolithic sequence, some bones bearing cut marks and burning.
Sorheh Rockshelter
Southern Alborz site with a stratified Initial Upper Paleolithic assemblage dated to roughly 50,000–35,000 years ago, demonstrating Upper Paleolithic technological diversity beyond the central Zagros.
Yafteh Cave
Key Upper Paleolithic site with blade-based technology, worked bone, and personal ornaments conventionally associated with, though not fossil-diagnostic of, Homo sapiens; central to discussions of the Baradostian and Zagros Aurignacian.
Mousterian Industry
Middle Paleolithic flake-based stone tool tradition documented extensively at Zagros cave sites.
Baradostian Industry
Upper Paleolithic blade-based stone tool tradition especially well documented at Yafteh Cave, one strand within a broader mosaic of Iranian Upper Paleolithic traditions.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to think historically about a period that left no writing, no named individuals, and no direct testimony of any kind. That absence does not mean the period lacks historical significance or that nothing responsible can be said about it. Instead, it requires a different, more explicitly evidence-conscious mode of historical reasoning, one that will recur, in modified form, throughout the remainder of this textbook whenever archaeological evidence must substitute for or supplement written sources.

The Paleolithic communities examined in this chapter inhabited the same mountains, valleys, and corridors that Chapter 1 described as the enduring physical framework of Iranian history, and that later chapters will show shaping the Achaemenid, Sasanian, Islamic, Safavid, Qajar, and modern history of the plateau. Their presence, extending across a span of time vastly longer than the rest of this textbook's subject combined, establishes that the human relationship with this landscape is far older than any state, any religion, or any language family examined in the chapters that follow.

Students who have absorbed this chapter's central methodological lessons, distinguishing evidence from inference, recognizing the limits of stone tool typology, and treating archaeological visibility as a product of research history rather than a complete record of ancient life, are well equipped to approach Chapter 3's account of the Neolithic transition with appropriate care. That chapter will examine how some of the behavioral and technological trends already visible in this chapter's discussion of the Epipaleolithic developed into the domestication of plants and animals and the beginnings of settled village life, a transformation that did not erase the deep human history of the plateau but built directly upon it.

The story of Paleolithic Iran is, in the end, a story about adaptation, resourcefulness, and the accumulation of practical knowledge across an immense span of human generations, recovered only through the patient, careful work of archaeological science. It is an appropriate and necessary foundation for the one hundred and six chapters that follow.
