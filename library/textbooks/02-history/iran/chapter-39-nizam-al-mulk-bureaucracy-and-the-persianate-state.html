Nizam al-Mulk, Bureaucracy, and the Persianate State

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

Conquest could place a Turkic dynasty on the thrones of Iran in a few decades. Governing what it had conquered required records, revenues, appointments, soldiers who were paid, officials who were watched, and an account of why the ruler deserved obedience. The vizier Nizam al-Mulk spent nearly three decades at the center of that work, and the system he helped direct was strong, adaptable, and at the same time dependent on people who could be removed in a single afternoon.

In October 1092, a procession of the Seljuk court was moving through the hills of western Iran toward Baghdad. The sultan Malik Shah and his household were traveling in the same season as the army, the treasury officials, the secretaries, and the animals and servants that a mobile court required. Among them was an old man in a litter, one of the best-known men in the Islamic world: Abu ʿAli al-Hasan b. ʿAli, called Nizam al-Mulk, "the order of the realm," who had served as chief minister for about thirty years. Near the place called Sahna, close to Nahavand, he was stabbed to death by a man dressed as a Sufi. Within about five weeks the sultan was dead as well. The empire that had been governed by the two men, one a sovereign of Oghuz descent and one a Persian from the district of Tus, had lost both its head and its principal administrator.

Chapter 38 told the story of how the Seljuks came to dominate Khurasan, the plateau, Iraq, and part of the Caucasus and Syria. This chapter asks a different question. It asks what made that territory governable. Armies can capture a city, but a state has to assess its crops, move money to soldiers who may be hundreds of miles from the capital, appoint and supervise governors, answer petitions, send orders that will be understood, and persuade influential scholars and preachers that the regime is legitimate. Those tasks were carried out by people trained in reading, accounting, law, and correspondence, and by institutions that had grown up under the Sasanians, the Abbasids, and the regional dynasties examined in Chapters 31 and 35 through 37.

The central question of the chapter is how Nizam al-Mulk helped turn Seljuk military domination into an organized Persianate system of government, and why that system remained vulnerable to the personal, dynastic, and military rivalries it was meant to control. The question has two halves, and both matter. The first half concerns achievement: the ways in which an experienced administrator, working within a tradition he inherited, helped make the Seljuk state financially coherent, politically legible, and religiously respectable. The second half concerns limits. His government depended on the favor of a sultan, on the cooperation of commanders who held revenue assignments, on the loyalty of provincial officials, and on a household network that produced as many enemies as friends.

The chapter will not treat Nizam al-Mulk as the inventor of the vizierate, of Persian bureaucracy, of the madrasa, or of the iqtaʿ. Each of these had a long earlier history, and part of the work of the chapter is to show what he inherited. His historical importance lies in adaptation, supervision, patronage, and political use. It will also not treat him as a saint of administrative virtue. Medieval biographies and chronicles often reshape him into the ideal minister, and a historian has to separate what the documents and the best-informed narratives support from the stories later writers wanted to tell.

The chapter draws on a very unequal body of evidence. The most famous text associated with him, the Siyasatnama ("Book of Government"), is a treatise on kingship and administration whose date, authorship, and relationship to real practice are all debated. Chronicles written in Arabic and Persian, including works composed a century or more after his death, supply most of the narrative. Biographical dictionaries preserve information on scholars and schools. Letters, chancery collections, and a small number of surviving documents show how officials wrote. Buildings and endowments survive unevenly. Almost no ordinary taxpayer has left a record of how this government felt from below, and the chapter therefore tries to read the sources for what they reveal about cultivators, merchants, and townspeople as well as about courtiers.

The chapter belongs to a division of labor within Volume III. Chapter 38 supplies the conquest narrative and the royal chronology; this chapter refers to them without retelling them. The religious and theological history of the Sunni revival and of Shiʿi communities belongs to Chapter 40, the history of Ismailism and the Nizari state to Chapter 41, Persian literary memory to Chapter 42, learning and philosophy to Chapter 43, and the Khwarazmian successors to Chapter 44. Here those subjects enter only as far as they explain the working of Seljuk government and the career of its most celebrated minister.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace the Iranian secretarial and vizierial tradition from the Sasanian and Abbasid periods through the Samanids, Buyids, and Ghaznavids, and distinguish what the Seljuks inherited from what they adapted.
  2. Evaluate the biographical evidence for Nizam al-Mulk's origins and early career, separating documented information from idealized anecdote.
  3. Explain how Nizam al-Mulk obtained and held the vizierate between about 1063 and 1092, and why his tenure depended on political alliances rather than institutional security.
  4. Describe the principal offices of the central Seljuk government and the difference between organization prescribed in texts and organization documented in practice.
  5. Analyze how taxation, iqtaʿ assignments, military pay, and provincial supervision worked together, and why the state could not fully control the military and local interests on which it relied.
  6. Interpret the Siyasatnama both as a statement of political ideas and as a historical source whose authorship, transmission, and relation to practice are debated.
  7. Compare the advice of the Siyasatnama on justice, appointments, intelligence, and the army with the evidence for actual Seljuk practice.
  8. Assess the Nizamiyya colleges and the wider network of scholarly patronage, explaining what Nizam al-Mulk inherited and what he extended.
  9. Examine how taxation, military demands, and official misconduct affected cultivators, townspeople, merchants, and religious elites, and who had effective access to justice.
  10. Evaluate competing accounts of the court conflict, the assassination of October 1092, and the succession crisis, distinguishing contemporary evidence from later attribution.
  11. Assess whether Nizam al-Mulk created durable institutions or a highly effective personal administration, and explain why the question cannot be answered by a single formula.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did Nizam al-Mulk help convert Seljuk military supremacy into a more durable system of government, and why did that system remain exposed to personal, dynastic, and military rivalries?
  2. What did Nizam al-Mulk inherit from Sasanian, Abbasid, Samanid, Buyid, and Ghaznavid administrative traditions, and what can fairly be attributed to him?
  3. Why is the early biography of a famous minister difficult to reconstruct, and how should a historian treat stories that make his life exemplary?
  4. How far could the central government supervise provincial officials, revenue-assignment holders, and soldiers who depended on it for pay?
  5. Does the Siyasatnama describe Seljuk administration as it was, as it ought to have been, or as its author wished the sultan to believe it could be?
  6. What difference does it make that the authorship and transmission of the Siyasatnama are debated?
  7. Why did Nizam al-Mulk's educational and religious patronage become central to his reputation, and how uniform was his religious policy?
  8. Who supplied the revenue and labor that sustained the state, who benefited from its patronage, and who had access to justice?
  9. How should historians evaluate the conflicting accounts of the conflict between sultan and vizier, the assassination of 1092, and responsibility for it?
  10. Was Nizam al-Mulk's achievement the building of lasting institutions, or the skilled management of a particular set of relationships that his death exposed?

Historical Context

The Seljuk state that Nizam al-Mulk served was the third successive military regime to dominate the eastern Islamic world in about a century. The Samanids, examined in Chapter 35, had ruled Transoxiana and Khurasan as Abbasid governors who became effectively independent, and their court at Bukhara had become a center of Arabic and New Persian writing. The Buyids, examined in Chapter 36, had taken Baghdad in 945 and supervised the caliphs while governing Iraq and much of western Iran through a family confederation. The Ghaznavids, examined in Chapter 37, had built a military monarchy based at Ghazna with a Persianate court and a powerful secretarial corps. Each regime had to answer the same practical questions about money, soldiers, and legitimacy, and each left a body of officials, procedures, and precedents that the next regime used.

The central problem of government in this world was the relation between a ruling military elite and a literate administrative class. Turkic and Daylami commanders held power through armed followings. Administrators, usually called secretaries (kuttab, singular katib), held power through knowledge: they could read the registers, calculate the tax due from a district, draft a diploma of appointment in correct Arabic or Persian, and correspond with the caliph's chancery. Neither group could easily do without the other, and neither fully trusted the other. Soldiers resented civilians who controlled their pay, and secretaries feared soldiers who could dismiss or kill them. A vizier stood at the point where those relations were negotiated, which is why the office was both powerful and precarious.

The administrative inheritance was long. Sasanian rule had relied on a class of scribes (dabirs) and on a developed memory of royal administration that Muslim writers later idealized. The early Abbasid caliphate had absorbed many Iranian families into its secretarial service and had produced a large literature on the duties of secretaries, the arrangement of the dīvāns, and the collection of the land tax. Under the Buyids, viziers such as Ibn al-ʿAmid and al-Sahib Ibn ʿAbbad became figures of literary as well as political importance, and under the Ghaznavids the administration was documented in the Persian history of Bayhaqi and in the chancery's correspondence. By the middle of the eleventh century there existed a recognizable professional culture of administration across Iraq, Iran, and Central Asia. Section 39.1 describes it more closely.

The social setting was also changing. Chapter 38 described how Oghuz groups entered Khurasan and how the Seljuk family established authority across the plateau and in Baghdad. The new rulers distributed power among relatives, maintained a mobile court, relied on Turkmen cavalry and slave soldiers, and paid their armies increasingly through assignments of revenue. They needed an administration able to assess and collect revenue on a large scale, to support armies that moved with the court or were stationed in distant provinces, and to deal with a caliphate whose recognition they sought. They also needed to persuade the urban Sunni scholars, judges, and preachers, who shaped opinion in the cities, that Turkic rule was legitimate. The Seljuk sultans had some of these resources themselves and acquired the rest by employing officials who already possessed them.

The religious setting was contested. Sunni Islam in the eleventh century comprised several schools of law and competing theological currents, and Shiʿi communities of several kinds lived in the same cities. The Fatimid caliphate in Egypt, an Ismaili rival to the Abbasids, sent missionaries into Iran and Iraq. After 1090 a new Ismaili movement based at Alamut posed a separate challenge to the Seljuk state. A minister like Nizam al-Mulk had to manage relations among scholars, schools, and sects while the sultan, who followed his own school of law, expected political loyalty. Religion was therefore part of administration, not a separate subject, and this chapter treats it as such while reserving doctrinal and institutional history for Chapters 40 and 41.

Finally, the chapter has to be read against a tradition of biography that made Nizam al-Mulk a model. Later Persian and Arabic authors recorded his piety, generosity, and shrewdness, and attached to him stories about his childhood, his wealth, and his friendships. Some of those stories were evidently invented or reshaped, and the most famous, a legend that he, the poet Omar Khayyam, and the future Ismaili leader Hasan-i Sabbah were schoolmates who swore a pact, is not accepted by historians as reliable evidence. A historian can respect the real importance of the man while refusing to rely on the legend that grew around him.

Section 39.1

The Iranian Bureaucratic Tradition before Nizam al-Mulk

A student who meets Nizam al-Mulk first as a famous vizier may assume that Persian bureaucracy begins with him. It does not. By the time he was born, administration in the eastern Islamic world had a history of several centuries, and the officials who served the Seljuks were the heirs of a professional tradition that had survived changes of dynasty, language, and religion. To understand what he did, it is necessary to see what he found.

The longest background is Sasanian. The Sasanian state, which earlier volumes of this textbook examined, employed a class of scribes, the dabirs, who kept the records of taxation and the army and drafted royal correspondence. Muslim writers of the ninth and tenth centuries remembered Sasanian administration as an orderly and just system, and they attributed to early Sasanian kings, especially Ardashir I and Khusraw I, reforms of taxation and the army. Historians treat much of this memory with care. The surviving evidence for Sasanian administrative practice is thin, and the later image of an ideal Iranian government was partly shaped by Abbasid-era authors who had reasons to present it as a model. What can be said with reasonable confidence is that the conquering Arabs did not replace the existing tax administration of Iran at once. In many provinces they retained Persian-speaking officials and Persian methods of record keeping for decades, and the translation of administrative practice into Arabic was a gradual process.

The Abbasid caliphate, founded in the middle of the eighth century, drew heavily on Iranian secretarial families. Some of them, such as the Barmakids, rose to extraordinary power, and the office of vizier became the chief ministerial position of the caliphate. The Abbasid period produced a rich literature on the profession: manuals for secretaries, treatises on the land tax, and works on the organization of the dīvāns. A secretary was expected to master Arabic prose, accounting, and the formulas of official correspondence, and to understand the legal basis of taxation. This professional culture, often called the culture of the adab of the secretary, was Arabic in language but transmitted many Iranian ideals. The vizierate itself varied in authority. In some periods the vizier was a delegated sovereign, in others a financial administrator whose power depended on the caliph's favor, and Abbasid writers debated the proper limits of the office. These debates were part of the background that Nizam al-Mulk knew.

When the Abbasid center weakened, regional dynasties built their own administrations from the same materials. The Samanids governed Khurasan and Transoxiana from Bukhara with a vizier, a body of dīvāns, and a literate elite that increasingly wrote in New Persian as well as Arabic. The Buyids, who held Baghdad and the plateau, employed Iranian and Arab secretaries and developed the use of revenue assignments for their soldiers. The Ghaznavids, whose sultans were of Turkic military origin, relied on a Persianate secretarial corps, and the chancery of Ghazna produced the correspondence and the narrative history of which the work of Bayhaqi is the best-known survivor. Each dynasty adapted the system, and officials moved among them. A secretary who served a Samanid prince might find employment with a Buyid amir or a Ghaznavid governor, bringing his methods and his connections with him.

The status of the principal officers can be described in general terms. The katib, or secretary, was a member of a profession whose prestige rested on literacy and skill. The vizier, wazir, was the highest civil officer, appointed by the ruler and responsible for the administration and finance of the state; in many courts he was also the main channel through which the ruler communicated with officials. Financial officers had charge of the registers of revenue and expenditure. Provincial governors, sometimes military commanders and sometimes civil officials, represented the ruler in the regions. Chapter 31 has examined how the Abbasid state incorporated Iranian elites, and the point to retain here is that these were not Seljuk inventions.

What then did the Seljuks change? They introduced a different relationship between ruler and administration. The Seljuk sultans were Turkic military leaders from a pastoral background, and they governed through a family confederation, a mobile court, and an army that was paid increasingly through revenue assignments. The administrators who served them had to work within that context. They had to move with a court that wintered in one region and campaigned in another. They had to coordinate with princes who had their own courts and officials. And they had to deal with commanders who were not simply servants of the vizier but holders of independent revenue and military followings. The inherited institutions persisted, but the political conditions under which they operated were new.

The sources for this background are mainly Arabic and Persian: administrative manuals, chronicles, biographical dictionaries, and literary works. They were mostly written by or for members of the secretarial class and therefore reflect its values and its sense of its own importance. They are more reliable on the vocabulary and ideals of administration than on how well any particular system worked. A treatise on the duties of a secretary describes what the author thought a good secretary should do, which may differ from what secretaries in fact did. This caution applies with special force to the Siyasatnama, discussed in Division IV.

Historical Vocabulary: Vizier, Katib, Dīvān, Barid
Vizier (wazir) · Katib · Dīvān · Barid

Definition. The vizier was the chief civil minister, appointed by a ruler to direct the administration and finance of the state. A katib (plural kuttab) was a secretary, a professional trained in writing, accounting, and official correspondence. A dīvān was both an administrative office and the register that it kept, and the plural dīvāns refers to the departments of government. The barid was the postal and information service by which messengers carried official correspondence and reports between the court and the provinces.

Historical context. All four terms are older than the Seljuks. The vocabulary of the dīvān is generally traced to the early caliphate, where Arabic words were attached to practices of record keeping that had existed in the Byzantine and Sasanian territories. The barid developed under the Abbasids as a system of relays and was associated with the gathering of news as well as the carrying of letters. The vizierate acquired much of its later form in the Abbasid period.

Why it matters. These words signal institutional continuity. When a Seljuk source speaks of the dīvān of the vizier, it uses the language of earlier regimes, and a reader must ask whether the word describes an identical institution or an inherited name applied to a changed practice.

Potential misunderstanding. The word "vizier" is often translated as "prime minister." That translation can mislead, because a medieval vizier did not hold office through an assembly or constitution and could be removed, imprisoned, or killed at the ruler's will. His authority was delegated and personal, and its extent varied with the ruler and the period.

Section 39.2

Nizam al-Mulk's Origins, Family, and Education in Khurasan

The man who became Nizam al-Mulk was born Abu ʿAli al-Hasan, son of ʿAli, son of Ishaq, in the district of Tus in Khurasan. The year is traditionally given as 408 of the Islamic calendar, which corresponds to about 1018, and the place is usually described as a village or small town in the Tus district, but specialist references differ over the precise locality: the TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi identifies Radkan, while Encyclopaedia Iranica identifies Nawqan (Nawghan), both in the Tus area. The birth year and place are reconstructed from medieval biography, not independently documented by a contemporary birth record.

Tus lay in northeastern Iran, a region of mixed farming, with a town that had been important in the early Islamic period and that later became associated with the poet Firdawsi, whose work is the subject of Chapter 42. The district was part of the larger Khurasani world of oasis cities, irrigated fields, and mountain valleys described in Chapter 1. At the time of his birth, Khurasan was ruled by the Ghaznavids, and the region's administration was in the hands of Ghaznavid governors and Persian-speaking officials. A boy born into a family of local standing could expect an education and, if fortunate, a career in the service of the state.

His family background is reported in general terms. Later sources say that his father, ʿAli, held a position as a revenue official or agent, and that the family belonged to the class of landholders and officials known in Persian as dihqans, a rural and provincial gentry with ties to the land and to local administration. This description is plausible and consistent with what is known of other officials of the period, but the details vary between sources, and the dihqan pedigree was a flattering label that a later author might apply to a famous man. The safest statement is that he came from a family with some local standing and with connections to the administrative world, which would explain both his education and his early opportunities.

His education followed the usual lines for a boy of that standing. He studied the Qurʾan and Arabic, the foundations of religious law, and the traditions of the Prophet, and he acquired the writing and accounting skills that a secretary required. The sources associate his studies with the scholarly centers of Khurasan, including Nishapur, a major city whose scholars were famous for their learning in law and traditions. He is described as a follower of the Shafiʿi school of law, and his lifelong association with Shafiʿi and Ashʿari scholars is a theme of Division V. It is probable that he received a serious religious education, because his later patronage of scholars and his ability to converse with them were widely noted. It is much less clear which teachers he had in particular, and what he learned from them is mostly inferred.

Students should be cautious about the childhood anecdotes that later authors supply. Biographies of exemplary men tended to project signs of destiny backward into childhood: early piety, extraordinary memory, a wise word at a decisive moment, a sacrifice made by a parent. Such stories are common in medieval biographical literature about scholars, saints, and ministers. They are valuable as evidence of how later generations wished to remember the man and of what qualities they associated with a good minister. They are not evidence of what happened in his boyhood. The best-known fabrication, the story that he studied with Omar Khayyam and Hasan-i Sabbah and that the three made a pact, fails on chronological grounds as well as on the character of the sources, since the three men were of different ages and the story first appears in much later works.

The sources for his early life thus fall into three layers. The first is information that other historians could have known, such as his origin in Tus and his later service. The second is information that comes from his own circle or later admirers, which may be accurate but is shaped by their purposes. The third is the legendary material, which belongs to the history of reputation. A historian reconstructing his formation has to rely mostly on the first layer, use the second with caution, and treat the third as evidence for the afterlife of his image. This method will be used throughout the chapter, because the same problem affects the stories about his wealth, prescience, and virtue.

One consequence is that any claim about what his early experience taught him is an inference. It is reasonable to say that a Persian-speaking official's son raised in Khurasan under the Ghaznavids would have seen a functioning provincial administration, would have known the practice of revenue collection, and would have lived through the disorder that came with the Seljuk conquest of the 1030s. A boy in his early twenties in Khurasan in 1040 saw the Ghaznavid regime collapse in the province, and his career was shaped by how he and his patrons responded. Section 39.3 turns to that response.

Section 39.3

Early Administrative Service: From the Ghaznavids to the Seljuks

Before the Seljuks appeared, the Ghaznavid state was the dominant power in Khurasan, and a young man of Nizam al-Mulk's education would have sought employment with its administration or with its officials. The standard biographical tradition says that he entered the service of a Ghaznavid official in the east, in the region of Balkh, and began a career in the secretarial and financial work of the dīvān. The names and the sequence of his early employers vary in the sources, and the account of his first steps is among the less secure parts of his biography. What is reliable is the pattern: a Persian secretary from Khurasan, trained in the Ghaznavid world, who found himself in the 1040s and 1050s serving the new Seljuk authorities.

The Ghaznavid collapse in Khurasan, which Chapters 37 and 38 described from different sides, produced a crisis for the officials of the old regime. The Seljuks won Merv and Nishapur in the late 1030s and defeated the Ghaznavid sultan Masʿud at Dandanqan in 1040. The administrators who had served the Ghaznavids had to decide whether to follow the defeated dynasty to Ghazna, to withdraw from public life, or to offer their skills to the conquerors. Many chose the third course. They had the knowledge that the new rulers lacked: how the land was assessed, who held what rights, which officials could be trusted, and how to write the documents that cities and the caliph's court would recognize. The Seljuk chiefs, who had not previously governed settled territory, had every reason to employ them.

Nizam al-Mulk was one of those who made the transition. According to the traditional account, he entered the service of the Seljuk authorities in Khurasan after leaving Ghaznavid employment, and he came to the attention of Chaghri, the brother of Tughril, who ruled Khurasan from Merv. His later association with Chaghri's son Alp Arslan is better attested than his first employment, and the surviving narratives agree that he became an administrator in Khurasan under Seljuk rule. How rapidly he advanced, how he obtained his first offices, and which patrons helped him are matters for which the sources give conflicting and sometimes embellished accounts.

The skills that made such a man valuable can be listed with reasonable confidence. He knew how to keep and audit registers of revenue. He could draft in correct prose the documents that a government used. He understood the system of land assessment and the methods of tax collection. He knew the families, scholars, and local notables of Khurasan. And he was a Muslim of the Sunni scholarly tradition, which meant that he could negotiate with judges, preachers, and the religious elite of the cities. These were precisely the competences that a Turkic dynasty without prior experience of governing cities required. Their value did not depend on a particular dynasty, which is why such secretaries survived regime changes.

The relationship that such an official had with his patron was a relationship of mutual dependence, not equality. The patron provided protection, salary, and the possibility of advancement. The official provided competence, information, and loyalty, and often a network of kin and clients who could be placed in the patron's service. The early career of Nizam al-Mulk can be read as a series of such relationships. In each, he gained position by serving the interests of a patron, and he gained influence by building his own following of subordinates. This pattern would continue when he became vizier, and it would later produce the household network that critics attacked and that his sons inherited.

It is also important to note the geographic dimension of his early life. Khurasan in the middle of the eleventh century was a region of many centers: Nishapur, Merv, Balkh, Herat, Tus, and others. Officials moved among them, and the new Seljuk rulers moved as well. The geography of his service, from Tus to Balkh and Merv and then to the court of the Great Sultan, is part of the story of how the Seljuk state organized its territory. The Geography Study below sets out the places that mattered to him and to the administration that he later directed.

Its cities were oases or river-valley centers whose wealth derived from irrigated farming, textile production, and trade routes to Central Asia and India. It was also a center of religious scholarship. Nishapur, in particular, had a strong tradition of law and theology.

The central and western plateau (Rayy, Hamadan, Isfahan). After the 1040s and 1050s, these cities became central to Seljuk government. Rayy had been a major city for centuries, and Isfahan became an important seat of the court in the later eleventh century. They lay on the main east-west routes of the plateau and controlled a rich agricultural hinterland.

Iraq (Baghdad). The seat of the Abbasid caliph, the largest city of the region, and the place where the sultan's legitimacy was affirmed. Baghdad housed the most famous Nizamiyya college, and the sultan's court visited it periodically.

Nahavand and the western road. On the route between the plateau and Iraq, Nahavand marks the area where the vizier was killed in 1092. The road from Isfahan through Hamadan and Nahavand toward Baghdad was a major axis of movement for the court.

Students should remember that the Seljuk court did not reside permanently in one capital. Officials had to travel or keep correspondence moving along these roads, and the administrative geography was shaped by itineraries as well as by fixed centers. The political boundaries of the period were not those of any modern state, and the cities named here lie today in several countries.

Section 39.4

The Rise of a Persian Secretary in the Service of Turkic Princes

By about 1060 Nizam al-Mulk was in the service of Alp Arslan, the son of Chaghri, who governed Khurasan on behalf of the Seljuk family. The prince was a seasoned military commander in his thirties who had campaigned on the frontier and was the leading figure of the eastern Seljuk lands. He needed an administrator who could manage the revenues and officials of Khurasan, and the sources describe Nizam al-Mulk as holding that role. Some accounts call him a secretary and administrator, others an effective chief of the prince's administration, and the precise title and powers at this stage are not clear. The essential point is the partnership: an able Persian official acting as the civilian arm of a Turkic prince.

The relationship between a military ruler and an experienced secretary rested on specific exchanges. The prince gave authority. He could order commanders to obey, impose sanctions, and grant offices. The secretary supplied information and organization. He knew how much revenue a district should yield, who owed what, how to pay troops on time, and how to write letters that would be accepted as legitimate in Baghdad or Nishapur. A prince who tried to govern without such a man risked financial confusion, disorder among his officials, and the loss of the support of the urban elite. A secretary who lost the prince's favor lost everything. Both parties knew this, and the relationship was therefore one of mutual need that could also become one of mutual suspicion.

Nizam al-Mulk's rise should not be treated as inevitable or as the simple reward of merit. He advanced in an environment of competing officials and patrons. At the court of the Great Sultan Tughril, the principal civil officer was the vizier al-Kunduri, a man of great experience who had held high office for years and who had his own network of followers. A rival in Khurasan, even a talented one, had to depend on the prince's protection and on his own ability to build a reputation among commanders and notables. When Tughril died in 1063, the question of who would be the next sultan, and who would be his vizier, depended on a contest that Nizam al-Mulk's patron won. In that sense his advancement was inseparable from Alp Arslan's success.

The personal qualities attributed to him in this period include shrewdness, patience, and an ability to manage commanders and scholars. These come from sources that wrote of him as a great man, and some of the specific anecdotes are doubtful. But the broad picture is corroborated by the facts of his career. He survived and prospered in a political world in which many officials fell, he retained the trust of two successive sultans, and he assembled a large network of supporters. These facts imply political skill of a high order, even if the details of how he exercised it are uncertain.

The partnership that developed between Turkic rulers and Persian secretaries is sometimes summarized in a formula that associates the Turks with the sword and the Persians with the pen. The formula expresses something that medieval writers themselves recognized, and Chapter 38 has already warned that it oversimplifies. Some secretaries commanded troops or held revenue assignments, some Turkic commanders took part in administrative decisions, and families of both groups intermarried and formed alliances across ethnic lines. Nizam al-Mulk's career illustrates the limits of the formula. As a civilian official he built up a military following, owned slaves who served as soldiers, and placed relatives in provincial governorships, and he became a significant political actor in his own right.

The mutual dependence also shaped the political culture of the Seljuk court. Turkic princes learned the language and conventions of Persian administration and patronized Persian poets and scholars. Persian officials adapted to the customs of a court in which the ruler might hunt, campaign, and move with his tents. The result was a Persianate court culture in which Turkic and Iranian elements combined, and which later dynasties would imitate. The Persianate character of the state was not simply an Iranian survival under foreign rulers. It was a joint product, created by officials such as Nizam al-Mulk in cooperation with rulers who valued it for its practical and ceremonial uses.

The close of this division brings the narrative to the threshold of 1063. By then Nizam al-Mulk had acquired a formation as scholar and secretary, a body of administrative experience in the Ghaznavid and Seljuk worlds, and a place in the entourage of the prince who would become Great Sultan. None of this made his later power certain. As the next division shows, the succession struggle of 1063 and 1064 was a dangerous test in which his career, and perhaps his life, depended on the outcome.

Section 39.5

The Succession of 1063 and Nizam al-Mulk's Appointment

Tughril died in September 1063 at Rayy without a surviving son, and Chapter 38 has described the military and dynastic contest that followed. This section concentrates on its administrative side: what the change of sultan meant for the office of vizier, and how the man who had directed Tughril's government came to be replaced by the secretary who had served Alp Arslan in Khurasan.

The vizier of Tughril was Abu Nasr Muhammad b. Mansur al-Kunduri, a man from the Nishapur region who is usually called by the honorific ʿAmid al-Mulk, "pillar of the realm." He had served Tughril from the early years of Seljuk power in Khurasan and had accompanied him west, and the sources credit him with a large role in organizing the administration of the first sultanate and in negotiating with the caliphate. He was therefore the senior civilian figure of the dynasty, with his own network of officials and clients. The sources also associate him with a measure that proved controversial in Khurasan, the pressure on Ashʿari preachers and scholars in the 1050s, which Chapter 38 has noted and Section 39.27 returns to. Whatever his merits, he was identified with a particular religious and political position.

According to the narratives, al-Kunduri supported the candidacy of Sulayman, the younger member of Chaghri's line whom Tughril had designated. This put him in opposition to Alp Arslan. When Alp Arslan prevailed, al-Kunduri's position became dangerous. Alp Arslan, who had served for years as ruler of Khurasan with Nizam al-Mulk at his side, had a ready alternative. The sources describe Nizam al-Mulk as already trusted by the new sultan and in a position to take over the civil government. The change was not a routine transfer of office. It combined a change of administrative leadership with the elimination of a rival who had backed the losing side.

What happened to al-Kunduri is reported in several versions that agree on the broad outline and differ on details. He was removed from office, held for a time in custody in Khurasan, and executed in 1064. Some sources describe him as having been mistreated, and a number of anecdotes record words or messages attributed to him before his death. These stories, which depict him accepting his fate with composure and sometimes with a rebuke to those who judged him, belong to a literary pattern of the doomed minister that appears elsewhere in the period. The historian can accept that he was executed on the orders of the sultan, that the sultan's court justified the act in terms of his earlier conduct, and that the new vizier benefited. It is harder to establish what accusations were actually made, how much Nizam al-Mulk influenced the decision, and how far the official justification concealed a political purge.

The question of Nizam al-Mulk's responsibility deserves a direct answer, even though the evidence does not allow a certain one. Later chroniclers, many of whom admired him, tended to present al-Kunduri's fall as the result of the latter's own mistakes and ambitions. Others, and modern historians who read the sources critically, note that the new vizier had an obvious interest in the removal of his predecessor, that the two men represented rival networks and somewhat different religious attitudes, and that the sultan, whatever his own motives, acted in a manner that favored the vizier. A reasonable conclusion is that the execution was a political decision of the sultan, taken in the context of a succession contest, and that it removed a rival of the new vizier. Whether Nizam al-Mulk actively urged it, or merely benefited, cannot be determined from the surviving accounts.

The episode matters for three reasons. First, it shows that high administrative office was dangerous. A vizier who backed the wrong claimant could lose his life, and the violence that accompanied his rise was not exceptional. Second, it shows that the vizierate was a position held at the sultan's pleasure and tied to the fortunes of a particular prince. Nizam al-Mulk's later tenure of nearly thirty years was therefore not an institutional guarantee but the product of continuing political success. Third, it demonstrates that the Seljuk administration did not simply continue under a new head. Each change of sultan and vizier involved a reordering of networks, offices, and clients, and officials who belonged to the wrong network could be dismissed or worse.

Primary Source Window I: The Rise of Nizam al-Mulk and the Fall of al-Kunduri
Source type: Arabic and Persian narrative chronicles · Authors and dates: Ibn al-Athir, al-Kāmil fī'l-taʾrīkh (Arabic, completed in the early thirteenth century); Muhammad b. ʿAli Ravandi, Rāhat al-sudūr (Persian, composed around the turn of the thirteenth century); and other later works drawing on earlier lost histories of the Seljuks

Context. None of the surviving narratives was written in 1063 or 1064. Ibn al-Athir wrote in Mosul in the generation after the Seljuk empire had broken up, working from earlier chronicles, some of which are lost. Ravandi dedicated his history to a Seljuk ruler of Rum and drew on an earlier Persian history of the dynasty by Zahir al-Din Nishapuri. Nishapuri's Saljuqnama also survives in an identified manuscript at the Royal Asiatic Society and was critically edited by A. H. Morton in 2004; it is not known only through later borrowings. Both authors therefore transmit traditions produced more than a century after the events by authors who knew how the story ended, and both were writing in a period when Nizam al-Mulk had become a celebrated figure.

Paraphrase and thematic synthesis, not a quotation: The chronicles agree that after Tughril's death al-Kunduri supported Sulayman, that Alp Arslan prevailed, and that al-Kunduri was imprisoned and put to death in 1064. They agree that Nizam al-Mulk took his place and became the central figure of the new sultan's government. Several add stories about al-Kunduri's last words and about the new vizier's qualities, which differ from one text to another.

Analysis. The agreement on the main events gives the outline a reasonable degree of confidence. The disagreements about the details are informative: they show that the story was told differently depending on whether the author wanted to portray al-Kunduri as a proud or flawed minister and Nizam al-Mulk as a wise or a fortunate one. The prominence of last-words stories shows how the execution was assimilated to a literary type.

Limitations. The accounts do not preserve the sultan's own explanation in a documentary form. They do not reveal what role the new vizier played in the decision. They reflect the sympathies of authors who belonged to the Sunni administrative and scholarly elite and who mostly admired Nizam al-Mulk.

Perspective. The perspective is that of the court and the secretarial class. The viewpoint of al-Kunduri's own clients, who lost their positions, and that of ordinary people in Khurasan, who experienced a change of regime and administration, is largely absent.

Section 39.6

Alp Arslan and Nizam al-Mulk: Sultan and Vizier as Political Partners

Alp Arslan reigned for about nine years, from 1063 to 1072, and they were years of campaigning. Chapter 38 has described the expansion into Armenia and Georgia, the victory over the Byzantine emperor at Manzikert in 1071, and the eastern campaigns that occupied the sultan at the end of his life. Because Alp Arslan was often at the head of an army, the question of how he and his vizier divided their responsibilities is central to the nature of the early vizierate of Nizam al-Mulk.

The general division is clear enough. The sultan commanded the army, made the principal decisions about war and diplomacy, and received the allegiance of the commanders. The vizier directed the civil administration, managed finance, supervised the officials, and ensured that the army could be maintained and paid. This division should not be pressed too hard. The vizier accompanied the court on campaign, was consulted on military and diplomatic questions, and sometimes handled negotiations. The sultan, for his part, intervened in appointments and in matters of revenue when he chose. The boundary between military and administrative affairs was porous, and the sources show both men acting in each field.

The authority of the vizier in this period was real but bounded. He could appoint and dismiss many officials, but the sultan's wishes and the interests of the great commanders limited his freedom. He could recommend policies, but the sultan decided. His influence depended on the sultan's confidence, which in turn depended on the vizier's ability to keep the treasury supplied and the army content. The narratives of Alp Arslan's reign present the sultan as a vigorous and forthright soldier who respected his minister's judgment, and Nizam al-Mulk's later writings recall the sultan with reverence. This portrait, drawn largely by admirers of both men, is plausible but idealized. It also reflects the retrospective interest of a later age in a period remembered as a golden one.

One of the practical tasks of the vizier in these years was the maintenance of the army's finances during campaigns far from the plateau. Soldiers needed pay, equipment, and supplies, and an army operating in Armenia or Anatolia depended on revenue collected elsewhere. The arrangement of revenue assignments, discussed in Sections 39.14 and 39.15, allowed commanders to support their troops from the districts assigned to them, but the vizier's office had to oversee the system, adjust assignments, and prevent disputes among commanders. A campaign that failed for lack of supplies, or an army that mutinied for lack of pay, could ruin a sultan. The vizier's competence was therefore a military asset.

Dynastic strategy also required administrative cooperation. Alp Arslan confirmed relatives in regional commands, as Chapter 38 noted, and placed his sons in provincial positions. The vizier had to coordinate the administration of these regions with the central dīvāns, and to ensure that princes' courts remained connected to the sultan's. The appointment of Malik Shah as heir and the preparations for his accession in the last years of the reign involved the vizier in the delicate business of securing the support of commanders and officials for a successor. This dynastic work was not separate from administration. It was one of its main purposes.

The limits of the vizier's independence appear in the fact that he served a sultan who could, if he chose, replace him. Alp Arslan did not do so, and the sources suggest a continuing trust. But the example of al-Kunduri was before everyone's eyes, and Nizam al-Mulk knew that his position depended on retaining the sultan's confidence. He cultivated allies among the commanders, built a household of dependants, and made himself useful in many fields. These are the techniques of a minister who understands that a position conferred by favor must be protected by usefulness and alliance.

When Alp Arslan was killed in December 1072 by a captive whom he had condemned during a campaign on the Oxus frontier, the vizier faced the test of a succession for the second time. This time he did so as the established head of the administration, with the experience of nine years and a wide network of officials.

Section 39.7

The Transition to Malik Shah, 1072–1073

Chapter 38 has told the political story of Malik Shah's accession: his father had designated him, he was a youth of about seventeen when Alp Arslan died, and his uncle Qavurd of Kirman challenged his right and was defeated near Hamadan in 1073. This section asks what the transition shows about the vizierate and about the way administration made succession possible.

A succession in a dynasty without a fixed rule was above all a problem of recognition. Commanders, officials, and cities had to accept the new ruler. They did so by taking oaths, having the sermon delivered in his name, and continuing to obey his orders, and each of these acts could be withheld. The sources credit Nizam al-Mulk with taking a leading part in securing that recognition. He is reported to have acted quickly, secured the loyalty of the army and the officials near the sultan's camp, and sent word to the principal centers of government. He may also have had a part in the arrangements by which the young sultan was brought to the main body of the army and installed. The details of these steps come from chronicles that celebrate his role, so the narrative may exaggerate his personal contribution. But the general picture is credible, since the administration controlled the correspondence and the payment of troops by which recognition was made effective.

The administrative dimension of succession is easy to overlook. The dīvāns were the means by which a new ruler's orders reached the provinces. If the vizier and the senior secretaries declared for a claimant, letters would go out in his name, taxes would be collected for him, and officials who hesitated would be pressed to choose. If they did not, the claimant had to rely on armed force alone. In 1072, by acting promptly, the administration helped to prevent the delay that might have encouraged other claimants. It would be a mistake, however, to say that the vizier made Malik Shah sultan. The commanders who accepted him did so because he was the designated heir, because his father's army was behind him, and because they expected to benefit from continuity.

The result was a continuity of government that was unusual in a society where changes of ruler often brought changes of officials. Nizam al-Mulk retained the vizierate, the officials he had appointed stayed in place, and the commanders who had been Alp Arslan's followers found themselves in a familiar structure. This continuity was one of the achievements of the transition. It also gave the vizier an exceptional position: he now served a ruler who was young, relatively inexperienced, and in some measure indebted to his minister's handling of the succession. The relationship between the two was bound to be different from that between Alp Arslan and his vizier.

The early years of Malik Shah's reign thus saw the vizier at the height of his influence over the young sultan. The sources describe him as acting as a tutor and counselor as well as a minister, and his titles in this period reflected his standing. Among the honorifics attributed to him in the sources is Qiwam al-Din, "mainstay of the faith," documented in inscriptions and court literature. The title Atabeg is also associated with him in medieval accounts of his guidance of the young Malik Shah, although historians differ over precisely when and by whom it was bestowed and how formally it was used in his case. These titles are evidence of how contemporaries understood the relationship, in which the vizier appeared as a protector and guide of the ruler, and they would later be used by his critics as evidence of overweening power.

Students should also see what had not changed. The Seljuk system remained one of shared family authority, and the relatives who accepted Malik Shah did so on terms. Qavurd's revolt showed that seniority within the family could be invoked against a designated heir, and the sultan's decision to leave the Kirman line in place after defeating Qavurd illustrates the pattern of negotiated subordination. The vizier's task was to preserve a governing structure that accommodated such arrangements, not to abolish them. This was also what made the system fragile when a succession occurred without the conditions of 1072, as Division VII will show.

Section 39.8

The Sultan, the Vizier, and the Abbasid Caliphate

The Seljuk sultans ruled with the recognition of the Abbasid caliph, and the management of that relationship was one of the central tasks of the vizierate. Chapter 38 has explained the settlement of 1055 and the debates over whether it transformed or perpetuated the earlier arrangement under the Buyids. The present section examines the relationship from the standpoint of administration and diplomacy, and asks what part the vizier played.

The caliph was weak in military terms but strong in religious and legal prestige. His recognition validated a ruler's claim to be a legitimate Muslim sovereign, and the ceremonies of investiture, the grant of titles, and the mention of the ruler's name in the Friday sermon were the visible signs of that recognition. The Seljuk sultan, for his part, controlled the armies and most of the revenue of the regions that mattered. The relationship was one of mutual need: the sultan wanted legitimacy, and the caliph wanted protection and an income. Each side tried to turn the arrangement to its own advantage, and the contacts between them were therefore a continuing negotiation.

The vizier was the principal official of the Seljuk side in this negotiation. He corresponded with the caliph's chancery and with the caliph's own vizier, he arranged embassies and ceremonial visits, and he was responsible for ensuring that the caliph's household and officials were paid and supported in the manner agreed. The titles awarded to the sultan, the language of the diplomas of investiture, and the arrangements for the sultan's visits to Baghdad were all matters on which administrators had to reach agreement. The Arabic chancery of Baghdad was the center of a long tradition of elaborate formulas, and a Seljuk official had to be able to deal with it on equal terms. Nizam al-Mulk, an educated Sunni, was well suited to this role.

Marriage alliances formed part of the arrangement. Tughril married a daughter of the caliph al-Qaʾim in the early 1060s, an event that carried great symbolic weight, and in the late 1080s a daughter of Malik Shah was married to the caliph al-Muqtadi. Such marriages linked the two houses and raised the possibility of a grandson who would combine caliphal and Seljuk descent. They also created risks. The question of the succession to the caliphate, and whether a grandson of the sultan might become caliph, entered the politics of the last years of Malik Shah's reign, and some sources link it to the strained relations between the sultan and his court at the end. The evidence on this point is fragmentary, and it should not be pressed beyond the claim that the dynastic implications of the alliance were a source of tension.

Baghdad was important politically as well as symbolically. It was the seat of the caliph, a large and turbulent city with strong Sunni scholarly circles and sometimes violent conflicts between religious and neighborhood groups, and it was the place where the sultan's authority could be seen or doubted by the whole Muslim world. Malik Shah visited the city in the late 1080s and again in the early 1090s, and the court's presence there tested relations with the caliph and with the city's population. The Nizamiyya college of Baghdad, discussed in Division V, was one means by which the vizier established a presence in the city, and it served both religious and political purposes.

The relationship was not always harmonious. Disputes occurred over revenues, over the status of the caliph's household and officials, over the behavior of Seljuk troops in Baghdad, and over religious conflicts in the city. When trouble occurred, the vizier was the person who wrote to the caliph and tried to resolve the matter, and his correspondence on such issues shows a concern with maintaining order and with avoiding conflicts that could damage the sultan's standing. His handling of religious tensions in Baghdad is examined further in Section 39.27. The general point is that the management of the caliphate required administrative skill and a capacity for diplomacy, and the vizier supplied both.

In the end, the relationship illustrates the limits of the vizier's power as well as its scope. He could negotiate, but he could not command the caliph, and he could not prevent the sultan from making demands that complicated the relationship. The last years of Malik Shah's reign saw growing friction, partly over the question of succession in the caliphate, and the vizier was involved in a dispute whose course neither he nor the sultan fully controlled.

Section 39.9

The Extraordinary Authority of Nizam al-Mulk under Malik Shah

By the late 1070s and 1080s Nizam al-Mulk held a position of exceptional authority. Chroniclers described the government in terms that made him almost a co-ruler, and the language of their praise, and later of their criticism, has to be read with care. The question is how far his authority extended in practice, what resources he commanded, and how it is possible to distinguish official power from personal influence.

The scale of his responsibilities was very large. The vizier oversaw the central dīvāns, the appointment of officials, the assessment and collection of revenue, the payment of the army, the correspondence of the state, and a large part of the relations with the caliphate and with provincial rulers. These duties did not constitute a modern ministry with fixed departments and written constitutions. They were held together by the person of the vizier and the people who owed their positions to him. His administrative household, which included secretaries, accountants, and agents as well as slaves and soldiers, was in effect a second court. It traveled with him, kept his records, and conducted much of the day-to-day business of government.

His power of appointment was among the most important. The sources report that many governors, judges, tax officials, and other office holders were his nominees, and that he placed relatives and clients in important positions. This gave him a network of supporters across the empire. It also exposed him to criticism, since the appointment of sons and associates to high office could be portrayed as favoritism and as a threat to the sultan's own authority. Appointments were never purely administrative decisions. They allocated income, status, and opportunities to subordinate, and they determined who would owe loyalty to whom. A vizier who controlled them controlled a large part of the political economy of the state.

His access to information was a further source of strength. The postal and intelligence network, whatever its effectiveness, reported to the court, and the vizier's office received and interpreted its reports. He was in a position to know what provincial officials were doing, what commanders were saying, and what scholars and notables were thinking. The control of information is a form of power, especially in an empire in which distance made verification difficult. Whether the information was accurate, and whether he used it fairly, are other matters, and Section 39.16 examines the evidence for intelligence and inspection in more detail.

His financial influence was also considerable. The vizier directed the treasury and the offices that assessed revenue, and he supervised the assignments that supported the army. He was also personally wealthy, according to the sources, and he used his resources to fund schools, charities, and a large household. The question of where official finance ended and private wealth began is one that the sources do not address clearly. This is a general problem in premodern administration, where officials were often paid through assignments and perquisites, and where the line between public revenue and private income was less sharp than in modern states. Critics of the vizier accused him of enriching his family, and defenders pointed to his charity. Neither claim can be verified in detail.

It is difficult to distinguish official authority from personal power because in this system the two were intertwined. His power rested on the office, but the office was powerful because of the man who held it and the network that he had built. A different vizier with the same title would not have exercised the same influence, and when the vizier was removed, his network had to be rebuilt or destroyed. This is the central theme of the chapter's closing debate: whether Nizam al-Mulk's achievement was institutional or personal. The evidence suggests that it was a combination of the two, and that the proportion varied from one area of government to another.

Finally, the length of his tenure should not be taken as a sign of security. For nearly thirty years he served two sultans and survived the dangers of court politics, but the same features that made him strong, his wide network, his control of appointments, and his closeness to the sultan, also made him a target. The final years of his career, discussed in Division VII, show the vulnerability of a minister whose authority rested on a relationship with a ruler who had grown to adulthood and who had his own household and advisers.

Historical Debate: Servant of the Sultan or Independent Power?

The question. Was the Seljuk vizier essentially a servant of the sultan, whose authority was delegated and revocable, or was he an independent political power with his own resources, followers, and ideology?

The servant interpretation. The vizier held office by appointment. The sultan could dismiss him, as Alp Arslan removed al-Kunduri, and a vizier who lost favor could be imprisoned or killed. The administrators were dependent on the favor of the ruler, and even the most powerful of them could not command the army or make final decisions of war and peace. On this view, the vizier's authority was always derivative.
The independent-power interpretation. Nizam al-Mulk controlled the appointment of officials, the treasury, a household of retainers and slave soldiers, and a network of relatives and clients across the empire. He founded colleges and shaped the religious establishment, and he wrote or was credited with a treatise on government. His power rested on resources and loyalties of his own, and the sultan could not easily remove him without disrupting the government. On this view, he was something like a co-ruler.
A combined interpretation. Many historians suggest that the relationship changed over time. Under Alp Arslan, when the sultan was an active soldier, the vizier was more clearly a delegate. Under Malik Shah, who came to the throne young, the vizier's independence grew. Eventually the sultan sought to reassert his own authority, and that attempt helps to explain the tension of 1092.

Evidence. The evidence includes the narratives of the succession of 1063–1064 and 1072–1073, the reports of the vizier's appointments of relatives and clients, accounts of the dispute between sultan and vizier in 1092, and the vizier's own titles. Each of these can be read in more than one way.

Points of disagreement. The disagreement is partly interpretive, concerning how to weigh formal dependence against actual influence, and partly evidentiary, since the sources that describe the vizier's power were written by admirers or enemies and often with hindsight about the crisis of 1092.

Why it matters. The answer affects how the achievements of the period are attributed. If the vizier was a servant, the credit for the government belongs to the sultans. If he was an independent power, the credit belongs to him, and his fall shows the instability of such a position. It also affects the understanding of the empire as a state in which administrative expertise could rival royal authority.

Section 39.10

The Vizierate and the Central Dīvān System

The central government of the Seljuk sultans was an assemblage of offices clustered around the vizier. Describing it is harder than it sounds, because the sources use a technical vocabulary that varied by region and period, because later authors tended to systematize what had been fluid, and because manuals of administration describe an arrangement that may never have existed in exactly that form. The aim of this section is to explain the main offices and to separate what the sources prescribe from what they document.

The vizier stood at the head of the civil administration. His own dīvān, often called the dīvān of the vizierate or of the sultanate, supervised the other offices, received their reports, and controlled the main registers of revenue and expenditure. Beneath or alongside it were several principal departments. The department of finance, headed by an official called the mustawfi, audited accounts, compared revenue due with revenue received, and kept the records on which the state's fiscal knowledge depended. The chancery, headed by an official often called the tughraʾi because he was responsible for the sultan's signature or monogram, the tughra, drafted and issued the diplomas, letters, and decrees of the state. The department of inspection, headed by the mushrif, examined the activities of officials and the condition of revenue. The department of the army, whose chief was the ʿarid, kept the muster rolls and the lists of soldiers and their pay. Other officials handled the sultan's household, the postal service, and the affairs of the courts.

These titles are attested in the sources for the Seljuk period and earlier, and the general outline is not in dispute. What is uncertain is how clearly the departments were separated and how they were staffed at different times. In small courts or at moments of crisis, one man might hold several offices. In the households of princes, each with its own vizier and chancery, the structure was reproduced in miniature. The sultan's court was itinerant, and some of the departments traveled with it while others remained in a major city. The administration that actually operated in 1075 or 1090 was shaped by these practical conditions.

The distinction between prescription and practice should be kept in view. Administrative manuals and treatises, including the Siyasatnama, describe offices and their duties in terms that suggest orderly hierarchy. The chronicles, which narrate the conduct of officials and the course of events, reveal a more fluid world in which officials were appointed and dismissed for political reasons, in which the line between the vizier's household and the state was blurred, and in which the same office might have different powers under different holders. Documents that would settle the matter, such as surviving registers and orders, are few. A historian must therefore reason from partial and indirect evidence and avoid describing a tidy organization chart for a government that probably never had one.

The central dīvāns also depended on the personal ties of the vizier. The heads of departments were often his appointees or protégés, and their loyalty ran through him. This had advantages: it gave the vizier effective control over the flow of information and orders. It also meant that the departments were extensions of a political network. When the vizier fell, as in the case of al-Kunduri, the senior officials associated with him could lose their positions as well. The continuity of the dīvān system over long periods was real, but it was a continuity of practices and personnel moving between networks and not the permanence of an autonomous bureaucracy.

Section 39.11

Secretaries, Documents, Language, and the Chancery

A government that rules at a distance rules through documents. The orders that reached a provincial governor, the diploma by which a judge was appointed, the register in which a district's revenue was recorded, the letter in which a sultan addressed a caliph, all depended on professionals who could write correctly and persuasively. The secretaries who produced them were therefore among the most important people in the state, and the control of written information was a major source of the vizier's power.

Training of secretaries was a combination of formal education and apprenticeship. A young man destined for the profession learned Arabic grammar and style, memorized models of correspondence, studied accounting and arithmetic, and acquired the legal knowledge needed to understand taxation and property. Many were sons or nephews of secretaries and learned the craft within the household. Others, like Nizam al-Mulk, came from landholding or scholarly families and entered the profession through patronage. The manuals of the secretarial art, which survive from the Abbasid and later periods, describe the qualities expected of a good secretary: eloquence, discretion, honesty, and reliability with figures. As with other literature of the profession, they describe an ideal, and corruption and incompetence are well attested.

The chancery produced documents of several kinds. Letters of appointment conferred office and defined its duties. Decrees ordered the collection or remission of taxes, the assignment of revenue, or the punishment of an offender. Diplomatic letters addressed the caliph, rulers of neighboring states, and the Byzantine emperor. Safe-conducts, grants, and judicial orders were issued to individuals and communities. A small number of collections of such documents survive, most of them compiled in the twelfth century and preserving model letters or copies of correspondence, and they are precious evidence of the style and conventions of Seljuk chancery practice. Because they were often edited for literary reasons, they must be used with care as evidence of what was actually written on a given date.

Language was a significant political choice. Arabic remained the language of the caliph's chancery, of law and religious scholarship, and of much formal correspondence. Persian was used increasingly in the east and in the records of administration, and the Persianate chanceries of the Ghaznavids and Samanids had already developed a tradition of Persian prose. Under the Seljuks, both languages were employed, and the choice depended on the audience and the purpose. A decree to a provincial official in Khurasan might be in Persian, while a letter to the caliph would be in carefully formal Arabic. Turkic, the native language of the sultans and many of their soldiers, had no official written use at court in this period. The Persianate character of the state, in the sense the textbook uses, therefore refers to administrative and cultural practice in which Persian played a large role, not to the exclusive use of Persian or to a dominance of Iranian rulers.

The control of written information gave the vizier enormous influence. He and his chancery decided what orders were issued and in what terms, which appointments were confirmed, what the sultan would be told, and how accounts would be presented. A vizier who controlled the chancery could shape the sultan's understanding of events. This explains the care with which rival factions sought to place their own people in the chancery, and it explains why the intrigues of the later years of Malik Shah's reign included competition for such positions. It also suggests limits: the sultan, his wives, his commanders, and the caliph had their own sources of information and their own correspondents, and no one controlled all of the channels.

The documentary practice of the state left material traces that historians can use, though less abundantly than for modern states. Registers have mostly perished, since they were written on paper and kept in offices that did not survive political change. Surviving examples of decrees and letters come mainly from collections preserved by later secretaries as models of style. Inscriptions on buildings record the titles of rulers and viziers and the dates of foundations. Coins show the titles of the sultan and the caliph. Each of these is a partial witness, and together they allow a general reconstruction of how the chancery worked without permitting a precise description of the volume or content of its output.

One result of the dominance of the secretarial class in documents is that the literary record reflects its viewpoint. The chronicles that narrate Seljuk history were written by men trained in the same traditions, many of them in the service of the court, and they tend to emphasize the contribution of viziers and secretaries. This is not a reason to discount their evidence, but it is a reason to ask whether other groups, such as military commanders, would have told the story differently. In the absence of commanders' own narratives, the historian must read the secretaries' accounts with an awareness of their professional pride.

Section 39.12

Provincial Government: Governors, Local Elites, and Royal Representatives

The Seljuk empire was large, the roads between its regions were long, and communication was limited to the speed of a messenger on horseback. A government that could not be everywhere had to delegate, and the question of how it supervised those to whom it delegated is the main problem of provincial administration. The answer varied by region, by period, and by the relationship of the local ruler to the sultan.

Several kinds of authority coexisted in the provinces. In some regions, members of the Seljuk family governed as princes, with their own households, officials, and military followings. Where a prince governed, the central administration had to deal with him as a quasi-sovereign who owed allegiance to the Great Sultan but exercised substantial autonomy. In other regions, commanders held districts as revenue assignments and exercised administrative and military authority within them. In others still, a governor appointed by the sultan or the vizier represented the central government, assisted by secretaries who kept the accounts. Alongside these officials stood the urban and rural elites: judges, scholars, heads of notable families, and local landholders who had influence with the population and who often controlled the practical operation of local life.

The center dealt with these different authorities through several means. Appointment was the most direct. The sultan and the vizier could grant, confirm, or withdraw offices, and officials held their positions through diplomas that defined their powers. Reporting provided a second means. Governors and secretaries were expected to send accounts of revenue and administration, and the postal and intelligence services supplied independent reports. Periodic inspection and audit allowed the central offices to check what was reported. Marriage alliances, the taking of hostages, and the residence of sons or relatives at court bound provincial rulers to the center. And the possibility of military intervention, though costly, remained as a final sanction.

These tools had real limits. Distance made verification slow, and a governor could often act on his own for months before the center learned of it. Powerful commanders held their positions by the strength of their followers as well as by appointment, and removing them was dangerous. Princes of the dynasty could not easily be dismissed without provoking conflict within the family. Local elites could resist or obstruct. The system worked well when the sultan was strong, when the vizier maintained good relations with commanders, and when governors expected benefits from loyalty. It worked less well when any of these conditions failed.

The local population experienced provincial government through its representatives: the governor or revenue holder, the tax collector, the judge, and the head of the local community. Their relations with these figures determined much of how the state appeared to them. A conscientious official could support irrigation and keep order. A rapacious one could extract more than the legal rates and leave the district poorer. The sources, written mostly by urban officials and scholars, report abuses and also report the actions of rulers who intervened to correct them, though the proportions of these two kinds of report are not a measure of actual conduct. They show that both occurred.

Regional variation was great. Khurasan, with its long tradition of administration and its strong scholarly and landholding elites, was governed differently from the Caspian provinces, where local dynasties and mountain communities retained considerable independence, or from Kirman, where a branch of the Seljuk family ruled for generations. The western regions bordering Iraq and the Caucasus faced different pressures from those of the eastern frontier. A student who pictured a uniform provincial system would misunderstand the empire. It was better described as a set of arrangements, negotiated one by one, within a general framework of obedience to the sultan and recognition of the caliph.

For Nizam al-Mulk and his colleagues, provincial government was a matter of managing relationships. They could not simply order governors and commanders; they had to persuade, reward, balance, and sometimes threaten. The appointment of relatives and clients to important posts was one method, since it gave the vizier allies in the provinces. The method had its own costs, because it entangled administrative policy with the interests of a family network. The same men who gave the vizier reach also gave his enemies a target.

Section 39.13

Taxation, Land Revenue, and Fiscal Administration

The Seljuk state, like most premodern states, lived on agriculture. The wealth that supported the court, the army, the officials, the schools, and the charities was drawn largely from the produce of fields, orchards, and pastures, and the means by which it was assessed and collected were the concern of the fiscal administration. Chapter 38 introduced the general outline of the system. This section looks at how it worked and at its consequences for those who paid.

The principal agrarian tax was the kharaj, a land tax levied on cultivated land and assessed in different ways in different regions. In some districts it was a fixed sum or a fixed quantity of produce per unit of land. In others it was a share of the harvest, collected in kind or converted into money. The rate depended on the type of land, the crop, the means of irrigation, and local custom, and varied widely between the Khurasani oases, the grain lands of the plateau, and the irrigated plains of Iraq. Other taxes included the poll tax paid by non-Muslim subjects of protected status, customs and tolls on trade, market taxes in towns, and a variety of dues and levies. The religious tax on Muslims' produce, the ʿushr, existed in principle and in some places was merged with or confused with the land tax in practice. Pastoral groups were also taxed, though the methods were less regular.

Assessment depended on knowledge, and knowledge was recorded in registers. The fiscal dīvān maintained surveys of districts, lists of villages and landholders, and records of what each owed. These registers were old, inherited from earlier regimes and revised as conditions changed. They were not always reliable. A register might record a village that had been abandoned, a rate that no longer corresponded to the land's yield, or a landholder who had died. Officials who understood the registers could manipulate them, and a skilled accountant could conceal the diversion of revenue. This is why the audit by the mustawfi and the inspection by the mushrif were so important, and why they were also so exposed to bribery and favoritism.

Collection took place through local agents. In districts assigned to a revenue holder, his agents collected the tax. In others, officials of the dīvān or local notables acting as tax farmers or guarantors collected it, handing over a stated sum to the government. The practice of tax farming, in which a person paid a fixed amount in advance and kept the surplus, was a common and controversial method, and sources criticize it as an incentive to extortion. Revenue then passed upward through a chain of officials, with portions retained at each stage to pay salaries and expenses, until the remainder reached the central treasury or was applied directly to the army's needs. The transfer of money across a wide empire relied on merchants, bankers, and bills of exchange as well as on convoys of coin.

The costs of maintaining the court and army were heavy, and the revenue system had to meet them. The sultan's household, which was large and included slaves, servants, guards, and the families of the dynasty, had to be supported. The army required pay and supplies. The officials had to be paid or rewarded. The state also spent on the maintenance of roads, bridges, and caravanserais, which served both military and commercial ends, and on gifts to scholars and religious institutions. Seljuk sources give accounts of such spending, but they do not provide a general budget, and attempts to calculate total revenue and expenditure from the narrative evidence are speculative. Students should be wary of any precise figure presented for the whole empire.

The consequences for cultivators and landholders were mixed and are known mostly from indirect evidence. A cultivator owed a share of the crop or a fixed payment regardless of the harvest, which meant that a drought or a flood could force the sale of livestock, borrowing at high interest, or flight. A landholder who held land on his own account paid the land tax and might also have to support the soldiers quartered in the district. Where taxation was moderate and security good, cultivation expanded. Where assessments were severe, officials corrupt, or warfare frequent, land fell out of cultivation. The Seljuk sources include complaints of oppressive taxation and the reports of rulers who abolished unjust levies. The balance varied across the empire and over time. Historical Vocabulary: Fiscal Registers, Revenue Assignment, Land Tax, Treasury, Chancery, Investiture
Fiscal register · Revenue assignment (iqtaʿ) · Kharaj · Treasury · Chancery · Investiture

Definition. A fiscal register was a written record of districts, villages, landholders, and the taxes due from them. A revenue assignment (iqtaʿ) was a grant of the right to collect certain revenues in return for service, usually military. The kharaj was the principal land tax. The treasury was the store of coin and valuables controlled by the ruler and his financial officials. The chancery was the office that drafted and issued official documents. Investiture was the formal conferral of office, titles, and insignia by a superior authority.

Historical context. All six terms describe practices that preceded the Seljuks and were adapted by them. Registers and the land tax came from the Sasanian and early Islamic worlds. The assignment of revenue to soldiers was used by the Buyids and others. The chancery and the ceremonies of investiture were integral to Abbasid government and to the relationship between caliph and sultan.

Why it matters. These institutions linked the ruler's authority to practical control of money and documents. A sultan who could not assess and collect revenue, or whose orders were not recognized, could not govern.

Potential misunderstanding. The treasury was not a modern public budget. The line between the ruler's private wealth and public revenue was fluid, and revenue assignments often functioned as salaries, so the central treasury did not necessarily see the full flow of money.

Section 39.14

The Iqtaʿ System: Revenue Rights, Soldiers, and State Authority

The iqtaʿ is among the most discussed institutions of the Seljuk period, and it is also among the most easily misunderstood. In popular and older scholarly accounts it is sometimes described as a "feudal" system in which land was granted to warriors, and sometimes credited entirely to Nizam al-Mulk. Both statements need correction. The iqtaʿ was an assignment of revenue, not a grant of ownership, and it had a long history before the Seljuks.

The word refers to a grant by the ruler of the right to collect certain taxes from a district, a village, or another source, in return for service. The holder, called the muqtaʿ, received the revenue that the state would otherwise have collected, and he used it to support himself and, if he was a military man, a body of troops. He did not acquire ownership of the land or of the cultivators. The legal distinction is important: the land remained, in principle, the property of its holders or of the state, and the muqtaʿ held only a right to the taxes due from it. In practice, as the next paragraphs show, the line between revenue right and local lordship could be blurred.

Assignments of revenue to soldiers had been used under the late Abbasids, who experimented with them when cash salaries could not be paid, and the Buyids employed them extensively. They were not a Seljuk innovation. What the Seljuks did was to make assignments the usual means of supporting their army and to extend them over a vast territory. Under the sultans, large assignments supported senior commanders and their followers, while smaller ones supported individual soldiers. Members of the dynasty also held assignments, which blended with their appanages. The system of such grants thus became a central feature of the state's financial and military organization. How far Nizam al-Mulk shaped it is a matter on which historians differ. He is credited by later tradition with reorganizing and regulating the system, and the Siyasatnama contains advice on the proper treatment of assignments. But the system developed over generations, and attributing it wholly to him goes beyond the evidence.

The political and military purposes were clear. The assignment solved a financial problem: a state that did not have a large cash reserve could still pay a large army by letting soldiers collect the revenue directly. It also tied soldiers to particular districts and spared the central treasury the burden of collection and transport. And it gave the sultan a means of rewarding and controlling commanders, since an assignment could in principle be reduced, moved, or revoked. In this sense it was an instrument of state authority as well as of military finance.

The risks were equally apparent. A muqtaʿ had an interest in extracting as much as he could from the district during his tenure, especially if he expected to be moved. He might neglect irrigation and the upkeep of the land, since the long-term benefits would go to others. He might use his soldiers to coerce cultivators. He might also try to turn the assignment into a hereditary possession and to resist transfer. Central officials tried to supervise the system by registering assignments, auditing their yields, and requiring service in return, but the effectiveness of such supervision depended on the strength of the center. The Siyasatnama itself, and other writings of the period, complain about abuses by holders of assignments, which is evidence that the problem was real and that the prescribed controls did not always work.

The system also changed over time. In the later eleventh century and the twelfth, the tendency for assignments to become hereditary and to fall under the control of local military families grew stronger. This was one of the ways in which central authority weakened after 1092. The vizier's efforts to regulate assignments, reduce abuses, and maintain central control were therefore efforts to hold back a development that the system itself encouraged. Whether they succeeded for a time, as the sources suggest, or merely delayed the tendency is a question on which the evidence is limited.

Cause and Effect: Military Expenditure, Provincial Revenue Collection, and the Iqtaʿ

Long-term causes

Earlier Abbasid and Buyid practice of assigning revenue to soldiers; a scarcity of cash relative to the cost of large armies; difficulty of transporting coin across a large territory.

Short-term causes

The Seljuk need to support Turkmen cavalry, slave soldiers, and commanders across a rapidly expanding territory; the weakness of a central treasury unable to pay all troops directly.

Immediate context

Under Alp Arslan and Malik Shah, long campaigns on distant frontiers and a growing number of provincial commands increased the demand for support of soldiers in the field.

Transformation

Assignments of revenue became the usual means of paying the army, tying military support to the tax-collecting administration of districts.

Immediate effects

Soldiers were maintained without large cash payments; commanders acquired local financial bases; administrators had to register and supervise assignments; cultivators dealt directly with military revenue holders.

Long-term consequences

Strengthened ruler's control when the center was strong; provided regional military families with local resources and encouraged hereditary claims; became a standard feature of later Muslim states.

Historical complexity. The pressure of military expenditure was a necessary condition for the spread of the system, but not a sufficient explanation. The system also depended on administrative capacity to record and supervise assignments, on political relations between sultan and commanders, and on the willingness of cultivators and local elites to accept military collectors. The same arrangement that solved a financial problem created a problem of control. No single cause or decision produced it.

Section 39.15

The Army, Military Salaries, Recruitment, and Administrative Control

The Seljuk army was not a single institution. It was a collection of forces with different origins, obligations, and ways of being paid, and the administrative task was to keep this heterogeneous body fed, equipped, loyal, and under control. A civilian vizier had no authority to lead troops in battle, but he had a decisive role in making an army possible.

The core of the original Seljuk force was Turkmen cavalry, groups of mounted archers who moved with their families and herds and who responded to the leadership of the dynasty and of their own chiefs. These troops were effective in campaigns and frontier warfare, but they were difficult to supervise, their obligations were as much personal as institutional, and their need for pasture sometimes brought them into conflict with settled populations. Chapter 38 described the tensions this produced. Alongside them the sultans maintained forces of military slaves, ghulams, purchased or captured, trained in the household of the ruler or of a commander, and attached to their master by personal loyalty. Slave soldiers were professional, paid regularly, and politically dependent on the master who had raised them. Commanders also maintained their own followings of retainers and mercenaries, and the army included contingents of other origins, among them infantry from the mountainous regions. The mixture varied across time and place.

Pay and support were arranged in several ways. Slave soldiers in the sultan's household received cash allowances, clothing, and equipment, and their cost was a charge on the treasury. Senior commanders and their followers were supported by revenue assignments. Turkmen groups might be given pasture rights, or assignments in border districts, or they might be paid for particular campaigns. The sultan also relied on booty and on gifts distributed after victories. Because arrangements differed among groups, the administration had to keep track of who was owed what. The ʿarid, the officer responsible for the muster, maintained the rolls of soldiers and judged whether the contingents commanders claimed to bring to the field were actually present and properly equipped.

Muster was an essential means of control. The inspection of troops allowed the government to verify the number and quality of soldiers for which a commander drew revenue. It was also the occasion on which fraud occurred, since commanders could inflate their rolls, borrow troops for the occasion, or collect pay for men who did not exist. The Siyasatnama advises regular and rigorous muster, which suggests both that the practice existed and that it was neglected or abused. Whether the administration of the later eleventh century inspected effectively cannot be established from the surviving evidence. Chroniclers mention occasions on which troops were reviewed, but they do not describe the system in detail.

Administrative knowledge of military obligations was necessary to Seljuk political authority for a reason that goes beyond efficiency. The sultan's power depended on the ability to call upon forces of known size at a given time, and a ruler who did not know what his commanders owed could not rely on them. Equally, commanders' expectations of pay and assignment were the basis of their loyalty. If the administration failed to provide what they expected, they would look elsewhere. The vizier was the person on whom this failure would fall, and the resentment of military men against civilian administrators who controlled their income is a recurrent theme in the sources.

The competition among military groups had political consequences. The Turkmen cavalry, the slave soldiers of the sultan, and the retinues of great commanders did not always cooperate, and the vizier had to manage their rivalries. Nizam al-Mulk himself kept a large following of slave soldiers, which gave him a military resource of his own and increased the suspicion of his rivals. After his death, these troops became a political force in the succession crisis, as Chapter 38 noted. The administration's relationship with the army was therefore never merely technical. It was a matter of power, and it involved the vizier in the same kind of competition for loyalty that governed the relations among the soldiers themselves.

Section 39.16

Communication, Intelligence, Inspection, and Royal Justice

A ruler who wishes to know what officials are doing, and to correct what they do wrongly, needs channels of information and means of redress. The Seljuk government had both, at least in principle, and this section examines what they were and what evidence exists about how they worked.

The barid, the postal and information service, was an inheritance from the Abbasid caliphate. In its classical form it consisted of relay stations along the main roads where messengers could change horses, and of officials who sent reports on local affairs to the capital. The service carried official correspondence, and its officers were also expected to report on the conduct of governors and tax collectors. How well the system functioned in the Seljuk period is hard to determine. The sources show messengers carrying letters between the court and provincial centers, and they mention officials responsible for reporting news. They also suggest that the network was uneven and that rulers sometimes relied on other informants, including agents sent specifically to observe. Rulers had every reason to want independent sources of information, since an official who controlled the only channel could conceal misconduct.

Inspection was a second means of supervision. Officials called mushrifs examined accounts and the condition of districts, and special agents might be sent to investigate complaints or audit officials. The office was valuable but exposed to corruption, since an inspector could be bribed or could himself abuse his authority. The Seljuk sources describe occasions on which officials were investigated, dismissed, and made to repay sums they had taken. Such accounts show that the mechanism existed and was used. They do not show how often it was used, or how far it constrained wrongdoing that was never discovered.

Justice was the third element. In the Islamic tradition, the ruler was expected to hear complaints against officials and to correct injustice, in addition to the regular administration of law by judges. The institution called the mazalim, the hearing of complaints, allowed a sultan or vizier to review cases in which ordinary courts were thought inadequate, especially cases involving the powerful. Petitions could be presented to the ruler on public occasions or sent to the court. The ideal, strongly expressed in the Siyasatnama, was that the ruler would make himself accessible, hear the grievances of his subjects, and punish oppressors. How often and for whom this ideal was realized is far less clear. A cultivator in a distant district had to find the means to reach the court, and the cost of the journey, the risk of reprisal, and the need for intermediaries were obstacles. Complaints from towns and from groups with organized representatives, such as scholars and notables, probably had a better chance of being heard.

The contrast between recommendation and practice is central to this chapter, and it is useful to separate three kinds of evidence. First, there are prescriptions: the advice in the Siyasatnama and similar treatises about how officials should be supervised and justice administered. These show what was considered desirable and what an experienced minister believed the system required. Second, there are narratives of events: chronicle accounts of investigations, dismissals, and acts of royal justice, which show that such things occurred. Third, there is the absence of evidence about the ordinary operation of the system, the great mass of cases that were never recorded. The first kind of evidence tells us about ideals, the second about exceptional events, and the third reminds us how limited our view is.

Section 39.17

Mirrors for Princes: The Iranian and Islamic Traditions of Political Advice

The Siyasatnama belongs to a genre. Long before Nizam al-Mulk, writers in Iran, Iraq, and the wider Islamic world produced works of advice to rulers, which modern scholars often call "mirrors for princes" because they were meant to reflect to a ruler the qualities and conduct appropriate to his office. To understand what the Siyasatnama says, and what it does not say, it is necessary to see the tradition within which it was written.

The oldest layer is the memory of Sasanian kingship. Under the Sasanians, as earlier volumes described, the court preserved a body of maxims, stories, and testaments attributed to great kings and sages, and the idea that kingship and religion supported one another was prominent. Little of this literature survives in its original Middle Persian form. Much of what we know of it comes from Arabic and Persian translations and adaptations made in the early Abbasid period, and these were shaped by the interests of their translators. A well-known example is the testament attributed to Ardashir, preserved in Arabic, which states in a compact form a view of society in which the ruler, the army, wealth, and prosperity depend on one another and on justice. The authenticity of such texts as records of Sasanian thought is debated, but their influence on later writers is not.

The second layer is Islamic. Religious and legal writers developed concepts of just rule, the duties of the ruler toward his subjects, and the obligations of subjects toward the ruler. The jurist al-Mawardi, who died in 1058 in Baghdad, wrote a systematic treatise on the rules of government that described the offices of caliph, vizier, governor, and judge in legal terms. It is a work of a different kind from the mirrors for princes, more juristic and less anecdotal, but it was part of the intellectual environment in which Nizam al-Mulk worked. Other writers in the Abbasid period produced adab literature, essays and anthologies of conduct and advice for secretaries and courtiers, and the tradition of translating and adapting Indian and Persian stories of wise counsel, of which the animal fables known in Arabic as Kalila wa Dimna are the most famous example, formed part of the same culture of advice.

The third layer is the Persian-language literature of the tenth and eleventh centuries. As Persian became a language of prose and poetry at the Samanid and Ghaznavid courts, authors began to write political advice in it. The epic tradition of Firdawsi, examined in Chapter 42, preserved the memory of Iranian kings and the ideal of just kingship in verse, and prose writers added collections of anecdotes. In the eleventh century, a prince of a minor dynasty on the Caspian wrote an advisory work for his son that is among the best-known examples of the genre in Persian. The Siyasatnama belongs to this Persian tradition, though it also draws on Arabic sources and on the Islamic conception of the ruler's duties.

The typical features of these works are worth noting. They are addressed to a ruler or prince, and they are written by someone with experience of the court. They advise on the ruler's personal conduct and on the practical running of government. They rely heavily on historical anecdotes, called exempla, in which kings, caliphs, viziers, and sages are shown acting wisely or foolishly, so that the reader can learn by example. They often combine religious piety with a pragmatic recognition that power must be organized and maintained. And they are written with a purpose: to persuade the ruler to adopt certain policies, to praise or criticize particular groups, or to assert the value of the author's own kind of expertise.

The use of moral storytelling poses a problem for the historian. The anecdotes in a mirror for princes are often set in the reigns of famous rulers, such as Sasanian kings, the early caliphs, or Mahmud of Ghazna, but they were told to illustrate a point and not to record events. Some may preserve genuine memories. Many are conventional stories attached to different names in different texts. Treating them as evidence for the events they describe would be a mistake, though they are good evidence for what the author and his audience believed about the past and about how a ruler should act. Students should therefore distinguish between the anecdote as a historical report and the anecdote as an argument.

A final point concerns the relationship between ideals and context. The mirrors for princes present a vision of ideal government, but they were written in particular political conditions, and their advice often addressed specific problems. When Nizam al-Mulk urged the ruler to appoint honest officials or to check his commanders, he was responding to a situation he knew. The task of the historian is to read the advice both as a statement of general principles and as a response to the conditions of the late eleventh century.

Section 39.18

The Composition, Structure, Manuscripts, and Authorship of the Siyasatnama

The work known as the Siyasatnama, "Book of Government," is also called Siyar al-muluk, "Conduct of Kings." It is written in Persian prose and is attributed to Nizam al-Mulk. The surviving text consists of fifty chapters, and it has a preface that explains how it came to be written. That preface, together with the structure of the text and the history of its transmission, raises questions that have occupied scholars for more than a century.

According to the account the work gives of itself, Malik Shah asked his leading officials around the year 1086 to submit memoranda describing the faults and weaknesses of the government and the means of correcting them. Nizam al-Mulk's response, as the preface tells it, was a set of thirty-nine chapters on the duties of the ruler and the conduct of the state. This initial version is said to have been presented to the sultan. The text then says that eleven further chapters were added at a later date, dealing with threats to the dynasty and the state, among them the activities of Ismaili missionaries and other heterodox groups. The final form of fifty chapters thus has a tradition of two stages, with an earlier and a later part.

The date of composition is therefore uncertain in several respects. The year 1086 is a traditional date, based on the work's own testimony and on the chronology of the preface, and historians generally treat it as approximate. The later chapters are linked in the text to the final years of the vizier's life, when the Ismaili challenge had become acute, and some scholars suppose that they were written or finished around 1091 or 1092. There is no independent contemporary record of the commission, and the earliest narrative evidence for it is in the text itself. This does not make it false, but it means that the commission is attested by the work's own claim.

The structure of the work is topical and anecdotal. The chapters are of unequal length and cover a wide range of subjects, beginning with the ruler's duties of justice and the hearing of complaints, and continuing through the appointment and supervision of officials, the organization of the army, the use of intelligence agents, the conduct of the court, the treatment of groups within the realm, and the dangers from religious dissent. Each chapter usually states a principle or recommendation and supports it with one or more stories. The tone is didactic, and the author repeatedly contrasts what he says is proper practice with what he describes as neglect or corruption in the government of his own time. This feature of the text is important, because it implies a critical stance toward contemporary administration, while also being a stylistic convention of the genre.

The transmission of the text is complex. The work survives in many manuscripts, which differ in the order of chapters, in the presence or absence of individual stories, and in wording. The text first became widely known to Western scholars through a Persian edition and French translation published in the 1890s, and an English translation based on a later critical edition appeared in 1960. These modern editions rest on comparison of manuscripts, and they represent scholarly choices about which readings to prefer. A student reading a translation is therefore reading an edited text that reflects decisions by its editor, and should keep in mind that no autograph manuscript in the author's hand is known to survive.

The question of authorship has several dimensions, and the Source Criticism Study below sets them out. Most scholars accept that Nizam al-Mulk was the author or the principal inspirer of the first part of the work. The attribution rests on the text's own claim and on the early tradition that associated it with him. Others have raised the possibility that the surviving text was revised, expanded, or reorganized after his death, either by members of his circle or by later editors. The later chapters, on heterodox movements, have received particular attention, since they address events of 1090–1092 and since some features of their style and content differ from those of the earlier chapters. Disagreement concerns both how much of the surviving text can be assigned to the vizier personally and how that should affect its use as evidence.

For the purposes of historical analysis, the most defensible approach is to treat the Siyasatnama as a text associated with Nizam al-Mulk and with the political culture of his circle, to use it as evidence for the ideas current in that environment, and to avoid statements that depend on proof that a given passage was written by him on a given date. This approach does not diminish the value of the work. It acknowledges that a document can be important as a statement of political thought and still be uncertain as a record of one man's intentions.

Source Criticism Study: Manuscript Transmission and Competing Views of Authorship

How should a historian use a text whose authorship is debated?

What the work says about itself. The preface attributes the work to Nizam al-Mulk, describes a commission from Malik Shah, and distinguishes an initial thirty-nine chapters from eleven that were added later.

What the manuscripts show. The surviving manuscripts were copied centuries after the vizier's death and differ in arrangement and content. This variation shows that the text circulated in more than one form and was subject to copying, selection, and editing.

Positions in the scholarly discussion.

Substantial authorship by Nizam al-Mulk. This view holds that the work, in its main lines, reflects the vizier's own composition, perhaps with assistance from secretaries, and that the later chapters were written in the last year or two of his life in response to the Ismaili threat.
Composite or revised text. This view holds that the surviving work is a composite, with an earlier core associated with the vizier and later material reflecting additions or revisions by his circle or by editors, so that individual passages cannot automatically be attributed to him.

Why it matters. If the work is the vizier's own, it is direct evidence of his political ideas and of his criticism of the government he directed. If it is composite, it is evidence for the ideology of the administrative circle that he led and that survived him, and for the use of his name as an authority for later political purposes.

Methods for testing authorship. Scholars compare manuscripts, examine the style and vocabulary of different chapters, check references to historical events against external chronology, and consider whether passages respond to circumstances after the vizier's death. None of these methods is conclusive taken alone, and each depends on the preserved manuscripts.

Section 39.19

The Just Ruler: Kingship, Divine Favor, and Responsibility

The opening chapters of the Siyasatnama place the ruler at the center of a moral order. In its view, God chooses a ruler from among men and endows him with qualities of rank and authority, and the ruler's task is to keep the world in order, to protect his subjects, and to administer justice. The idea that kingship is a divine gift is found in earlier Iranian and Islamic thought, and the text connects it with the claim that the ruler's authority depends on the protection of religion and the faith of the community.

Justice, in this context, has several meanings. It refers to the fair application of law, to the protection of subjects against oppression by officials, to the moderation of taxation, and to the punishment of wrongdoers. It also has a broader sense of keeping each social group in its place and ensuring that no group harms another. Behind the conception stands the older idea, found in Persian and Arabic literature, that justice sustains a cycle of stability: a just ruler secures prosperity, prosperity supports the army and the treasury, and the army and treasury enable the ruler to maintain justice. The idea is often called the circle of justice, and while the Siyasatnama does not necessarily state it in a single formula, the logic runs through its advice.

The text's emphasis on the ruler's personal conduct is notable. The ruler should hold public audiences at fixed times, in which subjects can present their complaints directly and officials can be questioned. He should not delegate this duty entirely to others, since officials may conceal wrongdoing. He should listen to petitions and give prompt decisions, and he should punish officers who oppress the people. He should also restrain his own desires, avoid cruelty and arbitrary punishment, and surround himself with wise advisers. The stories the text tells to illustrate these principles often show rulers who neglected their duty and suffered, or who listened to a humble petitioner and were rewarded.

The relationship between legitimacy and the ruler's conduct is complex. On one hand, the text treats the ruler's authority as given by God, which implies that subjects owe him obedience. On the other hand, it warns that a ruler who neglects justice may lose divine favor and his kingdom. This is not a doctrine of the right of subjects to resist. The text does not recommend rebellion, and it treats disorder as a grave evil. But it does hold rulers accountable before God and before history, and it uses the memory of fallen dynasties as a warning. In this way it combines an affirmation of authority with a moral critique of its abuse.

The audience and purpose of this ideal must be considered. The text was addressed to a sultan of Turkic dynastic tradition who ruled over a largely Persian-speaking and Arabic-reading Muslim population. Its recommendations stressed the interests of the settled population: moderate taxation, protection from oppression, reliable courts. It also reflected the interests of the administrative class, since a ruler who followed its advice would depend on experienced officials. A reader may ask whether the picture of the just king was intended partly to instruct the sultan in the standards that a Perso-Islamic audience would apply to him, and partly to assert the importance of the vizier and secretaries who would carry out the ruler's policies. Both purposes are consistent with sincere belief.

Section 39.20

Choosing Officials: Merit, Loyalty, Corruption, and Administrative Ethics

A large part of the Siyasatnama concerns the people through whom the ruler governs. The author knew from experience that the quality of government depended on the quality of officials, and he returned repeatedly to the problem of finding, testing, supervising, and, when necessary, removing them.

The text's advice on appointments stresses several principles. Officials should be chosen for their competence and honesty, and the ruler should inquire into their backgrounds before appointing them. They should not be selected only because of connections or flattery. The ruler should not entrust too much power to a single person, and he should avoid appointing one man to several offices, since this would allow him to conceal wrongdoing and build an independent power base. Officials should be paid enough to reduce the temptation to corruption, and they should be held accountable through audit and inspection. The ruler should also be willing to punish those who abuse their positions, and to do so openly enough to deter others.

The text devotes attention to the danger of corrupt courtiers and to conflicts of interest. A ruler who listens to favorites, it warns, will hear only what they wish him to hear. Officials who treat their position as a source of private gain impoverish the people and undermine the ruler's standing. The text also warns against appointing men from groups it regards as untrustworthy, a subject taken up in Section 39.22. These passages combine practical administrative guidance with moral exhortation, and it is worth distinguishing the two. Recommendations such as paying officials adequately, auditing accounts, and avoiding concentration of offices are specific and could in principle be implemented. Warnings about the moral character of officials and the evils of greed belong to the conventions of the genre, which expected an author to condemn vice.

One feature of these chapters deserves attention. The author, a minister who held extraordinary power and had placed many relatives and clients in office, advised the ruler against allowing officials to accumulate influence. Whether this reflects a sincere belief, a rhetorical position, or an attempt to criticize his rivals is a question that cannot be settled. The sources attribute to his critics the charge that he did not follow his own advice, since his sons and associates held many offices. The charge may be partly the product of hostility, but it reflects an obvious tension between the ideal of limited and accountable authority and the practice of a network-based administration. A historian can note the tension without resolving the question of the author's sincerity.

The discussion of administrative ethics also reflects the circumstances of the time. The ruling dynasty was of Turkic origin, and its officials were largely Iranian and Arabic-speaking. The ruler needed to trust the officials but could not always judge their competence or honesty. The text offers methods of verification: inquiries, comparison of reports, audit. It also encourages the ruler to cultivate the loyalty of officials by rewarding good service and protecting those who served well. The combination of vigilance and reward is a characteristic feature of the political thinking of the secretarial class, and it appears in other texts of the genre.

Section 39.21

Military Government, Surveillance, Revenue, and Public Order in the Siyasatnama

The Siyasatnama addresses the army, the treasury, and public order, and in doing so it offers a body of prescriptions that can be compared with the institutions described in Division III. The comparison is the main teaching value of this section, and it should be made with care for what the text can and cannot show.

On the army, the text recommends that soldiers be paid regularly and in full, and that the ruler ensure that commanders do not withhold what is owed to their men. It advises muster at regular intervals to verify the number and quality of troops. It also suggests that the ruler maintain a mixed force drawn from different peoples, so that no one group can dominate the others and that rivalry among them keeps each in check. The recommendation reflects the political reality of the Seljuk army, in which Turkmen cavalry, slave soldiers, and other contingents coexisted. It also reflects a mistrust of any single military group, including the Turkmen who formed the core of the dynasty's original following. The author's attitude toward the Turkmen appears to have been mixed, recognizing their claim on the dynasty while wishing to prevent them from dominating the state.

On revenue, the text advises moderation. Officials should not impose taxes beyond what the law and custom permit. Those who hold revenue assignments should be supervised to ensure that they do not oppress cultivators, and they should be reminded that their rights are to revenue and not to ownership. The ruler should look to the condition of agriculture, since the prosperity of the cultivators is the basis of revenue. These recommendations match the concerns of the fiscal system described in Sections 39.13 and 39.14, and they suggest that the author knew the typical abuses of assignment holders.

On intelligence and surveillance, the text urges the use of agents who report on the conduct of governors, officials, commanders, and notables. It treats such information as essential to the security of the state. The recommendation extends to the surveillance of potential opponents and to the gathering of news from the provinces and from foreign courts. As Section 39.16 noted, there is evidence that rulers used informants, but the extent and organization of the system are poorly documented.

On public order, the text recommends the swift and visible punishment of crimes and rebellion, together with the cultivation of the religious scholars whose influence helps to maintain social peace. It treats disorder as a danger that threatens the entire social structure, and it counsels vigilance against groups that might incite it. In this respect it reflects the anxieties of an administrator who had lived through succession struggles, military mutinies, and religious conflicts.

The comparison of prescription with practice yields a mixed result. In several areas, the text's recommendations correspond to institutions that existed: regular muster, supervision of assignment holders, use of informants, hearing of complaints. This suggests that it was written by someone familiar with the system and that it addressed real features of government. At the same time, the text's repeated complaints about neglect, corruption, and the failure to follow proper procedures indicate that practice often fell short. The text cannot be used as a description of what the state actually did. It is better read as evidence for what an informed insider thought the state should do, and for the problems he saw in its operation.

The comparison also reveals something about the nature of the text's authority. A work that criticizes the government as it is while claiming to describe how it should be gains force from the experience of its author. If its author was the vizier, the criticism is also self-criticism or a critique of his rivals and subordinates. If it was compiled by others using his name, the criticism served the interests of the circle that claimed his legacy. In either case the work has to be read as a political document as well as a practical manual.

Historical Debate: Does the Siyasatnama Describe Real Seljuk Administration or an Idealized Political Order?

The question. Can the Siyasatnama be used as evidence of how the Seljuk state actually worked, or is it primarily an expression of ideals and of the author's political purposes?

The descriptive interpretation. The author was an experienced minister who knew the institutions he wrote about. His recommendations on muster, assignments, informants, and audiences reflect existing practices, and his criticisms of abuses show where the system failed. On this view, the text is a valuable source for administrative practice.
The idealizing interpretation. The work belongs to a literary genre with conventions that shaped its content. Its anecdotes are set in the past and used as arguments, its advice is general, and its picture of a just and orderly state is a standard of judgment and not a report. On this view, it reveals ideology more than practice.
A mediating view. Many historians suggest that the text is both: its general principles and moral claims reflect literary convention, while particular recommendations and complaints may reflect real conditions. The task is to test each claim against independent evidence where possible.

Evidence. The evidence includes chronicle accounts of administrative measures, surviving chancery documents, and comparison with other mirrors for princes and treatises on administration. It also includes the internal evidence of the text, such as its references to contemporary conditions and its manuscript variation.

Points of disagreement. The disagreement concerns the relationship between a normative text and social reality. It is also affected by the unresolved question of authorship, since a composite or revised text would be a less direct witness than the work of a minister writing in office.

Why it matters. If the text is descriptive, it supports a picture of a well-organized Seljuk bureaucracy. If it is idealizing, the picture is more tentative, and the state appears more dependent on personal relationships and less regular than the text suggests.

Section 39.22

The Limits of the Ideal State: Religion, Gender, Hierarchy, and Political Exclusion

The vision of an orderly and just state in the Siyasatnama has limits, and some of them are inseparable from the picture of society that the text assumes. Reading it critically means asking who is included in the ideal and who is excluded or subordinated, and why polemical passages cannot simply be taken as neutral descriptions of Seljuk society or of official policy.

The text assumes a hierarchical society in which each group has its proper place. Rulers rule, officials administer, soldiers fight, scholars teach, cultivators farm, and each should remain in their station. The preservation of this order is treated as part of justice, and attempts to change it are regarded as dangerous. Servants and subordinates are to be disciplined and kept in their place, and the text shows little concern for the possibility that those at the bottom might have claims against those above them other than the claim to protection from unjust treatment. This reflects the assumptions of the elite author and audience, and it should not be mistaken for the views of the entire population.

The treatment of women is revealing. In passages that discuss the court, the text counsels against allowing women, especially those of the ruler's household, to interfere in affairs of state and to have access to men who hold office. It presents the influence of women over rulers as a source of trouble and illustrates the point with stories of rulers who were misled by their wives or favorites. This attitude is a common feature of advice literature and it reflects a conviction that political order depends on a separation of spheres. It also appears in a context in which royal women, such as Terken Khatun, in fact had large resources and political roles, as Section 39.30 and Division VII show. The text's disapproval is better read as evidence of the importance of women at court, and of the anxiety of male officials about their influence, than as a description of their actual exclusion.

On religion, the work reveals strong attitudes. It displays loyalty to Sunni Islam and to the authority of the caliphate and the Seljuk ruler, and in its later chapters it is hostile to groups that it regards as heretical or subversive, notably the Ismailis, whom it calls by terms that reflect the polemical vocabulary of its time, and to certain other Shiʿi and dissenting groups. It also expresses suspicion of non-Muslim officials in the administration, complaining that Christians and Jews and others had acquired positions of power and urging the ruler to exclude them. These passages should be read with great care. They are polemical, they reflect the author's views and the concerns of a particular group, and they may exaggerate the number and influence of those they attack. The actual composition of the Seljuk administration included Muslims of different persuasions and, at least at lower levels, non-Muslims, and the evidence shows that policies of exclusion were not applied consistently. The text is evidence of a hostile attitude, not of a uniform policy, and the existence of the complaint implies that the practice it condemned existed.

The later chapters on heterodox movements need particular caution. They were written, or are traditionally said to have been written, in the context of the Ismaili challenge after 1090, and they portray Ismailis as dangerous conspirators. The language is polemical, and it follows a long tradition of Sunni writing against the Ismailis in which accusations of secret subversion and doctrinal deviation were common. Such writing cannot be taken as an accurate account of Ismaili beliefs or practice, and the political conflict that gave rise to it cannot be reduced to the picture the polemic paints. Chapter 41 examines the Ismaili movement on its own terms.

Taken together, these features show that the ideal state of the Siyasatnama was an ideal for a particular group. It was a vision of order formulated by a Sunni Persian administrator for a Turkic sultan, serving the interests and anxieties of the administrative and scholarly elite. This does not make it unimportant. It was an influential statement of political thought that shaped later Persianate writing on government. But a student who reads it uncritically will take the standpoint of the palace and the chancery and miss the experiences of the many people whom the ideal state governed, taxed, and sometimes excluded. The following divisions return to those people.

Division V · §§39.23–39.27 Madrasas, Scholars, and Religious Patronage
Section 39.23

The Madrasa before Nizam al-Mulk

Nizam al-Mulk is more widely remembered for founding schools than for any act of government, and the colleges that carry his name, the Nizamiyyas, have long been treated as the beginning of the madrasa as an institution. The claim is an overstatement. Before the vizier established his first college, Muslim scholars had taught law, theology, and the study of the Prophet's traditions in a variety of settings for more than two centuries, and institutions that can reasonably be called madrasas already existed in several places. This section sets out what he inherited.

The earliest and most basic setting for teaching was the mosque. A scholar sat in a corner or beside a pillar, and students gathered around him in a circle to hear him lecture, to read texts with him, and to ask questions. Such study circles needed no endowment and no formal organization, and they were the main means by which religious knowledge was passed on. A student who wished to master law might travel from teacher to teacher, studying with a number of scholars over several years, and receive from each a certificate authorizing him to transmit what he had learned. This system was informal, personal, and flexible. It depended on the reputation of individual teachers and on the willingness of students to support themselves, often with help from patrons.

Over time, a number of supporting institutions developed. Lodgings for students, called khans, were established near mosques. Private homes of scholars served as places of instruction. Wealthy patrons endowed libraries and endowed scholars with stipends. By the tenth century, in the eastern lands, there is evidence of institutions devoted especially to legal teaching and supported by endowments, and these are the schools that historians call early madrasas. Khurasan, and Nishapur above all, was a center of this development. Sources mention teaching institutions in Nishapur associated with eminent scholars, among them schools connected with the names of Ibn Furak and Abu Ishaq al-Isfaraʾini, and with the Shafiʿi and Ashʿari scholars of the city. Others existed in Bukhara and elsewhere in Transoxiana under Samanid and Qarakhanid rule, serving Hanafi scholars especially.

The details of these early institutions are uncertain, and scholars disagree about how much to call them madrasas. The difficulty is partly one of definition. If a madrasa is defined as a building endowed for the teaching of law with a stipend for teachers and students, a number of eastern institutions before 1060 qualify. If it is defined as an institution with a formal curriculum, a salaried staff, and administrative links to a government, the Nizamiyya is a stronger example, but even then it is a matter of degree. The accurate statement is that the madrasa developed gradually, that earlier forms existed, and that the Nizamiyya colleges were a prominent and influential stage in a longer process.

The social context of this development matters. The scholars who taught law were not only religious teachers. They were judges, preachers, legal advisers, and community leaders, and their influence extended into politics. In a city such as Nishapur, the leading families of scholars formed an urban elite whose members competed for positions and for the support of the rulers. The study of law was also a path to careers in the judiciary and administration, and the schools served as places of recruitment. Their legal teaching was tied to schools of law, the Shafiʿi, Hanafi, and others, which competed with one another. The existence of such rivalries meant that patronage of a school was also a political choice, favoring one school over another.

What then did Nizam al-Mulk add? He extended and systematized a form of patronage that already existed, using the resources of a large state to found colleges in many cities, to endow them with regular income, and to appoint teachers of high repute. He linked the colleges to the political purposes of the Seljuk government. And he supported a particular combination of legal and theological teaching, that of the Shafiʿi school and the Ashʿari theology, which gave the colleges a recognizable character. These contributions were significant, but they presuppose an existing institution, and the vizier's reputation as the founder of the madrasa reflects the success with which the Nizamiyyas were remembered, not the absence of earlier schools.

Historical Vocabulary: Madrasa, Waqf, Nizamiyya
Madrasa · Waqf · Nizamiyya

Definition. A madrasa is an institution for higher religious learning, especially law, usually supported by an endowment. A waqf is a legal endowment in which property is dedicated to a pious or charitable purpose, with its income assigned to specified uses such as the maintenance of a school, mosque, or hospital. The Nizamiyya is the name given to the colleges established or patronized by Nizam al-Mulk, derived from his title.

Historical context. Endowments of this kind were developed in Islamic law in the early centuries and were widely used by the eleventh century. The madrasa developed from the older practices of study circles in mosques and private homes, and its forms varied across regions.

Why it matters. The waqf gave institutions a durable income, reducing their dependence on the favor of a ruler, while also giving founders and their families a lasting influence over appointments and administration. The Nizamiyya colleges are an example of how political patronage and religious learning were joined.

Potential misunderstanding. The Nizamiyya is not a "university" in the modern or later European sense, and it was not the first madrasa. It was one prominent kind of college within a network of schools, mosques, and teachers, and its significance lay in scale, patronage, and association with the state.

Section 39.24

The Nizamiyya of Baghdad: Foundation, Endowment, and Teaching

The college that most clearly established Nizam al-Mulk's reputation was the Nizamiyya of Baghdad. It was begun in the middle of the 1060s, probably about 1065, and inaugurated in 1067. It stood on the east bank of the Tigris, in a city that was the seat of the caliphate, the home of the most influential community of scholars in the Sunni world, and the principal center of the Hanbali and Shafiʿi traditions of law. The choice of this site was a statement of the vizier's intention to be present in the religious center of Islam.

The founding of the college was a public event. The sources describe the construction of a building with a mosque, teaching rooms, lodgings for students, and facilities for staff, and they report that the vizier assigned it a regular income through an endowment. The properties endowed to it, according to later biographical tradition, included shops, lands, and other sources of revenue, intended to pay teachers and students and to maintain the buildings. The precise composition and value of the endowment are not securely known, and figures reported by later writers should be treated with caution. What is clear is that the college was endowed on a scale that made it a stable and prestigious institution.

The college was associated from the beginning with the Shafiʿi school of law and with the scholars of the Ashʿari theological tradition. The foremost Shafiʿi jurist in Baghdad was Abu Ishaq al-Shirazi, a teacher of great repute and a prolific writer on law and legal theory, and he was the person whom the vizier wished to appoint as head of the new college. According to the biographical accounts, Abu Ishaq at first hesitated, or declined, because of doubts about the legitimacy of the site or the sources of the endowment, and the opening was delayed or begun under another teacher before he agreed to take up the post. The story appears in several later biographical works and is told in several versions. It may preserve an authentic memory of scruples about property acquired by a government, or it may be a pious anecdote, and it cannot be verified from contemporary records. It is useful as evidence of the ethical anxieties that surrounded state patronage of religious institutions.

The organization of teaching followed the pattern of legal study. A senior teacher held the principal chair and lectured on law, the foundations of law, and associated subjects. Assistants, repeaters, and students engaged in discussion and debate, and the training emphasized the skills of legal argument and disputation, in which students defended or attacked legal positions according to set conventions. The curriculum was centered on law, with theology, Arabic, and the study of the Prophet's traditions as supporting disciplines. It was not a general curriculum in the sense of a university offering medicine or philosophy, though individual teachers might teach other subjects privately.

The support given to teachers and students was a major feature of the institution. Teachers received stipends, and the college provided students with lodging, and in some cases with allowances and meals. Free instruction and support meant that students who could not otherwise afford a long education could pursue one, and it also meant that they depended on the college and its patron. The college thus served as a means of recruiting and training a body of scholars, judges, and officials who were shaped by its teaching and linked to its patron. Some of its graduates entered the Seljuk administration and the judiciary, and the vizier's patronage created ties that extended throughout the system.

The place of the Nizamiyya in the religious environment of Baghdad was not uncontested. The city had a strong Hanbali community with a history of conflict with other groups, and its scholars looked on the new college with some reserve. Shiʿi communities of the city, who had prospered under the Buyids, observed that a major institution had been created for the promotion of Sunni learning under Seljuk patronage. The caliph and his officials had their own interests. The college was therefore a participant in the competition among groups, and its establishment contributed to the sharpening of religious boundaries in Baghdad, even though the vizier's own policy was not one of simple confrontation, as Section 39.27 will show.

Later centuries remembered the Baghdad Nizamiyya as an outstanding institution, and its alumni and teachers included some of the best-known scholars of the age. This reputation partly reflects the work of later biographers, who emphasized its importance, and it needs to be separated from what can be established about its early operation. The evidence is largely narrative, since the college's own records have not survived, and its physical remains are limited. Even so, the combination of endowment, appointment of a leading teacher, regular stipends, and state backing made it a significant institution.

Section 39.25

The Nizamiyya Network across the Seljuk Lands

Baghdad was the most famous of the Nizamiyya colleges, but it was not the only one. Later sources attribute to Nizam al-Mulk the foundation or patronage of colleges in a number of cities across the Seljuk lands, and the name Nizamiyya became attached to a group of institutions. Evaluating this network requires distinguishing among colleges whose foundation by the vizier is well attested, those whose connection with him is traditional or uncertain, and the modern idea of a unified system.

The cities most often named are Nishapur, Balkh, Herat, Isfahan, Merv, Basra, Mosul, and Amul in Tabaristan, in addition to Baghdad. The colleges in these places were located in major centers of population and scholarship across a broad area, from the Khurasani cities of the east to the Iraqi cities of the west. The Nishapur foundation had special importance, because Nishapur was the leading center of Shafiʿi and Ashʿari learning in the east, and a college there, associated with the teaching of the great jurist al-Juwayni, connected the vizier with the intellectual center of his own region. The colleges of Balkh, Herat, and Merv extended his patronage across the eastern cities, and that of Isfahan was in the city that became the main seat of the court.

The evidence for these colleges is uneven. Some foundations are mentioned in the work of several independent writers and are described with details of their teachers and endowments. Others are mentioned in later sources without such details, and a few attributions may reflect the habit of naming a college after the vizier because he was the most famous patron of the age, or because later patrons associated their foundations with his name. In some cases, he may have patronized or expanded an existing school rather than founding a new one. Historians therefore speak of a network of foundations and patronage associated with Nizam al-Mulk and avoid claiming a precise list or a uniform origin.

It would also be wrong to picture the Nizamiyyas as a centralized system comparable to a modern university. There was no common administration, no standard curriculum issued from a central office, and no unified examination or degree. Each college had its own endowment, its own staff, and its own relations with local scholars and officials. The links among them were those of patronage and personal connection: a common founder, a shared legal and theological orientation, and the circulation of teachers and students. The vizier's role as patron gave the network a measure of coherence. After his death, the colleges continued under other patrons and officials, and their later histories diverged.

The reasons for creating such a network can be understood in several ways. Religious purposes were real: the vizier was a devout Sunni who valued learning and wished to support the study of law and theology. Political purposes were equally evident. The colleges trained men who would serve as judges, preachers, and officials, and who would be loyal to the system that had educated them. They created centers of Sunni scholarship in cities where the Seljuk government needed the support of the local elite. They also helped the dynasty present itself as a patron of Islamic learning and a defender of the faith against rival movements. Finally, the colleges served the vizier's own standing, since the scholars who benefited from them were likely to support him in the conflicts of court politics.

The impact on the cities in which the colleges were located varied. In Nishapur, a college added a major institution to a city that already had a vigorous scholarly life. In smaller cities, a college could represent a more substantial change, bringing teachers and students and a flow of endowment income. The effect on the established scholarly families, who had traditionally controlled teaching and appointments, was mixed: some benefited from the new positions, while others lost influence to scholars favored by the vizier. The sources provide only glimpses of these local conflicts, and the story of the Nizamiyya network in each city remains to be reconstructed.

Section 39.26

Al-Juwayni, al-Ghazali, and the Patronage of Religious Scholarship

The relationship between powerful scholars and the vizier illustrates how patronage worked in practice. Two figures have a special place: Abu'l-Maʿali al-Juwayni, called Imam al-Haramayn, and Abu Hamid al-Ghazali. The full intellectual biography and the writings of both belong to Chapters 40 and 43, and this section deals only with their relation to the vizier and the system of patronage.

Al-Juwayni was a leading Shafiʿi jurist and Ashʿari theologian of Nishapur. In the 1050s, when the administration of Tughril under al-Kunduri took measures against Ashʿari preachers, he left Nishapur and spent years in the Hijaz, in Mecca and Medina, a stay that gave him his epithet "Imam of the Two Sanctuaries." After Alp Arslan came to the throne and Nizam al-Mulk took office, the restrictions were lifted, and he returned to Nishapur, where he taught for the rest of his life and died in 1085. His return was part of a larger change in the religious atmosphere of Khurasan, and the sources associate his teaching with the Nizamiyya of Nishapur. The vizier's patronage gave him a base and a following. A scholar of his eminence was also a political asset, since his endorsement conferred prestige on the regime that supported him.

Al-Ghazali, born around 1058 in the district of Tus, studied with al-Juwayni in Nishapur and then entered the entourage of the vizier, whose camp was a center of scholarly discussion and debate. He impressed the vizier and in 1091 was appointed to teach at the Nizamiyya of Baghdad, a position of great prestige. His career there, his later crisis, and his writings are the subject of other chapters. For present purposes, the point is that the appointment of a young and brilliant scholar to the principal chair of the empire's best-known college was an exercise of patronage with political significance. It linked the vizier to a rising scholar, and it placed in Baghdad a teacher whose reputation would reinforce the college's standing.

The relationship between a patron and a scholar was reciprocal but unequal. The scholar received position, income, protection, and the means to teach and write. The patron received prestige, loyalty, and the services of a learned adviser who could argue his case in debates with rival groups and who could provide legal and theological support for his policies. Scholars like al-Juwayni and al-Ghazali were also independent figures whose standing depended on their learning, and they could not be treated simply as servants. The sources contain accounts of debates conducted in the vizier's presence, in which scholars of different persuasions argued before him, and these show that he sought to hear and judge disputes as well as to promote one side.

The example of al-Juwayni also shows that patronage could reverse earlier policy. The scholars who left Nishapur because of the pressures of the 1050s were able to return because the new vizier adopted a different course. This was a political decision as well as a religious one. It restored to Nishapur a group of respected scholars who had been displaced, and it won their support. It also demonstrated that the regime's religious policy was determined by the choices of those at the top, which meant that a change in office holders could produce a change of policy.

Students should avoid two simplifications. One is to imagine that the Nizamiyya colleges were mere instruments of state propaganda, since scholars of integrity taught in them and developed their own views, some of which were not entirely welcome to the authorities. The other is to imagine that the colleges were independent of political influence, since appointments and endowments came from the vizier. Both elements were present, and the interplay between scholarly independence and political patronage was one of the defining features of the institutions.

Section 39.27

Waqf, Shafiʿi Scholarship, Ashʿari Theology, and Sunni Political Networks

The endowment, the school of law, the theological orientation, and the personal network of patron and client together made up the religious policy of Nizam al-Mulk. This section looks at each and then asks how uniform the policy was.

The legal mechanism was the waqf. A person who wished to endow an institution would dedicate property, such as shops, farmland, or baths, to the purpose, specifying how the income was to be used and who was to administer it. A properly established waqf could not be sold or inherited, and its income was reserved for the stated purpose. For a college, this meant a stable income to pay teachers and students and to maintain the buildings. The administrator, often called a mutawalli, managed the property and distributed the income. The founder usually retained a role in appointing the administrator and the teachers, and could specify the school of law to be taught. This gave the founder lasting influence over the institution's character. In the case of the Nizamiyya, the vizier and his successors in the family could shape appointments, and the Shafiʿi orientation was built into the endowment.

The waqf also served other purposes. It provided a way of preserving wealth, since property in a waqf was protected, in principle, from confiscation by rulers. This was a concern for officials whose fortunes could be seized when they fell from favor, and endowments in favor of one's family or institutions could secure some part of a fortune. The system created opportunities for abuse and for conflict, since administrators could misuse the income and rulers could pressure endowments. But it was also a major source of the social infrastructure of Muslim cities, financing mosques, schools, hospitals, and public works, and the Seljuk period saw a large increase in such endowments.

The school of law and the theology that the colleges favored were the Shafiʿi school and the Ashʿari theological tradition. The Shafiʿi school was one of the major Sunni schools of law, strong among the scholarly elite of Khurasan and Iraq, while the Seljuk rulers themselves were Hanafi. The Ashʿari tradition was a school of Sunni theology that used rational argument to defend traditional doctrines, and its scholars were prominent in Nishapur. The vizier's support for these groups shaped the religious establishment of the Seljuk lands, and it benefited scholars of that orientation at the expense of others. The reasons for his preference included his own education and sympathies, the strength of Shafiʿi and Ashʿari scholars in Khurasan, and a political calculation that such scholars could provide support for the regime and a defense against Shiʿi and Ismaili rivals.

Relations with other groups were complicated. The Hanafi school was the school of the dynasty and of many Turkic and eastern Iranian Muslims, and the Hanafis continued to hold judicial and teaching positions. The Hanbalis of Baghdad, strongly traditionalist and suspicious of the rationalist methods of the Ashʿaris, were a frequent source of conflict, and violent clashes between Hanbali and Shafiʿi groups occurred in Baghdad in this period. The vizier's surviving correspondence and the narratives of these disturbances suggest that he was concerned to restrain disorder and to prevent religious competition from becoming a political threat. He sought to calm the parties, to protect his scholars, and to avoid alienating the caliph and the city's population. His policy therefore combined patronage of a favored group with efforts at accommodation.

Toward Shiʿi communities, the policy was one of Sunni preference combined with practical coexistence, as Chapter 38 observed. Shiʿi scholars and officials continued to be active, and the administration employed men of different persuasions. The hostility that appears in the Siyasatnama and in some chronicle accounts was real, and it affected relations, but it did not produce a uniform program of suppression. The Ismailis were treated as a political danger and as subversives, a perception that intensified after 1090. The distinction between rhetoric and administrative policy is essential. A text may denounce a group and a government may nonetheless employ or tolerate its members, depending on circumstances.

The overall picture is of a minister who used religious patronage as a tool of statecraft and who was also a sincere supporter of the learning he patronized. His policy was not uniform across time, region, or group. It reflected the balance of power within the Sunni community, the needs of the regime, and his own preferences. Interpreting it as a campaign of Sunni exclusion, or as a policy of broad toleration, misses its complexity.

Continuity and Change Over Time: Religious Education before and after Nizam al-Mulk

What changed? The scale and political connection of endowed teaching institutions increased. Colleges were founded in many cities with state backing, regular endowments, and appointments tied to the vizier's patronage. The Shafiʿi and Ashʿari orientation gained institutional support. Scholars gained new careers and new ties to officials.

What remained continuous? The informal study circle, the personal transmission of knowledge from teacher to student, the plurality of legal schools, the role of scholars as judges and community leaders, and the use of endowments all continued. Older schools, mosques, and teachers kept functioning.

When did change occur? The change was gradual and uneven. Endowed legal colleges existed before the 1060s, the Nizamiyya foundations of the 1060s to 1090s accelerated the process, and the institution spread more widely in the twelfth century under many patrons.

Was change gradual, uneven, abrupt, contested, or cyclical? It was gradual and uneven, and it was contested: Hanbali and other groups had reservations, and rivalries among schools affected who benefited.

Who experienced change most directly? Scholars of the favored schools, students who gained access to support, and officials trained in the colleges experienced it most directly. Scholars of other schools, and communities whose institutions were less favored, experienced it as a shift in the balance of patronage.

Who experienced greater continuity? Villagers and many townspeople, whose religious life centered on local mosques, preachers, and customs, probably saw less change.

Why does the pattern matter? It shows that institutional innovation often builds on existing practice, and that the creation of a famous institution can be less a break than an acceleration. It also shows how state patronage can alter the balance among groups without erasing earlier forms.

Division VI · §§39.28–39.31 State, Society, Patronage, and Competing Interests
Section 39.28

The State and Its Subjects: Justice, Taxation, and Rural Society

Most of the people governed by the Seljuk state were cultivators, herders, and villagers who left no written account of how the government looked to them. The sources that survive were written by officials, scholars, and chroniclers living in cities and courts, and they mention rural people mainly when taxes, revolts, or disasters brought them to the attention of the literate. A history of the state that speaks only through those sources risks repeating the point of view of the palace and the chancery. This section tries to read them for what they reveal about the people at the base of the system.

Rural society varied by region. On the Khurasani and plateau oases, villages lay along irrigation channels, and the community maintained its water supply collectively. Landholding was mixed: some cultivators owned small plots, some worked land belonging to larger landholders or to endowments and paid a share of the crop, and some were dependent laborers. Local headmen and landholders of long-established families, whom Persian sources call by terms such as dihqan or raʾis, mediated between the village and the officials. In pastoral districts, tribal leaders played a similar role. These intermediaries were crucial, since the central government rarely dealt directly with individual cultivators. They also had their own interests, and they could exploit those below them as readily as they could protect them.

The state touched rural people in several ways. It assessed and collected taxes, demanded labor or supplies on occasion, quartered soldiers or horses, and sometimes required service on roads, canals, or fortifications. Where land was assigned to a military holder, the holder's agents collected the taxes and might also exercise influence in other ways. A good official or holder could maintain the irrigation works, mediate disputes, and protect against raiders. A bad one could take more than was due, seize property, demand unlawful payments, and employ force. The sources contain both kinds of reports, and the proportions in the records do not measure the proportions in reality.

The ideal of protection, as the Siyasatnama expressed it, was that the ruler should shield cultivators from oppression, since their prosperity was the basis of revenue. The ideal had a practical foundation, because a ruined peasantry produced no taxes. It was also in tension with the realities of military finance. A commander who held an assignment for a short period and who needed to pay his troops had an incentive to collect as much as possible quickly. The center could try to limit such behavior through inspection and complaint procedures, but the gap between the ideal of protection and the possibility of exploitation was built into the structure of the system. How wide the gap was in a given district depended on the character of local officials and the strength of the center.

Redress was available in principle through the judge, through petitions to officials, and, in extreme cases, through appeal to the ruler. In practice, access was unequal. A village with a respected headman or a connection to a scholar or official might obtain a hearing. An isolated cultivator without such support faced obstacles of distance, cost, and the risk of retaliation by those he accused. Flight was a common response to unbearable conditions: families left for other districts or for cities, taking their labor with them. Revolt was rarer but occurred, and the sources mention rural unrest in connection with heavy taxation and military exactions. Such acts were often interpreted by chroniclers as disorder, and the grievances behind them have to be inferred.

Environmental conditions shaped the pressure on rural society. Drought, flood, and the failure of qanats or canals could ruin a village's harvest and make the usual tax impossible to pay. Armies marching through a district could consume its stores. Disease and famine, mentioned in the chronicles of the period, could depopulate areas. The state's policy of remission of taxes in bad years, which the sources attribute to some rulers and officials, was a response to these conditions, though remission depended on the ruler's awareness and goodwill. The fiscal system, with its fixed assessments, was ill-adapted to the variability of the plateau's climate, and cultivators bore the risk.

He values registers, audits, and the ability to move assignments. He also depends on commanders and local elites, and must balance their demands against the sultan's wishes and the needs of cultivators. His account of the system is that of a manager under pressure.
The Provincial GovernorHe represents the center but depends on local cooperation, on soldiers whose loyalty is uncertain, and on the favor of the court. He needs enough revenue to meet his obligations and to maintain order, and he is tempted to take advantage of distance from the center. His interest lies in a degree of autonomy.
The Tax Collector or Local AgentHe is paid by the system and is often a local man or a member of the secretarial class. He knows the villages and the registers, and he can be pressured from above to produce revenue and from below by neighbors who resent him. He has opportunities for gain through manipulation of assessments, and the risk of punishment if accounts are found wanting.
The CultivatorHe owes a share of the crop or a fixed payment regardless of the harvest, may owe additional dues, and may have to feed soldiers or supply labor. He wants stable and predictable demands, protection from extortion, and access to water. His options when conditions become intolerable include petition, appeal through a local notable, flight, or occasionally resistance. His perspective is the least directly documented.
The Soldier or Assignment HolderHe depends on revenue to support himself and his men, and he suspects that civilian officials, who control assignments and accounts, may cheat or delay him. He wants secure and productive assignments. His interests are served by long tenure and high yields, which may conflict with the interests of cultivators and of the center.
The JudgeHe is a legal scholar who applies the law to disputes over property, taxes, and contracts. He is appointed by or tied to the government but claims to speak for law. He may support complainants against officials, or may defer to the powerful. His standing depends on the respect of the local community and the support of the authorities.
The MerchantHe wants safe roads, predictable tolls, and the enforcement of contracts. He pays customs and market taxes and may lend money to officials or soldiers. Stable government benefits him, but demands for loans and arbitrary exactions harm him. He may also be a source of credit for the state.

Reflection. The perspectives overlap and conflict. All of them depended on the same fiscal system, but they stood in different relations of power to it. The vizier, governor, and soldier had direct influence on how it operated. The cultivator and merchant mainly experienced its effects. Students should consider how the preserved sources favor some of these groups, and what that implies for the history of the system.

Section 39.29

Cities, Merchants, Religious Elites, and the Distribution of Patronage

Cities were the places where the Seljuk state was most visible and where its relations with its subjects were most actively negotiated. Rulers resided in or near them, governors and judges worked in them, markets and mosques were concentrated in them, and the scholars and notables who shaped opinion lived in them. The government's dealings with urban society are therefore a central part of the story of the Persianate state.

Urban society in this period included several overlapping groups. Landholding families, often of long standing, held property in the city and in the surrounding countryside and supplied officials and scholars. Merchants engaged in local and long-distance trade, dealing in textiles, metals, spices, horses, and the produce of the region. Artisans worked in the markets, organized by craft and neighborhood. Religious scholars, judges, preachers, and teachers formed a prestigious class with influence out of proportion to their numbers. Slaves, servants, laborers, and the poor made up the larger part of the population. The cities also contained minority communities of Christians, Jews, and Zoroastrians, whose members worked as merchants, craftsmen, and sometimes officials.

The state's relationship to the urban elites involved bargaining over taxes, appointments, and recognition. The government depended on local notables to keep order, supply information, and help collect revenue. In exchange, the notables expected respect for their positions and protection of their interests. Leading families often held posts such as the headship of a quarter or the chief judgeship, and rulers who disregarded their claims risked trouble. Seljuk officials, including the vizier, cultivated these relationships through appointments, gifts, and endowments, and through attention to the demands of the Sunni scholars who spoke for the urban community.

Merchants benefited from security and the maintenance of roads, bridges, and caravanserais, and the Seljuk sources praise rulers who built them or abolished arbitrary tolls. Chronicles attribute to Malik Shah measures of this kind, including the removal of certain unauthorized levies on travelers. The reliability of such reports is limited, since chroniclers favored the memory of a great sultan, but they suggest that the regime recognized the value of commerce to its revenue. At the same time merchants were exposed to confiscation, forced loans, and extortion by officials and soldiers, and the evidence indicates that some regimes and officials treated them harshly. The relationship was ambivalent.

The distribution of patronage shaped urban politics. Appointments as judges, preachers, heads of colleges, and administrators of endowments were prizes that families and factions competed for. The endowments that supported mosques, colleges, and charitable institutions created large flows of resources that required administration and that benefited those who controlled them. The vizier's patronage placed a great deal of this in the hands of Shafiʿi and Ashʿari scholars and of his own clients, and this altered the balance of influence in many cities. Rival schools and families resented the change, and conflicts over positions and resources sometimes took the form of religious disputes.

Financial resources for these activities came from the revenue of the state and from the wealth of the patrons. The vizier's own income and that of his family were large, and part of it was devoted to endowments and charity. The question of how much came from legitimate salary and assignments, how much from gifts and perquisites, and how much from less defensible sources is one that the evidence does not answer. What can be said is that the scale of patronage in the later eleventh century depended on the exceptional concentration of resources in the hands of the vizier and the sultan, and that this concentration was itself a source of political tension.

Everyday Life and Social History: Taxation, Officials, Rural Populations, Urban Livelihoods, and Petitioning

In the village. A family's year followed the agricultural cycle: sowing, irrigation, harvest, and the threshing of grain. At harvest time the tax collector or the agent of a revenue holder appeared to assess the crop and to take the state's share, and the village headman negotiated on the community's behalf. A family with a small surplus could store grain, sell some in the market, and repay debts. A family hit by a poor harvest might borrow from a landholder or merchant, and the debt could bind it to a patron for years. The condition of the water channels, maintained by collective labor, decided much about the next year's prospects.

In the town. An artisan's workshop stood in a market district organized by craft. He paid market dues, bought materials from merchants, and sold to local customers and traveling traders. He might belong to a neighborhood association or a religious fellowship that provided support in hardship. The mosque and the college offered education for his sons if he could afford it, or if they were selected for a stipend. The presence of the court or a garrison increased demand for goods and also brought exactions and disorder.

With officials. Contact with the state meant contact with individuals: the tax collector, the judge, the market inspector, the soldier quartered in a house. The conduct of these individuals determined whether the state appeared as protector or oppressor. Bribery, favoritism, and patronage were part of everyday dealings, and a person without connections was at a disadvantage.

Petitioning. A person with a grievance could appeal to the local judge or official, or try to reach the vizier or sultan. Petitions were often written by professional scribes. A petitioner needed an advocate, a sponsor, or a gift to ensure that his case was heard. The ideal of public audience in which any subject could address the ruler was real in some circumstances, but it was filtered by distance, cost, and the gatekeeping of officials.

Sources and limits. This account is reconstructed from chronicles, legal and administrative treatises, and the general pattern of agrarian societies. It does not rest on diaries or village records, which do not survive. It describes typical conditions, and individual experiences varied.

Section 39.30

The Vizier's Household, Family Networks, and Political Rivals

The household of Nizam al-Mulk was a political institution. It comprised his immediate family, his secretaries and agents, his slaves and soldiers, and a larger circle of clients who depended on his patronage. It traveled with him, kept his records, conducted his business, and provided him with the means to act independently of the sultan's own household. Understanding it is necessary to understanding both his strength and his vulnerability.

His sons held important positions during his lifetime and afterward. The sources name several of them as governors, officials, or advisers, and some went on to serve later sultans as viziers or ministers. Among those mentioned are Fakhr al-Mulk, who served the later ruler Sanjar and was killed in 1106 in an attack attributed to Ismailis, and others who held office under Malik Shah's sons. The appointments of relatives were a normal feature of premodern administration, in which family loyalty was a basis of trust, and they gave the vizier a reliable network. They also created resentment among those excluded and invited accusations of nepotism, the charge that a few families monopolized the offices of state.

Beyond kin, the vizier built a following of protégés. Secretaries he trained, scholars he supported, and soldiers he equipped formed a body of dependents who owed their careers to him. The system of patronage by which officials acquired followers was a standard method of building influence, and it was neither unique to him nor illegitimate in its own terms. It meant that the administration was partly a collection of personal followings, and that a change of vizier could require the replacement of many officials. It also meant that he was a point of connection among otherwise separate groups, and that his removal would disrupt many relationships.

Competition for office was constant. The rivals of Nizam al-Mulk included commanders who disliked civilian control of revenue, secretaries who resented the dominance of his network, and members of the dynasty and court who sought influence over the sultan. The conflict with the circle of Terken Khatun and her administrator Taj al-Mulk, examined in Division VII, was the most serious, but it was not the only one. Earlier rivalries, such as that with al-Kunduri, had ended in the elimination of the rival. In the court of Malik Shah, similar struggles took place less violently for many years, until they culminated in the crisis of 1092.

The wealth of the household was both a means and a problem. It allowed the vizier to endow colleges, reward followers, and maintain a large retinue. It also drew criticism. The sources, both admiring and hostile, comment on his riches, and some stories present them as the fruit of honest service and others as the result of exploitation. The evidence does not allow a quantitative assessment. A fair statement is that the vizier, like other high officials of the period, accumulated substantial resources through salary, assignments, gifts, and perquisites, that the line between official and private resources was unclear, and that his enemies could use this ambiguity against him.

Royal women were political actors in this environment. Terken Khatun, Malik Shah's wife and the mother of one of his sons, had her own household, officials, and resources, and she supported her own candidate for the succession. Other women of the dynasty, including the wives and daughters of the sultans and the mothers of princes, took part in alliances and negotiations. The sources, written mostly by men with a negative view of women in politics, present them as intriguers. A more careful reading sees them as participants whose interests were bound up with those of their sons and households, and who used the means available to them. The conflict between the vizier's household and the household of Terken Khatun was thus a conflict between two networks of patronage, each with its own candidates and clients.

The relationship between administrative continuity and family power was thus double-edged. The vizier's household gave the government stability, since it provided a body of experienced and loyal officials. At the same time it linked administration to the fortunes of one family, which meant that the family's fall could disrupt government and that its rivals would see its power as a threat. The history of the later eleventh century shows both effects.

Section 39.31

Religious Opposition, Ismaili Politics, and the Language of Sedition

The final years of Nizam al-Mulk's tenure coincided with the emergence of a new and formidable challenge. The Ismaili movement organized by Hasan-i Sabbah established fortified bases in the mountains and challenged Seljuk rule. It became identified as Nizari only after the Fatimid succession dispute of 1094, two years after the vizier's death; for his lifetime, references to "Nizaris" are therefore retrospective. This section examines the challenge in the context of the vizier's political position and asks how historians should read the language in which it was described. The full history of the movement and its state is reserved for Chapter 41.

The Ismaili movement had a long history in Iran. The Fatimid caliphate in Egypt supported a network of missionaries who preached in Iranian cities and villages, seeking converts and political support, and the Seljuk authorities regarded them with suspicion. In 1090, a group led by Hasan-i Sabbah seized the fortress of Alamut in the Alborz mountains, and over the following years his Ismaili followers took other strongholds in Iran; the later Nizari network would also develop in Syria. They drew support from sections of the rural and urban population in various regions, and they held out against Seljuk sieges. The Seljuk state, whose power rested on control of cities, roads, and revenue, found it hard to dislodge opponents who held mountain fortresses and could mobilize local support.

The vizier's role in responding is described in sources that are hostile to the Ismailis and favorable to him, and it is difficult to disentangle fact from polemic. He is portrayed as an uncompromising opponent who warned the sultan of the danger and urged vigorous action. The later chapters of the Siyasatnama present the Ismailis as a deadly threat and attack those who were negligent in resisting them. Military expeditions against the Ismaili strongholds around Alamut were launched in the early 1090s with limited success. What can be said is that the vizier and the court regarded the movement as dangerous, that attempts to suppress it were made, and that its resilience undermined confidence in the government's ability to maintain order.

The language of sedition requires careful reading. In the political culture of the time, accusations of being Ismaili or heretical were a means of discrediting rivals and justifying severe measures. They were sometimes accurate, since there were real Ismailis, and sometimes a weapon in factional conflict. The word used by hostile Sunni writers for the Ismailis, batini, referred to the claim that they pursued a hidden meaning of religious texts, and it carried an implication of secrecy and conspiracy. The polemical literature attributed to them a range of subversive doctrines and practices, many of which cannot be verified, since much of what is known of their beliefs comes from their enemies. The Nizari movement's own writings, many of which were destroyed in later conquests, survive only in part and in later forms.

Administrative security was an additional concern. A government that feared infiltration might watch officials and scholars for signs of sympathy, and the suspicion of heterodoxy could be turned against individuals. The Siyasatnama's warnings about the dangers of Ismaili agents in the administration fit this atmosphere. How far they led to actual investigations and purges is not clear from the evidence. The sources record some executions and expulsions in this period, but their number and character are uncertain, and some may have had other motives. The distinction between political opposition and the portrayal of it in hostile sources must be maintained.

The Ismaili challenge influenced the vizier's final years in other ways. It encouraged the hardening of religious boundaries, sharpened the sultan's and the vizier's concern with security, and introduced a new element of danger into political life, since this network later became associated with targeted attacks on prominent figures. It also provided a ready explanation for the vizier's death in 1092, which later writers attributed to an Ismaili assassin. As Division VII will show, that attribution is plausible but not conclusively established. The emergence of Alamut was part of the context of the crisis, and it is wise to distinguish the context from the cause.

Division VII · §§39.32–39.34 Court Conflict, Assassination, and the Crisis of 1092
Section 39.32

Nizam al-Mulk, Malik Shah, Terken Khatun, and Court Rivalry

By the late 1080s the young sultan of 1072 had become a mature ruler in his thirties, with a household of his own, sons approaching adulthood, and a wife of strong political standing. Relations between the sultan and the vizier who had guided him in his youth changed accordingly. The narratives of the final years speak of growing tension, but they do so in accounts that favor one side or the other, and the central task of this section is to read them critically.

Several sources of conflict can be identified with reasonable confidence. The first was the vizier's power. After two decades of dominance, his household, clients, and relatives held a large part of the offices of state, and a sultan who wished to assert himself could regard this as a limitation on his authority. The second was competition within the court. Terken Khatun, a Qarakhanid princess by origin according to the sources, maintained a household with its own officials, and her chief administrator, Taj al-Mulk Abu'l-Ghanaʾim, was a rival of the vizier's circle. Appointments, access to the sultan, and control of resources became subjects of contention between the two networks. The third was the succession. Malik Shah had several sons by different wives, and the question of which would succeed him affected the interests of every faction. The sources associate Terken Khatun with the cause of her own young son Mahmud, and the vizier's circle with that of Barkiyaruq, the sultan's older son by another wife, though the reports of these alignments may reflect later knowledge of how events turned out.

The relations with the caliphate added a further complication. As Section 39.8 noted, the marriage of a daughter of Malik Shah to the caliph al-Muqtadi raised the possibility of a grandson of the sultan becoming caliph, and some sources report that Malik Shah pressed the caliph on the question of succession and even on the residence of the caliph in Baghdad. The details vary and cannot be settled from the sources, and it is possible that later writers exaggerated the tension. But they agree that the court's journey to Baghdad in 1092 occurred in an atmosphere of dispute.

The accounts of the vizier's dismissal or removal from authority are contradictory. According to one tradition, Malik Shah, influenced by the rivals of the vizier, sent him a message reproaching him for the power of his sons and followers and for presuming to act as if he shared in the sultanate. The vizier is said to have replied with a speech using the image of the inkwell and the turban, to the effect that the two were joined by God and that the removal of one would end the other. The exchange is a well-known anecdote. It is widely repeated and has the character of a story told to dramatize the relationship of a minister and ruler, and its authenticity cannot be established. Other sources say that the sultan dismissed him shortly before his death. Others say nothing of a dismissal and describe him as in office until the end. A historian who weighs these reports must conclude that tension existed, that some attempts to restrict his power may have been made, and that the precise sequence of events is unknown.

The political struggle over the succession explains much of the court's atmosphere. A sultan who might die, as all must, left his supporters and opponents scrambling to position themselves for the aftermath. The vizier's age, probably in his seventies, meant that his own tenure could not last, and the question of who would control the administration when the sultan or the vizier died was pressing. A vizier who had built a large following would want to ensure that it survived him, and a rival group would want to supplant it. In this light, the conflicts of 1092 can be seen as the working out of tensions that had been building as the generation that had established the government neared the end of its natural span.

Students should resist the temptation to see the story as a morality play in which a wise and loyal minister was undone by a jealous ruler and a scheming queen. That picture is the product of a tradition that admired the vizier and was hostile to the queen. The evidence supports a less dramatic account. Two networks of patronage, each with real interests, competed for influence in a court in which no rule governed the succession and no institution could arbitrate. The conflict was a structural feature of the system as well as a clash of personalities, and a historian can acknowledge the human elements without attributing the outcome to the virtues or vices of individuals.

Section 39.33

The Assassination of Nizam al-Mulk, October 1092

The fact of the assassination is not in doubt. In October 1092, while the court was traveling in western Iran, Nizam al-Mulk was stabbed to death. The sources agree on the broad circumstances: it occurred during the journey from Isfahan toward Baghdad, in the region of Nahavand, at a stopping place called Sahna, and the attacker approached him in the guise of a Sufi or a petitioner. The vizier is reported to have been struck while being carried in a litter after the evening meal, which in Ramadan was the meal that broke the fast. The date given in Chapter 38, 14 October 1092, corresponds to the tenth of Ramadan in 485 of the Islamic calendar, and while sources differ slightly in their dating, the autumn of 1092 is secure.

The reports of the attacker differ. Many sources say that he was killed on the spot by the vizier's guards or soon afterward, and a number of later accounts, including those of the Nizari tradition, supply a name and describe him as a devoted agent of the Ismaili cause. The name and background given vary, and the earliest sources are less specific than the later ones. As in other cases, details tend to increase with the distance from the event. A historian should therefore regard the identification of the assassin by name as uncertain and should treat the claim that he acted for the Nizaris as a probable but unproven attribution.

The evidence for the Ismaili attribution rests on several grounds. The Nizaris were active and hostile to the Seljuk establishment, and they carried out attacks on prominent figures in the following years. The Nizari tradition later celebrated the killing as an early success of the movement. Sunni writers, including those close to the Seljuk court, accepted the attribution, and many repeated it. The method, an assassin who approached his victim in disguise and used a knife, resembles later Nizari attacks. On the other side, the contemporary evidence is limited, the later accounts have an interest in assigning the act to the movement, and the Nizari tradition may have claimed credit for an act performed by others. The earliest surviving narratives were written decades after the event. Moreover, alternative explanations circulated even in the sources that endorse the Ismaili attribution.

Those alternatives involve the court. Some sources report suspicion that the vizier's rivals arranged or encouraged the murder, or that the sultan was aware of it or privately welcomed it. The reports are mostly allusive, and they appear in the context of accounts that portray the court as divided. Since the vizier's death benefited Taj al-Mulk's circle and Terken Khatun's household, suspicion naturally fell on them, and the quick death of the sultan a few weeks later contributed to the speculation. No source provides decisive evidence of a conspiracy. The existence of the rumors tells us about the atmosphere of the court and about the difficulty of distinguishing between factional intrigue and religious violence, but not about who gave the order.

The questions of sponsorship, then, can be stated clearly. Who struck him is a matter of a probable but uncertain identification. Who sent the attacker is not conclusively established. It is possible that the Nizaris did it independently, that others used or encouraged them, or that the killing was commissioned by someone within the court. These possibilities are not mutually exclusive, and the surviving evidence does not permit the elimination of any of them with confidence. A textbook should not state more than the sources allow, and it should explain to students why the question remains open.

The significance of the event does not depend on resolving the question. The vizier's death removed the principal administrator of the empire at a moment when the sultan was already in conflict with him and when the succession was unsettled. It also made him a martyr in the eyes of Sunni writers, who recorded his death as that of a righteous minister struck down by the enemies of the faith, and this image shaped his reputation. Later writers used the assassination to dramatize the dangers of heresy and the value of the vizier's service. The memory of the event thus became part of the way in which later generations interpreted the Seljuk state.

Primary Source Window IV: Chronicle Accounts of the 1092 Court Crisis and the Assassination
Source type: Arabic and Persian narrative chronicles and later histories · Examples: Ibn al-Athir, al-Kāmil fī'l-taʾrīkh (Arabic, early thirteenth century); Ravandi, Rāhat al-sudūr (Persian, c. 1200); the Ilkhanid-era history of Rashid al-Din (c. 1300) and the history of Juvayni (c. 1260) for the Nizari tradition

Context. None of these works is contemporary with the event. The Sunni narratives were composed from a century to a century and a half later by authors drawing on earlier lost histories of the Seljuks. The Ilkhanid-period historians wrote of the Nizaris from the perspective of the Mongol conquest of Alamut, using material from the Nizari library there, and so they preserve a tradition shaped by that moment.

Paraphrase and thematic synthesis, not a quotation: The chronicles report that Nizam al-Mulk was killed by a man disguised as a Sufi at a stage of the court's journey near Nahavand in the autumn of 1092, and that the sultan died within weeks. Most attribute the killing to an Ismaili agent. Some report tension between the sultan and his vizier, and some mention the hostility of Terken Khatun and Taj al-Mulk. The reports differ on the identity of the killer, on whether the sultan had dismissed the vizier, and on who bore responsibility.

Analysis. The agreement on the main fact and the disagreement on sponsorship illustrate how a historian can combine sources. The core event is secure because it is reported by independent traditions, including hostile ones. Attribution depends on the interests of each tradition. The Sunni sources favor an Ismaili explanation consistent with their view of the movement; the Nizari tradition's celebration of the act is consistent with a claim of responsibility, though also with later appropriation; court rumors reflect factional suspicion.

Limitations. No source is a contemporary eyewitness account in surviving form. The authors were removed from the events in time and writing for audiences with their own concerns. Names, speeches, and dramatic details cannot be accepted without corroboration.

Perspective. The perspectives are those of Sunni court historians, later Persian historians writing under Mongol rule, and the Nizari tradition as filtered through hostile or later sources. The views of ordinary soldiers and servants who witnessed the event, and of the vizier's enemies who might have explained their own role, are missing.

Section 39.34

Malik Shah's Death, the Succession Crisis, and the Fragility of Administrative Unity

Malik Shah died in Baghdad in mid-November 1092, about five weeks after his vizier, after a brief illness. He was about thirty-seven. Some sources suspect poison and implicate the vizier's enemies or his friends, but Chapter 38 has noted that there is no secure evidence for it, and the simplest assumption is a natural death, possibly following a hunting expedition. Whatever its cause, the combination of the two deaths left the empire without its two principal leaders and without an agreed successor.

The events that followed are the subject of Chapter 38, and only their administrative aspects need discussion here. Terken Khatun moved quickly to secure recognition for her son Mahmud, a small child, obtaining the support of some commanders and the caliph's agreement to have him named in the sermon. Taj al-Mulk, her administrator, took charge of the central administration. The other main claimant, Barkiyaruq, Malik Shah's older son, was supported by the faction associated with the late vizier, including his slave soldiers and his sons, and he took refuge at Rayy, where his supporters gathered. Other relatives, among them Malik Shah's brother Tutush in Syria, entered the contest. Taj al-Mulk was killed in 1093 by members of the vizier's faction, Terken Khatun died in 1094, and her son died in the same year.

For the administration, the crisis was a test and a failure. The central offices, accustomed to take their orders from the sultan and the vizier, found themselves divided between rival claimants, each issuing orders in his own name. Revenue assignments, the means by which soldiers were paid, became objects of competition, since a claimant who could confirm or reassign them could win commanders' support. The vizier's household and clients, who had been the backbone of the administration, now acted as a political party, and their fortunes depended on the success of their candidate. The network that had been a source of stability under Nizam al-Mulk became a faction in a civil war.

The fate of the vizier's network was mixed. His sons and clients continued to hold office under the successor sultans, and several of them served as viziers for the sultans Barkiyaruq, Muhammad Tapar, and Sanjar. This demonstrates the continuity of the administrative class and of the household that he had built. It also shows that the network could survive his death, but only by adapting to new rulers and new patrons. The family was repeatedly drawn into the conflicts of the succeeding decades, and some of its members were killed. The attacks on members of the family and on its associates, including the killing of Fakhr al-Mulk in 1106, show that the political violence of 1092 did not end with that year.

The crisis demonstrates why institutional consolidation had not eliminated the empire's dependence on individuals and negotiated authority. The dīvāns, the registers, the system of assignments, and the colleges were real institutions, and they continued to function in the decades after 1092. But the essential decisions of the state, such as who would be sultan, who would hold which assignments, and who would control the treasury and the army, were settled by political and military negotiation among individuals and factions. There was no constitutional rule of succession, no independent civil service with its own authority, and no court of law that could decide between claimants. When the personal relationships that held the system together failed, no institutional mechanism could replace them.

It would be a mistake, however, to read the crisis as proof that the government of Nizam al-Mulk was an illusion. The system had worked for two decades, and the fact that it was challenged and strained in 1092 does not show that it had been fictitious. The civil wars after 1092 were not the end of Seljuk government, which continued in modified form under Muhammad Tapar, Sanjar, and the regional dynasties, and used the administrative methods developed in the earlier period. The crisis shows the limits of a system built on personal authority. It does not negate its achievements.

Division VIII · §39.35 Historical Synthesis and Legacy
Section 39.35

Nizam al-Mulk's Legacy: Persianate Statecraft, Institutional Continuity, and the Limits of Government

The governing question of this chapter was how Nizam al-Mulk helped transform Seljuk military domination into an organized Persianate system of government, and why that system remained vulnerable to the personal, dynastic, and military rivalries it sought to control. The evidence examined in the preceding sections permits a direct answer, which has two parts.

The first part concerns what he and his colleagues achieved. They took the inherited institutions of Iranian and Abbasid administration, the vizierate, the dīvāns, the chancery, the fiscal registers, and the practice of assigning revenue, and adapted them to a state ruled by a Turkic dynasty with a mobile court, a family confederation, and an army of mixed composition. They developed means of supervising officials and soldiers, extended the system of assignments across a large territory, maintained correspondence between distant regions, and managed the relations between the sultan and the caliph. They supported a network of colleges that trained scholars and officials and bound them to the regime, and they associated the dynasty with a Sunni scholarly tradition that carried weight in the cities. A body of political thought, represented by the Siyasatnama and associated with the vizier, set out an ideal of just and well-supervised government that influenced later writers. These achievements were not trivial, and they help to explain why the Seljuk state could govern a large territory for decades.

The second part concerns the limits. The system depended on personal relationships: between sultan and vizier, between the central government and commanders who held assignments, between the vizier's household and its rivals. It had no fixed rule of succession and no institution able to arbitrate among competing claimants. Its administrators could supervise provincial officials and soldiers only imperfectly, since distance, the strength of local interests, and the logic of the assignment system limited the center's control. Its ideals of justice and protection coexisted with the possibility of exploitation, and its religious patronage favored some groups over others and sharpened conflicts. When the vizier and the sultan died within weeks of each other in 1092, the structure that they had balanced was thrown into the conflicts of a dynasty without a rule of succession.

The chapter has treated Nizam al-Mulk as an influential minister and not as the inventor of Persian bureaucracy, the madrasa, the iqtaʿ, or the theory of just kingship. Each had a long history before him. His distinctive contribution lay in adaptation, supervision, patronage, and the political use of these inherited tools, together with the personal authority and long tenure that allowed him to give them coherence. This conclusion avoids the older tendency to attribute a whole system to one man, and it avoids the opposite error of discounting his role altogether.

His reputation among later writers is itself a historical subject. Sunni historians and biographers remembered him as the model vizier, pious, generous, learned, and loyal, and the manner of his death confirmed an image of the righteous minister struck down by enemies of the faith. Later mirrors for princes and works on government cited him as an authority and used his name for their own purposes, and the Siyasatnama was read, copied, and adapted for centuries. Legends attached themselves to his life, among them the story of the three schoolmates, which historians do not accept. Hostile traditions also existed, which portrayed him as ambitious and as an enricher of his family. The task of the historian is to distinguish the figure of the sources from the figure of the legend and to evaluate both.

The adoption of Persianate governing practices by later Islamic dynasties is a real historical pattern, but it needs careful statement. The Seljuk successors and the atabeg dynasties of the twelfth century used viziers, dīvāns, and revenue assignments, and they employed secretarial families with ties to the earlier administration. The Khwarazmian state examined in Chapter 44 inherited institutions and personnel from the Seljuks, and later Muslim dynasties in Anatolia, Central Asia, and South Asia developed forms of government that drew on the same traditions. These continuities are demonstrated by the persistence of terminology, offices, and families of officials. They do not demonstrate that all later bureaucracy derived from one minister. The Persianate model was a shared inheritance, developed over centuries by many officials, rulers, and writers, of whom Nizam al-Mulk was one of the most prominent.

The chapter's closing debate asks whether he created durable institutions or a highly effective personal administration. The preceding sections suggest that the question is best answered by distinguishing among the elements of his work. The dīvāns, the use of revenue assignments, the chancery, and the colleges proved durable, and they continued under his successors and under other dynasties. The close coordination of the central government with commanders and provincial officials, the control of appointments, and the capacity to manage relations with the sultan and the caliph were personal and could not be transferred automatically to successors. The durable institutions and the personal administration were intertwined, and the history of the later eleventh century shows how each depended on the other.

Historical Debate: Did Nizam al-Mulk Create Durable Institutions or a Highly Effective Personal Administration?

The question. Was Nizam al-Mulk's achievement the construction of lasting institutions, or the skilled management of relationships that depended on his person?

The institutional interpretation. The offices, practices, and colleges that he developed or supported outlasted him. His sons and clients continued to serve, the Nizamiyya colleges continued to operate, the assignment system spread, and the Siyasatnama became a standard of political thought. On this view, he helped to create a pattern of government that later states adopted.
The personal-administration interpretation. The effectiveness of the government under Malik Shah rested on the vizier's control of appointments, his household, his relations with the sultan, and his skill in managing commanders. When he died and the sultan died, the system fell into factional conflict, which suggests that its coherence was personal. On this view, the institutions were forms through which a particular network operated.
An integrated interpretation. Many historians argue that the institutions and the personal network were inseparable. Institutions needed capable and trusted people to operate, and the network gained legitimacy and continuity from institutions. The outcome in 1092 shows the limits of the institutions without showing that they were unreal.

Evidence. The evidence includes the continued operation of offices and colleges after 1092, the service of the vizier's sons under later sultans, the factional conflicts of 1092–1094, the later history of the iqtaʿ and the vizierate, and the reception of the Siyasatnama.

Points of disagreement. The disagreement concerns how to weigh continuity of forms against the discontinuity of political cohesion, and how to judge durability when almost every state of the period fragmented. It also involves differing definitions of an institution.

Why it matters. The answer shapes how historians describe the achievement of a bureaucratic tradition: as a source of stability that could outlast individuals, or as a tool dependent on the political conditions in which it was used. It also affects comparisons with other states and periods.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Nizam al-Mulk helped turn Seljuk military supremacy into an organized Persianate system of government, and why that system remained vulnerable to the personal, dynastic, and military rivalries it was meant to control. Where Chapter 38 explained how the Seljuks conquered and ordered territory, this chapter asked what made that territory governable: records, revenues, appointments, soldiers who were paid, officials who were watched, scholars who were supported, and a theory of just rule.

The chapter began with the bureaucratic tradition that Nizam al-Mulk inherited. Sasanian scribal practice, Abbasid secretarial government, and the administrations of the Samanids, Buyids, and Ghaznavids had produced a professional culture of administration and a vocabulary of offices that the Seljuks used. The vizier, the katib, the dīvān, and the barid were older than his career. The Seljuk contribution lay in adapting these institutions to a Turkic dynasty with a mobile court, shared family authority, and an army increasingly supported through assignments of revenue.

Nizam al-Mulk's own early life is only partly documented. He was born about 1018 in the district of Tus, received a religious and secretarial education, served in the Ghaznavid and then the Seljuk administrations of Khurasan, and by about 1060 was associated with Alp Arslan. The biographical tradition idealizes his youth, and legends such as the pact with Omar Khayyam and Hasan-i Sabbah are not reliable evidence. The partnership between a Persian secretary and Turkic princes was one of mutual dependence, and it was not a simple division between sword and pen.

His tenure as vizier from about 1063 to 1092 began in factional violence, with the dismissal and execution of al-Kunduri in 1064, and it depended throughout on the favor of successive sultans, on alliances with commanders, and on a growing household of relatives and clients. He helped secure the succession of Malik Shah in 1072–1073, managed relations with the Abbasid caliphate, and acquired exceptional authority. The debate over whether the vizier was a servant of the sultan or an independent power showed that his position changed over time and cannot be reduced to either label.

The machinery of government comprised the central dīvāns, the chancery, the fiscal administration, provincial government, the iqtaʿ system, military administration, and the means of communication, inspection, and justice. The sources describe offices more tidily than practice probably warranted, and the center's control of distant officials, local elites, and military commanders was limited. The iqtaʿ was an assignment of revenue and not ownership of land. It solved a financial problem for a state with large armies and little ready cash, and it also encouraged exploitation and the growth of hereditary local power.

The Siyasatnama belongs to a long tradition of mirrors for princes that drew on Sasanian memory, Islamic ideas of justice, and Abbasid advice literature. It presents an ideal of the just ruler who hears complaints, appoints capable officials, supervises them through independent informants, pays and inspects his soldiers, and protects cultivators. Its authorship and transmission are debated: the text reports a commission of about 1086, an initial version of thirty-nine chapters, and eleven later chapters, and the surviving manuscripts differ. It is valuable as evidence of political thought and of the problems an experienced administrator saw, but it cannot be treated as a description of what the government actually did.

The work also has limits as a vision of society. It assumes a hierarchical order, disapproves of women's involvement in affairs of state while documenting their importance, and attacks Ismailis and other groups in polemical language. The Nizamiyya colleges, founded and patronized by the vizier from the 1060s, extended an existing tradition of endowed legal teaching and bound scholars to the regime through stipends and appointments. They were a network of patronage and not a centralized university, and the vizier's religious policy combined support for Shafiʿi and Ashʿari scholars with efforts to contain sectarian conflict.

Taxation, military demands, official misconduct, and unequal access to justice shaped the experience of cultivators and townspeople, whose perspectives survive mainly through the sources of their rulers. The vizier's household and family network gave the government continuity and exposed it to charges of nepotism and to the hostility of rivals, including the household of Terken Khatun and her administrator Taj al-Mulk. The Ismaili challenge centered on Alamut after 1090 added a new danger and a vocabulary of sedition that must be read critically.

In October 1092 Nizam al-Mulk was assassinated near Nahavand, and Malik Shah died about five weeks later. The fact of the killing is secure, but its sponsorship is not: the attribution to an Ismaili agent is probable, while court rivalry and royal complicity are alternative explanations that the evidence neither confirms nor excludes. The succession crisis showed that institutional consolidation had not removed the empire's dependence on individuals and negotiated authority, although the vizier's sons and clients and many of his institutions continued under later sultans.

The chapter's central argument is that Persianate statecraft was neither the personal genius of one vizier nor an impersonal bureaucracy operating independently of dynastic power. It was a historically developed system whose effectiveness depended on political relationships, administrative expertise, and institutions that could outlast their founders, though not always withstand the conflicts within the ruling order. Nizam al-Mulk's importance lies in adaptation, supervision, patronage, and political use of inherited institutions, and the later adoption of Persianate practices by other dynasties reflects a shared inheritance and not the work of one minister.

Key Terms

Vizier
The chief civil minister, appointed by the ruler to direct administration and finance; his authority was delegated and revocable.
Katib
A secretary trained in writing, accounting, and official correspondence who staffed the dīvāns and the chancery.
Dīvān
An administrative office and the register it kept; the plural names the departments of government.
Chancery
The office that drafted and issued official documents such as decrees, appointments, and diplomatic letters.
Tughraʾi
The head of the chancery, responsible for the sultan's signature or monogram (tughra) on documents.
Mustawfi
The senior financial officer who audited accounts and kept fiscal registers.
Mushrif
An inspector who examined officials, accounts, and local conditions on behalf of the center.
ʿArid
The officer in charge of the muster and the army's rolls.
Barid
The postal and information service that carried correspondence and reports between the court and the provinces.
Kharaj
The principal land tax, assessed as a fixed sum or a share of the harvest.
Fiscal register
A written record of districts, landholders, and taxes due, which required periodic revision and was open to manipulation.
Iqtaʿ
An assignment of the right to collect revenue from a district in return for service, usually military; not ownership of land.
Muqtaʿ
The holder of an iqtaʿ.
Ghulam
A military slave, trained in the household of a ruler or commander and bound to him by personal loyalty.
Muster
The inspection of troops to verify their number, quality, and equipment against the rolls on which pay or assignments were based.
Mazalim
The hearing of complaints by a ruler or senior official, especially against the powerful and against officials.
Mirror for princes
A work of advice to a ruler about conduct and government, typically combining principles with illustrative stories.
Siyasatnama
The Persian "Book of Government" associated with Nizam al-Mulk, whose authorship and textual history are debated.
Circle of justice
The idea that justice, prosperity, revenue, and military strength sustain one another in a cycle that sustains rule.
Exemplum
A story from the past used to illustrate a principle; evidence of values more than of events.
Madrasa
An institution for higher religious learning, especially law, usually supported by an endowment; it existed before the Nizamiyya.
Nizamiyya
The name given to colleges founded or patronized by Nizam al-Mulk, the most famous being in Baghdad (inaugurated 1067).
Waqf
A legal endowment dedicating property to a pious purpose, with its income reserved for that purpose.
Mutawalli
The administrator of a waqf, who managed the property and distributed its income.
Shafiʿi
One of the major Sunni schools of law, strong among the scholars of Khurasan and Iraq and favored by the vizier's patronage.
Ashʿari
A school of Sunni theology that defended traditional doctrines through rational argument; prominent in Nishapur.
Investiture
The formal conferral of office, titles, and insignia, as when a caliph recognized a sultan.
Dihqan
A member of the provincial landholding gentry of Iranian tradition, linked to local administration.
Appanage
A territorial share assigned to a member of a ruling family to govern, with an obligation of service to the senior ruler.
Batini
A polemical term used by hostile Sunni writers for Ismailis, implying that they pursued a hidden meaning in religious texts.
Persianate
Describing administrative, literary, and cultural traditions rooted in New Persian that spread beyond Iran and were adopted by rulers of varied origins.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows what lies behind the word "state." A dynasty's armies may win a region, but the region becomes a realm only when someone records what is owed, pays those who must be paid, writes the orders that others will obey, and persuades influential groups that the arrangement is legitimate. Nizam al-Mulk's career lets students see that work in unusual detail, and it also shows how much of it rested on inherited skills and on relationships that no document could guarantee.

The chapter reveals larger processes in the history of the region. It shows how the administrative culture of the Sasanian, Abbasid, and regional Iranian worlds survived a change of ruling dynasty and acquired new patrons. It shows how religious patronage became an instrument of government and how institutions of learning gained scale and political connection. It shows how a system that delegated revenue to soldiers gained flexibility and lost control. And it shows how a text of political advice could become both a practical guide and a monument of political thought.

Later developments built on these features. The factional conflicts after 1092, the growth of atabeg dynasties and hereditary assignments, the continued operation of the colleges, and the adoption of Persianate administrative forms by Khwarazmian, Anatolian, Central Asian, and South Asian states all reflect the legacy of the period. The memory of the vizier as the model minister and the reading of the Siyasatnama as a standard of government also outlasted the Seljuks. Chapter 40 takes up the religious and educational institutions of the Sunni revival, Chapter 41 the Ismaili challenge, and Chapter 43 the scholarship that the colleges supported.

Finally, the chapter invites students to think about how historians know what they know. The record of Nizam al-Mulk comes from admirers, critics, and later legend, and the text most closely associated with him raises unresolved questions of authorship and relation to practice. The person at the center of the story left no private papers that would settle them. Learning to ask who wrote a source, when, for whom, and with what purpose, and to say plainly where the evidence ends, is the central skill of the chapter and of the discipline.
