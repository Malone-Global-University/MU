Sasanian Iran and Rome-Byzantium: Four Centuries of Imperial Rivalry

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

For nearly four hundred years, two of the ancient world's largest states, Rome and Sasanian Iran, faced one another across a frontier running through northern Mesopotamia, Armenia, and the Caucasus. They fought repeatedly. They also negotiated repeatedly. Neither empire ever permanently destroyed the other, and understanding why requires looking past any single battle toward the durable system of war, diplomacy, and shared frontier institutions the two empires built together.

In 260 CE, somewhere near the Mesopotamian city of Edessa, Roman soldiers watched their emperor, Valerian, taken prisoner by the forces of the Sasanian king Shapur I. It was among the most humiliating events in Roman imperial history, later commemorated by Shapur himself in a triumphant rock relief carved into the cliff face at Naqsh-e Rustam, within a monumental landscape long dominated by the tombs of the Achaemenid kings. A visitor examining that relief today might reasonably conclude that Rome's eastern power had been broken. Yet within little more than a decade, Roman forces, with the Palmyrene ruler Odaenathus playing an important early role and a sequence of vigorous Roman emperors subsequently restoring central authority, had reversed most of Shapur's gains. Sasanian Mesopotamia was not annexed to Rome, but neither was the Roman East absorbed into the Sasanian Empire. The frontier, disturbed but not destroyed, endured.

This pattern, dramatic victory followed by negotiated or contested reversal, recurs so often across the fourth, fifth, and early sixth centuries that it becomes one of the central puzzles of Roman-Sasanian history. Shapur I captured a Roman emperor and still could not hold Antioch. The emperor Julian marched an army to the walls of Ctesiphon in 363 and still died on the retreat, forcing his successor to surrender territory Rome had held for six decades. Sasanian kings besieged fortress cities such as Nisibis and Amida for months at tremendous cost, sometimes taking them, sometimes failing, without any single siege deciding the war between the two empires as a whole. If overwhelming victories so rarely produced lasting conquest, what, then, did nearly four centuries of confrontation actually accomplish?

This chapter argues that the answer lies not in any individual campaign but in the durable inter-imperial system the two states constructed, largely without intending to, through repeated contact. Rome and Sasanian Iran developed institutions of diplomacy alongside their institutions of war: formal treaties negotiated by senior envoys, the exchange of hostages and gifts, recognized procedures for ransoming prisoners, and a shared, if contested, vocabulary in which each ruler's counterpart was addressed as a legitimate emperor rather than dismissed as a mere barbarian chieftain. Both states invested enormous resources in fortified cities along the Euphrates, Khabur, and Tigris river systems, transforming northern Mesopotamia into one of the most heavily garrisoned and elaborately fortified frontier zones in the ancient world. And both states repeatedly found themselves drawn into the internal politics of Armenia and the Caucasus, regions whose own rulers, nobles, and religious institutions were never simply passive prizes to be divided between larger powers but active participants pursuing their own interests within, and sometimes against, the ambitions of their much larger neighbors.

Chapter 19 explained how the Roman-Iranian frontier took shape under the Parthian Arsacid dynasty, establishing many of the geographic flashpoints, diplomatic conventions, and strategic patterns this chapter now examines in their Sasanian form. When Ardashir I overthrew Artabanus IV, the principal Arsacid king of kings, in 224 CE and founded a new Iranian dynasty, he did not invent a new geopolitical problem. He inherited an existing rivalry, together with its fortresses, its client kingdoms, and its accumulated diplomatic precedent, and then transformed that inheritance according to the ambitions and ideology of his new royal house. This chapter follows that transformed rivalry from Ardashir's early campaigns through the extraordinary reign of Shapur I, the treaty settlements of Narseh and Galerius, the prolonged fourth-century struggles under Shapur II, a fifth century more often defined by diplomacy than by war, and finally the renewed frontier crisis of Kavadh I and the emperor Anastasius around the year 500, which sets the stage for Khosrow I and the war's final, catastrophic phase examined in Chapter 27.

Students should resist two tempting but historically inaccurate simplifications while reading this chapter. The first treats Rome and Sasanian Iran as civilizations destined by culture, religion, or geography to fight one another endlessly, a kind of ancient clash of civilizations running in an unbroken line from Ardashir to the Arab conquests. The evidence does not support this. Long stretches of the fifth century passed with little or no warfare between the two empires, and even the fourth century's harshest conflicts were repeatedly interrupted by negotiated settlements that both sides observed, sometimes for decades. The second simplification treats the frontier itself as a fixed, modern-style border separating two sealed territorial blocks. In reality, the frontier zone functioned as a contested, permeable, and intensely governed space, home to fortress garrisons, merchant communities, deported populations, competing religious institutions, and local elites, particularly in Armenia, whose loyalties could not simply be assigned to one empire or the other.

Understanding this four-century rivalry matters for reasons extending well beyond military history. It shaped how the Sasanian state organized its western provinces and its army. It shaped the political and religious history of Armenia and the wider Caucasus, a subject already introduced through Chapter 24's discussion of Armenian Christianity and now examined here in its geopolitical dimension. It produced institutions of diplomacy, prisoner exchange, and treaty-making that later societies inherited and adapted. And it left both empires, by the early sixth century, still standing, still powerful, and still locked in an unresolved contest whose final and most destructive phase, examined in Chapter 27, would eventually exhaust both sides on the eve of the Arab-Muslim conquests examined in Chapter 28.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the Sasanian-Roman rivalry both inherited and transformed the Arsacid-Roman frontier system examined in Chapter 19.
  2. Trace the major military and diplomatic episodes of the Roman-Sasanian rivalry from Ardashir I through Kavadh I.
  3. Analyze why dramatic military victories, including the capture of a Roman emperor, repeatedly failed to produce permanent territorial conquest.
  4. Describe the diplomatic institutions, including treaties, envoys, hostages, and prisoner exchange, that regulated the rivalry alongside warfare.
  5. Evaluate the political role of Armenia and the Caucasus as a contested zone shaped by, but not reducible to, Roman and Sasanian ambitions.
  6. Assess the strategic significance of fortified Mesopotamian cities such as Nisibis, Amida, and Dara for both empires.
  7. Distinguish periods of intensive warfare from periods of negotiated or de facto coexistence across the fourth and fifth centuries.
  8. Interpret Sasanian royal inscriptions and reliefs as politically motivated sources requiring critical analysis rather than literal military records.
  9. Compare the perspectives of Roman, Sasanian, and Armenian actors on the same shared frontier system.
  10. Explain why historians increasingly describe Rome and Sasanian Iran as an inter-imperial system rather than as two isolated civilizations facing one another.
  11. Identify the chronological and thematic boundary between this chapter's coverage and Chapter 27's treatment of Khosrow I and the final Roman-Persian wars.
  12. Apply careful historical judgment when assessing sweeping claims of victory, defeat, or civilizational superiority in the surviving sources.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did Rome and Sasanian Iran fight repeatedly for nearly four centuries without either empire permanently destroying or absorbing the other?
  2. How much of the Sasanian-Roman rivalry represented genuine change from the earlier Arsacid-Roman frontier, and how much represented continuity?
  3. What made northern Mesopotamia and Armenia such persistent flashpoints across so many centuries?
  4. Why should historians be cautious about treating Roman literary sources, or Sasanian royal inscriptions, as neutral accounts of the rivalry?
  5. In what ways did Armenian rulers, nobles, and religious institutions act as independent historical agents rather than passive territory?
  6. Why did a single dramatic event, such as the capture of an emperor or the sack of a great city, so rarely translate into permanent conquest?
  7. What made diplomacy, rather than warfare alone, a normal and recurring feature of the Roman-Sasanian relationship?
  8. How did each empire's rulers understand the legitimacy of their imperial counterpart, and how did that understanding change over time?
  9. Why does this chapter stop its detailed narrative around 500 CE rather than continuing through the reign of Khosrow I?

Historical Context

Chapter 19 examined how Rome and the Parthian Arsacid dynasty developed a lasting rivalry over Armenia and northern Mesopotamia, beginning with the disastrous Roman defeat at Carrhae in 53 BCE and continuing through several centuries of intermittent war and negotiated settlement. That chapter established several patterns this chapter now carries forward: the fortified Euphrates and Khabur river frontier; a diplomatic culture built around dynastic recognition, client kingship, and the exchange of envoys and hostages; and the historically important principle that Rome and Iran, whatever their propaganda claimed, generally treated one another as legitimate imperial powers rather than as mere barbarians to be conquered outright.

When Ardashir I overthrew Artabanus IV, the principal Arsacid king of kings, in 224 CE, founding the dynasty examined in Chapter 22, he inherited this entire frontier system rather than confronting an entirely new geopolitical situation. Rome, for its part, brought its own inheritance: fortified eastern provinces, an experienced frontier military establishment, and generations of institutional memory regarding Parthian warfare. What changed under the Sasanians was not the basic geography of the rivalry but its political intensity and ideological framing. Sasanian royal ideology, examined in Chapter 23, presented the new dynasty as heir to a more visibly articulated dynastic and territorial ideology than the Arsacids had emphasized, and early Sasanian kings, beginning with Ardashir and continuing dramatically under his son Shapur I, pursued the western war with unusual vigor.

This chapter's chronological scope extends from Ardashir's accession in 224 CE to the early sixth-century frontier crisis associated with the Sasanian king Kavadh I and the Roman emperor Anastasius, a span of roughly two hundred and eighty years within the chapter's titular "four centuries," which extends conceptually forward to the final catastrophic wars examined in Chapter 27. Readers should understand this chapter as covering the first three quarters of that four-century rivalry in detail, while reserving the final and most destructive phase, associated above all with Khosrow I and his successors, for separate treatment.

Section 25.1

From Arsacid-Roman Rivalry to a New Sasanian-Roman Contest

When Ardashir I defeated and killed Artabanus IV, the principal Arsacid king of kings, in 224 CE, he became ruler of an empire whose western frontier had already been shaped by nearly three centuries of contact, negotiation, and warfare with Rome. Chapter 19 traced that earlier history in detail: the catastrophic Roman defeat at Carrhae in 53 BCE, subsequent Roman campaigns that at times reached Ctesiphon itself, periodic contests over the allegiance of Armenia, and a diplomatic culture in which Roman and Parthian rulers, despite mutual suspicion and periodic war, generally recognized one another's legitimacy as sovereign emperors. Ardashir did not confront a blank geopolitical slate. He inherited fortresses, garrison towns, established invasion routes along the Euphrates and Tigris, and a body of diplomatic precedent regarding how disputes over Armenia and Mesopotamia might be negotiated as well as fought.

What changed under the Sasanians was less the underlying geography of the rivalry than its political temperature and ideological framing. Sasanian royal ideology, examined in detail in Chapter 23, presented Ardashir and his successors as restorers of a more visibly articulated dynastic and territorial ideology than the Arsacids had typically emphasized, even though Sasanian rule continued to depend heavily on aristocratic and regional power in practice. Early Sasanian inscriptions and later Perso-Arabic historical tradition present Ardashir as an unusually energetic and expansionist ruler, and whatever the precise accuracy of these later traditions, the pattern of renewed campaigning against Rome in the 230s CE is consistent with a new dynasty seeking to demonstrate its strength through the same western contest its Arsacid predecessors had fought for centuries. Roman sources for these very early Sasanian campaigns are fragmentary and sometimes contradictory, and modern historians remain cautious about reconstructing precise campaign details for the 230s.

Rome, for its part, brought its own substantial inheritance to the renewed contest: an established eastern military establishment, fortified provincial capitals, decades of institutional experience fighting Arsacid Parthia, and an official ideology that likewise proclaimed universal or near-universal imperial legitimacy. Neither side, in other words, approached the renewed rivalry from a position of ideological neutrality. Both Rome and the new Sasanian dynasty claimed forms of universal or exceptional sovereignty that were, in practice, difficult to reconcile with the existence of an equally powerful rival empire on the opposite bank of the Euphrates.

This tension between rhetorical claims of universal supremacy and the practical reality of a durable, roughly balanced rival empire is one of the central interpretive threads running through this entire chapter. Sasanian inscriptions repeatedly proclaim overwhelming, comprehensive victories over Rome. Roman sources repeatedly claim eventual triumph and the restoration of proper order along the frontier. Both traditions, read uncritically, would suggest that the rivalry should have ended in outright conquest on one side or the other. It did not. Understanding why requires examining not only the campaigns themselves but the structural and institutional reasons, treated at length in later sections of this chapter, that repeatedly prevented dramatic military success from translating into lasting territorial control.

Historical Vocabulary
Inter-Imperial System

Definition: A framework historians use to describe the long-term relationship between Rome and Sasanian Iran as a durable, mutually shaping system of interaction, encompassing warfare, diplomacy, fortification, and trade, rather than as a simple story of two isolated civilizations occasionally colliding.

Historical context: Across the nearly three centuries examined in this chapter, Rome and Sasanian Iran developed recurring institutions, including treaties, hostage exchange, envoys, and a shared frontier fortification zone, that regulated their relationship even amid intense warfare. Neither side ever achieved the total conquest its most triumphant sources sometimes proclaimed.

Why it matters: Thinking in terms of an inter-imperial system, rather than a simple sequence of wars, helps explain why periods of diplomacy and negotiated coexistence were just as historically significant as periods of open conflict, and why institutions built through repeated contact, such as the Nisibis trade arrangement or the Mesopotamian fortress network, outlasted any single ruler or campaign.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not conclude that describing the relationship as a "system" implies formal equality between the two empires at every moment, or that the system was ever peaceful by default; it was a contested, frequently violent relationship that nonetheless produced durable shared institutions.

Section 25.2

Ardashir I, Shapur I, and the Transformation of the Western War

The reign of Shapur I, Ardashir's son and successor, transformed the Sasanian-Roman rivalry into one of the most dramatic sequences of warfare in the ancient Mediterranean and Near Eastern world. Shapur's western campaigns are known chiefly through two very different kinds of evidence: fragmentary and sometimes inconsistent Roman and Greek literary sources, and Shapur's own extraordinary trilingual inscription at the Kaʿba-ye Zardosht near Naqsh-e Rustam, examined in detail in this chapter's Primary Source Window. Reading these sources against one another, rather than relying on either alone, is essential to understanding what actually happened.

The first major war of Shapur's reign pitted Sasanian forces against the young Roman emperor Gordian III in the early 240s CE. Roman sources describe an initial Roman advance into Mesopotamia, a subsequent reversal, and Gordian's death under circumstances that remain disputed, followed by a settlement negotiated by his successor, Philip the Arab, that reportedly included a substantial payment to Shapur. Shapur's own inscription frames this episode as an unambiguous Sasanian triumph, describing Gordian's death in battle and Philip's submission to unfavorable terms. Roman tradition, unsurprisingly, offers a considerably more face-saving account. The most defensible historical conclusion is that Shapur secured a real and significant advantage, including a negotiated payment, without the encounter constituting the total destruction of Roman power that his inscription proclaims.

A second and even more dramatic phase followed roughly a decade later, when Shapur's forces campaigned deep into Roman Syria, on at least one and probably two separate occasions, sacking the great city of Antioch, one of the largest and wealthiest cities in the Roman East. Precise dating for these Syrian campaigns remains debated among specialists, partly because Shapur's inscription compresses and reorders events for rhetorical effect rather than presenting a strictly chronological military record. What is clear is that Sasanian forces demonstrated a capacity to strike deep into core Roman territory, well beyond the immediate Mesopotamian frontier zone, inflicting serious material and symbolic damage on Roman prestige in the eastern provinces.

The culmination of Shapur's western wars came in 260 CE, when his forces defeated a Roman army near Edessa and captured the reigning emperor Valerian, along with a substantial portion of his army, an event unparalleled in the entire history of Roman-Iranian conflict. No earlier or later Roman-Sasanian war produced a comparable outcome; no Roman emperor before or after Valerian was taken captive by a Sasanian king. Shapur's inscription describes the encounter as a complete rout, listing an extensive roster of Valerian's officials and companions taken prisoner alongside the emperor himself, а detail that, unusually, finds some corroboration in later, admittedly hostile, Roman literary tradition, which likewise depicts Valerian's captivity, and eventual death in Sasanian custody, as a national humiliation.

Students should resist two opposite errors when interpreting these events. The first error would treat Shapur's inscription as an objective battlefield record rather than a carefully constructed piece of royal political communication, commissioned specifically to proclaim the king's cosmic and military legitimacy to multiple audiences across the empire. The second error would dismiss the inscription's basic outline as mere propaganda unconnected to real events. The most responsible historical approach treats the inscription as strong, though rhetorically shaped, evidence for the general scale and sequence of Shapur's victories, while remaining cautious about its precise numerical claims, its compressed chronology, and its silence regarding any Sasanian setbacks during the same years.

Section 25.3

Palmyra, Roman Recovery, and the Limits of Sasanian Victory

Shapur I's capture of Valerian represented the single most spectacular Sasanian success of the entire four-century rivalry, and yet it did not produce anything resembling permanent Sasanian control over Roman Syria or Mesopotamia. Understanding why requires attention to a development often underemphasized in narratives focused primarily on Shapur's inscription: the emergence of Palmyra, a wealthy caravan city in the Syrian desert, as an important regional military actor in the Roman recovery.

In the years following Valerian's capture, Palmyra's ruler, Odaenathus, organized and led military campaigns against Sasanian forces retreating from, or still occupying positions within, Roman Syrian and Mesopotamian territory. Odaenathus's Palmyrene forces played an important role in checking Sasanian forces and restoring Roman-aligned power in the East at a moment when Rome's central government was itself weakened by the political and military crises of the mid-third century sometimes described by modern historians as the Crisis of the Third Century, and Odaenathus was granted extensive authority over the Roman East as a result, authority his widow Zenobia would later attempt to expand dramatically, with consequences examined more fully in later chapters addressing Rome's third-century political crisis. The precise scale and success of Odaenathus's Persian campaigns, however, are difficult to reconstruct from the surviving evidence and may have been magnified in later, favorable Roman tradition. The subsequent restoration of stronger central Roman authority, including the emperor Aurelian's defeat of the Palmyrene regime itself rather than any comparably large direct campaign against Sasanian Iran, further stabilized Roman control in the eastern provinces during this period.

This sequence illustrates a pattern that recurs throughout the entire rivalry examined in this chapter: dramatic Sasanian battlefield success did not automatically translate into administrative or military control of conquered territory, particularly territory as distant from the Iranian plateau as Syria. Maintaining garrisons, administering conquered cities, and defending extended supply lines across hostile or uncertain territory required resources and sustained attention that Shapur's court, however militarily formidable, could not indefinitely commit to a theater so far removed from the Sasanian heartland. Rome's recovery, moreover, did not require the full mobilization of its central government, since Palmyrene forces could contribute to the immediate Roman-aligned response while the central government remained weakened.

By the time of Shapur I's death, probably in the early 270s CE, the frontier between Sasanian Iran and Rome had been substantially restored to something resembling its earlier configuration, notwithstanding the extraordinary events of the intervening decade. Neither Shapur's victories nor Rome's subsequent recovery, achieved partly through Palmyrene intervention and partly through the later restoration of stronger central Roman authority under emperors such as Aurelian, produced a durable transformation of the underlying territorial balance. This outcome previews a thesis developed at greater length in Section 25.9: across the entire span of the Roman-Sasanian rivalry, dramatic campaigns repeatedly failed to produce equally dramatic and lasting geopolitical settlements.

Section 25.4

Narseh, Galerius, and the Peace of Nisibis, 298 CE

Several decades after Shapur I's death, following a period of internal Sasanian succession disputes that need not be narrated in detail here, the Sasanian king Narseh renewed large-scale warfare against Rome in the mid-290s CE. This conflict deserves close attention not primarily for its battles, though these were significant, but for the treaty settlement it eventually produced, one of the most consequential negotiated agreements in the entire history of the Roman-Sasanian rivalry.

Initial fighting favored Narseh, whose forces defeated a Roman army commanded by the Caesar Galerius, junior colleague of the senior emperor Diocletian, in Mesopotamia around 296 CE. Diocletian's government, however, responded by reinforcing Galerius substantially, and in a subsequent campaign, probably in 298 CE, Galerius decisively defeated Narseh, reportedly capturing members of the Sasanian royal household in the process. This reversal set the stage for negotiations conducted at Nisibis, producing the settlement historians generally call the Peace of Nisibis.

The Peace of Nisibis reorganized the frontier substantially in Rome's favor. No authentic text of the treaty survives; the fullest surviving account comes from the later Roman official Peter the Patrician, whose summary indicates that Rome gained additional territory east of the Tigris, secured a more favorable arrangement regarding Armenia, and that Nisibis was designated the sole officially sanctioned location for cross-frontier trade between the two empires, a provision with significant economic as well as strategic implications. The treaty is generally understood to have held, with the frontier remaining comparatively stable, for approximately four decades, until renewed warfare broke out under Shapur II in the late 330s.

The Peace of Nisibis deserves emphasis not simply as a record of Roman military success but as evidence of how the two empires had, by the late third century, developed genuine institutional mechanisms for converting military outcomes into workable, if unequal, political arrangements. A negotiated treaty, formally concluded, publicly announced, and generally observed for a full generation, represents a very different kind of historical evidence than either side's inflated claims of total victory examined in the preceding sections. It demonstrates that both Roman and Sasanian rulers, whatever their public rhetoric of universal sovereignty, were capable of accepting formal, negotiated limits on their own territorial ambitions when circumstances required it.

Section 25.5

Armenia, the Caucasus, and the Politics of Buffer Kingdoms

No single region recurs more persistently across this chapter's four centuries of rivalry than Armenia, and no region more clearly demonstrates the danger of treating contested frontier territory as merely a passive prize divided between larger neighbors. Armenia possessed its own royal dynasty, descended from a branch of the same Arsacid family that had earlier ruled Parthia, its own landed aristocracy organized into powerful noble houses, and, from the early fourth century onward, its own increasingly influential Christian religious establishment, a development already introduced in Chapter 24's discussion of Armenia's early adoption of Christianity as a state religion.

Roman and Sasanian rulers both recognized Armenia's strategic importance as a mountainous buffer commanding routes into eastern Anatolia, northern Mesopotamia, and the wider Caucasus, and both repeatedly sought to install or support Armenian rulers favorable to their own interests. This competition took multiple forms across the period examined in this chapter: military intervention, the sponsorship of rival claimants to the Armenian throne, marriage alliances, and, especially from the fourth century onward, the manipulation of religious allegiance, as Sasanian rulers, themselves patrons of Zoroastrian religious institutions examined in Chapter 24, viewed Armenia's Christian establishment with some suspicion as a potential vector of Roman influence.

It would be a serious historical error, however, to treat Armenian nobles, clergy, and rulers themselves as mere pawns lacking independent agency. Armenian aristocratic houses frequently pursued their own family and regional interests, at times allying with Rome, at times with Iran, and at times resisting both powers simultaneously in defense of local autonomy. Armenian Christian institutions, once established, developed their own internal dynamics, theological traditions, and political interests that did not simply mirror Roman ecclesiastical policy, notwithstanding shared Christian identity. Armenian elites, in other words, were skilled participants in a dangerous geopolitical environment, not passive territory awaiting division.

This dynamic came to a formal, if unsatisfying, resolution in 387 CE, when Rome and Sasanian Iran agreed to partition Armenia into distinct spheres of influence, with the larger eastern portion falling under Sasanian influence and a smaller western portion remaining under Roman oversight. This partition did not end Armenian political life, nor did it fully resolve the tension between Armenian aristocratic autonomy and the ambitions of the two neighboring empires. It did, however, formalize a pattern of divided influence that would persist, with periodic adjustments, through much of the remaining period examined in this chapter, and that recurs again in the geopolitics of the sixth century addressed in Chapter 27.

The wider Caucasus, including the kingdom of Iberia in what is now eastern Georgia, exhibited related dynamics on a smaller scale, with local rulers similarly navigating competing Roman and Sasanian pressure while pursuing their own regional interests. Students should understand Armenia and the Caucasus, across this entire chapter, not as a fixed strategic prize but as a zone of active, contested, multi-sided politics in which local actors, not only the two great empires, repeatedly shaped historical outcomes.

Historical Vocabulary
Buffer State / Client Kingdom

Definition: A smaller political entity, such as Armenia, situated between two larger rival powers and subject to significant external influence or interference from both, while typically retaining its own ruling dynasty, aristocracy, and institutions capable of independent political action.

Historical context: Armenia's Arsacid dynasty, noble houses, and, from the early fourth century, its Christian religious establishment repeatedly navigated pressure from both Rome and Sasanian Iran across the period this chapter examines, at times allying with one power, at times with the other, and at times resisting both in defense of local autonomy, culminating in the kingdom's formal partition in 387 CE.

Why it matters: Recognizing Armenia as a buffer state with genuine internal agency, rather than as passive territory automatically controlled by whichever larger empire held greater regional influence at a given moment, is essential to understanding both this chapter's Armenian material and the wider Caucasian politics examined in later chapters.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that a buffer state's rulers or population were uniformly aligned with one outside power, or that Christian communities within Armenia were automatically pro-Roman; local political and religious allegiances were considerably more complex than any simple two-sided alignment would suggest.

Section 25.6

Shapur II, Constantius II, Julian, and the Crisis of the Fourth Century

The long reign of Shapur II, spanning most of the fourth century, produced the rivalry's most sustained and, in several respects, most militarily instructive phase of warfare. Renewed conflict broke out in the late 330s CE, ending the roughly forty-year peace established at Nisibis in 298, and continued intermittently for decades, focused overwhelmingly on control of the fortified Mesopotamian frontier cities whose strategic significance this chapter has already introduced.

Nisibis itself, ironically the city whose name had marked Rome's earlier treaty triumph, endured repeated Sasanian sieges during this period, reportedly on three separate occasions, without ultimately falling to Shapur II's forces during his lifetime. These prolonged sieges illustrate a broader point about the nature of Roman-Sasanian warfare in this era: victory frequently depended less on decisive field battles than on the grinding, resource-intensive business of siege warfare against heavily fortified cities, whose garrisons, supplies, and civilian populations could sustain extended resistance against even a determined and well-resourced besieging army.

The city of Amida provides the clearest illustration of both the strategic stakes and the human cost of this fortress warfare. In 359 CE, Shapur II's forces besieged Amida for seventy-three days, according to the detailed eyewitness account of the Roman soldier and historian Ammianus Marcellinus, who served in the city's garrison and later produced one of the most valuable surviving narrative sources for this period of the rivalry. Ammianus's account describes not only the military dimensions of the siege but its devastating impact on the city's civilian population, providing modern historians with an unusually vivid, if inevitably one-sided, window into the human experience of fortress warfare on this frontier. Amida eventually fell to Shapur's forces, at very heavy cost to the besieging army, illustrating again that even successful Sasanian sieges came at a price that limited how rapidly such victories could be converted into further conquest.

The reign of the emperor Constantius II, and more dramatically that of his eventual successor Julian, brought the fourth-century conflict to its most consequential turning point. In 363 CE, Julian led a major Roman invasion deep into Sasanian Mesopotamia, advancing toward Ctesiphon itself, a campaign that briefly threatened the Sasanian capital in a manner reminiscent of earlier, celebrated Roman expeditions under the Arsacid-era emperors examined in Chapter 19. Julian's campaign, however, ended in disaster: he won an engagement outside Ctesiphon and reached its defenses but failed to capture the fortified capital, and facing supply difficulties during a difficult retreat, Julian was killed, probably by a spear thrown during a skirmish, though the precise circumstances remain debated in the ancient sources.

Julian's death left his successor, Jovian, in an extraordinarily weak negotiating position, isolated deep within hostile territory with a demoralized and endangered army. The resulting treaty of 363 CE required Jovian to cede substantial Roman territory, including the fortress city of Nisibis itself, to Shapur II, reversing much of the settlement Rome had secured at Nisibis sixty-five years earlier. This episode offers one of the clearest illustrations in the entire chapter of how quickly a dramatic offensive campaign, launched from a position of apparent strength, could collapse into a humiliating and strategically significant reversal, reinforcing this chapter's broader argument that neither warfare nor diplomacy alone determined the rivalry's long-term trajectory.

Section 25.7

From War to Managed Coexistence in the Fifth Century

Readers who encounter only the dramatic episodes examined in the preceding sections might reasonably conclude that Roman-Sasanian relations consisted of nearly continuous warfare. The fifth century corrects this impression decisively. Compared to the intensity of the fourth-century conflicts under Shapur II, the fifth century was characterized by considerably longer intervals of peace, punctuated by shorter and generally less consequential wars, alongside sustained diplomatic engagement between the two courts.

Several factors help explain this relative moderation. Both empires faced significant external pressures elsewhere during much of the fifth century: the Roman West confronted escalating challenges from various peoples entering its territory, developments examined in the companion volume of this series devoted to European history, while the Sasanian Empire faced growing pressure along its northeastern frontier from Hunnic and related steppe confederations, particularly the Hephthalites, examined in detail in Chapter 26. These simultaneous pressures gave both the Roman and Sasanian courts strong practical incentives to manage their shared frontier through negotiation rather than committing scarce military resources to renewed large-scale warfare against one another.

This period also saw the maturation of specific diplomatic institutions and practices that had developed more gradually across the preceding two centuries. Formal embassies traveled between Ctesiphon and Constantinople, the new Roman imperial capital whose founding and rise are examined in the companion European history volume, carrying not only political messages but gifts and ceremonial recognition. The Byzantine historian Procopius later reported, in an account whose historicity modern scholars debate, that the Roman emperor Arcadius entrusted the guardianship of his young son and eventual successor Theodosius II to the Sasanian king Yazdegerd I, a striking illustration of the degree of mutual trust, or at least mutual utility, that fifth-century diplomacy could be imagined to embody, difficult as such an arrangement would have been to picture during the height of Shapur II's wars. Prisoner exchange, ransom arrangements, and negotiated boundary adjustments in contested border districts likewise became more routine features of the relationship during this period.

None of this should suggest that the fifth century passed entirely without friction. Sporadic conflicts did occur, often connected to disputes over Armenia, the treatment of Christian communities within Sasanian territory, or disagreements over financial contributions Rome periodically made toward the defense of Caucasian mountain passes against nomadic incursions, a recurring point of contention that would resurface with greater intensity in the sixth-century conflicts examined in Section 25.8 and in Chapter 27. The essential point for students to grasp, however, is that diplomacy during this period functioned not merely as an interlude between wars but as the normal, default condition of the relationship, with warfare representing the exceptional disruption rather than the underlying rule.

Section 25.8

Kavadh I, Anastasius, and the Changing Frontier around 500 CE

The relative moderation of the fifth century gave way to renewed and intensified conflict around the turn of the sixth century, under circumstances that reveal how quickly the underlying tensions examined throughout this chapter could reassert themselves once favorable conditions receded. The Sasanian king Kavadh I, whose reign was itself complicated by significant internal political challenges examined more fully in later chapters addressing Sasanian society and religious politics, launched a major campaign against Roman territory beginning in 502 CE, reopening large-scale warfare after decades of comparative calm.

The immediate diplomatic trigger for this war is reported more clearly than many of its deeper causes. According to Procopius and the Syriac historical tradition, Kavadh sought financial assistance from Anastasius, a request commonly connected to the fiscal and political burdens created by Sasanian obligations toward the Hephthalites, the steppe power examined further in Chapter 26. Anastasius refused the request. Kavadh subsequently attacked Roman Armenia and upper Mesopotamia in 502 CE. This refusal should not be treated as the sole or sufficient cause of the war, since longer-term frontier tensions and internal Sasanian political pressures also contributed, but it helps explain why a long period of relative peace ended when it did.

Kavadh's forces achieved a significant early success with the capture of Amida in 503 CE, following another prolonged and costly siege reminiscent of Shapur II's earlier campaign against the same city nearly a century and a half before. The Roman government under the emperor Anastasius responded with a substantial military and diplomatic effort, eventually recovering Amida through negotiation rather than outright reconquest, and the war concluded around 506 CE without either side achieving a decisive or lasting territorial transformation, a pattern that should by this point in the chapter feel familiar to students.

The most historically significant consequence of this conflict, however, lay not in its immediate battles but in Rome's subsequent investment in frontier fortification. Around 507 CE, Anastasius's government constructed a major new fortress city, Dara, positioned close to the frontier and explicitly intended to counterbalance Sasanian-held Nisibis, still in Sasanian hands since the treaty of 363 CE. Procopius reports that Persian objections to Dara rested on an earlier treaty, associated with the reign of Theodosius II, forbidding new frontier fortifications, and Sasanian objections to Dara would resurface repeatedly as a source of tension in the diplomatic and military history of the following decades, developments that connect directly to the frontier situation inherited by Khosrow I and examined in Chapter 27.

This chapter's detailed narrative concludes at this point, on the threshold of the sixth century's escalating tensions, deliberately stopping short of Khosrow I's later reign, his major wars against Byzantium, and the eventual catastrophic conflict of the early seventh century, all reserved for Chapter 27. Students should carry forward from this section a clear sense of the situation Khosrow I would inherit: a frontier newly disturbed by renewed warfare, freshly fortified on the Roman side in a manner Sasanian Iran regarded as provocative, and still fundamentally unresolved after nearly three centuries of intermittent conflict and negotiation.

Section 25.9

What Four Centuries of Rivalry Created

Having traced the Roman-Sasanian rivalry from Ardashir I's accession through the frontier crisis of Kavadh I and Anastasius, this concluding section steps back from campaign narrative to ask a broader question: what did nearly three centuries of this particular phase of the rivalry actually produce, beyond the individual battles, sieges, and treaties already examined?

The rivalry produced, first, a set of durable frontier institutions. The heavily fortified zone running along the Euphrates, Khabur, and Tigris river systems, anchored by cities such as Nisibis, Amida, and eventually Dara, represented one of the most militarily developed and closely governed frontier regions anywhere in the ancient world, supported by roads, garrisons, supply networks, and administrative arrangements that both empires continually adjusted in response to the other's actions.

The rivalry produced, second, a genuine diplomatic culture. Formal treaties, negotiated by senior officials and publicly proclaimed, recurred throughout the period examined in this chapter, from the Peace of Nisibis in 298 through the treaty of 363 to the negotiated settlements ending the conflicts of the fifth century and of 502–506 CE. These treaties involved recognized procedures for territorial adjustment, financial payment, hostage exchange, and prisoner ransom, procedures that both sides generally observed once concluded, notwithstanding occasional violations. This diplomatic culture rested on a deeper, if grudging, mutual recognition: despite the ideological claims of universal sovereignty examined in Section 25.1, both Roman and Sasanian rulers, in practice, treated their counterpart as a legitimate emperor whose realm could not simply be conquered outright, whatever their public rhetoric occasionally suggested.

The rivalry produced, third, profound and lasting consequences for Armenia and the Caucasus, whose own political and religious history, examined further in this chapter's discussion of Section 25.5 and in Chapter 24's treatment of Armenian Christianity, became permanently entangled with the ambitions of two much larger neighboring empires, even as Armenian elites continued to pursue distinct interests of their own within that entanglement.

The rivalry produced, fourth, significant population movement across the frontier, including captives taken during Sasanian campaigns into Roman Syria and Mesopotamia, some of whom, according to later Iranian historical tradition, were resettled within Sasanian territory and contributed skills, including in some traditions specific craft and administrative expertise, to their new communities, a pattern of forced population transfer this textbook will examine further in later chapters addressing Sasanian economic and social history.

Perhaps most importantly, the rivalry demonstrated, repeatedly and across widely different periods and rulers, that dramatic military victory did not translate automatically into permanent conquest. Distance, logistics, the expense and difficulty of maintaining garrisons deep within hostile or newly conquered territory, the recurring need to address other frontiers and internal political challenges, and the sheer scale and resilience of both empires combined to prevent any single campaign, however spectacular, from permanently resolving the underlying contest. Shapur I's capture of a Roman emperor did not produce lasting Sasanian control of Syria. Julian's advance to the walls of Ctesiphon did not produce lasting Roman control of Mesopotamia. Each dramatic episode, examined in isolation, might suggest imminent civilizational collapse on one side or the other. Examined together, across the full span of nearly three centuries this chapter has covered, they instead reveal two large, resilient, evenly matched imperial systems locked in a contest neither could decisively win.

This unresolved balance is precisely what Chapter 27 will examine breaking down. The extraordinary feature of the Roman-Sasanian system, as this chapter has argued, was not that the two empires fought repeatedly, but that for centuries they survived those wars without either permanently destroying the other. Chapter 27 asks what happened when the final rounds of warfare, associated above all with Khosrow I and his successors, became so prolonged, so total in their mobilization of resources, and ultimately so mutually exhausting, that the old equilibrium this chapter has described ceased to recover, with consequences extending directly into the Arab-Muslim conquests examined in Chapter 28.

Primary Source Window

Shapur I, the Res Gestae Divi Saporis (Kaʿba-ye Zardosht Inscription), 3rd century CE

Creator: Commissioned by the Sasanian king Shapur I · Date: probably composed shortly after 260 CE · Type: Trilingual royal inscription (Middle Persian, Parthian, and Greek) carved on a stone structure near Naqsh-e Rustam

Context: The inscription was carved on the Kaʿba-ye Zardosht at Naqsh-e Rustam, within a monumental landscape dominated by Achaemenid royal tombs and increasingly reused by Sasanian rulers. Its placement gave Shapur's proclamation an ancient royal setting, although how accurately the early Sasanians identified or understood the Achaemenid monuments, and how directly they intended to invoke that historical dynasty rather than the Kayanian and other traditions examined in Volume I, remains debated. Its trilingual form, in Middle Persian, Parthian, and Greek, suggests an intended audience extending beyond Iranian speakers alone, potentially including Greek-speaking populations within the Sasanian realm and beyond.

Paraphrase: The inscription describes Shapur's western campaigns as a sequence of overwhelming victories, recounting the death of Gordian III, the submission of Philip the Arab under unfavorable terms, extensive destruction inflicted upon Roman Syrian cities including Antioch, and the capture of the emperor Valerian along with a long list of his officials and companions.

Analysis: The inscription provides invaluable, and in the case of Valerian's capture partially corroborated, evidence for the scale of Shapur's western campaigns, evidence unmatched by any comparably detailed contemporary Roman source for these specific events. Its trilingual character and physical placement within this older royal landscape demonstrate how Sasanian kings used monumental inscriptions to construct royal legitimacy for multiple audiences simultaneously, even if the precise historical meaning early Sasanian rulers attached to the surrounding Achaemenid monuments remains uncertain.

Limitations: The inscription is a royal political document, not a neutral campaign record. It compresses and reorders events for rhetorical effect, omits any Sasanian setbacks, and offers no perspective on the experience of conquered populations, captured soldiers, or the eventual reversal of most territorial gains described in Section 25.3. Its precise chronology for the Syrian campaigns remains debated partly because the text itself does not present a strictly linear narrative.

Perspective: The text represents the Sasanian royal court's self-presentation, intended to proclaim Shapur's cosmic and military legitimacy; it says nothing directly about Roman, Syrian, or Armenian perspectives on the same events.

Questions for students:

  1. What might the placement of Shapur's inscription within an older royal monumental landscape have communicated, and why should historians be cautious about assuming precise Sasanian knowledge of the Achaemenid past?
  2. What details in the inscription can be considered reasonably reliable, and which require more cautious treatment?
  3. Why is it significant that the inscription omits any mention of Rome's subsequent recovery of lost territory?
  4. How might comparing this inscription with Roman literary accounts of the same events help historians reconstruct a more balanced picture?
Historical Debate

Was the Roman-Sasanian Frontier a Zone of Permanent Conflict or a System of Managed Coexistence?

The question: Should historians characterize the roughly four-century Roman-Sasanian relationship primarily as a story of near-continuous hostility interrupted by occasional peace, or primarily as a durable, mutually recognized inter-imperial system in which warfare represented a periodic disruption of an underlying, largely stable diplomatic order?

Interpretation A — A frontier defined by persistent conflict: Some historians emphasize the sheer frequency and destructiveness of the wars examined in this chapter, from Shapur I's campaigns through the sieges of Nisibis and Amida to the conflict under Kavadh I, arguing that recurring warfare, not the intervals between it, best defines the relationship's essential character.
Interpretation B — A frontier defined by managed coexistence: Other historians emphasize the frequency, durability, and institutional sophistication of negotiated treaties, the long peace following 298, the relative calm of much of the fifth century, and the consistent mutual recognition of each ruler's imperial legitimacy, arguing that diplomacy and coexistence, not warfare, represent the relationship's underlying norm.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of evidence, Roman and Greek literary sources, Sasanian royal inscriptions, and the surviving record of treaties and diplomatic exchanges, but weigh the balance between conflict and cooperation differently.

Why it matters: This distinction shapes how students interpret this chapter's overall argument and how they should understand the transition, examined in Chapter 27, to a final phase of warfare severe enough to help exhaust both empires before the Arab-Muslim conquests.

Student task: Using specific evidence from this chapter's timeline, construct an argument for each interpretation, and then explain which you find more persuasive, considering what each approach clarifies and what it risks obscuring.

Multiple Perspectives

Living the Rivalry: Perspectives on the Frontier

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor.

From the perspective of a Sasanian court official at Ctesiphon: Managing the western frontier requires balancing the king's public claims of universal victory against the practical difficulty of holding conquered Syrian and Mesopotamian territory far from the Iranian heartland, making treaties and hostage arrangements as important as any single campaign.
From the perspective of a Roman frontier officer stationed at Nisibis or Amida: Life centers on the maintenance of fortifications, supply lines, and garrison readiness, since a besieged city's ability to hold out for weeks or months, rather than any single field battle, often determines the outcome of an entire campaign season.
From the perspective of an Armenian noble: Survival and influence depend on skillfully balancing relationships with both the Roman and Sasanian courts, since outright loyalty to either power risks catastrophic consequences from the other, while calculated independence offers the best chance of preserving family and regional autonomy.
From the perspective of a merchant trading through Nisibis: Commerce across the frontier depends directly on the political relationship between the two empires, since treaties such as the Peace of Nisibis can concentrate legitimate trade at a single sanctioned crossing point, while renewed warfare can disrupt established trading relationships built up over years.
From the perspective of a civilian resident of a repeatedly besieged Mesopotamian city: Successive sieges bring the ever-present possibility of starvation, captivity, forced resettlement, or death regardless of which empire's forces eventually gain control of the city, making the political abstractions of imperial rivalry a matter of direct, repeated, personal danger.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Arsacid-Roman Rivalry to the Sasanian System

What Changed

  • Sasanian royal ideology, examined in Chapter 23, articulated dynastic and territorial claims more visibly and assertively than surviving Arsacid royal ideology typically did, intensifying the rivalry's ideological stakes without implying that Sasanian government itself was uniformly more centralized
  • Early Sasanian kings, especially Shapur I, pursued offensive campaigns into core Roman territory, including Syria, on a scale not clearly matched by earlier Arsacid rulers
  • Fortress cities such as Nisibis, Amida, and eventually Dara acquired new strategic prominence as the frontier's fortification intensified over the fourth, fifth, and sixth centuries
  • Armenia's Christianization from the early fourth century onward added a new religious dimension to a rivalry earlier centered primarily on dynastic and territorial competition

What Stayed the Same

  • Northern Mesopotamia and Armenia remained the central contested zones, exactly as they had been under the Arsacids examined in Chapter 19
  • Both empires continued to recognize one another as legitimate imperial powers even amid intense warfare
  • Diplomacy, including envoys, hostages, and negotiated treaties, remained a normal and recurring feature of the relationship rather than a rare exception
  • Dramatic military victories continued to prove difficult to convert into permanent territorial conquest

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both continuity and change prevents treating the Sasanian-Roman rivalry as either a complete break from the Arsacid past or an unchanging repetition of it; the underlying geography and diplomatic logic persisted even as new dynasties, religious developments, and fortification strategies reshaped how that logic played out.

Cause and Effect

Why Repeated Victories Failed to Become Permanent Conquest

Geographic and logistical causes

Vast distances separated core territories, especially Ctesiphon from Roman Syria and Anatolia, making sustained occupation of deeply conquered territory extremely costly and difficult to supply.

Institutional and military causes

Heavily fortified frontier cities such as Nisibis and Amida could absorb prolonged sieges, buying time for relief efforts, negotiated settlements, or the besieging army's exhaustion.

Political causes

Both empires faced other frontiers and internal political pressures, including succession disputes and, from the fifth century, steppe confederations threatening Sasanian Iran's northeast, that limited how many resources either side could commit indefinitely to the western or eastern rivalry.

Effect

Neither empire ever achieved the total conquest its most triumphant inscriptions or literary accounts sometimes proclaimed; instead, the frontier repeatedly returned, after each dramatic episode, to a contested but enduring rough balance.

Historical complexity: This outcome was not simply a passive result of distance and fortification; both empires actively invested in diplomatic mechanisms, from the Peace of Nisibis to fifth-century embassies, precisely because their rulers recognized that the underlying military balance made total conquest unlikely, making negotiated coexistence a rational as well as a default strategy.

Object Study

The Naqsh-e Rustam Victory Relief of Shapur I

Description: Among several monumental rock reliefs Shapur I commissioned near Naqsh-e Rustam and other sites in Fars, one depicts the Sasanian king on horseback receiving the submission of Roman emperors, generally identified with Philip the Arab and Valerian, the latter shown grasped directly by the king's hand in a gesture of physical domination.

Analysis: The relief functions as a visual companion to the Res Gestae Divi Saporis examined in this chapter's Primary Source Window, translating the inscription's textual claims of victory into an immediately legible visual statement intended for viewers who might never read the trilingual text itself. The relief's placement within the ancient royal landscape of Naqsh-e Rustam strengthened its monumental authority, although the precise historical meaning the early Sasanians attached to the nearby Achaemenid monuments remains uncertain.

Limitations: Like the inscription it accompanies, the relief is a work of royal political art rather than a literal photographic record. It compresses distinct historical episodes, the defeat of Philip the Arab and the capture of Valerian, into a single unified composition, and it offers no indication of the subsequent reversal of Sasanian gains in Syria examined in Section 25.3.

Why it matters: Read critically, alongside the textual inscription and the fragmentary Roman literary evidence, the relief provides valuable evidence for how the Sasanian court wished its greatest western triumph to be remembered, permanently and monumentally, by later generations, even as the actual territorial consequences of that triumph proved considerably less permanent.

Everyday Life and Social History

Ordinary Lives on a Militarized Frontier

The wars, sieges, and treaties examined in this chapter were not experienced only by kings, generals, and envoys. Populations living along the Mesopotamian and Armenian frontier faced the recurring possibility of siege, deportation, and displacement across the entire period this chapter covers. Ammianus Marcellinus's eyewitness account of the siege of Amida, referenced in Section 25.6, describes civilian suffering during the siege in unusually direct terms, offering historians a rare window into the human cost of fortress warfare, even though Ammianus wrote as a Roman participant with his own perspective and priorities.

Population transfer represented another significant dimension of ordinary experience on this frontier. Later Iranian historical tradition credits Shapur I with resettling captives taken during his Syrian campaigns within Sasanian territory, where some are said to have contributed craft and administrative skills to their new communities, a pattern of forced but sometimes economically significant resettlement that recurs at various points throughout Sasanian history and will be examined further in later chapters addressing Sasanian economy and society. Merchants operating out of Nisibis, the officially sanctioned crossing point established by the Peace of Nisibis, experienced the rivalry's diplomatic fortunes directly, since renewed warfare could abruptly disrupt trading relationships built up over years of relative peace. Armenian peasants and townspeople, meanwhile, lived amid a political environment shaped by the competing religious and dynastic ambitions of their own nobility, the Roman court, and the Sasanian court simultaneously, a three-sided pressure quite different from the experience of populations living closer to either empire's core territory.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the Roman-Sasanian rivalry from Ardashir I's overthrow of the Arsacid dynasty in 224 CE through the frontier crisis associated with Kavadh I and the emperor Anastasius around the year 500, examining how a new Iranian royal house inherited, and then transformed, a contest over Armenia and northern Mesopotamia already centuries old by the time the Sasanians came to power.

The chapter began by distinguishing what genuinely changed under Sasanian rule, particularly a more assertive royal ideology and a renewed capacity for offensive campaigning into core Roman territory, from what remained continuous with the earlier Arsacid-Roman frontier examined in Chapter 19, including the persistent strategic importance of Mesopotamia and Armenia and the underlying pattern of alternating warfare and negotiated settlement.

Shapur I's extraordinary western campaigns, including the sack of Antioch and the unprecedented capture of the emperor Valerian in 260 CE, represented the rivalry's most dramatic early phase, but the chapter emphasized that these victories, however spectacular, did not produce lasting Sasanian control over Roman Syria, a reversal to which Palmyrene forces under Odaenathus contributed, alongside the subsequent restoration of central Roman authority, rather than a reversal Odaenathus alone can be confidently credited with achieving.

The Peace of Nisibis in 298 CE, following Galerius's defeat of Narseh, illustrated how the two empires had developed genuine institutional mechanisms for converting military outcomes into durable, negotiated settlements, producing roughly four decades of comparative peace before renewed warfare under Shapur II reopened large-scale conflict in the late 330s.

The fourth century's prolonged sieges of Nisibis and Amida, and the dramatic reversal following Julian's failed invasion and death in 363, demonstrated both the strategic centrality of fortified frontier cities and the speed with which an ambitious offensive campaign could collapse into significant territorial loss. The 387 partition of Armenia formalized, without fully resolving, the long contest over that kingdom's allegiance, a contest this chapter emphasized required treating Armenian nobles, clergy, and rulers as active historical agents rather than passive territory.

The fifth century, by contrast, was characterized chiefly by managed coexistence, with extended diplomatic engagement and comparatively limited warfare, shaped partly by both empires' need to address serious pressures elsewhere, including growing Hephthalite pressure on Sasanian Iran's northeastern frontier, examined further in Chapter 26.

Renewed conflict under Kavadh I and Anastasius beginning in 502 CE, including the siege of Amida and the subsequent Roman construction of the fortress city of Dara, brought the chapter's detailed narrative to its conclusion, establishing the tense and newly fortified frontier situation that Khosrow I would inherit at the opening of the period examined in Chapter 27.

Throughout, the chapter emphasized a central interpretive argument: repeated, sometimes spectacular, military victories on either side consistently failed to produce permanent conquest, a pattern explained by geographic distance, the resilience of fortified cities, the pressures each empire faced on other fronts, and the durable, if uneasy, mutual recognition of imperial legitimacy that underlay even the rivalry's most violent episodes.

The chapter's featured sources, including Shapur I's Res Gestae Divi Saporis and his victory relief at Naqsh-e Rustam, were treated as valuable but rhetorically shaped evidence requiring critical interpretation rather than literal acceptance, a method students should carry forward when encountering triumphant royal inscriptions or celebratory literary accounts throughout the remainder of this textbook.

These conclusions prepare students directly for Chapter 27, which examines how the equilibrium this chapter has described eventually broke down under Khosrow I and his successors, producing a final, catastrophic phase of warfare that would help set the stage for the Arab-Muslim conquest of Iran examined in Chapter 28.

Agreement dividing Armenia into Roman and Sasanian spheres of influence without ending Armenian political life or aristocratic autonomy.
Fortress diplomacy
The strategic practice of investing heavily in fortified frontier cities, such as Nisibis, Amida, and Dara, whose capacity to withstand siege shaped the pace and outcome of the wider rivalry.
Hostage exchange
A diplomatic practice in which each court sent or received royal or noble hostages as a guarantee of treaty compliance or goodwill.
Dynastic recognition
The mutual, if often grudging, acknowledgment by Roman and Sasanian rulers of one another's legitimate imperial status.
Universal monarchy
An ideological claim, made by both Roman and Sasanian rulers, to unique or supreme sovereignty, in practical tension with the enduring existence of an equally powerful rival empire.
Naqsh-e Rustam
Site near Persepolis featuring Achaemenid royal tombs and later Sasanian royal reliefs, including Shapur I's victory relief over Roman emperors.
Kaʿba-ye Zardosht
Stone tower structure near Naqsh-e Rustam bearing Shapur I's trilingual inscription describing his Roman campaigns.
Sasanian royal relief
Monumental rock-carved imagery commissioned by Sasanian kings to proclaim royal legitimacy and military victory to a broad public audience.
Deportation and resettlement
The forced relocation of captured populations, such as those taken during Shapur I's Syrian campaigns, into Sasanian territory.
Nisibis
Fortified Mesopotamian frontier city central to the treaties of 298 and 363 CE and repeatedly besieged during the fourth century.
Amida
Fortified Mesopotamian city captured by Shapur II after a 73-day siege in 359 CE and again contested during the war of 502–506 CE.
Dara
Fortress city built by the emperor Anastasius after 506 CE to counterbalance Sasanian-held Nisibis, a source of continued Sasanian objection.
Ctesiphon
Sasanian imperial capital, threatened by Roman campaigns including Julian's 363 CE invasion.
Arsacid Armenia
Armenian kingdom ruled by a branch of the same Arsacid dynasty earlier deposed in Iran by the Sasanians, contested by Rome and Sasanian Iran until its 387 CE partition.
Managed coexistence
A historical characterization of the fifth-century Roman-Sasanian relationship, emphasizing sustained diplomacy and relative peace over active warfare.
Valerian
Roman emperor captured by Shapur I in 260 CE, the rivalry's most dramatic single military event.
Odaenathus
Ruler of Palmyra whose forces campaigned against Sasanian territory in the 260s, contributing to the reversal of Shapur I's territorial gains in Syria, though the scale of his successes is difficult to reconstruct precisely.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has examined nearly three centuries of a rivalry that shaped the political, military, and religious history of two of the ancient world's largest empires, along with the peoples of Armenia and the Caucasus caught between them. The habits of mind it has introduced, reading royal inscriptions critically, distinguishing dramatic events from lasting consequences, and recognizing smaller regional actors as historical agents rather than passive territory, should inform the study of the chapters that follow.

The Roman-Sasanian rivalry was never simply a story of one civilization triumphing over another. It was a sustained, mutually shaping relationship between two resilient imperial systems that alternated between war and negotiation without either side achieving lasting dominance. Understanding this pattern helps explain not only the military history of late antiquity but the deeper structural realities, distance, fortification, competing frontiers, and the practical limits of even the most celebrated victories, that governed how ancient empires actually interacted with one another.

Students who understand that Shapur I's capture of a Roman emperor did not produce lasting conquest, that treaties such as the Peace of Nisibis represented genuine and durable achievements rather than mere pauses in an inevitable war, and that Armenian nobles and clergy pursued their own interests within a dangerous three-sided political environment, are well equipped to understand the final, catastrophic phase of this same rivalry examined in Chapter 27, and the profound consequences that phase would have for the Arab-Muslim conquest of Iran examined in Chapter 28.

The story this chapter tells is one of balance sustained across centuries, not a single unbroken triumph and not a predetermined outcome. Understanding how that balance held, imperfectly and at great cost, for nearly three hundred years is essential to understanding why its eventual collapse mattered so profoundly for both empires and for the wider world they shaped.
