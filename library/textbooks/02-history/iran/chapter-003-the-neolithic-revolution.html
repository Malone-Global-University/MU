The Neolithic Revolution, Farming, and the First Settlements

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

Somewhere in the central Zagros, in a small settlement of mudbrick rooms built and rebuilt across many generations, a household kept goats. Not wild goats hunted on a mountainside and carried home, but goats that lived near the settlement, that were watched, that were selectively killed young if male and kept longer if female. No single moment marked the passage from hunting to herding. The change unfolded slowly enough that no person living through it could have named the revolution now used to describe it.

Chapter 2 left the human communities of the Iranian plateau at the close of the Pleistocene, moving with the seasons, exploiting an increasingly diverse range of plant and animal resources, and, in the Zarzian industries of the Zagros, showing signs of more intensive settlement and more elaborate microlithic technology than earlier Paleolithic populations had employed. That chapter was careful to insist that none of these late Epipaleolithic developments made agriculture inevitable. Communities intensified their use of the landscape around them because doing so solved immediate problems of subsistence and social life, not because they were moving along a predetermined path toward farming.

This chapter picks up that story and asks how, over the course of several thousand years, some of those late hunting and gathering patterns developed into cultivation, animal management, more permanent architecture, and village life. The transformation conventionally called the "Neolithic Revolution" is one of the most thoroughly studied and most frequently misunderstood episodes in world history. Popular accounts often compress it into a single dramatic moment: people supposedly realized that planting seeds produced more food than gathering them, abandoned hunting and gathering, and built the first farming villages. The archaeological record from the Zagros Mountains and the lowlands of Khuzestan, along with comparable evidence from across Southwest Asia, tells a considerably more complicated and more interesting story.

At sites such as Ganj Dareh in the central Zagros, archaeologists have recovered evidence that goats were being managed, in some still-debated sense, centuries before their skeletons show the physical changes associated with full domestication. At Chogha Golan, in the western Zagros foothills of what is now Ilam Province, a very long sequence of occupation shows communities exploiting wild plants for many centuries before a shift toward domesticated-type cereals appears late in the sequence. On the Deh Luran Plain, at sites such as Ali Kosh, settlement patterns different again from those of the Zagros highlands developed as communities adapted to a seasonally wet lowland margin bordering the Khuzestan plain rather than mountain valleys. No single site, no single valley, and no single decisive innovation can account for this transformation. It emerged, unevenly and over a very long span of time, from many separate communities experimenting with plants, animals, architecture, and social arrangements in parallel and sometimes divergent ways.

This chapter therefore asks students to hold several distinctions in mind simultaneously, distinctions that popular narratives routinely collapse into a single undifferentiated idea called "farming." Cultivating a plant is not the same as that plant becoming genetically domesticated. Managing a herd of animals is not the same as those animals becoming fully domesticated livestock. Living in one place for most of the year is not the same as practicing agriculture. Building a village is not the same as building a complex society with specialized labor, social hierarchy, or centralized government. And the appearance of pottery, often used casually to mark "the beginning of the Neolithic," in fact arrives long after many of the transformations this chapter examines were already well underway, a fact reflected in archaeologists' use of the term Pre-Pottery Neolithic for the period before ceramic technology became common.

The geographic stage for this chapter spans two related but distinct environments already introduced in Chapter 1: the folded valleys, intermontane basins, and piedmont zones of the Zagros Mountains, and the lowland plain of Khuzestan where rivers descending from the Zagros created different opportunities and different constraints. Communities in these zones were in contact with one another, exchanged materials such as obsidian across considerable distances, and adapted the same broad set of plants and animals, wild wheat and barley, wild goats and sheep, in ways shaped by their local environments. Understanding both zones, and the connections between them, is essential preparation for Chapter 4, which examines how some of these village societies developed into the larger, more differentiated, and more centralized settlements from which the civilization of Elam eventually emerged.

Finally, this chapter asks students to think carefully about how archaeologists know any of this. Domesticated animals do not arrive in the archaeological record with a label attached. Recognizing early animal management requires reading subtle statistical patterns in the ages and sexes of slaughtered animals recovered from ancient middens. Recognizing early plant cultivation requires close examination of charred seeds under magnification, looking for the small anatomical changes that distinguish a domesticated cereal from its wild ancestor. This chapter treats these interpretive methods as part of its subject matter, not as a footnote to it, because understanding how fragile and painstaking this evidence is will help students avoid the confident, oversimplified narratives that still circulate in popular accounts of "the invention of agriculture."

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish cultivation from domestication, animal management from fully domesticated livestock, sedentism from agriculture, and village residence from political complexity.
  2. Explain why the phrase "Neolithic Revolution" is a useful but potentially misleading shorthand for a gradual, multi-stranded, and geographically uneven transformation.
  3. Trace the transition from the late Epipaleolithic patterns described in Chapter 2 to the early Neolithic developments examined in this chapter.
  4. Describe the environmental changes associated with the end of the Pleistocene and the beginning of the Holocene, and evaluate their role in shaping, without determining, the emergence of food production.
  5. Analyze the "mosaic model" of agricultural origins and explain why much current scholarship favors it over older, single-origin diffusion models, while recognizing that debate continues.
  6. Explain how archaeologists identify early animal management and domestication using faunal age and sex profiles, and apply this method to the case of Ganj Dareh.
  7. Explain how archaeologists identify early plant cultivation and domestication using archaeobotanical evidence, and apply this method to the case of Chogha Golan.
  8. Compare Zagros highland and southwestern lowland (Deh Luran and Khuzestan) patterns of early settlement, using Ganj Dareh, Chogha Golan, and Ali Kosh as case studies.
  9. Assess the social changes associated with increasingly permanent settlement, including household organization, storage, and possible early social differentiation.
  10. Evaluate evidence for exchange networks and continued mobility among early Neolithic communities, avoiding the assumption that sedentism meant isolation.
  11. Distinguish the developments examined in this chapter from the urbanism, specialized production, and political complexity examined in Chapter 4.
  12. Apply careful archaeological terminology when discussing plant and animal management, avoiding anachronistic or overstated claims about "the first farmers" or "the first domesticated animals."

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. How did the Epipaleolithic patterns discussed in Chapter 2 create conditions for the changes examined in this chapter without making those changes inevitable?
  3. How did the shift from Pleistocene to Holocene climate reshape the plants, animals, and landscapes available to human communities on the plateau?

Historical Context

Chapter 2 traced human presence on the Iranian plateau from the earliest Paleolithic evidence through the Epipaleolithic period at the close of the Pleistocene. That chapter emphasized the Zarzian industries of the Zagros region, associated with more intensive site occupation, more elaborate microlithic tool technology, and increasingly focused exploitation of particular plant and animal resources within defined territories. It also emphasized that these developments, however suggestive in hindsight, did not by themselves constitute agriculture, and that historians and archaeologists must resist reading the Epipaleolithic as though its communities were unconsciously progressing toward a farming economy that they could not yet foresee.

This chapter begins where that account left off, examining developments generally dated across a broad span from roughly the tenth through the sixth millennia BCE, though the pace and timing of change varied considerably across the different environments this chapter examines, and precise dates remain subject to ongoing archaeological revision. The chapter's geographic focus includes two zones introduced in Chapter 1: the central and northern Zagros highlands, with their intermontane valleys and adjacent piedmont belts, and the Khuzestan lowlands, where rivers descending from the Zagros created a different, and in some respects more agriculturally favorable, environment.

These developments unfolded within a broader Southwest Asian context. The Zagros zone examined in this chapter formed one part of a wider region, extending from the Levant through Anatolia and into the Zagros and Khuzestan, within which communities were independently and semi-independently experimenting with the management of similar plants and animals: wild wheats and barleys, wild goats and sheep, and eventually cattle and pigs. This textbook's geographic scope, as established in Chapter 1, centers on the Iranian plateau and its immediate connections, and this chapter accordingly concentrates on the Zagros and Khuzestan evidence rather than attempting a comprehensive survey of the entire Fertile Crescent, while acknowledging that developments on the plateau cannot be fully understood in isolation from this wider zone of contact and parallel experimentation.

Students should also bear in mind the chapter's place within the larger textbook. Chapter 4 will examine the rise of complex societies at Susa and elsewhere in the Zagros and Khuzestan region, developments that depend directly on the subsistence, settlement, and social changes examined here. This chapter deliberately stops short of urbanism, specialized craft production organized at a settlement-wide scale, and the institutional and administrative developments associated with early states. Students who finish this chapter should understand village societies characterized by cultivation, animal management, storage, and exchange, but should not yet expect to encounter cities, writing, or centralized political authority, developments Chapter 4 and later chapters will address in their appropriate context.

Section 3.1

What Was the Neolithic Revolution?

The term "Neolithic Revolution" was popularized in the twentieth century to describe the transition from a way of life based on hunting and gathering to one based on food production, involving cultivated plants, managed and eventually domesticated animals, and more permanent settlement. The word "revolution" captures something real: over the span this chapter examines, human societies in the Zagros and Khuzestan region, along with communities across the wider Southwest Asian world, developed subsistence strategies and settlement patterns that differed profoundly from anything that had come before, with consequences that shaped nearly every later chapter of this textbook. But the word also risks serious misunderstanding if students imagine a rapid, decisive event rather than a slow, uneven, and frequently reversible set of changes unfolding across many centuries and even millennia.

This textbook uses "Neolithic" primarily as a term for a broad set of interrelated cultural and subsistence developments rather than as a label for a single fixed moment. Before proceeding, students must learn to keep several concepts distinct that popular usage often collapses into a single idea of "farming."

Cultivation is not the same as domestication. Cultivation refers specifically to plant-management behavior: planting, tending, harvesting, and otherwise deliberately managing a plant population. Domestication, whether of a plant or an animal population, is a multigenerational biological process produced by sustained human management and selection, through which a managed population becomes increasingly differentiated from its wild ancestors. Different dimensions of this process may become archaeologically detectable at different times: behavioral management can precede detectable genetic differentiation, and genetic differentiation can in turn precede conspicuous morphological change, so domestication is best understood as a staged process rather than a single trait or threshold. Communities can cultivate a wild plant, in the behavioral sense, for many generations before that plant's population shows clear domesticated traits. Chogha Golan, examined in Section 3.8, demonstrates why this distinction must be handled cautiously: its long sequence clearly documents intensive wild-plant exploitation followed eventually by domesticated-type emmer, but deliberate cultivation of the earlier wild cereals cannot simply be assumed.

Animal management is not the same as fully domesticated livestock. A community can control where, when, and how animals move, feed, and reproduce, exercising real influence over a herd's composition, well before that population becomes genetically and behaviorally distinct from its wild ancestors in ways an archaeologist could recognize solely from bone morphology. Much of the recent archaeological literature on the Zagros Neolithic focuses precisely on identifying this intermediate stage of management, discussed further in Section 3.5.

Sedentism is not the same as agriculture. Communities can settle in one place for most or all of the year while continuing to rely primarily on hunting and gathering, particularly where local wild resources are unusually abundant, seasonally concentrated, or storable. Conversely, some early cultivators combined cultivation with continued seasonal mobility rather than adopting fully sedentary village life immediately. Settlement permanence and food production are related processes, but they are not identical, and this chapter tracks them as separate, if often intertwined, developments.

Village residence is not the same as political complexity. Living in a settlement of several dozen or more households, built from durable architecture and reoccupied across generations, does not by itself imply specialized full-time craft producers, formal political leadership, social classes, or the administrative institutions examined in Chapter 4's discussion of early complex society. This chapter's village societies show important social changes, discussed in Section 3.10, but nothing resembling the institutional landscape from which Elam later emerged.

Pottery does not mark "the beginning of the Neolithic." Archaeologists commonly use the term Pre-Pottery Neolithic for the earlier portion of this period, during which communities were cultivating plants, managing and in some cases domesticating animals, and building substantial architecture, all without regularly using fired ceramic containers. Baskets, skins, and other perishable containers, along with pits and stone-lined features, served many storage functions before pottery became common. Students should therefore resist any assumption that the absence of pottery at an early site indicates the absence of significant Neolithic developments.

Holding these distinctions in mind, this textbook defines the Neolithic transformation examined in this chapter as a set of interrelated processes, cultivation, domestication, sedentism, architectural investment, storage, and social reorganization, that developed at different rates, in different combinations, and in different sequences across the Zagros and Khuzestan region. No single site or valley experienced all of these changes simultaneously, and no single cause explains why they occurred when and where they did.

Section 3.2

From the Epipaleolithic to the Early Neolithic

Chapter 2 described the Zarzian industries of the late Epipaleolithic Zagros as characterized by more intensive occupation of favored locations, more elaborate microlithic tool kits, and a broadening range of exploited plant and animal resources compared to earlier Paleolithic patterns. Zarzian groups appear to have returned repeatedly to particular rockshelters and open-air locations, in some cases across multiple seasons, suggesting a pattern of resource use more geographically anchored than that of earlier, more thinly documented Paleolithic populations.

This chapter's task is to explain how, without positing any single decisive break, these late Epipaleolithic patterns opened onto the developments examined here. Several continuities deserve emphasis. Increasingly intensive collection of wild cereals and legumes, evident in the plant remains from sites spanning the Epipaleolithic to early Neolithic transition, extended a foraging strategy already visible, in less developed form, in the Zarzian record. Increasingly focused hunting of particular species, including wild sheep and goats in suitable Zagros habitats, likewise extended patterns of resource specialization already underway. And the repeated reoccupation of favored locations, a hallmark of Zarzian sites, anticipates, without directly causing, the more architecturally permanent settlements examined later in this chapter.

At the same time, students must resist reading this continuity as evidence that the outcome was foreordained. The relationship between Epipaleolithic intensification and Neolithic food production is best understood as one of expanded possibility rather than determined outcome. Epipaleolithic communities developed technologies, ecological knowledge, and social practices, including more elaborate microlithic toolkits suited to processing plant materials and more developed knowledge of the life cycles and habitats of key plant and animal species, that later communities could draw upon as they began cultivating and managing those same resources more intensively. But similar Epipaleolithic patterns existed in regions and periods that did not lead to agriculture, and specialists continue to debate why particular communities in particular places crossed from intensive foraging into cultivation and management while others, facing broadly similar environments, did not.

The transition examined in this section therefore should not be imagined as a single Zarzian population "becoming" Neolithic in a smooth, uninterrupted sequence at one location. The archaeological record instead shows a scatter of sites across the Zagros and adjacent regions, occupied at different times across a span of centuries, showing different combinations of continued foraging, expanding plant management, and, at some sites, the beginnings of animal management, examined in the sections that follow. Piecing together the relationship among these sites, and between them and the preceding Epipaleolithic record, remains an active area of archaeological research rather than a settled narrative.

Part of the difficulty in tracing this transition directly is a genuine archaeological problem rather than a simple gap in excavation coverage. Many Zagros rockshelters and open-air locations occupied during the Zarzian show occupational sequences that do not continue smoothly, without interruption, into the earliest recognizably Neolithic layers at the same or nearby locations. Some sequences show apparent gaps in occupation, whether because a location was abandoned for a period, because deposits from the intervening centuries were removed by later erosion or disturbance, or because a shift in settlement location moved a community's activity away from a spot that had previously been favored. Other sequences appear more continuous but still show a transitional zone of ambiguous layers in which the artifact assemblage combines older, Epipaleolithic-style microlithic technology with elements, such as early ground stone tools or the beginnings of substantial architecture, that specialists more readily associate with Neolithic communities. Archaeologists refer to sites and horizons of this kind, provisionally and with appropriate caution, as transitional or "proto-Neolithic," a label meant to flag genuine ambiguity rather than to assert a settled position in an unbroken developmental sequence.

This ambiguity has a direct methodological consequence that students should understand clearly: archaeologists cannot simply point to a Zarzian assemblage at one site and a Neolithic assemblage at a nearby site and assume, without further evidence, that the second population descended directly from the first, occupying the same territory across generations. Population continuity of this kind is a plausible hypothesis in many cases, supported in some instances by continuities in raw material use, site location preference, or other aspects of material culture, but it is a hypothesis that must be argued from specific evidence at each site or valley rather than assumed as a general rule for the region as a whole. Some transitional sites may instead represent new populations moving into an area, adopting elements of Neolithic technology and subsistence through contact with communities elsewhere, or some combination of local continuity and incoming influence that current evidence cannot always fully disentangle.

The site type itself also shifted gradually rather than at a single moment. Late Zarzian occupation is documented predominantly at rockshelters and modest open-air locations reoccupied across multiple seasons but without heavy investment in permanent construction. Early Neolithic sites, including the earliest occupation phases at Ganj Dareh discussed fully in Section 3.7, begin to show more deliberate investment in built structures, even where those structures remain comparatively simple relative to later phases at the same site. This shift in site type, from lightly modified natural shelters and camps toward constructed dwellings, did not happen everywhere at the same time or pace, and several Zagros valleys show local sequences in which older, more mobile patterns of occupation persisted alongside, or gave way only gradually to, more architecturally invested early Neolithic settlement.

Section 3.3

Climate Change and the Beginning of the Holocene

The end of the Pleistocene epoch and the beginning of the Holocene, conventionally dated to approximately 9700 BCE though the transition itself was neither instantaneous nor perfectly synchronous across all regions, brought significant environmental change to Southwest Asia, including the Zagros and the wider Iranian plateau. Broadly speaking, the Holocene brought warmer temperatures and, across much of the region relevant to this chapter, increased and more reliable precipitation compared to the preceding late glacial conditions, although the immediately preceding centuries had already included significant climatic fluctuation, including a well-documented cold, dry interval in the millennium before the Holocene began.

These environmental changes altered the distribution and abundance of the wild plants and animals that Zagros and Khuzestan communities depended upon. Wild cereal stands, including the wild ancestors of wheat and barley, appear to have expanded in range and density under improving Holocene conditions in many parts of Southwest Asia, potentially increasing the reliability and productivity of intensive wild cereal exploitation of the kind already practiced, in less developed form, by late Epipaleolithic communities. Comparable shifts likely affected the distribution of wild goat and sheep populations, key resources for the animal management discussed in Section 3.5.

Students must resist treating this environmental context as an explanation in itself. Climate change altered what was ecologically possible in the Zagros and Khuzestan region; it did not, by itself, cause cultivation, animal management, or sedentism to occur. Comparable Holocene climate improvements affected many regions of the world without producing comparable transformations, and even within Southwest Asia, communities in environmentally similar zones adopted food production practices at markedly different rates and in different combinations. The most defensible historical claim is a modest one: Holocene climatic conditions expanded the range of subsistence possibilities available to Zagros and Khuzestan communities, creating an environment within which the experimentation examined in this chapter's later sections became more likely to succeed and more likely to be sustained, without determining that any particular community would pursue it.

Regional variation matters as much as the broad Pleistocene-to-Holocene shift. The high Zagros valleys, the piedmont zone at somewhat lower elevation, and the Khuzestan lowlands each responded differently to changing Holocene conditions, given their different elevations, rainfall patterns, and proximity to permanent water sources. This regional variation helps explain why the sites examined later in this chapter, Ganj Dareh in a Zagros intermontane valley, Chogha Golan in the western foothills, and Ali Kosh on the Deh Luran Plain adjacent to Khuzestan, show meaningfully different subsistence trajectories despite operating within the same broad climatic transition and drawing on an overlapping set of plant and animal resources.

The transition into the Holocene was itself preceded by a well-documented episode of climatic reversal, generally known by the name given to its European counterpart, the Younger Dryas, during which much of the Northern Hemisphere, including large parts of Southwest Asia, experienced a return to colder and, in this region, generally drier conditions after an earlier phase of post-glacial warming. This cold, dry interval, falling in the millennium or so immediately before the conventional Holocene boundary, would have placed renewed pressure on the wild cereal stands and animal populations that late Epipaleolithic communities depended upon, potentially encouraging exactly the kind of intensified, more geographically anchored resource exploitation already visible in the Zarzian record discussed in Chapter 2 and Section 3.2. The subsequent warming and, in many parts of the region, increased effective moisture that marked the beginning of the Holocene proper would then have represented a fairly abrupt improvement in conditions for communities that had recently weathered a difficult climatic interval, though the pace and completeness of this improvement varied by location and continues to be refined through ongoing paleoclimatic research.

These shifts affected vegetation zones in ways that mattered directly for subsistence, though the paleoenvironmental record shows a considerably more heterogeneous picture than a single, uniform "warmer and wetter" narrative would suggest. Moisture histories and vegetation responses differed substantially by elevation and locality across the Zagros, and specialists caution against assuming a simple, uniform sequence running from warming, to oak woodland expansion, to denser cereal stands, to settlement. In parts of the region, mature deciduous-oak woodland of the kind associated with the classic "oak-pistachio woodland-steppe" description expanded only after a considerable delay of several millennia into the Holocene, with substantial areas continuing to support more open, semi-arid grassland vegetation well into the early Holocene rather than converting promptly to denser woodland once climatic warming began. Where and when woodland expansion did occur, it likely improved conditions for the wild cereals and legumes this chapter emphasizes, but the delayed and geographically uneven character of this vegetation shift means that any given Zagros community's actual local environment during the early Holocene depended heavily on its specific elevation, aspect, and position relative to these unevenly advancing vegetation zones, rather than on a single regional climatic trend applying uniformly everywhere at once.

Water reliability likely improved in a general sense across much of the region, though, again, unevenly rather than uniformly. Increased precipitation in some areas would have made seasonal streams and springs more dependable sources of water for both human communities and the animal populations, including wild goats and sheep, that depended on them, potentially reducing the risk associated with settling for extended periods near a single water source rather than moving frequently in search of new ones. Altitudinal differences shaped how directly communities in different zones experienced these changes: the high Zagros valleys, where Ganj Dareh would later develop, remained subject to cold winters and significant seasonal variation even under improved Holocene conditions, while the lower piedmont zone, where Chogha Golan is located, and the still-lower southwestern lowlands, where Ali Kosh developed, experienced milder winters and a hotter, more arid summer regime offset partly by proximity to watercourses rather than by rainfall alone. This altitudinal and regional variation is one reason this chapter treats the Zagros highlands, the piedmont, and the southwestern lowlands as related but environmentally distinct settings, each following its own particular and incompletely reconstructed environmental trajectory, rather than as a single uniform stage on which the Neolithic transformation played out identically and simultaneously everywhere.

Section 3.4

The Zagros and the Origins of Food Production

For much of the twentieth century, archaeologists working across Southwest Asia debated where and how agriculture originated, and this debate itself unfolded in identifiable stages rather than as a single fixed position later simply overturned. An early influential proposal, associated with the archaeologist V. Gordon Childe, held that agriculture arose as an adaptive response to increasing aridity at the end of the Pleistocene, which supposedly forced humans, animals, and plants into closer proximity around shrinking oases and water sources, an "oasis hypothesis" that later fieldwork did not substantiate. A subsequent and highly influential model, developed chiefly by the archaeologist Robert Braidwood, proposed instead that agriculture most plausibly originated within the "hilly flanks" of the Fertile Crescent, the natural habitat zone of the wild cereal and animal species later domesticated, a zone running through the Taurus and Zagros foothills rather than through the river lowlands of Mesopotamia. Braidwood's model, far from marginalizing the Zagros, directed sustained archaeological attention toward it, motivating his own influential excavations at Jarmo in the Zagros foothills of what is now Iraqi Kurdistan specifically in order to investigate the region's role in early food production. Later twentieth century scholarship built on this foundation with a range of core-area and diffusion arguments, some emphasizing the Levant specifically as the primary center from which cultivation practices and, eventually, already domesticated plants and animals spread outward to neighboring regions including the Zagros.

Accumulating archaeological evidence from sites such as Ganj Dareh, Chogha Golan, and Sheikh-e Abad has substantially complicated any simple version of a single-core diffusion model. Much current scholarship instead favors what this textbook calls the mosaic model of agricultural origins: a pattern in which multiple communities across Southwest Asia, including in the Zagros region examined in this chapter, experimented with the management of locally available wild plants and animals at broadly overlapping but not identical times, developing food production through a protracted, geographically distributed process of parallel, interacting, and in some cases independently arrived-at developments rather than a single invention subsequently diffused outward. Students should understand this as the currently dominant interpretive framework rather than as a fully settled consensus: specialists continue to debate the relative contributions of local, in-place domestication versus the movement and adoption of already-domesticated stock through contact and exchange, and the precise number, location, and degree of independence of the region's various domestication processes remains an active area of ongoing research rather than a closed question.

This revision matters for several reasons beyond simple factual correction. First, it restores historical agency to Zagros communities, who no longer appear merely as recipients of an innovation developed elsewhere, but as active participants in a broader Southwest Asian process of experimentation with locally available resources, particularly wild goats, which appear to have been managed unusually early in the Zagros relative to some other regions. Second, it helps explain regional diversity in the archaeological record: if agriculture had a single point of origin and spread outward in a simple wave, one would expect a fairly uniform and chronologically ordered sequence of adoption; instead, the evidence shows considerable local variation in which plants and animals were emphasized, in what combinations, and on what timeline, precisely what a mosaic, multi-centered process would predict.

The Zagros contribution to this wider mosaic centers on several strands of evidence examined in the sections that follow: early goat management at Ganj Dareh, discussed in Sections 3.5 and 3.7; long-term wild cereal and legume exploitation at Chogha Golan, discussed in Sections 3.6 and 3.8; and comparative evidence from sites such as Sheikh-e Abad. Together, this evidence positions the Zagros not as a passive frontier of the Southwest Asian Neolithic but as one of several regions, alongside parts of the Levant and Anatolia, where communities independently developed elements of the food-producing economy that later spread, combined, and intensified across the wider region.

Sheikh-e Abad, located in the central Zagros not far from Ganj Dareh, contributes an independent line of evidence to this regional picture rather than simply duplicating the Ganj Dareh case. Excavations at Sheikh-e Abad have documented an early occupation sequence, beginning at a date broadly comparable to or, on some interpretations, somewhat earlier than the earliest levels at Ganj Dareh, together with faunal and botanical evidence for a subsistence economy combining wild plant exploitation with early management of goats and, at this site, some evidence for the exploitation of wild sheep as well. The site's early architecture, along with features interpreted by excavators as possible ritual or communal structures distinct from ordinary domestic buildings, has made Sheikh-e Abad an important point of comparison for questions about community organization in the earliest Zagros Neolithic, questions this chapter approaches more cautiously than some specialist literature, given the difficulty of inferring ritual function from architectural form alone. For this chapter's purposes, Sheikh-e Abad matters chiefly as independent corroboration that the patterns documented at Ganj Dareh, early goat management combined with continued wild plant and animal exploitation, were not unique to a single valley or a single community, but were shared, in broadly similar if not identical form, by more than one central Zagros settlement during the same general period, evidence that directly supports the mosaic model's emphasis on parallel, regionally distributed experimentation rather than a single point of innovation.

Section 3.5

Goats, Sheep, and the Domestication of Animals

Wild goats (Capra aegagrus) inhabited the mountainous terrain of the Zagros in substantial numbers, favoring steep, rocky slopes that offered both forage and protection from predators. Their availability, combined with their manageable size, social herding behavior, and tolerance for varied forage, made them strong candidates for the kind of sustained human management that, over time, could develop into full domestication. Wild sheep occupied overlapping but somewhat different habitats and were subject to comparable, if not always identical, processes of management in various parts of the wider region.

Understanding how archaeologists identify early animal management requires appreciating a basic methodological problem: newly managed animal populations do not immediately develop the skeletal changes, such as reduced body size or altered horn morphology, that make fully domesticated livestock easy to distinguish from wild populations in the archaeological record. For the earliest phases of management, archaeologists instead rely primarily on kill-off patterns, statistical analysis of the ages and sexes of animals represented in a faunal assemblage recovered from a site's refuse deposits.

The underlying logic is straightforward. Hunters targeting a wild population without active management tend to produce an age and sex profile in their kills that broadly reflects the natural demographic structure of the hunted population, or reflects the vulnerabilities and preferences of hunting strategy in ways that differ from herd management strategies. Herders actively managing a population for meat and reproduction, by contrast, tend to cull young males disproportionately, since a herd requires comparatively few males for successful reproduction, while retaining females longer to maximize reproductive output and secondary products such as milk. When zooarchaeologists find an assemblage dominated by the bones of young male goats, with mature females comparatively underrepresented among the slaughtered animals, this pattern suggests deliberate herd management, a form of human control over a population's reproduction and composition, even before that population's skeletal morphology has changed enough to be recognized as domesticated in the narrower biological sense.

This is precisely the kind of evidence that has made the Zagros, and Ganj Dareh in particular, central to scholarly discussion of early goat management, examined in full in Section 3.7. Students should understand that this method, while powerful, involves interpretive judgment rather than unambiguous proof: kill-off patterns can be affected by factors other than deliberate herd management, including seasonal hunting strategies, taphonomic processes affecting which bones survive and are recovered, and small sample sizes at any individual site. Responsible archaeological interpretation therefore treats a suggestive kill-off pattern as one important line of evidence to be weighed alongside others, including settlement context, associated artifacts, and comparison with patterns from other, better-understood sites, rather than as conclusive proof by itself.

Sheep, and somewhat later cattle and pigs, underwent broadly comparable processes of management and eventual domestication in various parts of Southwest Asia, though the pace, sequence, and regional emphasis differed considerably from one species and one zone to another. This chapter concentrates on goats because the Zagros evidence for early goat management is unusually well studied and because it best illustrates the general interpretive principles that apply, with appropriate adjustment, to other species as well.

Students should also resist assuming that early managed herds functioned as part of a fully developed pastoral economy comparable to later historical periods. Early Zagros goat management most likely supplemented, rather than replaced, continued hunting, gathering, and, increasingly, plant cultivation. The mixed subsistence economies of the early Neolithic combined multiple strategies simultaneously, and it would be a significant overstatement to describe early Zagros communities as "pastoralists" in the fully developed sense examined in later chapters of this textbook.

Archaeological Method

Identifying Domestication in the Archaeological Record

The problem: Newly managed plant and animal populations do not immediately display the physical traits that make fully domesticated organisms easy to recognize. Archaeologists must therefore identify subtler, indirect signs of human management long before clear morphological domestication appears.

For animals — kill-off patterns: Zooarchaeologists examine the ages and sexes of animals represented in bone assemblages from ancient refuse deposits. A profile skewed toward young male animals, with mature females comparatively rare among the slaughtered individuals, suggests deliberate herd management rather than opportunistic hunting, since managers benefit from culling surplus males early while retaining females for reproduction and secondary products.

For animals — later morphological change: Over subsequent generations, sustained management under human control can produce measurable changes, including reduced overall body size and altered horn-core shape in goats, that archaeologists can identify through careful measurement and comparison with wild populations, providing a second, later line of evidence.

For plants — archaeobotanical evidence: Paleobotanists recover charred plant remains, especially seeds, from ancient hearths, floors, and refuse deposits, using flotation techniques to separate light plant material from soil. They examine features such as seed size, the structure of the seed's attachment point, and the presence or absence of a brittle versus tough rachis, the small stem structure that in wild cereals shatters to disperse seed naturally but in domesticated cereals remains intact, a trait selected for because it makes cereals easier to harvest by cutting or pulling rather than gathering already-scattered seed.

For plants — cultivation without domestication: Because morphological domestication in cereals can take many centuries of sustained cultivation to become fixed in a population, paleobotanists also look for indirect evidence of cultivation behavior, including the presence of weed species characteristically associated with disturbed, cultivated soil, and changes in the proportion of large-seeded, easily processed plant taxa relative to the full range of locally available wild plants.

Interpretive caution: No single line of evidence is conclusive by itself. Sample size, preservation conditions, and the specific depositional history of a site can all distort the picture. Responsible interpretation weighs multiple lines of evidence together and remains open to revision as new data become available, a point especially relevant to ongoing debates about sites such as Ganj Dareh and Chogha Golan.

Section 3.6

Wheat, Barley, Pulses, and Plant Cultivation

Wild cereals, including wild forms ancestral to einkorn and emmer wheat and to barley, grew across parts of the Zagros piedmont and adjacent regions in dense, seasonally harvestable stands, particularly in years and locations favored by adequate rainfall. Wild legumes, including species ancestral to lentils and peas, grew alongside these cereals in many of the same habitats, providing a complementary source of plant protein. Communities in the region had exploited these wild plants for many generations before the developments examined in this chapter, as the Epipaleolithic evidence discussed in Chapter 2 and Section 3.2 indicates.

The transition from intensive wild plant gathering to cultivation, and eventually to the management of morphologically domesticated crops, unfolded gradually and left a distinctive, if incomplete, trace in the archaeological record. Cultivation, in the behavioral sense of deliberately planting, tending, and harvesting a plant population rather than simply gathering it where it grew wild, can be inferred from several kinds of indirect evidence: the presence of characteristic "weed" species associated with disturbed or tilled ground, changes in the proportion of large-seeded, easily processed cereals and legumes relative to a fuller range of locally available wild plants, and, at some sites, physical evidence for the tools associated with harvesting and processing, including stone sickle blades bearing a distinctive polish, often called sickle gloss, produced by repeated contact with the silica-rich stems of cereal plants.

Full morphological domestication of wheat and barley, in which the population's rachis becomes predominantly tough rather than brittle, so that ripe grain remains attached to the stem until deliberately threshed rather than shattering and dispersing naturally, appears to have developed gradually over many centuries of sustained cultivation, rather than arising quickly or being achieved through a single deliberate selection event. This slow pace matters considerably for how students should think about the relationship between cultivation and domestication: a community could cultivate wild-type cereals, in the behavioral sense, for generation after generation, gradually and likely not always consciously favoring plants with tougher rachis and larger seeds through the ordinary practice of harvesting and replanting, long before the population's genetic composition shifted decisively toward the domesticated type. The long occupation sequence at Chogha Golan, examined fully in Section 3.8, provides some of the clearest available evidence for this gradual process anywhere in Southwest Asia.

Alongside wheat, barley, and pulses, grouped together under what is traditionally called the "founder crop" model of Southwest Asian agriculture in recognition of their early and central role, other plants, including flax, contributed to the developing early Neolithic plant economy in various parts of the region, though the Zagros evidence for the cultivation of these secondary crops is less extensive than for cereals and legumes. Students should treat the traditional founder-crop model as a useful organizing label rather than as an archaeologically fixed or universally applicable package; recent archaeobotanical reassessment has questioned aspects of the classic model, including how uniformly these particular species were emphasized together across different regions and periods, a caution consistent with this chapter's broader rejection, in Section 3.1, of any fixed "Neolithic package" adopted as a single bundle. Students should understand this developing plant economy as a gradually expanding and diversifying set of managed resources, varying by region, rather than a fixed package adopted all at once.

The specific plants involved deserve closer attention than a single general reference to "wheat and barley" allows. Among the wheats, wild einkorn, a hulled wheat with a single grain per spikelet, and wild emmer, a hulled wheat with two grains per spikelet, both contributed to the early Zagros and wider Southwest Asian plant economy, though their relative importance varied by site and region; emmer in particular appears to have been especially significant in parts of the wider Fertile Crescent, while einkorn shows a somewhat different, though overlapping, distribution. Wild barley, generally more tolerant of poorer soils and drier conditions than the wheats, provided a complementary resource especially valuable in marginal or variable growing environments, a property that may help explain its continued importance across a wide range of Southwest Asian environments in later periods as well. Among the pulses, wild lentils and wild peas are both attested in the archaeobotanical record of the region, and chickpea, though its precise early cultivation history is less securely documented for the Zagros specifically than for lentils and peas, belongs to the same broader category of legumes that early cultivators increasingly favored alongside cereals, likely valued for the complementary nutrition, particularly protein, that legumes provide alongside a cereal-based diet.

The process by which harvesting itself could reshape a plant population deserves emphasis, because it demonstrates one influential model for how cultivation could produce domestication as an unintended cumulative outcome of ordinary practical behavior rather than requiring any deliberate breeding program. Wild cereals possess a brittle rachis, the small stem structure connecting each grain to the seed head, which shatters readily when the grain ripens, scattering seed onto the ground where it can germinate the following season. This trait serves the wild plant's own reproductive interests but works against a human harvester, who loses grain to shattering before or during collection. Under the sickle-selection hypothesis, a harvester using a sickle to cut whole seed heads, rather than gathering already-fallen grain by hand, will tend, without necessarily intending to, to harvest disproportionately from those individual plants whose rachis remained tough enough to hold the grain in place through the moment of cutting; a fully shattered plant has already dropped its seed and contributes little to the harvested yield. If a portion of the harvested grain is then used as seed stock for the following season's planting, this simple, repeated harvesting behavior could gradually and cumulatively increase the proportion of tough-rachis individuals in the cultivated population, generation after generation, eventually producing a population in which the tough-rachis, non-shattering trait predominates, the condition understood as full morphological domestication in cereals.

Students should treat this sickle-selection mechanism as one influential and clearly plausible model rather than as an empirically settled explanation for the pace of cereal domestication. Archaeological evidence for the actual timing of increased sickle use does not always closely track the timing of increasing tough-rachis morphology at sites across the region, and some studies of the eastern Fertile Crescent specifically have found that the two developments are not tightly synchronized, suggesting that sickle harvesting technique was probably one contributing factor among several, rather than the single or primary driver of the domestication process. This lack of close synchronization is itself consistent with, and indeed reinforces, this chapter's broader emphasis on domestication as a slow, uneven, multi-causal process, precisely the kind of pattern documented in unusually fine detail at Chogha Golan and discussed further in Section 3.8.

Cultivation also altered the surrounding plant community in ways that leave a distinct archaeobotanical signature. Preparing and repeatedly working the same plot of ground favors certain "weed" species, plants adapted to disturbed soil and capable of thriving alongside a cultivated crop, over the fuller range of species found in undisturbed wild vegetation. The presence of such disturbed-ground weed species in the archaeobotanical assemblage from a site, alongside cereal and legume remains, provides paleobotanists with indirect but meaningful evidence that a plot of ground was being deliberately and repeatedly cultivated rather than simply harvested where it grew without human intervention, complementing the more direct evidence available from cereal rachis morphology itself.

Processing cultivated grain required additional labor and left its own material trace. Ground stone tools, including querns and handstones used to grind harvested grain into flour or meal, appear with increasing frequency at early Neolithic sites across the Zagros and Khuzestan, providing artifactual evidence for the growing importance of cereal processing in the daily economy of these communities, a point returned to in this chapter's discussion of everyday life. Once processed or simply threshed and cleaned, grain needed to be kept safe from moisture, pests, and spoilage until it was needed, a requirement that connects the plant cultivation examined in this section directly to the storage practices examined more fully in Section 3.10.

Taken together, this evidence supports a graduated sequence of plant-human relationships rather than a single binary between "wild" and "domesticated." Communities in this chapter's geographic focus moved, at different paces in different places, from simple gathering of whatever wild plants were locally available, toward more selective and repeated exploitation of favored wild stands, toward possible tending and cultivation of selected wild-type seed, and only eventually, after a long and not necessarily steady interval, toward reliance on populations that had become morphologically and genetically domesticated crops. The long occupation sequence at Chogha Golan, examined in Section 3.8, provides unusually detailed evidence for a prolonged transition from intensive wild-plant exploitation to eventual reliance on a domesticated crop, while also showing why deliberate cultivation cannot simply be assumed throughout that interval.

Section 3.7

Ganj Dareh and the Early Zagros Villages

Ganj Dareh, located in a high intermontane valley of the central Zagros, is among the most intensively studied sites for understanding early village life and early animal management anywhere in the Zagros region. Its principal Aceramic Neolithic occupation was concentrated in the late ninth to early eighth millennium BCE and accumulated rapidly over only a few centuries; the site preserves a sequence of architectural levels, faunal remains, and other material that together provide an unusually detailed picture of a community in the midst of the transformations this chapter examines.

Architecturally, Ganj Dareh shows a progression from simpler structures in its earliest levels to more substantial rectangular, multi-room buildings constructed of chineh, a rammed-earth or coursed mud technique, and later mudbrick, in its later levels. These buildings were repeatedly rebuilt on the same footprint across successive occupation phases, a pattern that, as later sections of this chapter discuss, suggests both practical continuity in construction technique and a developing sense of place attached to particular household locations across generations.

The site's faunal assemblage, dominated by goat remains, has been central to scholarly discussion of early goat management. Analysis of the ages and sexes of goats represented in the assemblage shows a kill-off pattern consistent with deliberate herd management along the lines described in Section 3.5: a disproportionate representation of young male goats among the slaughtered animals, alongside better-represented adult females, a pattern that specialists have interpreted as evidence for human control over the reproduction and composition of the local goat population, predating clear skeletal evidence for full morphological domestication.

Students must exercise real caution here. It is tempting, and it has sometimes been claimed in popular accounts, that Ganj Dareh preserves evidence for "the first domesticated goats," as though domestication were invented at this one site and subsequently spread outward. This textbook's discussion of the mosaic model in Section 3.4 should make clear why such a claim overstates what the evidence can support. Ganj Dareh provides some of the clearest and best-studied evidence anywhere in Southwest Asia for early goat management, but comparable processes were likely underway, with varying degrees of documentation, at other Zagros sites during a broadly overlapping period. The site is best understood as an exceptionally well-preserved and well-excavated window onto a regional process, rather than as the singular point of origin for goat domestication.

Recent archaeogenomic research has added an important refinement to this picture, one that requires distinguishing three separate evidentiary dimensions rather than treating "management" and "domestication" as a simple either-or category. The first dimension is management behavior, the kind of deliberate human control over herd composition and reproduction that the kill-off pattern evidence discussed above documents. The second dimension is genetic differentiation, measurable changes in a population's DNA that distinguish it from wild, unmanaged populations, changes that can accumulate under sustained human control well before they produce any changes visible to the eye or measurable on a bone. The third dimension is conspicuous morphological domestication, the visible skeletal changes, including reduced body size and altered horn-core shape, that traditionally serve as the clearest, if latest-appearing, sign of domestication in the archaeological record. Ancient DNA analysis of goat remains from Ganj Dareh and related Zagros sites indicates that a population showing genuine genetic domestic affinity, meaning statistically distinguishable from wild goat populations in ways consistent with sustained human breeding control, was already present by approximately 8200 BCE, a finding that places meaningful genetic domestication roughly a millennium before the fully developed morphological changes traditionally used to mark "complete" domestication in the zooarchaeological record. This finding actually reinforces, rather than complicates, this chapter's central teaching point: domestication is better understood as a staged process unfolding across behavioral, genetic, and morphological dimensions on different timescales, rather than a single threshold that a population either has or has not crossed.

Beyond its zooarchaeological significance, Ganj Dareh has also yielded evidence relevant to the social history examined in Section 3.10, including burials associated with specific structures, suggesting a developing relationship between particular households and particular places that would have reinforced the kind of long-term attachment to a settlement location central to the broader Neolithic transformation this chapter traces.

The site's occupational sequence is generally divided by excavators into several successive levels, together spanning roughly two to three centuries following approximately 10,100 years ago on the calibrated radiocarbon timescale, a considerably tighter chronological span than older, more impressionistic dating once suggested. These levels document successive phases of building, use, and modification, in some cases including rebuilding on essentially the same footprint. One level in particular, generally labeled Level D, was extensively burned in an intense fire, an event that, however destructive at the time, had the fortunate archaeological consequence of hardening and preserving its mud-walled architecture and much of its contents in unusually fine detail. This well-documented conflagration should not be generalized into a claim that fire routinely terminated occupation at the site; the evidence for intense burning is concentrated at this one level, and the broader sequence of construction and modification across the site's other levels is better described simply as successive building and rebuilding rather than as a repeated cycle of destruction by fire.

Within individual structures, excavators have identified hearths, storage installations, and other features indicating that these buildings served as genuine domestic space for cooking, processing, and storing food, rather than functioning as purely specialized or ceremonial architecture. The site's paleobotanical evidence, while considerably thinner and less thoroughly reanalyzed than its celebrated faunal assemblage, includes reports of barley showing domestic-type characteristics alongside wild-type barley, and lentil remains whose dimensions fall within the wild range, from deposits associated with some of these structures. This plant evidence should be treated with real caution: the principal published study is now dated, later work has questioned some of its specific identifications, and the sample sizes involved are modest. The most defensible statement available is that Ganj Dareh's inhabitants very likely exploited both wild and, in some contexts, cultivated cereals and legumes alongside their goat management, but the site's plant evidence cannot support the same level of confident, sequence-based interpretation that this chapter develops for Chogha Golan in Section 3.8. Ganj Dareh's primary contribution to this chapter therefore remains its animal-management and, increasingly, its archaeogenomic evidence, rather than its plant record.

Scholarly interpretation of the Ganj Dareh evidence has itself changed over time, a point worth making explicit for students learning to evaluate archaeological claims critically. Early interpretations, published not long after the site's initial excavation, sometimes described the Ganj Dareh goat assemblage in terms that implied a more advanced or complete stage of domestication than later, more methodologically cautious reanalysis, and now the genomic evidence discussed above, has supported. This revision illustrates a broader point relevant throughout this textbook: archaeological interpretation is provisional and subject to revision as methods improve and comparative evidence accumulates, and students should treat even well-established site interpretations as the current best reading of the evidence rather than as final, unrevisable conclusions.

Section 3.8

Chogha Golan and Long-Term Plant Management

If Ganj Dareh provides this chapter's central case study for early animal management, Chogha Golan, located in the western Zagros foothills of what is now Ilam Province, provides the central case study for long-term plant management. The site's unusually long and continuous occupation sequence, spanning roughly 2,200 years and extending from approximately the tenth into the eighth millennium BCE, has made it one of the most important sources of archaeobotanical evidence anywhere in Southwest Asia for tracking the relationship between cultivation behavior and plant domestication over an extended span of time.

Excavations at Chogha Golan recovered an extensive and well-preserved sequence of charred plant remains, allowing paleobotanists to track changes in the site's plant economy across successive occupation layers with a level of chronological resolution rarely available at other sites. The earliest layers show a broad-based exploitation of numerous wild plant species, consistent with intensive gathering of the kind already established, in less developed form, in the late Epipaleolithic record discussed in Chapter 2. For a very long time thereafter, however, the sequence does not show the smooth, steadily narrowing progression toward domesticated cereals that earlier interpretive models predicted and that this chapter's own earlier discussion once suggested. Wild barley and a genuinely diverse range of wild grasses and other plants remained important components of the plant economy across a substantial portion of the sequence, and a systematic reanalysis of the site's full plant assemblage, published in 2018, concluded explicitly that the traditional model of steadily intensifying "pre-domestication cultivation," in which gathered diversity narrows in an orderly fashion toward wild-type cereals and then toward domesticated ones, does not adequately describe the Chogha Golan sequence as a whole.

The actual pattern documented at the site is more genuinely complicated, and, properly understood, more instructive than a simple gradual narrowing model. Chogha Golan documents nearly two millennia of intensive exploitation of a diverse wild-grass economy, including abundant wild barley alongside numerous other wild taxa, sustained across the greater part of the site's long occupation sequence. Only late in this sequence does the evidence show the clear emergence and growing importance of domesticated-type emmer wheat, around 9,800 years ago on the calibrated radiocarbon timescale. The archaeobotanical evidence does not establish that the wild cereals exploited throughout the earlier and middle portions of the sequence were necessarily under deliberate cultivation; what is securely documented is a long, resilient wild-grass economy giving way, only near the end of the sequence, to an assemblage clearly dominated by domesticated-type emmer.

This pattern still supports, and in some respects sharpens, the distinction introduced in Section 3.1 between cultivation as a behavior and domestication as a biological outcome, but it does so more cautiously than a simple gradual-cultivation narrative would allow. A systematic reanalysis of the site's full plant assemblage, published in 2018, concluded explicitly that the traditional model of steadily intensifying "pre-domestication cultivation," in which gathered diversity narrows in an orderly fashion toward wild-type cereals and then toward domesticated ones, does not adequately describe the Chogha Golan sequence as a whole, and that whether the site's wild cereals were being deliberately cultivated throughout the long interval before domesticated emmer became prominent remains genuinely unclear from the plant evidence alone.

The pedagogical value of Chogha Golan lies precisely in this complexity rather than in any tidy, textbook-friendly gradualism. Because the site's sequence is so long and so well documented, it allows specialists to demonstrate that the relationship between wild-plant exploitation and eventual cereal domestication was neither instantaneous nor a simple, one-directional march. What the evidence most securely documents is a prolonged, resilient economy of intensive wild-grass exploitation, sustained across many centuries and quite possibly involving some degree of deliberate management, followed only late in the sequence by a plant economy clearly centered on domesticated-type emmer. Whether the wild cereals exploited throughout the earlier and middle portions of the sequence were being deliberately cultivated in the fuller behavioral sense, as opposed to being intensively but not yet horticulturally managed, remains genuinely uncertain, and a systematic 2018 reanalysis of the site's full plant assemblage concluded specifically that the traditional model of steadily intensifying "pre-domestication cultivation" does not adequately describe this sequence. This pattern still supports, and in some respects sharpens, the distinction introduced in Section 3.1 between cultivation as a behavior and domestication as a biological outcome, but it does so by showing that even the cultivation side of that distinction cannot always be assumed with confidence from a long interval of sustained wild-plant use; the gap between exploitation and domestication at Chogha Golan was long, uneven, and, in its earlier and middle portions, not yet clearly a story of deliberate cultivation at all.

Chogha Golan's location in the western Zagros foothills, at a somewhat lower elevation than Ganj Dareh and adjacent to the Khuzestan lowlands examined in Section 3.9, also illustrates the environmental diversity within the broader Zagros zone discussed in Section 3.4. The site's plant economy, emphasizing a genuinely wide range of wild taxa for most of its occupation before narrowing toward domesticated-type emmer late in the sequence, differs in both emphasis and pace from the goat-centered economy documented at the higher-elevation site of Ganj Dareh, even though both sites developed within the same broad Holocene climatic transition and the same wider Zagros Neolithic mosaic.

Together, Ganj Dareh and Chogha Golan illustrate a central theme of this chapter: no single site captures the full range of Zagros Neolithic developments, and comparing sites emphasizing different resources, animal management in one case, plant cultivation in the other, is essential to appreciating the varied, regionally specific, and often non-linear character of the wider transformation.

The way archaeobotanists actually work with a sequence like Chogha Golan's deserves closer explanation, because the site's significance depends entirely on the quality and resolution of this comparative method. Excavators recover soil samples systematically from each identified occupation layer, or horizon, proceeding from the deepest, and therefore earliest, layers upward through the sequence to the latest. Each sample undergoes flotation, a technique in which soil is gently agitated in water so that light, charred plant remains, including seeds, chaff, and other plant fragments preserved through carbonization, float to the surface and can be skimmed off and collected separately from the heavier soil matrix, while denser materials such as unburned soil and small stones sink and are typically discarded or set aside for other analysis. The recovered charred plant remains from each horizon are then sorted, identified to species or genus where possible, and quantified, allowing specialists to compare the relative proportions of different plant taxa, and, crucially, the proportion of tough-rachis versus brittle-rachis cereal remains, from one horizon to the next up through the sequence. It is precisely this fine-grained, horizon-by-horizon comparison that allowed the 2018 reanalysis to document the long persistence of a diverse wild-grass economy, including abundant wild barley, across most of the sequence, and to identify the late timing of the shift toward domesticated-type emmer, a level of detail that a coarser, less systematically sampled sequence could not have revealed.

This stratigraphic method also allows specialists to estimate, with appropriate caution given the uncertainties involved in radiocarbon dating and site formation processes, roughly how many centuries separate the earliest evidence for intensive wild-plant exploitation at the site from the horizons showing the clearest and most consistent evidence for domesticated-type emmer. The resulting estimate, spanning more than a millennium between the earliest intensive wild-plant exploitation and the late emergence of consistently domesticated-type emmer, provides some of the most direct chronological evidence available anywhere in Southwest Asia for just how slow, and how genuinely prolonged, the underlying process of cereal domestication actually was, reinforcing this chapter's central caution against imagining domestication as a rapid discovery rather than a gradual, unevenly documented outcome of long-term wild-plant use and, quite possibly, management.

Section 3.9

The Southwestern Lowlands: Deh Luran and Khuzestan

Moving from the Zagros highlands and piedmont into the southwestern lowlands introduced geographically in Chapter 1 brings a different environmental setting and, correspondingly, a somewhat different pattern of early Neolithic settlement. This lowland zone includes both the Deh Luran Plain, now within Ilam Province and immediately adjacent to Khuzestan, and the broader Khuzestan plain proper that Chapter 4 will examine in connection with Susa. Rivers and seasonal watercourses descending from the Zagros offered opportunities for cultivation not entirely dependent on rainfall alone, though, as this section discusses further below, the specific form this cultivation took in the period examined by this chapter was considerably more modest than the irrigation systems that would later develop in this same general region and in adjacent Mesopotamia. At the same time, the lowland environment presented its own challenges, including summer heat considerably more intense than in the Zagros highlands and a different, generally less mountainous, range of locally available wild animal species.

Ali Kosh, on the Deh Luran Plain, provides this chapter's principal case study for lowland Neolithic settlement. Recent re-excavation and chronological reassessment place the site's main Neolithic sequence at approximately 7500 to 6500 BCE, a considerably more precise dating than older, broader estimates once suggested, and one that reveals an instructive internal structure to the site's early occupation. The earliest recognized phase, generally called the Boz Mordeh phase and dated to approximately 7500 to 7350 BCE, appears on current evidence to represent seasonal rather than fully year-round occupation, a finding directly relevant to this chapter's insistence that sedentism and food production did not necessarily arrive together. Only in the subsequent phase, generally called the Ali Kosh phase and dated to approximately 7350 to 7000 BCE, does the evidence indicate increasing sedentism, alongside increasing reliance on sheep and on goats that by this point show clearer evidence of full domestication. This sequence suggests that at least some of the domesticated or semi-domesticated plants and animals discussed earlier in this chapter as Zagros highland developments had, by the time of the site's Boz Mordeh phase, already begun moving into the lowland zone, whether through the movement of people, the exchange of livestock and seed stock, or some combination of both, well before the community occupying the site settled into the more fully sedentary pattern documented in the following phase.

Architecturally, Ali Kosh's early levels show relatively modest mudbrick structures, with evidence for rebuilding and modification increasing across the site's occupation sequence, particularly from the Ali Kosh phase onward, broadly comparable, in general pattern if not in specific detail, to the architectural continuity documented at Ganj Dareh. This textbook does not treat Ali Kosh as a direct offshoot or colony of any single highland site; rather, the lowland Neolithic economy documented there appears to reflect a combination of local adaptation to the Deh Luran environment and ongoing contact with the highland communities examined earlier in this chapter, a relationship examined further in Section 3.11's discussion of exchange networks.

The lowland environmental regime that Ali Kosh's inhabitants confronted differed from the Zagros highlands and piedmont in ways that shaped subsistence strategy directly. Summer temperatures on the Deh Luran Plain regularly exceed those of the Zagros highlands by a considerable margin, and rainfall, while adequate in winter and spring for some dry farming in favorable years, is generally less reliable and less abundant than the precipitation received by the higher-elevation Zagros zones discussed in Section 3.3. This made proximity to a dependable water source considerably more important for lowland communities than for many highland communities. Cultivation in this period is best understood as taking advantage of seasonally wet, marsh-margin, and floodplain conditions, land that received natural seasonal flooding or retained moisture near watercourses long enough to support crops without requiring constructed irrigation works, rather than as anything resembling deliberate irrigation agriculture in the fuller sense of channels, canals, or controlled water diversion. The more developed irrigation systems sometimes associated with this general region belong to later periods, beyond the Neolithic sequence examined in this chapter, and should not be read backward onto the Boz Mordeh or Ali Kosh phases.

Ali Kosh's occupation sequence shows a gradual shift in subsistence emphasis over time, consistent with the phase divisions described above. The Boz Mordeh phase shows a subsistence mix weighted toward the exploitation of locally available wild resources, including fish and waterfowl associated with the site's setting near seasonal water, alongside cultivated and wild cereals and legumes and only limited evidence for managed livestock. The subsequent Ali Kosh phase shows an increasing role for sheep and for goats showing clearer evidence of domestication, alongside continued plant cultivation and continued, if reduced, exploitation of wild lowland resources. This sequence suggests that the site's inhabitants adjusted their subsistence strategy and their degree of residential permanence together, but not simultaneously from the outset, drawing more heavily on managed livestock and settling more fully into one location only as that combination became more established and reliable.

The archaeological question of how domesticated plants and animals first reached the Deh Luran and Khuzestan lowlands, whether through in-place local domestication from wild lowland populations, through the movement of already partially managed seed stock and livestock from Zagros communities into the lowlands, through the movement of people themselves, or through some combination of these processes, remains genuinely open and is unlikely to be resolved by evidence from Ali Kosh alone. Wild goat populations were not equally abundant in the lowland environment compared to the Zagros highlands, which makes an independent, fully local origin for lowland goat management somewhat less straightforward to argue than for cereals and legumes, whose wild relatives were more widely distributed across both zones. The most cautious position available from current evidence is that the Deh Luran and Khuzestan Neolithic economy developed through significant interaction with, and likely partial dependence on, the highland Zagros developments examined earlier in this chapter, while also involving local adaptation to a lowland environment that highland communities did not need to address, a combination of inheritance and innovation that this textbook's discussion of exchange networks in Section 3.11 helps to contextualize further.

This section deliberately stops short of narrating the fuller history of Khuzestan settlement that Chapter 4 will provide. Its purpose is to establish that by the period this chapter examines, village-based food production, arriving in a staged sequence that separated seasonal use from full sedentism, had extended beyond the Zagros highlands into the Deh Luran and Khuzestan lowland zone where, as Chapter 4 will show, some of the region's first larger and more socially differentiated settlements, including early Susa, eventually developed. Students should carry forward from this section the basic understanding that this lowland world's Neolithic villages, including Ali Kosh, developed within the same broad mosaic of food production traced throughout this chapter, adapted to a lowland environment whose agricultural potential Chapter 4 will show becoming increasingly significant, and that food production and sedentism, even here, did not arrive as a single, simultaneous package.

Section 3.10

Village Life, Houses, Storage, and Community

The developments examined so far in this chapter, cultivation, animal management, and the geographic spread of these practices across the Zagros and into Khuzestan, cannot be fully understood without attention to the social changes that accompanied increasingly permanent settlement. Living in the same location year after year, in structures substantial enough to be rebuilt repeatedly rather than abandoned, changed the texture of daily life and the relationships among households in ways that a narrow focus on subsistence economy alone would miss.

Architecture itself provides important evidence. The progression at Ganj Dareh from simpler early structures to more substantial rectangular, multi-room mudbrick buildings, rebuilt repeatedly on the same footprint across generations, suggests a developing relationship between particular households and particular physical locations. Rebuilding on the same spot, rather than shifting to a new location with each generation, implies both practical continuity, reusing established foundations and orientation, and a social or symbolic investment in place that differs meaningfully from the more mobile, less architecturally permanent patterns of the preceding Epipaleolithic.

Storage represents another significant social development. Communities cultivating cereals and legumes needed to store surplus harvest between growing seasons, and archaeological evidence for storage features, including storage pits and, at some sites, possible storage rooms or structures, indicates investment in preserving food beyond immediate consumption needs. Stored food created a durable reserve whose access, allocation, and protection had to be socially organized. Depending on where storage was located and how it was used, such reserves could have been controlled primarily by households, shared more broadly, or governed through arrangements that archaeology can only partially reconstruct.

Cooperative labor likely became increasingly important as communities invested more heavily in cultivation, construction, and animal management, all of which benefit from coordinated effort beyond what a single household could easily provide alone. Construction of substantial mudbrick or chineh buildings, management of larger goat herds, and coordinated harvesting of cereal fields all plausibly required cooperation extending beyond the immediate household, though the precise social mechanisms organizing such cooperation, kinship obligation, reciprocal exchange, or some other arrangement, remain difficult to reconstruct directly from archaeological evidence alone.

Burial practices, including the household-associated burials documented at Ganj Dareh, provide another line of evidence for developing social relationships. Burying the dead in close association with domestic structures, rather than in separate, more distant locations, suggests an evolving relationship between households, their ancestors, and their claims to particular places, a pattern that, while it should not be overinterpreted as evidence for formal inheritance systems or property law in any developed sense, does suggest growing social and perhaps symbolic investment in the connection between specific families and specific locations.

Closely related to storage is the question of food sharing and allocation, an aspect of Neolithic social life that deserves direct attention rather than remaining implicit in the discussion of storage alone. Once a household or community accumulates a store of grain or controls access to a managed herd, questions inevitably arise about who may draw on that store, under what circumstances, and with what obligations attached. Archaeological evidence bears on this question only indirectly, since the social norms governing allocation and obligation do not fossilize in any direct sense, but the physical evidence for storage itself offers some guidance, though that guidance is more limited than it might first appear. At sites where storage features are plausibly associated with individual domestic structures, they may indicate that households exercised some immediate control over stored resources. At Ganj Dareh, for example, small cubicle-like spaces within some structures have been interpreted by excavators as possible storage areas, but this evidence does not by itself establish formal household property rights over stored food, nor does it exclude the possibility that such reserves were shared more widely under arrangements archaeology cannot directly recover. The archaeological question is therefore better framed as one of access and allocation, who could draw on a given store, under what circumstances, rather than as a simple inference of private ownership from the presence of a storage feature. Communities in which storage was concentrated at the household level plausibly depended on some form of reciprocal sharing, lending, or obligation between households in years of uneven harvest or herd loss, an arrangement well documented ethnographically in many small-scale agricultural societies but one that this chapter's archaeological evidence can only suggest rather than confirm directly for the specific communities under discussion. Seasonal surplus, when it occurred, may have created or reinforced relationships of exchange and mutual obligation between households and, potentially, between settlements, a plausible but not directly demonstrable extension of the exchange networks examined in Section 3.11.

Increasingly permanent settlement also raised a set of practical and biological consequences that archaeology can trace only partially but that deserve mention as genuine, if open, questions rather than settled conclusions. Communities remaining in one location year after year, rather than periodically moving away from accumulated waste and its associated pests, faced new challenges of waste accumulation and sanitation that mobile foraging communities encountered to a lesser degree. Closer and more sustained daily contact with managed animals, living near or even within areas of human habitation, plausibly altered patterns of disease exposure between human and animal populations in ways that specialists studying the broader history of zoonotic disease, disease transmitted between animals and humans, have connected to the wider Southwest Asian Neolithic transition, though tracing specific disease consequences at individual sites such as Ganj Dareh or Ali Kosh remains difficult given the limitations of skeletal and other paleopathological evidence from this period. Workload likely shifted in character rather than simply increasing or decreasing in some single direction: labor associated with mobile foraging, including long-distance travel and the processing of a broad range of wild resources, gave way in part to labor associated with cultivation, herd management, construction, and food processing, activities with their own demanding, and in some cases seasonally concentrated, labor requirements. Childhood and household labor almost certainly played some role in this economy, as they do in most small-scale agricultural societies documented ethnographically and historically, though direct archaeological evidence for the specific tasks performed by children in Zagros or Khuzestan Neolithic households remains limited. Finally, some specialists have proposed that increasingly permanent settlement and a more reliable, if not necessarily more varied or nutritious, food supply may have had demographic consequences, including changes in birth spacing and population growth rates, though this remains an active area of research and debate rather than an established conclusion, and this textbook treats it as a genuinely open question raised by the archaeology rather than as a settled fact to be presented without qualification.

Students should approach the question of social differentiation with particular caution. Some early Neolithic sites across Southwest Asia show modest variation in house size, burial treatment, or access to particular goods that some archaeologists interpret as early evidence for emerging social distinctions among households. The Zagros and southwestern lowland evidence examined in this chapter does not provide strong grounds for asserting developed social hierarchy, formal leadership institutions, or economic classes of the kind Chapter 4 will examine in the context of larger, more differentiated settlements. The most defensible position is that early Neolithic village societies in this chapter's geographic focus may indicate emerging household-level economic differentiation, alongside the social investments associated with permanent settlement, without yet showing convincing evidence for the kind of formalized social and political hierarchy associated with the complex societies examined in the following chapter.

Section 3.11

Networks, Exchange, and Movement

Increasing settlement permanence did not mean increasing isolation. One of the clearest lines of evidence against any assumption that early Neolithic villages were self-contained, inward-looking communities is the presence, at sites across the Zagros and Khuzestan, of materials that could only have arrived through exchange or direct movement across considerable distances.

Obsidian, a volcanic glass prized for its ability to be worked into extremely sharp cutting edges, provides the clearest example. Obsidian sources suitable for tool production are geologically limited and occur at specific locations, including sources in eastern Anatolia, well outside the Zagros and Khuzestan region examined in this chapter. The presence of obsidian artifacts at sites within this chapter's geographic focus, though it must be interpreted with appropriate caution regarding specific source attributions and exact quantities at any individual site, demonstrates that materials, and very likely the people, information, and other goods that accompanied their movement, traveled across substantial distances during the period this chapter examines.

Establishing exactly where a particular obsidian artifact came from is itself a specialized archaeological method worth explaining directly. Different volcanic sources produce obsidian with distinctive trace-element chemical signatures, reflecting the specific geological conditions under which each source's obsidian formed. Using techniques such as neutron activation analysis or X-ray fluorescence, specialists can measure these trace-element signatures in an excavated obsidian artifact and compare the results against a reference library of chemically characterized samples collected directly from known geological sources. Where a close chemical match is found, this geochemical sourcing allows archaeologists to identify, with a good degree of confidence, which specific source, and therefore which general region, supplied a particular piece of obsidian recovered many hundreds of kilometers away at a Zagros or Khuzestan site. This method has shown that obsidian reaching sites in this chapter's geographic focus derived from more than one Anatolian source, indicating that Zagros and Khuzestan communities were connected, whether directly or through intermediary communities, to more than a single distant supply network rather than a single fixed trade route.

Understanding how such material actually moved requires distinguishing between two general models of exchange that archaeologists use, often in combination, to interpret sourced material. Direct procurement describes a scenario in which members of a community, or specialized individuals traveling on the community's behalf, journeyed to the source region themselves to acquire raw obsidian, an arrangement that becomes progressively less plausible as the distance between findspot and source increases. Down-the-line exchange describes a scenario in which material passed through a series of intermediary communities, each trading small quantities onward to neighboring groups, so that a piece of obsidian could travel a very long total distance from its geological source without any single individual or community making the full journey. Given the considerable distances separating known Anatolian obsidian sources from Zagros and Khuzestan sites, many specialists favor down-the-line exchange, or some combination of the two models operating at different stages of a longer route, as the more plausible general mechanism. It is important to be clear about what geochemical sourcing can and cannot establish here: chemical analysis reliably identifies the geological origin of a given piece of obsidian, but it does not by itself reveal the social mechanism, whether direct travel, chained exchange between neighbors, periodic gatherings, kinship networks, or some combination of these, by which that piece of obsidian actually traveled from its source to the site where archaeologists recovered it. That social dimension must be inferred from other evidence, including the relative abundance and consistency of a given source's material across sites at increasing distances from it, and it remains considerably more difficult to reconstruct in detail than the sourcing itself.

Exchange networks of this kind likely carried more than finished obsidian tools or raw obsidian nodules. Marine shell, discussed briefly above, moved through comparable mechanisms from coastal sources toward inland communities. Livestock and seed stock, whose movement between the Zagros highlands and the Deh Luran and Khuzestan lowlands was discussed as a live archaeological question in Section 3.9, may have moved along some of the same general routes and through some of the same general social mechanisms as obsidian and shell, even though domesticated plants and animals leave a different, and in some ways less directly traceable, kind of evidence than a chemically sourceable stone artifact. Networks that moved materials also created opportunities for the movement of information, people, livestock, and seed stock, although the archaeological record is much better at tracing sourceable objects such as obsidian than at reconstructing the specific social ties through which knowledge or breeding stock moved.

Marine shell ornaments, when they occur at sites well inland from any coastline, provide comparable evidence for long-distance connections, whether through direct travel, exchange chains passing through multiple intermediate communities, or some combination of both. Similarly, certain stone materials used for tool production, when their geological sources can be identified and shown to lie outside the immediate vicinity of a settlement, offer further evidence for the movement of materials across the landscape.

Zagros passes, intermontane valleys, and piedmont routes provide plausible corridors through which such movements could have linked highland, foothill, and lowland communities, including Ganj Dareh, Chogha Golan, and Ali Kosh, but the exact itineraries and social mechanisms of most exchanges cannot be reconstructed directly from the artifactual evidence alone.

Students should also resist assuming that increasing sedentism meant that all members of every community became permanently immobile. Even communities with substantial, repeatedly rebuilt architecture may have continued to engage in seasonal movement for specific purposes, including herding livestock to seasonal pasture, a pattern with clear later parallels in the transhumant pastoralism examined in later chapters, or travel connected to exchange relationships with other communities. The archaeological evidence for continued mobility alongside increasing settlement permanence is more difficult to document directly than evidence for architecture or storage, but the presence of exchanged materials at settled sites makes clear that mobility of some kind, whether of people, goods, or both, remained an important feature of Neolithic life even as villages themselves became more permanent.

Section 3.12

From Neolithic Villages toward Complex Societies

By the later centuries examined in this chapter, communities across the Zagros and Khuzestan region had developed, in varying combinations and at varying rates depending on local environment and history, several interrelated characteristics: cultivated cereals and legumes, in some cases showing clear morphological domestication and in others still predominantly wild-type but behaviorally cultivated; managed and, in the case of Zagros goats, increasingly domesticated animal populations; increasingly permanent, frequently rebuilt architecture; dedicated storage features reflecting growing investment in preserving surplus food; participation in exchange networks moving obsidian and other materials across considerable distances; and social patterns, including household-associated burial and probable cooperative labor arrangements, suggesting a developing relationship between specific households and specific places.

This chapter closes by stating plainly what these developments do not, by themselves, establish. None of the changes examined in this chapter automatically produced cities, writing, centralized government, formal social classes, or the other institutional hallmarks of what is conventionally called "civilization." A village of several dozen households practicing cultivation and animal management, however architecturally substantial and however well connected to distant exchange networks, remains a fundamentally different kind of society from the larger, more differentiated, and more institutionally complex settlements examined in Chapter 4.

The developments traced in this chapter should be understood as important enabling conditions within the particular historical trajectories examined in Chapter 4, not as sufficient causes or universal prerequisites of political complexity. Cultivation, animal management, storage, more persistent settlement, exchange, and accumulated ecological knowledge could support larger and more differentiated communities, but they did not by themselves require such communities to emerge. The specific processes by which some settlements expanded, specialized, and developed more formal institutions, including the specialized craft production, more pronounced social differentiation, and eventually the institutional and administrative structures associated with early complex society and, still later, with the civilization of Elam, require additional explanation that this chapter has deliberately not attempted to provide.

Chapter 4 accordingly opens by asking a question this chapter has set up but not answered: how did some of the village societies examined here, particularly in the Deh Luran and Khuzestan lowlands where Ali Kosh and comparable sites developed, grow into the larger, more complex settlements from which Susa and eventually Elamite civilization emerged? Answering that question will require attention to processes, population growth, craft specialization, expanding social differentiation, and the beginnings of more formal community-wide organization, that lie beyond this chapter's scope but that depend directly on the subsistence and settlement foundations this chapter has established.

Historical Vocabulary
The Neolithic Package

Definition: A conventional archaeological shorthand referring to the combination of traits, including cultivated plants, domesticated or managed animals, ground stone tools, permanent architecture, and eventually pottery, often associated together with "the Neolithic." The term is a descriptive convenience rather than evidence that these traits appeared together, in fixed combination, at any single time or place.

Historical context: Early twentieth century archaeology sometimes treated the Neolithic package as a coherent bundle adopted more or less as a unit, spreading outward from a point of origin. The Zagros and Khuzestan evidence examined in this chapter, including the gap between cultivation and domestication documented at Chogha Golan and the Pre-Pottery Neolithic architecture at Ganj Dareh, shows that these elements in fact developed at different rates, in different sequences, and in different combinations across different sites and regions.

Why it matters: Treating the Neolithic package as a fixed, simultaneous bundle risks obscuring the very regional diversity and gradualism that this chapter's mosaic model, discussed in Section 3.4, is designed to capture. Students should use the term cautiously, as a broad organizing label rather than a claim about how these traits actually developed together.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that the presence of one element of the package, such as permanent architecture, implies the presence of all the others, such as fully domesticated plants and animals or pottery, at the same site or period.

Historical Vocabulary
Cultivation versus Domestication

Definition: Cultivation is specifically a plant-management behavior: the deliberate planting, tending, and harvesting of a plant population. Animal management, or herding, is the analogous behavior applied to animal populations: deliberate control over a herd's movement, feeding, and reproduction. Domestication, whether of a plant or an animal population, is a multigenerational biological process produced by sustained human management and selection through which the managed population becomes increasingly differentiated from its wild ancestors; genetic and morphological signals of this process need not appear at the same time, and behavioral management typically precedes both.

Historical context: The long occupation sequence at Chogha Golan, examined in Section 3.8, documents prolonged, intensive exploitation and possible management of wild plants, but whether its wild cereals were deliberately cultivated throughout this long interval remains uncertain; its most secure evidence is for a resilient wild-grass economy sustained across many centuries, followed only late in the sequence by a plant economy clearly dominated by domesticated-type emmer. A comparable but more clearly staged gap separates the onset of goat management at Ganj Dareh, examined in Section 3.7, from the point at which Zagros goat populations show measurable genetic domestic affinity, and, later still, from the point at which they display the classic morphological changes traditionally used to mark full domestication.

Why it matters: Collapsing cultivation and domestication into a single idea of "farming" obscures one of the most important findings of recent Southwest Asian archaeology: that the behavioral and biological dimensions of the Neolithic transformation unfolded on different timescales, with cultivation and management typically preceding domestication, in the strict biological sense, by a considerable margin.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that a site showing evidence of cultivation behavior, such as sickle gloss or weed-species assemblages, has necessarily achieved plant or animal domestication in the genetic sense, or that the absence of clear morphological domestication at an early site means that no meaningful management or cultivation was occurring there.

Creator: Zooarchaeological specialists analyzing animal bone assemblages recovered during excavation at Ganj Dareh · Date: analysis published across several decades following the site's excavation · Type: Quantitative zooarchaeological report presenting age and sex data for goat remains recovered from successive occupation layers

Context: Zooarchaeological reports of this kind present detailed statistical breakdowns of the animal bones recovered from a site's excavated deposits, typically organized by species, skeletal element, and, where determinable, the age and sex of the individual animal represented. No specific report is quoted verbatim here; the paraphrase below summarizes the kind of evidence and argument such reports typically present regarding Ganj Dareh's goat assemblage.

Paraphrase: Analysis of the goat bones recovered from Ganj Dareh's occupation layers found a marked overrepresentation of young male individuals among the animals killed, alongside a comparatively higher survival of adult females, a demographic pattern that specialists have interpreted as consistent with deliberate herd management rather than opportunistic hunting of a wild population.

Analysis: This kind of quantitative faunal evidence exemplifies the archaeological method described in this chapter's Archaeological Method feature: rather than relying on skeletal morphology alone, which does not clearly distinguish early managed populations from wild ones, zooarchaeologists use demographic patterns in the kill assemblage to infer human control over a population's reproduction and composition.

Limitations: A kill-off pattern, however suggestive, depends on adequate sample size and can be influenced by factors including seasonal hunting strategies and the specific depositional history of the excavated deposits. Specialists continue to refine and, in some cases, debate the interpretation of Ganj Dareh's faunal data as analytical methods and comparative datasets from other sites have developed.

Perspective: The report represents a specialist zooarchaeological perspective built from statistical analysis of physical remains; it cannot by itself recover the perspective, decisions, or knowledge of the ancient herders whose activities produced the assemblage.

Historical Debate

One Origin or a Mosaic? Interpreting the Southwest Asian Neolithic

The question: Did food production in Southwest Asia, including the Zagros region examined in this chapter, originate at a single especially favorable location and subsequently diffuse outward, or did it emerge through multiple, partly independent processes occurring in different regions at broadly overlapping times?

Interpretation A — Core-area and diffusion models: Several distinct twentieth century proposals located agriculture's origins in one especially favorable zone. An early "oasis hypothesis" pointed to shrinking water sources at the end of the Pleistocene; a subsequent and highly influential "hilly flanks" model, associated with Robert Braidwood, instead located the natural habitat of the key wild species, and therefore the most plausible origin zone, within the Taurus and Zagros foothills specifically, motivating Braidwood's own excavations at Jarmo in the Zagros. Later scholarship sometimes shifted emphasis toward the Levant as a primary center from which cultivation practices and, eventually, domesticated stock spread outward to surrounding regions including the Zagros. These models differ considerably among themselves and should not be treated as a single unified position.
Interpretation B — A mosaic, multi-regional model: More recent scholarship, informed heavily by evidence from sites such as Ganj Dareh and Chogha Golan, argues that communities across a broad Southwest Asian zone, including the Zagros, independently and semi-independently developed elements of the food-producing economy at broadly overlapping times, with regional variation in which plants and animals received emphasis and in the pace and even direction of that development, reflecting a protracted mosaic of locally rooted processes rather than a single point of origin.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on overlapping archaeobotanical and zooarchaeological evidence from sites across the wider region. The mosaic interpretation draws particular strength from the chronological depth and detail of sites such as Chogha Golan, whose long sequence documents sustained exploitation of a diverse wild-grass economy followed only late by the clear emergence of domesticated-type emmer, while leaving deliberate cultivation of the earlier wild cereals uncertain, rather than showing either a sudden appearance of an already-domesticated package or a smooth, steadily narrowing curve, and from the strong, locally specific evidence for early goat management and staged genetic domestication at Ganj Dareh.

Points of disagreement: Specialists continue to debate the relative weight that should be given to independent local innovation, contact and exchange between neighboring regions, and, in later phases, the movement of already domesticated plants and animals between regions. These are not always mutually exclusive processes, and much current research focuses on distinguishing local domestication from local adoption of already-domesticated stock introduced through contact, as well as on how many genuinely independent domestication events occurred within the broader mosaic.

Why it matters: Adopting a mosaic interpretation changes how this textbook, and Iranian and Southwest Asian archaeology more broadly, narrates the origins of food production: it restores active historical agency to Zagros communities rather than treating them as passive recipients of innovation from elsewhere, while also cautioning against overstating any single Zagros site as an exclusive point of origin, and against expecting the underlying archaeobotanical and zooarchaeological evidence to show a tidy, uniform trajectory.

Student task: Using the evidence from Ganj Dareh and Chogha Golan presented in this chapter, construct an argument for why the mosaic model better accounts for this evidence than a simple core-area diffusion model, while noting what a diffusion model would still need to explain, and what a strict mosaic model must still account for regarding contact and exchange between regions.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Camp to Village: What Changed and What Continued

What Changed

  • Architecture shifted from the more ephemeral traces associated with Epipaleolithic sites toward substantial, repeatedly rebuilt mudbrick and chineh structures at sites such as Ganj Dareh and Ali Kosh
  • Subsistence broadened from intensive foraging toward increasing reliance on cultivated plants and managed animals, though foraging and hunting continued to supplement these new practices throughout the period this chapter examines
  • Storage investment increased considerably as communities sought to preserve surplus cultivated food between growing seasons
  • Cereal and, in the Zagros, goat populations gradually acquired the genetic and morphological traits associated with full domestication

What Stayed the Same

  • Hunting and gathering of wild resources continued alongside cultivation and animal management throughout the period this chapter examines, rather than disappearing once food production began
  • Exchange of materials such as obsidian across considerable distances continued, and in some respects intensified, as settlements became more permanent
  • Communities remained organized primarily at the household and small-village level, without the specialized labor, formal hierarchy, or centralized institutions examined in Chapter 4
  • Regional environmental diversity, already significant in the Epipaleolithic, continued to shape different subsistence emphases across the Zagros highlands, piedmont, and Khuzestan lowlands

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity prevents the common error of treating the Neolithic as a total replacement of an earlier "hunter-gatherer" way of life with a wholly new "farming" way of life. The reality this chapter documents is a long period of layered addition and gradual reorientation, in which older subsistence strategies persisted alongside, and remained entangled with, newly developing ones.

Cause and Effect

Why Settlement Became More Permanent

Long-term causes

Holocene climatic amelioration expanding the range and reliability of wild cereal stands and suitable habitat for wild goats and sheep, combined with accumulated Epipaleolithic knowledge of these resources' life cycles and habitats.

Short-term causes

Specific local decisions by households and communities to intensify cultivation of particular cereal and legume stands and to manage particular local goat and sheep populations more closely, decisions that, repeated and reinforced across generations at particular locations, favored continued reoccupation of those locations.

Effect

Communities increasingly invested in durable, repeatedly rebuilt architecture, dedicated storage, and long-term relationships between specific households and specific places, producing the village settlement pattern documented at Ganj Dareh, Chogha Golan, and Ali Kosh.

This chapter's response

Careful attention to the archaeological methods, kill-off patterns, archaeobotanical morphology, and architectural sequences, that allow historians to trace this gradual process without overstating the speed, uniformity, or inevitability of the transition.

Historical complexity: Increased settlement permanence was not simply a passive response to improved environmental conditions. It reflected active, ongoing choices by households and communities about where to invest labor in construction, storage, and resource management, choices that, once made and repeated across generations, became self-reinforcing even though no single decision determined the eventual outcome.

Everyday Life and Social History

Daily Life in an Early Zagros or Khuzestan Village

The developments examined in this chapter were experienced first and most directly not by any distant observer but by the ordinary households whose daily labor produced them. A household near Ganj Dareh might have spent portions of the year moving goats to seasonal pasture, monitoring herd composition, and making the ongoing, largely unconscious decisions about which animals to slaughter and which to retain that, over many generations, produced the kill-off patterns zooarchaeologists now study. A household at or near Chogha Golan might have spent much of the growing season across many generations harvesting and processing diverse wild grasses, including wild barley, with stone-bladed sickles, and, in later phases of the site's long occupation, perhaps tending or cultivating some plant populations, including the emmer wheat that eventually came to dominate the site's late plant economy, more deliberately.

Construction and maintenance of mudbrick or chineh housing required significant labor, likely organized at the household level but plausibly involving cooperation among neighboring households for larger tasks. Food processing, including grinding cereal grain, a labor-intensive task attested archaeologically through the ground stone tools recovered from Neolithic sites across the Zagros and Khuzestan, would have occupied considerable daily time, most likely falling disproportionately on particular members of each household according to social patterns that are difficult to reconstruct directly from the material record but that later ethnographic and historical evidence, discussed cautiously and by analogy rather than direct projection, suggests often fell along gendered lines in comparable subsistence economies. Because no written sources survive from this period, historians must reconstruct this daily labor and its social organization indirectly, through careful analysis of tools, food remains, architecture, and burial evidence, always remaining alert to the limits of what such indirect evidence can establish with confidence.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the transformation, conventionally called the Neolithic Revolution, through which some communities of the Zagros Mountains and the Khuzestan lowlands developed cultivation, animal management, and increasingly permanent village settlement over the course of several thousand years, beginning from the intensified late Epipaleolithic patterns examined in Chapter 2. It opened by insisting on a set of conceptual distinctions essential to understanding this transformation accurately: cultivation is not domestication, animal management is not fully domesticated livestock, sedentism is not agriculture, village residence is not political complexity, and pottery does not mark the true beginning of Neolithic developments, a point reflected in archaeologists' use of the term Pre-Pottery Neolithic.

The chapter situated this transformation within the environmental context of the Pleistocene-to-Holocene transition, explaining that improving climatic conditions expanded the range of subsistence possibilities available to Zagros and Khuzestan communities without determining that any particular community would pursue cultivation or animal management, a point reinforced throughout the chapter's insistence on avoiding environmental determinism.

Students were introduced to the mosaic model of agricultural origins, which much current scholarship favors over older core-area diffusion models in explaining how food production developed across Southwest Asia, while specialists continue to debate its details. The chapter traced this historiography through several distinct stages, from Childe's oasis hypothesis, through Braidwood's hilly flanks model, which directed sustained attention toward the Taurus-Zagros zone rather than marginalizing it, to later core-area and diffusion arguments and, eventually, to increasingly protracted, multi-regional models. Rather than a single point of invention spreading outward, this current scholarship understands the Zagros region, alongside the Levant, Anatolia, and other zones, as one of several areas where communities independently and semi-independently developed elements of the food-producing economy at broadly overlapping times, adapted to local environments and locally available plant and animal species.

The chapter examined in detail how archaeologists identify early animal management, through the analysis of kill-off patterns in faunal assemblages, and early plant cultivation, through archaeobotanical analysis of seed morphology and associated weed species, emphasizing that both forms of evidence require careful, cumulative interpretation rather than yielding immediate, unambiguous proof of domestication.

Ganj Dareh, in the central Zagros, served as this chapter's central case study for early goat management, showing a kill-off pattern consistent with deliberate herd management, genetic evidence for domestic affinity by approximately 8200 BCE, and, later still, the classic morphological changes traditionally associated with domestication, alongside a tightly dated sequence of occupation levels including the intensively burned Level D. Chogha Golan, in the western Zagros foothills of Ilam Province, served as this chapter's central case study for long-term plant management, its unusually long occupation sequence documenting prolonged exploitation of diverse wild grasses followed only late by the clear emergence and growing importance of domesticated-type emmer, illustrating why intensive wild-plant exploitation, deliberate cultivation, and biological domestication must be distinguished rather than treated as a single continuous process. Ali Kosh, on the Deh Luran Plain adjacent to Khuzestan, extended this chapter's account into the lowland environment central to Chapter 4, showing a staged sequence in which a seasonal Boz Mordeh phase preceded an increasingly sedentary Ali Kosh phase with growing reliance on sheep and fully domesticated goats.

Beyond subsistence, the chapter examined the social dimensions of increasingly permanent settlement, including architectural continuity across generations, growing investment in food storage, probable cooperative labor arrangements, and household-associated burial practices suggesting a developing relationship between specific families and specific places. The chapter urged caution regarding claims of developed social hierarchy or formal political leadership, arguing that the evidence from this chapter's geographic focus may indicate emerging household-level economic differentiation without yet supporting the kind of institutional complexity examined in Chapter 4.

The chapter also demonstrated that increasing settlement permanence did not mean isolation, using the presence of obsidian and other exchanged materials at Zagros and Khuzestan sites to show that these communities remained connected to distant sources and, plausibly, to continued patterns of seasonal mobility even as their settlements themselves became more architecturally fixed.

Finally, the chapter closed by identifying, and explicitly declining to explain, the further developments that separate the village societies examined here from the complex societies Chapter 4 will address: population growth, craft specialization, more pronounced social differentiation, and the beginnings of formal community-wide organization. These developments depend on the subsistence and settlement foundations this chapter has established, but they required additional historical processes that lie beyond a chapter whose deliberate purpose was to explain how communities moved from mobile foraging to settled, food-producing village life, and no further.

These conceptual distinctions, archaeological methods, and case studies together prepare students to approach Chapter 4's account of Susa and the rise of complex societies with an accurate understanding of the village world from which that later, more differentiated society emerged, and with the interpretive caution appropriate to evidence this fragmentary and this dependent on careful specialist analysis.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Neolithic
A broad archaeological period term for the cultural and subsistence developments associated with cultivation, animal management, and increasingly permanent settlement, understood in this chapter as a gradual, multi-stranded, and regionally uneven transformation rather than a single event.
Pre-Pottery Neolithic
The earlier portion of the Neolithic period, prior to the regular adoption of ceramic technology, during which cultivation, animal management, and substantial architecture developed at sites such as Ganj Dareh and Ali Kosh.
Cultivation
The plant-management behavior of deliberately planting, tending, and harvesting a plant population, distinguished in this chapter from domestication, the biological process such behavior can, over many generations, help produce.
Domestication
A multigenerational biological process under sustained human management and selection that increasingly differentiates a managed population from its wild ancestors; genetic and morphological signals need not appear at the same time.
Sedentism
The practice of remaining settled in one location for most or all of the year, distinguished in this chapter from agriculture, since communities could be sedentary without full reliance on cultivation, or mobile while practicing some cultivation.
Mosaic model
An interpretive framework holding that food production in Southwest Asia emerged through multiple, partly independent, regionally varied processes rather than a single point of origin followed by outward diffusion.
Holocene
The current geological epoch, conventionally beginning around 9700 BCE, associated with warmer and, in much of Southwest Asia, wetter conditions than the preceding late Pleistocene.
Pleistocene
The preceding geological epoch, examined in Chapter 2, encompassing the Paleolithic and Epipaleolithic periods of human occupation on the Iranian plateau.
Faunal assemblage
The collection of animal bone remains recovered from an archaeological site, analyzed by zooarchaeologists to reconstruct hunting, herding, and domestication.
Archaeobotany
The archaeological subfield concerned with recovering and analyzing plant remains, especially charred seeds, to reconstruct ancient plant use, cultivation, and domestication.
Zooarchaeology
The archaeological subfield concerned with recovering and analyzing animal bone assemblages to reconstruct hunting, herding, and domestication.
Kill-off pattern
The age and sex distribution of animals represented in a faunal assemblage, used by zooarchaeologists to infer whether an assemblage reflects opportunistic hunting or deliberate herd management.
Morphological domestication
Physical, measurable changes in a plant or animal population, such as tough versus brittle cereal rachis or reduced goat body size, that develop as a result of sustained human selection and management.
Founder crops
The traditional model grouping early cultivated plants, including wheat, barley, and various pulses, as a central cluster in the early Southwest Asian agricultural economy; a useful organizing label rather than a fixed or universally applicable archaeological package.
Managed herd
An animal population whose movement, feeding, and reproduction are subject to sustained human control, a stage that can precede full morphological domestication by a considerable period.
Ganj Dareh
An early village site in the central Zagros, central to this chapter's discussion of early goat management and Pre-Pottery Neolithic architecture.
Chogha Golan
A long-occupied site in the western Zagros foothills of Ilam Province, central to this chapter's discussion of long-term, non-linear plant management and eventual domestication.
Ali Kosh
An early village site on the Deh Luran Plain, adjacent to Khuzestan, central to this chapter's discussion of a staged sequence in which seasonal occupation preceded full sedentism.
Sheikh-e Abad
A comparative Zagros Neolithic site referenced alongside Ganj Dareh for evidence of early village life.
Obsidian exchange
The long-distance movement of volcanic glass, sourced from locations such as eastern Anatolia, used as evidence that Zagros and Khuzestan Neolithic communities remained connected to distant regions.
Household storage
Dedicated features, including pits and possible storage rooms, reflecting investment in preserving food and seed reserves and raising questions about household versus community access and allocation.
Rectangular architecture
The increasingly common building form, seen at Ganj Dareh's later levels, associated with repeated rebuilding on the same footprint across generations.
Sickle gloss
A distinctive polish on stone sickle blades produced by repeated contact with silica-rich cereal stems, used as evidence for cereal harvesting.
Environmental determinism
The flawed interpretive approach, cautioned against throughout this chapter, of treating climatic or environmental change as automatically producing cultivation, domestication, or sedentism.
Tell
An archaeological mound formed by successive phases of mudbrick construction, occupation, and rebuilding at the same location over long periods, a formation process visible in the architectural sequences at sites such as Ganj Dareh and Ali Kosh.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has traced one of the most consequential, and most frequently oversimplified, transformations in human history as it unfolded specifically on the Iranian plateau and in adjacent Khuzestan. The habits of mind it introduces, distinguishing cultivation from domestication, resisting environmental determinism, reading archaeological evidence with appropriate methodological caution, and recognizing regional diversity rather than assuming a single point of origin, are not narrow technical skills relevant only to Neolithic archaeology. They are ways of evaluating historical claims and evidence that should inform the study of every subsequent chapter in this textbook.

The Neolithic transformation examined here made possible nearly everything the remainder of this textbook will examine. The cultivated cereals and legumes, the managed and increasingly domesticated goats and sheep, and the increasingly permanent village settlements documented at Ganj Dareh, Chogha Golan, and Ali Kosh created the subsistence and demographic foundations upon which the larger, more differentiated societies of Chapter 4 could eventually develop, and upon which, many centuries later, the complex agrarian economies of Elam, the Achaemenid Empire, and every subsequent Iranian state would ultimately depend.

Yet this chapter has deliberately avoided treating the Neolithic transformation as a story with a predetermined destination. The communities at Ganj Dareh managing their goat herds, and the communities at Chogha Golan gradually reshaping their cereal stands through generations of cultivation, could not have known that their accumulated practices would, many centuries later, underlie the emergence of cities, states, and eventually empires. Reading this chapter's evidence honestly requires resisting the temptation to write history backward from known later outcomes, and instead attending closely to what the archaeological record can and cannot establish about how ordinary Zagros and Khuzestan households actually lived, worked, and gradually transformed their relationship to the plants, animals, and landscapes around them.

Students who understand the gradual, uneven, regionally varied character of this transformation, who can distinguish cultivation from domestication and animal management from full domestication, and who appreciate the careful archaeological methods that allow historians to make any claims about this period at all, are well prepared to move forward into Chapter 4's examination of how some of these village societies grew into the larger and more complex settlements from which the recorded history of Elam and, eventually, of Iran itself would emerge.
