Islamization without Arabization: Religion and Iranian Identity

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

Iran did not become Muslim in a single generation, and becoming Muslim did not mean becoming Arab. Across several centuries, families, villages, cities, and regions adopted Islam at different speeds and for different reasons, while Iranian languages, customs, and memories continued, changed, and were reinvented within the new religious order.

Consider a historian trying to answer a deceptively simple question: when did the people of Iran become Muslim? The conquest of the Sasanian Empire, narrated in Chapter 28, was largely complete by the middle of the seventh century. Yet no surviving source records a moment when a society of several million people changed religion. What survives instead is a scattering of evidence: legal treatises written in Iraq, biographical dictionaries compiled generations later, local histories written in the tenth century and after, Middle Persian books composed by Zoroastrian priests, the records of Christian and Jewish communities, coins, inscriptions, and the foundations of mosques. Each reveals a fragment of a process that was gradual, uneven, and experienced very differently by a landholder in Fars, a merchant in Nishapur, a peasant household in the Caspian highlands, and a Sogdian townsman in Bukhara.

This chapter treats three processes that are easily confused. Political conquest was the establishment of Arab-Muslim rule over former Sasanian territories, a matter of decades. Islamization was the adoption of Islam by individuals, families, and communities, a matter of generations and, in some regions, centuries. Arabization was the spread of the Arabic language and of Arab tribal identity. Arabic became the language of much administration, religious scholarship, and law, as Chapters 31 and 33 explain, but it did not replace the Iranian languages of ordinary life, and most Iranian converts did not become Arabs in any ethnic or linguistic sense. The chapter's title names the central finding: Iranians, in large numbers, became Muslims without ceasing to be Iranians.

That finding does not mean that conversion was painless, voluntary, or free of cost. Non-Muslims paid a tax from which Muslims were exempt and lived under legal inequalities. Converts faced social ambiguity as clients, or mawālī, of Arab patrons. Religious communities that had held institutional authority under the Sasanians lost it. Some movements of resistance ended in massacre. At the same time, many people converted for reasons of conviction, family, friendship, advancement, or the attraction of a community with a new moral and legal vocabulary. The chapter avoids both a narrative of forced conversion and a narrative of effortless, voluntary acceptance. Neither is supported by the evidence.

The chapter also resists a second simplification: that Iranian identity and Shiʿism were bound together from the start. In the eighth, ninth, and tenth centuries, the Muslims of Iran included Sunni jurists and traditionists, Murjiʾite thinkers, Kharijite communities, several currents of Alid loyalism, and a majority who had no firm sectarian label at all, alongside Zoroastrians, Christians, Jews, Buddhists, and Manichaeans. The close association of Iranian state identity with Twelver Shiʿism belongs to the Safavid era, discussed in Chapter 58, and is not a feature of the period studied here.

The evidence is difficult, and the chapter treats it as part of the subject. Most narrative sources were written in Arabic or New Persian, by Muslims, often long after the events they describe. Zoroastrian priestly writing survives chiefly from the ninth and tenth centuries and reflects a clerical perspective. Sources for women, children, villagers, and the poor are very scarce. Where the chapter offers a reconstruction of ordinary life, it says so. Where it reports conversion narratives from later local histories, it distinguishes them from contemporary evidence.

The chapter's boundaries follow the textbook's division of labor. The administrative and fiscal history of the Abbasid state, and the careers of Iranian secretaries, belong to Chapter 31 and appear here only where they affected religious change. The literary and intellectual history of Arabic and New Persian belongs to Chapter 33. The cultural program of the Samanids is reserved for Chapter 35, the later Sunni and Shiʿi religious landscape for Chapter 40, and Ismailism and the reconstruction of historical memory through epic poetry for Chapters 41 and 42. This chapter supplies the religious and social foundation on which those chapters build. As Chapter 1 argued, historical Iran extended beyond the borders of the modern state, and this chapter follows conversion across Fars, the Jibal, the Caspian highlands, Khurasan, Sistan, and Transoxiana.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Distinguish political conquest, Islamization, and Arabization, and explain why they were neither identical nor simultaneous.
  2. Describe the religious diversity of Iran at the end of Sasanian rule, including Zoroastrian, Christian, Jewish, Manichaean, Buddhist, and regional traditions.
  3. Explain the concepts of dhimma, jizya, and kharāj, and distinguish legal prescription from historical practice.
  4. Evaluate the evidence for the chronology of conversion, including Richard Bulliet's reconstruction and its limitations.
  5. Analyze the plural motives for conversion, rejecting any single explanation.
  6. Compare urban and rural patterns of Islamization and the roles of mosques, courts, and landholding families.
  7. Contrast regional patterns in Fars, the Jibal, the Caspian highlands, Khurasan, Sistan, and Transoxiana.
  8. Trace the institutional adaptation and literary activity of Zoroastrian communities and the experience of other non-Muslim communities.
  9. Describe the early formation of Sunni legal and theological communities and of Alid and Shiʿi loyalties without projecting later orthodoxies backward.
  10. Interpret religious movements of resistance and the hostile sources that describe them.
  11. Assess how Muslim Iranians preserved and reworked language, custom, and memory, and explain Shuʿubiyya as one expression of that process.
  12. Connect the chapter's findings to Chapter 33's treatment of language and intellectual life.

Historical Context

The Sasanian Empire, examined in Chapters 22 through 27, was a state with a strong tradition of royal and priestly Zoroastrianism, but it governed a population that was religiously mixed. Zoroastrian priests and fire temples held institutional prominence, especially in Fars and the Iranian heartland, while the Church of the East was established in Mesopotamia and Khuzestan, Jewish communities lived in Mesopotamia and on the plateau, and Buddhist and Manichaean communities were present in the east. The empire's final decades, marked by the wars with Byzantium and civil conflict, weakened the political structure that had supported Zoroastrian institutions, as Chapter 27 explained.

The Arab-Muslim conquest, treated in Chapter 28, did not proceed as a single campaign. Regions were taken, lost, and retaken over decades, some through negotiated surrender and tribute agreements, others by force. In many regions, local rulers, landowners, and religious leaders reached terms with Muslim commanders that left local administration and religious life largely intact in exchange for tribute. Arab-Muslim settlement concentrated in particular garrison cities, Merv in Khurasan being the best known, and in Iraq, with smaller settlements elsewhere, as Chapter 29 described for the Umayyad period. The revolution of 747–750 that brought the Abbasids to power, narrated in Chapter 30, drew on Khurasani support, and the Abbasid caliphate then integrated Iranian elites into its administration, as Chapter 31 showed.

This history sets up the problem of the present chapter. By the end of the Umayyad period, Muslim rule was established across Iran, but the population of the plateau and the east remained overwhelmingly non-Muslim in the sense that matters for conversion history. Over the following centuries, a Muslim majority took shape. The evidence for how this occurred is fragmentary and is mostly literary. For that reason, this chapter proceeds by examining the categories of evidence, the regional variation they reveal, and the arguments scholars have built on them, rather than offering a single narrative.

Two further points of context matter. First, the Islamic religion itself was still being defined during the period under study. Law, theology, the collection of the Prophet's sayings, and the boundaries between communities developed alongside conversion. Iranian Muslims were participants in this process, not only recipients of it. Second, Iran's inhabitants were not a single people. They spoke Persian and other western Iranian dialects, Sogdian, Bactrian, Khwarazmian, Kurdish and other northwestern Iranian languages, Aramaic and Syriac, and Arabic, and they lived in very different ecological and political settings, as Chapter 1 established.

Section 32.1

Conquest Was Not Conversion: Three Different Historical Processes

Popular accounts often compress the seventh-century conquest and the Islamization of Iran into a single event, as though the defeat of the Sasanians produced a Muslim society. The evidence points the other way. The conquest was a political and military process that placed Muslim commanders and garrisons in authority over Sasanian territories, usually in return for tribute and the recognition of Muslim sovereignty. Muslim rulers had no systematic program to convert the conquered, and the early fiscal system in fact rested on the existence of a large non-Muslim population that paid taxes. A state that depended on tribute from non-Muslims had little financial reason to encourage rapid conversion, a tension that recurs throughout this chapter.

Islamization, by contrast, was a social process carried out by individuals and communities over generations. In the earliest period, becoming Muslim was not a single clearly defined act. A declaration of faith was central, but recognition by the community, participation in prayer, and attachment to a patron could all bear on whether a convert was accepted, and sources show disputes over such questions. A society, for its part, became Muslim only through the accumulated decisions of many families, shaped by local circumstances. Historians therefore speak of conversion not as an event but as a diffusion across populations, with early adopters, a long middle period, and late adopters whose communities might retain older religious identities for centuries. Some urban districts of Iran may have had Muslim majorities by the ninth century; many regions reached that point in the tenth or later, and for the countryside the date is largely unknown; and Zoroastrian and other minority communities persisted in several places well beyond that, as the later sections show.

Arabization was a third process, partly independent of the other two. Arabic became the language of the caliphal administration in the late seventh and early eighth centuries, a development treated in Chapter 31, and the language of Islamic law and religious scholarship. A convert who wished to take part in prayer, study, or legal life had reason to learn some Arabic. But Arabization in the sense of a shift of everyday language and ethnic identity occurred in Iraq, Syria, and parts of Khuzestan, not across the Iranian plateau. In most of Iran, Persian and other Iranian languages remained the languages of home, village, and market, and New Persian emerged as a written language in the ninth and tenth centuries, a development reserved for Chapters 33 and 35.

The distinction matters analytically because the three processes had different causes and different timelines. Conquest depended on military and political factors; Islamization on a complicated mix of social, economic, and religious motives; Arabization on administrative and scholarly needs. A convert might Islamize without Arabizing, and a speaker of Arabic might be Christian or Jewish. Keeping the processes separate allows the chapter to avoid two common errors: assuming that the Iranian experience was a forced replacement of one culture by another, and assuming that the persistence of Iranian culture implies the persistence of Iranian religion.

The demographic geography of the conquest reinforces the point. Muslim settlement in Iran was far thinner than Muslim political reach. The principal concentrations of Arab settlers were in garrison cities, above all Merv and other centers of Khurasan, and in the Iraqi cities of Basra and Kufa, which sent out many of the conquering forces, as Chapter 29 described. Elsewhere on the plateau Arab communities were small. In the Caspian highlands and parts of the east, Muslim authority was for long periods exercised from a distance and sometimes barely at all. A population that rarely meets Muslims has few occasions for conversion, and that simple fact is part of the reason the political conquest and the religious transformation were separated by generations. Even where Arab settlers were numerous, as in Khurasan, they did not stand apart from local society for long. Intermarriage, shared economic interests, and the use of local languages drew many settler families into Iranian-speaking society by the later Umayyad period, so that the movement of cultural influence ran in both directions.

Section 32.2

The Religious Landscape of Iran after the Sasanian Collapse

The religious world that the conquerors encountered was diverse. Zoroastrianism was the tradition most closely associated with the Sasanian monarchy, with a priesthood, a network of fire temples, a body of ritual and legal practice, and a body of sacred and commentarial literature preserved largely in oral transmission and, later, in Middle Persian books. Its institutional weight was greatest in Fars and the western plateau, though its local forms varied, and historians caution that "Zoroastrianism" in practice included a range of regional ritual and belief traditions rather than a uniform orthodoxy. The collapse of the Sasanian state removed royal patronage, but the priestly and temple structures continued to operate at local levels.

Christianity was represented chiefly by the Church of the East, the East Syriac church that had developed within Sasanian territory and maintained dioceses in Mesopotamia, Khuzestan, Fars, and the east, including Merv. Other Christian communities, including Miaphysite groups, were also present in some regions. Jewish communities lived in Mesopotamia and on the plateau, with significant populations in Khuzestan and Isfahan and elsewhere. Manichaeism, a missionary religion founded by Mani in the third century, had been persecuted under the Sasanians but survived, especially in Central Asia and Sogdiana, where it enjoyed considerable following. Buddhism was established in the far east of the Iranian world, notably in Balkh and Bamiyan and parts of Transoxiana, with monasteries that were also centers of landholding and learning.

Beyond these named traditions lay a range of regional and popular religious practice that the sources describe poorly. Local cults, shrines, and festivals persisted across the Iranian world, and in the Caspian highlands and other regions, communities followed local traditions that outsiders found hard to classify. The sources, mostly written by outsiders who were Muslim or by the clerical elites of the main religions, tend to flatten this diversity into a handful of named categories. Students should keep in mind that the labels in the sources are less precise than the lived religious worlds they describe.

The key point for this chapter is that Islam entered a landscape that was already religiously plural. Iranians had long experience of living alongside communities of different faiths, and the Sasanian state itself had at times persecuted and at times tolerated its minorities. The new order introduced a new ruling religion and a new legal framework for minorities, but it did not introduce religious diversity into a uniform society. Conversion to Islam therefore occurred as one of several religious choices available within an already complex field, and the survival of non-Muslim communities in Iran for centuries was not an anomaly but a continuation of the pre-Islamic pattern.

The evidence for this landscape is itself uneven, and it is worth knowing what each body of evidence can and cannot show. Chapter 24 examined Zoroastrian institutions and the religious communities of late antique Iran in detail, and its sources, notably the Middle Persian books, are overwhelmingly clerical. The Church of the East left synodical records, saints' lives, and chronicles in Syriac that document dioceses and bishops from Mesopotamia into Fars and Khurasan. The Babylonian Talmud and related rabbinic literature illuminate Jewish communal life in Mesopotamia, though far less on the plateau. In the east, the Chinese Buddhist pilgrim Xuanzang, who passed through Balkh in the 630s on his way to India, described large Buddhist monastic establishments there, while Manichaean texts in Middle Persian, Parthian, and Sogdian, recovered chiefly from the oasis sites of Central Asia, preserve the literature of a missionary church. All of these sources tell us about institutions, learned elites, and sacred texts. They tell us much less about the beliefs of an ordinary farmer in the Zagros or a Sogdian artisan, whose religion probably combined elements that no clerical text would classify neatly. When this chapter speaks of a community as "Zoroastrian" or "Christian," it is using the sources' categories, and students should remember that the categories are coarser than the lives they describe.

Section 32.3

Non-Muslims under Islamic Rule: Protection, Taxation, and Inequality

The Islamic legal framework for non-Muslims who were "People of the Book" rested on the concept of dhimma, a pact of protection under which Jews, Christians, and, in practice, Zoroastrians and others were permitted to retain their religion and communal institutions in exchange for payment of a tax and acceptance of Muslim political authority. The Quran refers to the payment of jizya by People of the Book (9:29), and later jurists developed detailed rules concerning who paid, how much, and what restrictions applied. In the early conquest period, however, arrangements were often negotiated locally and varied considerably. Agreements with local rulers and communities specified tribute, and only later did jurists systematize these into a general law of dhimma. The famous "Pact of Umar," a list of restrictions on non-Muslims, was attributed to the second caliph but is generally dated by scholars to a later period.

The earliest evidence for how these arrangements worked comes from the surrender agreements preserved in later Arabic histories, notably al-Baladhuri's Futūḥ al-buldān and al-Tabari's Tārīkh. These works report terms made with the rulers, notables, or communities of regions such as Azerbaijan, Jurjan, and Tabaristan. The texts were transmitted for generations before they were written down, and jurists and historians had reasons to shape them to later legal doctrine, so historians treat them as evidence of a general pattern, tribute in return for security with a measure of religious and local autonomy, and not as verbatim documents. A key feature of the pattern is that tribute was frequently a sum owed by a district or community and gathered through its own leaders. In regions that never fully submitted, the arrangement could amount to little more than an acknowledgment of Muslim sovereignty and the periodic delivery of money. The Ispahbad rulers of Tabaristan, for example, kept control of their mountain territory until the Abbasid campaigns of the 750s and 760s, and their local coinage, which kept Pahlavi legends, shows how much of the older order survived in the interval.

Documents that survive from the far east give a rare glimpse of the system from within. Sogdian documents found at Mount Mugh, in the upper Zarafshan valley, date from about 722 and belong to the last independent ruler of Panjikent, Dewashtich, who withdrew to a mountain fortress as Arab forces advanced and was captured and executed. The Mugh archive contains letters and legal and economic documents written in Sogdian, and it shows a local ruling family administering land, disputes, and obligations in its own language and idiom while Muslim armies operated in the region. Bactrian documents from northern Afghanistan, written in the seventh and eighth centuries and published in modern editions, similarly reveal local lords, landholdings, and obligations persisting under the new overlords. Neither group of documents describes religious policy directly, but together they establish a point that the legal treatises obscure: for decades after conquest, the practical machinery of local administration, including the assessment and delivery of taxes, was in the hands of local elites who operated within familiar frameworks.

Historical Vocabulary
Dhimma, Jizya, and Kharāj

Definition: Dhimma was the pact or covenant of protection extended to non-Muslim communities under Muslim rule; a dhimmī was a non-Muslim protected person. Jizya was a tax paid by non-Muslims, often described as a poll tax, though its early meaning varied. Kharāj was a land tax, and in the early period the word was also used for tribute in general.

Historical context: In the first generations after the conquest, terminology was fluid. Sources use jizya and kharāj for tribute paid by a community, a poll tax on individuals, and a land tax. Only in the later eighth and ninth centuries did jurists such as those writing the legal treatises discussed below separate the categories more clearly.

Why it matters: The distinction between the poll tax and the land tax helps explain conversion. Conversion could in principle relieve an individual of the poll tax, but the land continued to bear the land tax according to later legal doctrine, so that a convert who owned land might gain less fiscal relief than is often assumed.

Potential misunderstanding: Dhimma status was not a single uniform condition. Rates, enforcement, and social restrictions varied by period, region, and local agreement, and the surviving legal texts describe norms rather than documenting uniform practice.

The fiscal burden on non-Muslims is a key part of the story, but it should not be exaggerated into a general explanation of conversion. Rates varied, sometimes by wealth, and in many places the tax was collected collectively through community leaders rather than from each individual. Communities with strong leaders could negotiate or resist. Equally, the legal and social restrictions on non-Muslims, including limits on building new places of worship and on bearing arms, and the inferior legal status of non-Muslim testimony in some matters, were applied unevenly. Historians therefore distinguish between the legal ideal, as described in treatises, and the practice, which must be reconstructed from scattered administrative and narrative evidence.

The question of who counted as a protected person was also a matter of legal argument, not simply of Quranic text. The Quran speaks of "People of the Book," which jurists understood to include Jews and Christians. Zoroastrians, called majūs in Arabic sources, presented a difficulty because their scripture and practice did not fit the category neatly. Most jurists resolved the problem by extending the status of dhimmī to them on the strength of a tradition ascribed to the Prophet that they should be treated like the People of the Book, and this reasoning gave Zoroastrian communities a recognized legal place. The status did not extend to every group. Religions regarded as polytheist, and groups accused of dualist heresy, could not claim the same protection, as Section 32.12 shows for the Manichaeans.

Restrictions on dhimmīs developed unevenly and were often enforced at the initiative of individual rulers. In 850 the caliph al-Mutawakkil issued a decree requiring distinctive dress and imposing other limits on non-Muslims, an episode reported in several chronicles. The very fact that such measures had to be decreed in the middle of the ninth century suggests that earlier practice had been less uniform than the later legal rules imply, and the fact that they had to be repeated in later reigns suggests that enforcement was inconsistent. Historians therefore treat the long list of restrictions attributed to the early caliphs with caution. It is a record of what jurists believed a proper Islamic order required, and of what some rulers at some moments tried to impose, and it cannot be read as a description of what a Zoroastrian villager in Fars or a Christian merchant in Merv encountered in a given year.

Primary Source Window

Abu Yusuf, Kitāb al-Kharāj (Book of Land Tax), late eighth century CE

Creator: Abu Yusuf Yaʿqub (d. 798), a leading Hanafi jurist and chief judge under the caliph Harun al-Rashid · Date: composed in the later eighth century · Type: Legal and fiscal treatise addressed to the caliph

Context: The work is a response to the caliph's request for guidance on taxation, land, and the treatment of subjects. It was composed in Iraq, at the center of the Abbasid state, by a jurist writing from within the Muslim legal tradition. It is addressed to the ruler and combines legal argument with advice on just administration.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): Abu Yusuf discusses the taxation of non-Muslim subjects at length. He treats the poll tax as a levy that should be assessed according to the capacity of the payer, with higher rates for the wealthy and lower for the poor, and he warns officials against oppressive collection and against imposing burdens that subjects cannot bear. He appeals to precedent from the early caliphs and to ideals of just governance in advising the ruler.

Analysis: The text shows how a leading jurist at the Abbasid court understood the proper treatment of non-Muslims in the later eighth century. It reveals that tax collection was a subject of legal and ethical argument, that abuses were a concern the jurist felt compelled to address, and that the ideal of assessing the poll tax by capacity was already established in elite legal thinking. It also shows how the law was framed through reference to early authorities.

Limitations: The text states a legal ideal and advice. It cannot establish how often officials followed it, how taxes were actually collected in Fars or Khurasan, or what ordinary dhimmīs experienced. A jurist's warnings against abuse also suggest that abuses occurred, but they do not measure how common they were. The treatise concerns the Abbasid heartland and says little about specific Iranian regions.

Perspective: The perspective is that of a Muslim legal scholar serving the ruling power. The voices of non-Muslim taxpayers, local community leaders, and tax collectors are not represented.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the source say about the proper assessment of the poll tax?
  2. Who created it, for whom, and in what circumstances?
  3. What purposes might have shaped Abu Yusuf's advice to the caliph?
  4. What can historians learn about elite attitudes toward non-Muslim subjects from this source?
  5. What cannot be established about actual enforcement in Iranian regions from this source alone?

In sum, non-Muslims in Iran lived under an arrangement that protected their religious life while imposing fiscal and legal inequality, and the degree of inequality varied widely. This arrangement provided a context in which conversion could be attractive without being compulsory. It also meant that non-Muslim communities, including Zoroastrians, had a legal basis for continued existence, which helps to explain why many persisted long after the conquest.

Section 32.4

When Did Iranians Become Muslim? Evidence and the Problem of Conversion Rates

No census, register of conversions, or contemporary population count survives that would allow historians to state the proportion of Muslims in Iran in any given decade. The evidence is indirect. Historians use biographical dictionaries, which record the names of scholars, jurists, and transmitters of the Prophet's sayings; genealogical information in those dictionaries; local histories of cities such as Nishapur, Isfahan, and Bukhara; chronicles that report revolts and events; legal and fiscal texts; Zoroastrian religious writing; coins and inscriptions; the archaeology of mosques and other religious buildings; and geographical works by tenth-century authors who describe the religious composition of regions. Each source type has its own bias. Biographical dictionaries record the learned, who were disproportionately urban and male; chronicles favor political events; local histories were often written generations after the events they narrate.

Some of these sources survive in forms that are themselves problems. The history of Nishapur by al-Hakim al-Nisaburi (d. 1014), the major source for that city's scholars, survives mainly in an abridged Persian version, and Narshakhi's history of Bukhara, as the source study below explains, passed through a later redaction. Tenth-century geographers such as al-Istakhri, Ibn Hawqal, and al-Muqaddasi describe the religious composition of regions in passing, noting, for instance, the continued presence of fire temples in Fars, but they were travelers and compilers reporting what they saw or were told in particular places, and their remarks cannot be converted into proportions. Numismatic evidence is valuable for a different reason. The earliest Muslim coinage in Iran copied Sasanian types, retaining Middle Persian legends and adding short Arabic religious phrases, which shows the new rulers adapting inherited forms before gradually replacing them. Coins demonstrate the pace of political and administrative change. They do not indicate how many of the people who used them were Muslim.

The most influential attempt to estimate the pace of conversion is that of Richard Bulliet, in Conversion to Islam in the Medieval Period: An Essay in Quantitative History (1979). Bulliet collected the names of individuals recorded in biographical dictionaries and analyzed the personal names of their ancestors. He reasoned that converts often gave their children distinctly Muslim names, so that the proportion of names with an Islamic character in successive generations could serve as a measure of the proportion of Muslims in the wider population. Plotting the data, he proposed that conversion followed an S-shaped curve: slow at first, accelerating in the middle, and slowing as the remaining non-Muslim population dwindled. For Iran, his curve suggested substantial conversion following the middle of the eighth century, the fastest change in the ninth and early tenth centuries, and a Muslim share that might reach something like four-fifths of the population by about 950. These are outputs of a statistical model applied to urban biographical data, not counts of a population, and Bulliet himself drew the data chiefly from urban converts. This work gave scholars a framework for thinking about conversion as a social diffusion and not only as the result of state policy.

The method rests on a simple chain of reasoning that students can follow step by step. Biographical dictionaries often give a person's name together with his father's and grandfather's, sometimes extending back several generations. Bulliet identified the first ancestor in such a chain who bore a recognizably Muslim name, and treated that generation as the one in which the family converted. Counting converts by the period of their conversion generation, across many families, yielded a distribution over time, and he fitted a curve to it. The reasoning is ingenious, but each step requires an assumption. It assumes that a family's first Muslim name marks the conversion and not merely a later adoption of a prestigious name. It assumes that names were not also used by non-Muslims, and some names of biblical origin, such as Ibrahim or Yusuf, were borne by Jews and Christians as well. It also assumes that families which retained Persian names after conversion, and many did, were not systematically missed. Bulliet's earlier study of Nishapur, The Patricians of Nishapur (1972), had already shown how rich the biographical tradition could be for the history of a single urban elite, and the later book generalized from similar material across the Islamic world.

The reconstruction has limits that scholars have emphasized. The biographical dictionaries on which it relies record a selected population, mostly urban religious scholars, and it is uncertain whether their naming patterns represent villagers, women, or non-scholarly urban families. The assumption that the adoption of Muslim names tracks conversion in a regular way can be questioned, since names might be adopted for social reasons or retained after conversion. The data are also uneven across regions, so the aggregate curve may blur important regional differences. For these reasons, many historians treat Bulliet's curve as a stimulating hypothesis about urban Muslim elites and a rough guide to the chronology of change, not as a precise measure of the whole population. The chapter's Historical Debate below returns to this problem.

What can be said with reasonable confidence, then, falls between the precision of the curve and complete agnosticism. Conversion was slow in the first generations after the conquest outside the garrison cities. It accelerated markedly in the eighth and ninth centuries. By the tenth century Muslims were probably a majority in many cities and in much of the plateau and the east, though not everywhere, and non-Muslim communities remained significant in Fars and in parts of the countryside and highlands. For villages and for the Caspian and Transoxianan highlands, the chronology is uncertain and probably later. Students should hold this bounded conclusion, and the grounds for it, in mind when they meet confident statements about "when Iran became Muslim."

Historical Debate

How Quickly Did Islam Spread, and Can Surviving Sources Establish Conversion Rates?

The question: Can historians reconstruct the pace of Islamization from surviving evidence, and if so, how fast did it proceed?

Interpretation A — A measurable S-shaped curve: Following Bulliet's approach, conversion can be traced through the proportion of Muslim names in successive generations of recorded families, showing slow early conversion, rapid change in a middle phase (for Iran, centered on the ninth and early tenth centuries), and a late slowing.
Interpretation B — The evidence supports only qualified conclusions: Other historians accept that Islamization was gradual and that the ninth and tenth centuries were crucial, but argue that the data represent elite urban scholars, that naming patterns are an uncertain proxy, and that rural populations and regional variation cannot be recovered with the same confidence. They favor a more modest, regionally differentiated account.

Evidence: Both views draw on the same biographical dictionaries, local histories, and chronicles. Neither can rely on population records.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns method and the representativeness of the sources rather than the broad claim that conversion was gradual, uneven, and prolonged.

Why it matters: If the curve is reliable, state policy and the early conquest had less influence on the timing of conversion than social diffusion; if the evidence is more limited, the chronology for villages and distant regions remains open.

Student task: Explain what kind of additional evidence, such as archaeological survey of mosques or rural burial practice, might help test the biographical reconstruction, and why it might yield different answers for towns and villages.

Historiographical Source Study

Narshakhi, History of Bukhara, on the Conversion of a Transoxianan City

Creator: Abu Bakr Muhammad al-Narshakhi · Date: completed around 943–944 (Arabic original, now lost); surviving Persian version abridged and revised in 1128 by Abu Nasr al-Qubavi · Type: local history

Context: The work was written in the Samanid period, roughly two and a half centuries after the events it narrates for the early conquest, and it survives through a later Persian redaction. It describes the history of Bukhara, including its conquest by Arab-Muslim forces and the gradual and repeatedly reversed acceptance of Islam by its inhabitants.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): The account describes repeated reversions of Bukharans to their earlier religion after early conquests, efforts by Muslim commanders to establish the new religion, including the building of a mosque and arrangements for sharing houses between Muslims and locals, and the use of Persian in worship because the locals could not understand Arabic.

Analysis: The narrative is valuable because it preserves local memory of a drawn-out and contested Islamization, with Arab and local actors, concrete institutions such as the mosque, and the persistence of local language in religious practice. It suggests that conversion in a Central Asian city involved negotiation, setbacks, and the accommodation of local culture.

Limitations: It is a later narrative, shaped by a Muslim, Samanid-era perspective and by later redaction. Details of the early events, such as house-sharing arrangements and the dates of reversion, cannot be verified by independent contemporary evidence. Stories of this kind may also serve to explain the origins of local institutions. Historians treat it as evidence of how the Muslim community of Bukhara remembered its past in the tenth century, and as a source for the later conditions it takes for granted, more than as a precise record of the eighth century.

Section 32.5

Why People Converted: Faith, Opportunity, Pressure, and Social Change

Historians who study conversion in Iran emphasize that there was no single reason. Religious conviction was real. Islam offered a monotheistic message, a prophetic scripture, a ritual and moral framework, and a community of believers, and some Iranians were evidently drawn to it as a matter of belief. Contemporary evidence for individual conviction is thin, since sources rarely record why ordinary people converted, but the fact that Iranians became leading participants in Islamic scholarship in the eighth and ninth centuries shows that Islam was taken up as a living faith, not only as a social convenience.

Narratives of conversion among elites give some concrete pictures of how conviction, calculation, and circumstance combined, though their reliability varies. Later Arabic histories report that Hormuzan, a Sasanian commander in Khuzestan who was captured by the Muslim forces, was taken to Medina, accepted Islam, and became an adviser to the caliph Umar. The story is a conquest-era report preserved in later narratives, and its details cannot be verified, but it was told because it illustrated a pattern of cooperation between the conquerors and some members of the old Iranian military aristocracy. For the Abbasid period the evidence is firmer. Families of Iranian background, among them the Barmakids, whose ancestors according to a tradition that some modern scholars question had served the Buddhist monastery of Nawbahar near Balkh, rose to high office in the caliphate after becoming Muslim, and Chapter 31 follows their careers as officials. What the example shows for the present chapter is that conversion and social ascent could go together, which made conversion attractive to ambitious families while leaving open, as it always does, the question of what a particular individual believed.

Social and political opportunity also mattered. Muslims belonged to the ruling community. Access to administrative posts, military careers, and patronage networks, though not formally restricted to Muslims in all cases, was easier for those who shared the religion of the rulers. A landholder or secretary seeking advancement might convert for reasons that combined belief and ambition, and the sources for Iranian elites, discussed in Chapter 31, show families who adopted Islam while retaining their social position. Fiscal considerations played a part, since conversion could reduce the poll tax, but the persistence of the land tax and the limits of tax relief meant that this was not an automatic benefit. Family and community ties shaped decisions: a marriage, a patron, a neighbor, or a village leader who converted could influence others. In some contexts, communal leaders, such as dehqāns, set a pattern that their dependents followed.

The fiscal motive is best documented for Sogdiana in the early eighth century, where the sources, mainly al-Tabari's Tārīkh, describe a dispute that reveals how conversion and taxation interacted. According to these accounts, the Umayyad authorities at first exempted converts from the poll tax in order to win local support, and many Sogdians accepted Islam. Sogdian notables, who lost revenue when their dependents converted, objected, and governors of Khurasan then re-imposed the tax on non-Arabs or demanded proof of sincerity before granting exemption, using tests such as circumcision, ritual prayer, and the recitation of the Quran. The caliph Umar II (r. 717–720) is reported to have ordered that converts be exempt, but the order seems to have had limited effect at such a distance from Syria. Converts protested, some leaders were arrested, and local elites appealed for help to the Türgesh ruler of the steppe, whose forces entered Sogdiana. These reports come from later narratives, and their details and chronology are debated. They still demonstrate three things that matter here: that conversion could carry a fiscal advantage, that officials and local elites had reasons to resist it, and that the benefits of becoming Muslim were contested by the very people who administered the tax system.

Material inducement could take simpler forms. Narshakhi reports that, when the Arab commander Qutayba ibn Muslim built the first mosque in Bukhara in 712–713, inside the citadel and on the site of an earlier temple, he found it necessary to pay local people two dirhams each time they attended Friday prayer. The anecdote, preserved in a text written two centuries after the event, may reflect a tradition shaped by later concerns, but it presents conversion as a process that early Muslim authorities had to cultivate and that local people did not simply embrace. The same history says that the ruling Bukhar Khudahs were, by the middle of the eighth century, at least nominally Muslim but prone to apostasy. Conversion in this period was reversible, and relapse was a social and political fact as well as a religious one.

Historical Vocabulary
Mawlā (plural mawālī)

Definition: A mawlā was a client or associate of an Arab patron or tribe, a status through which non-Arab converts were admitted into Muslim society in the first Islamic centuries.

Historical context: In the Umayyad period, converts generally had to be attached to an Arab tribe as clients. This created a distinction between Arab Muslims and non-Arab Muslims, with disputes over taxation, military pay, and status. The Abbasid Revolution, discussed in Chapter 30, drew on this grievance among others.

Why it matters: The institution explains why early conversion did not automatically equal Arabization: converts were attached to Arabs socially but remained culturally and often linguistically Iranian.

Potential misunderstanding: The term did not describe a single status. It covered freed slaves and free converts, and its social meaning changed over time.

Pressure and coercion occurred in particular circumstances. Narratives of conquest describe the destruction of fire temples, forced mosque construction, and executions in some places, and fiscal and social disabilities created pressure on non-Muslims. But the surviving record does not support a general picture of mass forced conversion by the state. The caliphal government generally preferred stable tribute to conversion, and many accounts of coercion come from later narratives written to glorify conquerors or to explain local institutions. Where documented, coercion was episodic and local, and it coexisted with other motives. The historian's task is to weigh these factors for specific places and periods, not to assign a single cause.

Examples of coercion illustrate why the question cannot be answered in general. The scholar al-Biruni, writing around the year 1000, reports that Qutayba ibn Muslim killed the learned class of Khwarazm and destroyed their records, so that much of the region's pre-Islamic knowledge was lost. Al-Biruni was himself a native of Khwarazm and had reasons to lament that loss, and historians debate how far his account can be taken literally, but it is a rare report by an educated Muslim of the region about destruction of non-Muslim learning in the conquest period. At Bukhara, by contrast, Narshakhi describes a shared-housing arrangement and inducements to prayer, a story of pressure and negotiation more than of massacre. Alongside such local episodes there is the structural pressure of living in a society where legal status, taxation, and social access differed by religion. Historians distinguish these forms of pressure, because open violence against a community, fiscal incentives, and the gradual social cost of remaining outside the dominant community operated differently and affected different people.

Cause and Effect

The Influences on Conversion to Islam in Iran

Long-term causes

The weakening of Sasanian institutions; the establishment of a Muslim ruling class; the legal and fiscal distinctions between Muslims and non-Muslims; and the spread of Muslim institutions into cities and provinces.

Short-term causes

Local conquest settlements; individual career and patronage opportunities; marriages and communal networks; the influence of local leaders and dehqāns; and episodes of fiscal or other pressure.

Event or transformation

A gradual diffusion of Islam across Iranian populations over several centuries, faster in some cities and regions, slower in villages and highlands.

Consequences

A predominantly Muslim Iranian society, with persisting minority communities, a distinctive Muslim Iranian role in Islamic scholarship and culture, and Iranian languages and customs continuing within Islamic frameworks.

Historical complexity: Conviction, opportunity, fiscal calculation, social ties, and pressure interacted, and their relative weights differed for a secretary in Merv, a priest in Fars, and a peasant in Daylam. No single factor, including taxation or coercion, accounts for the whole process.

Section 32.6

Urban Islamization: Cities, Mosques, and New Muslim Communities

Cities were the principal sites of early Islamization because they concentrated the institutions that drew people into Muslim life: congregational mosques, courts, markets, schools, and the offices of the state. Merv, in Khurasan, was a major garrison and administrative center from the early conquests and later served as the base of the caliph al-Maʾmun (r. 813–833) during the years before he moved to Baghdad. Nishapur, Rayy, and Isfahan developed substantial Muslim communities in the eighth and ninth centuries, and biographical and local-history literature about these cities, including the Nishapur biographical tradition that Bulliet studied in his earlier work on its elite families, provides some of the best evidence for urban Muslim society.

The mosque was both a place of worship and a social center. Friday prayer assembled the Muslim community and tied it to the ruler through the sermon. Mosques were often built at the center of cities, sometimes on sites with earlier religious significance, a pattern reported in several local histories and sometimes confirmed by archaeology. Alongside the mosque, the qāḍī, or judge, applied Islamic law to disputes among Muslims and, where appropriate, supervised matters involving non-Muslims. Markets and craft guilds, neighborhood networks, and religious scholars created an urban environment in which Muslim practice was part of daily life. For a city dweller, daily contact with Muslim neighbors, officials, and institutions made conversion easier and the maintenance of a non-Muslim identity more costly.

Urban Islamization did not mean urban homogeneity. Zoroastrian, Jewish, and Christian communities persisted in many cities, often in distinct quarters, as Section 32.12 discusses. Muslim urban populations themselves were diverse, including Arab settlers and their descendants, Iranian converts, and members of different legal and theological currents. Cities also developed competing factions, sometimes along legal-school lines, as Section 32.14 explains. The evidence for cities is also biased toward the educated and the male. We know much more about the transmitters of religious learning than about artisans, women, or the urban poor, whose religious lives have to be inferred.

Bukhara offers the clearest picture of how a mosque entered a city, though it must be read as Narshakhi's later narrative. According to the history, Qutayba ibn Muslim stationed an Arab garrison in the city and required householders to share their dwellings with Arab Muslims, a measure that placed Muslim families inside the neighborhoods of the city. He built a mosque inside the citadel in 712–713 on the site of a temple, and the early congregation had to be encouraged to attend. The account treats the early arrangements as provisional and describes later changes in the city's religious buildings, though its details cannot be checked, and it recalls that, because many Bukharans did not know Arabic, Persian was used in religious practice for a time. The account is not a simple record of events, since it also explains how Bukhara's institutions came to have the form they had in the tenth century, but it captures how conversion in a city was bound up with housing, money, language, and the physical presence of new buildings.

Archaeology offers material evidence for urban Islamic society that complements the texts. The expedition of the Metropolitan Museum of Art at Nishapur, which worked at the site between the 1930s and 1940s, uncovered residential quarters of the ninth and tenth centuries with carved stucco, wall paintings, and a rich assemblage of glazed pottery. The finds show a prosperous urban society with substantial houses, active craft production, and long-distance connections, and they confirm that the city was flourishing in the period when its Muslim scholarly community was at its most visible. They do not tell us who lived in each house or what religion the householders practiced, and the excavation methods of the period leave many questions unanswerable. Archaeology can show the framework of urban life, and the religious identity of its inhabitants must be inferred from texts.

Within these cities, religious life was far from uniform. Nishapur, in particular, had a rich scholarly culture in the ninth and tenth centuries, with major transmitters of the sayings of the Prophet, such as Ishaq ibn Rahwayh, a scholar born in Merv who settled in Nishapur and died in 853. The city was also a center for the ascetic and preaching movement of the Karramiyya, founded by Muhammad ibn Karram, a preacher from Sistan who died in 869, whose followers established communal hospices and attracted a following in Khurasan. From the tenth century at the latest, urban notable families and legal-school affiliations also shaped factional politics, with rivalries between the followers of different schools sometimes breaking into open conflict in the cities of Khurasan and the Jibal. These details show that city life did not simply replace one religious community with another. It produced new forms of religious association, competition, and leadership among Muslims themselves, in addition to bringing non-Muslims into daily contact with Muslim institutions.

Section 32.7

Village Society, Dehqāns, and the Slower Transformation of the Countryside

Most Iranians lived in villages and worked the land, and the evidence suggests that religious change in the countryside generally lagged behind change in the cities. Villages had fewer of the institutions that drew people toward Islam. There were fewer courts, fewer mosques, and less contact with Muslim officials and scholars. Village life was organized around agriculture, irrigation, kinship, and local religious practice, with local shrines and festivals linked to the agricultural year. Where there was a Zoroastrian temple or a Christian church, it served as a communal center. Without regular contact with Muslim institutions, rural families had fewer immediate reasons to change religion, and the cost of changing, in terms of communal ties, could be high.

The geography of settlement affected the pace of change. Many villages in the Iranian plateau depended on irrigation systems, among them underground channels known as qanāts, that required coordinated maintenance and were managed by local landholders and village communities. This dependence on collective management bound villagers into communal and kinship networks that had their own religious dimension, since festivals, rituals, and ceremonies were tied to the agricultural year. In the Caspian lowlands and the mountain valleys, small communities lived at a distance from urban institutions, and the effective authority of the caliphal state was weaker. In these settings the mosque, the judge, and the tax collector arrived late, and sometimes never in the form familiar from the cities. Our evidence for these conditions is, however, mostly indirect and comes from outsiders, and it is more reliable for what was missing than for what was present.

The dehqāns, local landholding families who acted as intermediaries between the state and the villages, had a central role. Under the Sasanians they had held social status and local authority, and under the caliphate many retained that position by managing tax collection and serving as local notables. Chapter 31 discusses their administrative role. For this chapter the point is that their religious choices could shape those of the dependent population around them. A dehqān who converted might bring a village along, but not necessarily; a dehqān who remained Zoroastrian could sustain local temples and priests. In some regions, dehqān families converted relatively early while retaining their inherited social and cultural position, a pattern that contributed to the continuity of Iranian customs within Muslim society.

Sources from Transoxiana give a more detailed view of how landholding elites coped with the new order. The ruling dynasty of Bukhara, the Bukhar Khudahs, remained in place as co-rulers with Arab governors for a time, and their family estates, according to the account summarized in modern scholarship, persisted until the Samanid period, when the land was confiscated and its value paid out in cash. The family was not unified in its response to Muslim rule: some members were killed by Arab commanders, and at least one, Bonyat, is said to have been executed for complicity in the revolt of al-Muqannaʿ, as Section 32.16 discusses. Narshakhi also describes fortified rural residences held by local landowners around Bukhara, which suggests how a landholding class combined local authority with accommodation of the new rulers. The dehqān families that survived in later centuries preserved genealogies and traditions of the Iranian past, a role that becomes important in Chapters 33 and 42. For the present argument, the essential point is that the countryside's local leaders were able to change religion without losing their social position, and that their gradual conversion helped shape a Muslim society that remained recognizably Iranian in its elites and local culture.

Fiscal arrangements could also slow rural conversion. According to the legal doctrine that developed in the eighth and ninth centuries, land continued to bear the land tax whether its owner was Muslim or not, so that a convert farmer who stayed on his land gained less from changing religion than a townsman who paid mainly the poll tax. Conversion in the countryside also carried social risks: a family that broke with its community's practices might lose the networks of labor sharing, marriage, and mutual aid on which its livelihood depended. Against these deterrents, rural populations were also exposed to the influence of mosques built by landholders, to preaching, and to marriage ties with urban Muslim families. The sources do not allow a reliable estimate of how these opposing forces balanced in any village. They do support the general conclusion that change in the countryside was slower and, in the highlands, often later than in the cities.

The evidence for the countryside is thin. Rural populations left few written records, and the surviving sources mostly view villagers through the eyes of urban authors. Historians rely on indirect indicators: references to rural fire temples and churches in tenth-century geographical works, the persistence of Zoroastrian communities in particular districts into later centuries, mosque construction in villages recorded in local histories, and the references in jurists' writings to practical problems of rural communities. These indicators support the view of a slower and more uneven transformation in the countryside, but they do not permit precise chronology. The countryside, like the cities, was not uniform: lowland agricultural districts with strong state presence differed from mountain and highland regions where local authorities and religious practices persisted longer.

Section 32.8

Households, Marriage, Gender, and Everyday Religious Change

Conversion was often a family matter. Religious belonging in the premodern Iranian world was closely tied to household, kin, and community, and the decision of one member to convert had consequences for others. Islamic law, as it developed, regulated marriage across religious lines. A Muslim man could marry a woman who was a Jew or Christian, while a Muslim woman could not marry a non-Muslim man, and children of a Muslim father were considered Muslim. Such rules created channels through which households could become mixed, and over generations, shift toward Islam. The legal treatment of inheritance, which generally restricted inheritance between persons of different religions, added further pressure on families with property.

Some of the most telling evidence concerns practices that conversion would have forced a household to change. Zoroastrian law, as expressed in Middle Persian texts, encouraged marriage between close relatives, a practice the texts call xwēdōdah and treat as meritorious. Islamic law, by contrast, prohibited marriage within a defined set of close relatives, following the Quran (4:23). Muslim writers of the Abbasid period criticized the Zoroastrian practice, and Zoroastrian authors defended it, so it was a recognized point of difference between the communities. How often such marriages actually occurred is unknown, and modern scholars debate how widespread they were. The legal contrast nevertheless shows how conversion could require a household to reorganize its kinship arrangements, not only to adopt new beliefs.

Burial practice offers material evidence of a different kind. Zoroastrian custom called for exposing the dead so that the flesh would be removed by birds and dogs, after which the bones were collected and placed in containers. In Sogdiana and Khwarazm, archaeologists have found numerous ossuaries, called astōdāns, which attest the practice, and in Fars later sources describe towers and enclosures used for exposure. In Sogdiana the use of ossuaries appears to decline over the eighth and ninth centuries, a change usually connected with the spread of Islam, though the precise chronology varies from site to site, and the evidence reflects burial customs rather than belief. Muslim burial, with the body interred whole and the grave oriented to Mecca, was a visible marker of community membership, and a family that began to bury its dead in the Muslim manner announced a new affiliation to its neighbors.

Daily life incorporated religious differences in subtle ways. Dietary customs, the observance of holidays, the burial of the dead, and the use of ritual objects distinguished communities. Zoroastrian practice regarding the treatment of the dead, for instance, differed sharply from Muslim burial, and practices regarding ritual purity shaped contact between communities. Muslims and non-Muslims shared markets, neighborhoods, and festivals in complicated ways. Seasonal festivals such as Nowruz continued to be observed by many communities, as Section 32.17 explains, and the boundaries between religious and customary practice were not always clear to participants.

Slavery and concubinage were another route by which Muslim identity entered households. Captives taken in conquest and later campaigns were enslaved, and enslaved persons who accepted Islam could be freed and attached to a master as clients, becoming mawālī. Their children grew up in Muslim households. Sources also record that Persian women entered the households of Muslim rulers as concubines. The most prominent case is that of Marajil, a woman from Badghis in the northwest of present-day Afghanistan, who became the mother of the caliph al-Maʾmun. Such cases show that the Muslim ruling household itself was shaped by Iranian women, whose influence on the cultural life of the court can be inferred from the later prominence of Persian traditions there, though the sources say little about their own religious choices or lives. The ordinary experience of enslaved and freed persons, whether Iranian or not, is documented mainly through legal texts and elite narratives, and their perspectives are largely absent.

The sources are poor on women, children, and ordinary households. Legal texts address women as subjects of marriage and inheritance law, biographical dictionaries include some learned women, and anecdotal literature occasionally refers to women's piety, but systematic evidence for women's religious choices in Iran is scarce. Historians should therefore be cautious about generalizing, and should recognize that most statements about household religious change are inferences from law, from the later situation, and from comparison with better-documented societies. The following feature offers a reconstruction of this kind, clearly labeled as such.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Hypothetical Village Household in the Ninth Century

This is a hypothetical reconstruction, not a documented case. It combines patterns described in the sources and the scholarship, and no individual person or village is attested.

Imagine a village in the Jibal. The household grows wheat and barley and tends a few animals. The head of the household, a small landholder, pays a land tax through the local dehqān. The family follows Zoroastrian ritual, and a local fire temple lies a short walk away. A relative has moved to a nearby town and become Muslim, in part through a business partnership with a Muslim merchant. When the household's daughter reaches marriage age, the family must consider whether a match with a Muslim family in the town is advantageous. Such a marriage, if it occurred, would raise questions of religion for the children.

Over two or three generations, such connections could draw some branches of the family toward Islam while others stayed with the older religion. Neighbors might share agricultural labor and festivals, while observing different burial customs. The village might eventually have a small mosque, but the temple might persist for generations. What this example illustrates is that the process was incremental, driven by relationships, and uneven even within a family.

Limits of the reconstruction: The sources do not document the decisions of particular rural households. The example is an illustration of plausible mechanisms drawn from law and from the general patterns the sources suggest.

Section 32.9

Regional Patterns I: Fars, Western Iran, and the Caspian Highlands

Regional variation was one of the most important features of Islamization. In Fars, the heartland of the Sasanian dynasty and of Zoroastrian priestly institutions, the Zoroastrian community was large and well organized, and Zoroastrian institutions persisted for centuries after the conquest. Tenth-century geographers describe fire temples in Fars, and Zoroastrian priestly writings of the ninth and tenth centuries, discussed in Section 32.11, were produced here. Muslim communities grew, especially in cities such as Shiraz (founded as a Muslim city in the early Islamic period) and Istakhr, but the Zoroastrian presence remained significant. Fars also had a sizable Christian presence, with dioceses of the Church of the East.

The contrast between Fars and the other regions is not simply one of dates. Fars had been the dynastic homeland of the Sasanians, and its Zoroastrian priesthood was embedded in a landscape of temples, estates, and endowments that had supported it for centuries. Tenth-century geographers, including al-Istakhri, Ibn Hawqal, and al-Muqaddasi, mention fire temples and Zoroastrian communities in the region, and the priestly literature of the ninth and tenth centuries discussed in Section 32.11 reflects a community still able to teach, argue, and write. At the same time Muslim institutions were growing. Shiraz developed as a Muslim city in the early Islamic period, with traditions attributing its foundation to Arab governors in the later seventh century, and grew into a major regional center. The coexistence of a strong Zoroastrian presence with an expanding Muslim city is the typical Fars pattern: not replacement but long overlap, with Muslim institutions concentrated in the new urban centers and Zoroastrian institutions anchored in the older towns and the countryside.

In the Jibal, the central and western Zagros and plateau region including Isfahan, Rayy, Hamadan, and Qom, Muslim communities were established earlier in the larger cities, and Arab settlement was significant in some areas. Qom was settled by Arab Ashʿarites who came from Iraq and became an early center of Shiʿi scholarship, as Section 32.15 notes. Rayy developed into a large and diverse city with Muslim, Zoroastrian, and other communities. Isfahan had a notable Jewish community and a growing Muslim scholarly life. The Jibal shows that even within one broad region, towns could follow different paths.

Qom provides the best-documented example of an Arab settlement becoming a distinct religious community. The main settlement of Arab Ashʿarites from Kufa is placed by specialist scholarship in the early eighth century, following events after 702 and possibly as late as about 713, and it was preceded by smaller Arab settlements. The town's earliest history is known chiefly from a local history by Hasan ibn Muhammad al-Qummi, composed in Arabic in 988–989 and surviving in a Persian translation made in 1402–1404, a reminder that what we know of early Qom has passed through a local Muslim memory and a later translator. Qom's importance as a center of Shiʿi scholarship belongs to the ninth and tenth centuries and should not be projected back onto the original settlement. According to later Shiʿi tradition, a sister of the eighth imam, Fatima, died at Qom in the early ninth century and was buried there, and her shrine became a focus of devotion. The tradition, which cannot be corroborated from contemporary sources, indicates how the town came to be seen within the Shiʿi imagination, and it should be distinguished from the town's documented role as a place where scholars collected and transmitted the sayings attributed to the imams.

The Caspian highlands, Tabaristan and Daylam, offered a different pattern. The mountainous terrain and distance from the center allowed local rulers to resist or limit caliphal authority. In Tabaristan, local dynasties such as the Ispahbads maintained a degree of autonomy after the conquest and the Abbasids did not fully subdue the region until the eighth century. Islamization proceeded later and more slowly in the highlands than on the plateau, and in Daylam, scholars generally place significant conversion in the ninth and tenth centuries, partly through the activity of Alid preachers, a development noted in Section 32.15. These regions illustrate how geography and local political circumstances, rather than any fixed rule, helped determine the timing of religious change. Chapter 1 introduced the distinctiveness of the Caspian littoral, and conversion followed that distinctiveness.

The sequence of events in the Caspian region shows how political history shaped religious history. The Ispahbad dynasty held Tabaristan until the Abbasid conquest of the 750s and 760s, after which local noble houses such as the Qarenids and Bavandids continued to exercise authority in the mountains and to resist direct control. The Qarenid Mazyar, who had himself accepted Islam under the caliph al-Maʾmun, rebelled in 839 and was defeated. In 864 Hasan ibn Zayd, a descendant of the Prophet's family, established a Zaydi state in Tabaristan with local support, and around the turn of the tenth century another Alid leader, Hasan al-Utrush, is credited in the sources with preaching among the Daylamites and bringing many of them to Islam, and he ruled Tabaristan from 914 to 917. This is a case in which conversion was closely tied to a religious and political movement led from outside the local elite, and in which the form of Islam adopted was Shiʿi and Zaydi, not the Sunni forms that predominated on the plateau. The Daylamites later became prominent as soldiers and as founders of the Buyid dynasty, discussed in Chapter 36, which shows how a region once on the margins of the caliphate could become politically central after its own religious and social transformation.

Section 32.10

Regional Patterns II: Khurasan, Sistan, and Transoxiana

The eastern Iranian world presented a different religious environment. Khurasan, a region extending beyond the modern provinces that bear its name, as Chapter 1 explained, had a large Arab-Muslim settlement centered on Merv and other garrison cities, and it became a base of Muslim scholarship and of the Abbasid movement. Khurasan's cities, especially Nishapur, Merv, and Balkh, developed large Muslim populations, and the region was a center of Hanafi and Murjiʾite thought. At the same time, Zoroastrian, Buddhist, Christian, and Manichaean communities remained present, and the countryside likely changed more slowly. Trade routes passing through Khurasan connected it to Central Asia and the Silk Road networks, and merchants were an important channel for the spread of Islam eastward.

The settlement of Khurasan was the largest Arab migration into Iranian lands, and it created a distinctive situation. Arab troops and their families were settled in Merv and other garrison cities in the later seventh century, and by the early eighth century many were integrated into landholding and local affairs, as Chapter 29 explained. Their children, raised in an Iranian-speaking environment, often used Persian, and the Abbasid movement that emerged from Khurasan drew on both Arab and non-Arab supporters, as Chapter 30 showed. The result was that the Muslim community of Khurasan included people of Arab descent who had become deeply local and converts of Iranian descent who participated in the same religious institutions. This local variant of Muslim society, shaped by both groups, is part of the reason Khurasan became a center for the legal, theological, and scholarly currents discussed in Sections 32.13 and 32.14.

Sistan, in the southeast, was a frontier region of Iranian, Afghan, and Central Asian interaction. Its religious life included Zoroastrian communities and, in the ninth century, a significant Kharijite presence. Kharijite movements, which emphasized a strict view of Muslim leadership and community, found followers in Sistan and parts of Khurasan, illustrating that Islam in Iran was not initially a unified tradition. Transoxiana, beyond the Oxus, was a land of Sogdian, Bactrian, and other cultures, with Buddhist, Manichaean, Zoroastrian, and Christian communities. Arab-Muslim conquest there occurred in the early eighth century under commanders such as Qutayba ibn Muslim, and Narshakhi's account of Bukhara, examined above, suggests the conversion was contested and drawn out. Local rulers and elites, such as the Sogdian princes, often kept their positions under Muslim suzerainty, and Islamization advanced alongside their accommodation of the new rulers. By the tenth century, under the Samanids, whose cultural history belongs to Chapter 35, the major cities of Transoxiana had substantial Muslim populations and became centers of Islamic learning.

Buddhism offers the clearest example of an institutionally strong religion in the east whose decline can be traced, if only roughly. Xuanzang's account from the 630s describes substantial monasteries at Balkh, and Arabic sources later describe the Nawbahar, a major Buddhist monastery near the city, as a notable religious complex. The monasteries were also landowners and centers of learning, which meant that their fate depended on political arrangements as well as on belief. Some were damaged in the conquest, and others survived by agreements with Muslim commanders. By the ninth and tenth centuries the Buddhist presence in Balkh and the surrounding region had declined, though Buddhism remained visible in regions farther east and in the monastic landscape of Bamiyan. Beyond the Hindu Kush, rulers of the Zunbil dynasty in Zabulistan continued to resist Muslim authority for generations, until Saffarid campaigns of the 870s, discussed in Chapter 34, reduced their independence. The religion of the east was, in short, a mosaic, and Muslim expansion into it proceeded unevenly, region by region and valley by valley.

Missionary activity played a role in the east. Accounts describe preachers sent to Transoxiana in the early eighth century, and later Muslim merchants and scholars helped spread Islam along trade routes. Political circumstances also mattered. Local rulers who adopted Islam, or who sought alliance with Muslim powers, helped bring their subjects into the Muslim community. In some cases, as in the Sogdian rebellions against Arab rule, conversion and resistance were entangled, since the promise of tax relief for converts and its non-fulfillment became a grievance in the early eighth century according to later chronicles. Historians treat the details of these reports cautiously, but they illustrate the interaction of conversion, taxation, and local politics in the east.

Section 32.11

Zoroastrian Survival, Religious Learning, and Institutional Adaptation

Zoroastrianism did not disappear after the conquest. Priests continued to perform rituals, fire temples operated, and communities maintained religious law and practice. Under Muslim rule, Zoroastrians were generally treated as having a status comparable to that of People of the Book, though this extension involved legal reasoning and local agreement. The community lost state support and the political protection of the Sasanian monarchy, and it was exposed to fiscal and social pressures that encouraged conversion. Yet the tradition showed considerable resilience, especially in Fars, Kerman, and Yazd and in some other districts.

One of the most important developments was the writing of Middle Persian religious books. Although the sacred texts of the Avesta had been transmitted largely orally for centuries, and a commentarial tradition existed in Middle Persian, many of the surviving Middle Persian works in their present form were compiled or edited in the ninth and tenth centuries. These include the encyclopedic Dēnkard, associated with priests such as Adurfarnbag, son of Farroxzad, and Adurbad, son of Emed, as well as the Bundahišn and other compilations. Another work, the Škand-gumānīg wizār by Mardan-Farrukh, is an apologetic text that defends Zoroastrianism against other religions, including Islam, Judaism, Christianity, and Manichaeism. These works show a priestly community actively preserving, systematizing, and defending its tradition in a Muslim-ruled society. They also show engagement with Muslim and other religious arguments, since the Zoroastrian texts respond to external challenges.

A concrete episode illustrates the world these books reflect. A Middle Persian tradition, preserved in Zoroastrian texts, reports a debate held before the caliph al-Maʾmun (r. 813–833) between the Zoroastrian priest Adurfarnbag, son of Farroxzad, and a man named Abalish, who had abandoned the Zoroastrian religion. The account appears in Zoroastrian sources, which present Adurfarnbag as the victor and as a leading spokesman for the community, and Muslim sources do not tell the story in the same terms, so the details cannot be independently confirmed. The episode is nevertheless informative. It suggests that a Zoroastrian priest could address a caliph on theological questions, that apostasy from Zoroastrianism was a live issue within the community, and that religious argument was carried on in an institutional setting that included the caliphal court. The Dēnkard itself, which describes the history of the sacred texts and gathers ritual, legal, and doctrinal material, can be read as a priestly effort to define and preserve the tradition against the loss of knowledge that conversion and the end of royal patronage threatened.

The sources reflect clerical perspectives and do not describe the beliefs or practices of lay Zoroastrians in detail. Their dating and authorship are debated in specific cases, and some of the materials they contain are much older than the compilations. Historians use them with care, recognizing that they reveal the concerns of a minority priesthood in the early Islamic centuries. Over time, Zoroastrian communities declined in number and concentrated in particular regions, a process that continued over many centuries and extended well beyond the period of this chapter. Later migrations of Zoroastrians to western India are reported in traditions whose chronology is uncertain, and this chapter does not rely on them.

Zoroastrian communities also adapted socially. Priestly families remained attached to local temples and to the endowments that supported them, and the tradition of transmitting ritual knowledge within priestly families provided continuity. The fire temples that tenth-century geographers noted in Fars and elsewhere had to be maintained by local patrons, and their survival depended on the willingness of landowners and townspeople to support them. In practice, Zoroastrians and Muslims could live side by side, but the pressures of the fiscal and legal system, the attraction of Muslim institutions, and the movement of families between the communities gradually reduced the Zoroastrian share of the population. The process continued past the period of this chapter, and communities that remained, particularly in the districts around Yazd and Kerman, became the core of later Zoroastrian Iran. The later history of these communities is outside the scope of this chapter.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Zoroastrian and Islamic Religious Institutions over Successive Generations

What Changed

  • Zoroastrian priests lost royal patronage and the legal protection of a Zoroastrian state
  • Muslim institutions, mosques, courts, and teaching circles, spread through cities and gradually the countryside
  • Zoroastrian priestly literature was compiled and defended in written form in the ninth and tenth centuries
  • The number of Zoroastrians declined relative to the Muslim population, unevenly across regions

What Stayed the Same

  • Local fire temples and priestly families continued in many districts
  • Many agricultural festivals and calendar customs persisted
  • Dehqān families and local notables continued as local leaders, whether Zoroastrian or Muslim
  • Religious authority remained tied to learned families and local institutions

Why the pattern matters: Religious change was gradual and uneven. Institutions of the old and new religions coexisted for generations, and change was experienced most directly by priests and by families who moved between communities, while many villagers experienced more continuity.

Section 32.12

Other Religious Communities in Islamic Iran

Christians of the Church of the East continued to have dioceses and monasteries in Iran and further east. They had an established position in Mesopotamia and Khuzestan, and Christian physicians, scribes, and translators had a role in the caliphal court that is discussed in Chapter 31 and Chapter 33. In Iran, Christian communities were present in Fars, Khuzestan, Khurasan, and Transoxiana, and their numbers declined unevenly in the following centuries. Jews lived in Isfahan, Khuzestan, and other communities across Iran, maintaining their own religious institutions under dhimma and linked to the wider Jewish world through scholarly and commercial networks. Jewish communities in Iranian cities often occupied recognized quarters and were important in trade and crafts.

The Church of the East was well organized and, in the eighth and ninth centuries, extended its reach even as the center of gravity shifted toward Baghdad, where the patriarchal seat was established after the founding of the Abbasid capital. The patriarch Timothy I (r. 780–823) left a substantial body of letters that reveal an administrator in active contact with bishops and metropolitans across a very large territory, and the church maintained metropolitan sees in Iranian and Central Asian cities. Christian communities also supported monasteries, schools, and hospitals, and Christian physicians and translators were prominent at the caliphal court, a subject explored in Chapters 33 and 43. In the Iranian lands their numbers declined over time through conversion, emigration, and the effects of local violence, though the process was slow and regionally varied.

Buddhists in the east lived in regions such as Balkh and Bamiyan, where monasteries were institutional centers. The Nawbahar monastery near Balkh is known from Arabic sources and was an important Buddhist center, and the later fate of Buddhist communities varied, with gradual decline under Muslim rule in the western parts of Central Asia and survival in some areas longer. Buddhist monasteries were targets of conquest in some cases and survived by agreements in others. Manichaeans were treated more harshly. In the eighth century, the Abbasid state pursued zindīqs, a term applied to Manichaeans and to others suspected of heresy or dualist belief, and the caliph al-Mahdi (r. 775–785) is reported to have organized inquisitions. Manichaeans did not enjoy the legal protection of dhimma in the same way, and their communities diminished in the west, though they persisted in some eastern regions.

Jewish communities offer a particularly clear example of adaptation to a new linguistic environment. The Jews of Iran spoke and wrote Persian, and a form of Persian written in Hebrew script, known as Judeo-Persian, appears in surviving documents. An inscription at Tang-i Azao in present-day Afghanistan, dated by scholars to about 752–753, is among the earliest dated texts in this language, and it shows that a Jewish community in the Iranian east was using Persian in writing in the middle of the eighth century. Jewish life in cities such as Isfahan, which had a long-established Jewish quarter, was organized around communal leadership and religious learning, and Jewish merchants took part in the commercial networks that crossed Iran. The example is important for this chapter's argument. Judeo-Persian shows an Iranian language being used by a non-Muslim community, in a script of its own, at the very moment when Arabic was becoming the language of the ruling religion.

These communities differed in legal treatment, communal organization, and relations with Muslim neighbors. People of the Book received protection under dhimma, while groups regarded as polytheists or heretics were more vulnerable. In practice, the treatment of Zoroastrians and of Buddhists depended on local agreement, and in some places Buddhists and Zoroastrians were treated analogously to People of the Book. The coexistence of these communities with growing Muslim populations produced interaction in trade, learning, and daily life, as well as periodic tension. Chapter 43 treats the role of scholars from several communities in medieval science and medicine.

The Manichaeans illustrate the risks of falling outside the dhimma system. In the late eighth century the caliph al-Mahdi launched investigations against zindīqs in the Abbasid lands, and later sources report trials and executions. The accusation of zandaqa was also used against individuals for reasons that were partly political or personal, so that it is often difficult to tell whether a person charged with the offense actually held Manichaean beliefs. Manichaean communities in Iraq and Iran declined under such pressure, and the leadership of the Manichaean church is reported to have moved its residence from Baghdad to Samarkand in the tenth century, well after al-Mahdi's investigations, with Manichaean communities continuing in Central Asia for some time. The treatment of the Manichaeans shows that Muslim authorities did not apply a single standard to all non-Muslims and that religious groups with weaker legal protection or perceived political danger could face severe repression.

Section 32.13

Building Islamic Religious Life: Mosques, Judges, Teachers, and Communities

As Muslim populations grew, Islam became embedded in social institutions. The congregational mosque anchored the Muslim community, and as numbers increased, neighborhood mosques multiplied. Judges (qāḍīs) were appointed by the state to settle disputes, and their courts brought Islamic law into daily life for Muslims and, for some purposes, for others. Teachers and preachers transmitted the Quran, the sayings of the Prophet, and legal knowledge, often in informal circles around a scholar. Charitable practices, including endowments (waqf) that supported mosques, schools, and the poor, gave religious institutions material foundations, though the elaborate endowed institutions of later centuries belong to a later stage of development.

The details of this institutional growth are better documented for some places than others, and the emphasis in the surviving sources is on the learned. Instruction in the Quran was commonly given in elementary schools and in the circles of individual teachers, and the transmission of the Prophet's sayings was often done orally in the mosque, with students traveling long distances to hear a respected authority. Judges were appointed by governors or the caliphal government, and their authority depended on the support of the state, though the substance of the law they applied was developed by jurists who were often independent of the court. Endowments for mosques and the poor are attested in the ninth and tenth centuries, but the large endowed teaching colleges that became a hallmark of later medieval Iran belong mostly to the eleventh century and are discussed in Chapters 39 and 40. It is important not to read that later institutional landscape back into the earlier period, when religious learning was organized more around the person of the teacher than around the endowed institution.

Religious scholars formed networks that connected Iranian cities to Iraq, Syria, the Hijaz, and each other. Students traveled to study with leading authorities, collecting sayings of the Prophet and legal opinions, and Iranian scholars rose to positions of prominence in this system. This scholarly mobility is evident in biographical dictionaries and reveals that Iranian Muslims were participants in the creation of Islamic religious culture and not only recipients of it. Chapter 33 explores the intellectual and linguistic dimensions of this participation.

The participation of scholars from Iranian regions in the compilation of the most authoritative collections of the Prophet's sayings illustrates their role. The compilers whom later Sunni tradition regarded as most authoritative included al-Bukhari, who came from Bukhara and died in 870, and Muslim ibn al-Hajjaj of Nishapur, who died in 875, along with Abu Dawud from Sistan, al-Tirmidhi from Tirmidh on the Oxus, al-Nasaʾi from Nasa in Khurasan, and Ibn Maja from Qazvin. These scholars wrote in Arabic, studied in the cities of Iraq, Syria, Egypt, and Arabia, and worked within a scholarly discipline whose methods were defined across the Muslim world. Their origins in Iranian regions do not mean that their work was "Iranian" in any distinctive doctrinal sense, and it would be an error to claim that it was. What the pattern shows is that within a few generations of the conquest, people from the Iranian lands were central contributors to the shaping of Islamic religious learning, which is one of the most important ways in which Islamization did not involve a passive reception of an Arab religion.

The growth of religious institutions followed conversion and also encouraged it. A city with a thriving mosque, a respected judge, and an active scholarly circle offered a person a place in a community with its own authority, moral expectations, and opportunities. Local notables who patronized mosques and scholars gained status, and this created incentives for the families of landowners and merchants to take part. The process was gradual and decentralized. There was no single religious bureaucracy directing conversion, and many institutions emerged from local initiative.

Section 32.14

Early Sunni Traditions, Legal Schools, and Debates over Muslim Belonging

The terms "Sunni" and "Shiʿi" are convenient but must be used carefully for the eighth and ninth centuries. The consolidated Sunni religious establishment, with fixed legal schools and a defined canon of prophetic traditions, took shape over a long period and is not a feature of early Islamic Iran. In the eighth century, Muslim communities in Iranian lands followed emerging currents of legal and theological thought associated with individual scholars and their followers. The Hanafi tradition, named for the jurist Abu Hanifa (d. 767) of Kufa, became influential in Khurasan and Transoxiana, partly through students who came from or returned to the east. The Shafiʿi tradition, named for al-Shafiʿi (d. 820), developed networks in the ninth century, with a notable presence in Nishapur. These are early stages of legal communities that were not yet the rigid structures of later centuries.

Scholars from the east were prominent in the legal and theological debates of the period. Abu Hanifa's student Abu Muti al-Balkhi, from Balkh, was a Hanafi jurist who held office as a judge, and the Quran commentator Muqatil ibn Sulayman of Balkh was an influential and controversial figure in the eighth century. The Khurasani traditionist Ishaq ibn Rahwayh, mentioned above, represented a scholarly orientation that placed great weight on the sayings of the Prophet, in contrast to the more rationalizing methods associated with some of the Hanafi circles. Disputes among these approaches helped define what would later be called the Sunni community, but in the ninth century they were debates among scholars and their followers, and not a contest between fixed confessional blocs. The term ahl al-sunna, "the people of the Prophet's way," came into use as a label for those who emphasized adherence to tradition, and its meaning and limits changed over time.

Theological debate also shaped Muslim identity. Murjiʾite ideas, which emphasized faith over works and deferred judgment on the sinful, were widespread in Khurasan. They offered an inclusive view of Muslim belonging that could accommodate new converts, since it did not exclude believers on the basis of Arab descent or minor disputes. Some Khurasani figures associated with Murjiʾite views, including the rebel Harith ibn Surayj in the early eighth century, challenged Umayyad rule on behalf of non-Arab Muslims, showing how theological views and social grievances overlapped. Other movements disputed leadership more sharply. Kharijite communities in Sistan and Khurasan insisted on strict standards for leadership and community membership, and some opposed both Umayyad and Abbasid authority. Early rationalist and speculative theology, including the Jahmiyya associated with Jahm ibn Safwan of Tirmidh in Transoxiana, also circulated, and the Mutazilite school gained support at the Abbasid court, though these developments are better treated in the intellectual history of Chapter 33 and later chapters.

The career of Jahm ibn Safwan is a useful illustration of the political stakes. Jahm, from Tirmidh, served as a secretary to the rebel Harith ibn Surayj and was executed by the Umayyad authorities in about 746. His teachings on God's nature and human belief were condemned by later critics, who used the label "Jahmite" to attack opponents in theological disputes long after his death, and so the relationship between what Jahm actually taught and what the label came to mean is uncertain. The example shows two things relevant to this chapter. First, a thinker from Transoxiana could become a significant figure in debates that spread across the Muslim world. Second, theological positions were tied to political loyalties and to the experience of non-Arab Muslims, and the labels used for them were often weapons in later controversy. Chapter 40 will explain how Sunni religious institutions were later consolidated, and the reader should keep in mind that in the period of this chapter such consolidation lay in the future.

The point for this chapter is that Muslim belonging was debated and that Iranian Muslims were actors in the debates. The question of who counted as a full member of the community mattered directly to converts, and the answers offered by different groups had consequences for their social standing. Chapter 40 will discuss the later consolidation of Sunni institutions and the Sunni revival, and this chapter does not narrate that development in advance. Students should avoid reading later categories back into the eighth century and should recognize that many Iranian Muslims followed local teachers and customs without strong sectarian identity.

Section 32.15

Alid Loyalties and the Early Development of Shiʿism in Iranian Lands

Loyalty to the family of the Prophet, and especially to ʿAli ibn Abi Talib and his descendants, formed a significant current in early Islamic politics and religion. In Iran, such Alid loyalties took several forms. Some people supported claimants from the Prophet's family as rightful leaders of the community; others held a more general reverence for the Prophet's family without commitment to a particular line or doctrine. The Abbasid movement itself drew on loyalty to the Prophet's house in general terms, which helped it gather support in Khurasan, as Chapter 30 explained. After the Abbasids came to power, Alid claimants periodically rebelled, and Alid sympathies remained a source of opposition.

The caliph al-Maʾmun's decision in 817 to name ʿAli al-Rida, a descendant of ʿAli, as his heir while based in Merv shows the political significance of Alid loyalties in the east. ʿAli al-Rida died in 818 and was buried near Tus in Khurasan, in a place that later became the site of Mashhad, a major pilgrimage center. The political and religious meaning of these events was contested, and the later Shiʿi tradition interpreted them in particular ways. At the center of this chapter's concern, the burial brought a shrine into Khurasan that would develop into a center of devotion in later centuries, a development outside the period studied here.

Not all Alid movements in Iran were of the same character, and the differences matter. Zaydi Shiʿism, which took root in the Caspian region, holds that leadership of the community belongs to any qualified descendant of the Prophet through ʿAli and Fatima who takes up arms against unjust rule, and so Zaydi imams were rulers and fighters as well as scholars. The tradition that became Twelver Shiʿism, by contrast, traced leadership through a line of hereditary imams, and its doctrine of the concealment of the last imam took shape in the late ninth and tenth centuries. The Twelver scholarly tradition at its early stages is associated with figures such as al-Kulayni, who came from a village near Rayy and died in 941, and Ibn Babawayh, a scholar of Qom who died in 991. These men belong at the very end of the period studied here and to the beginning of the story told in Chapter 40. Other Shiʿi currents, including those that would develop into Ismaili Shiʿism, sent missionaries into Iranian lands in the ninth and tenth centuries, a development reserved for Chapter 41. The early Shiʿi world, in short, included Zaydis, proto-Twelver communities, and others, and it was not a unified confession.

Qom, in the Jibal, was settled by Arab Ashʿarites, and became a center of Shiʿi scholarship and the transmission of Shiʿi traditions. The community of Qom produced scholars who collected and transmitted sayings attributed to the imams, and its role in the development of Shiʿi tradition is important, though the details of its early history, including dates of settlement, are known mostly from later local tradition. In the Caspian region, Alid claimants gained followers in Tabaristan and Daylam in the later ninth century, and Zaydi Shiʿi rule, a form of Shiʿism that recognizes a different line of leadership from the later Twelver tradition, was established there. Other Shiʿi currents, including those that evolved into Ismailism, also developed, a subject for Chapter 41.

It would be a serious error to portray Iran as predominantly Shiʿi in this period. Shiʿi communities existed in particular places, notably Qom and parts of the Caspian region, and Alid sympathies were widespread, but the majority of Iranian Muslims were not organized in a Shiʿi religious structure, and many Shiʿi groups differed from one another in doctrine. The later Twelver Shiʿi religious establishment developed gradually, and Chapter 40 discusses the Shiʿi communities of the later medieval period, while Chapter 58 examines the institutionalization of Twelver Shiʿism under the Safavids. Iranian identity and Shiʿism were not equivalent in the early Islamic centuries.

Section 32.16

Religious Resistance, Alternative Movements, and Contested Authority

The Abbasid period saw a series of rebellions in Iran with religious dimensions. In 755, shortly after the killing of the Abbasid revolutionary leader Abu Muslim, Sunbadh, a figure from Nishapur described in sources as a Zoroastrian of the Magian class, led a rising that drew on loyalty to Abu Muslim. According to later accounts, his forces took Rayy and moved west, where they were defeated near Hamadan, and he was killed. In Transoxiana, a movement associated with al-Muqannaʿ, known as "the Veiled One," arose in the 770s in the Sogdian region around Kish and Bukhara. Hostile sources describe him as claiming divine status and leading followers dressed in white, and the movement was defeated and his death reported at a date that sources give variously between about 779 and 783. Other movements in Khurasan and Sistan, such as that of the Ustadhsis in the 760s, are also reported.

These movements shared a common background in the disillusionment that followed the Abbasid Revolution, discussed in Chapter 30. Abu Muslim, the Khurasani leader of the revolutionary army, was killed in 755 on the order of the caliph al-Mansur, and the sources suggest that many of his followers refused to accept his death. Devotion to his memory, sometimes expressed in forms that Muslim authors described as heretical, gave rise to groups known in the sources as Abū Muslimiyya. The charismatic and religious language attached to Abu Muslim, his supposed return, and his link to the family of the Prophet illustrates how religious ideas could be mobilized by political events. Whether these movements held doctrines that can be called Zoroastrian, Shiʿi, or something else is a question the sources do not answer clearly, and the labels applied by hostile writers often combined several categories.

The Khurramites (Khurramiyya) were a group of related movements, often described in hostile sources as holding syncretic beliefs combining Iranian religious ideas with Islamic elements, and associated with devotion to Abu Muslim and with northwestern and western Iran. Babak, who led a Khurramite revolt in Azerbaijan from about 816, is the best-known leader. His revolt endured for more than two decades and required a major Abbasid military effort. The campaign ended in 837 with the fall of his stronghold and his capture by the commander Afshin; Babak was executed in January 838. The distinction between the defeat of the movement and the death of its leader matters, since later accounts compress them. Mazyar, a Qarenid ruler in Tabaristan, rebelled in 839 and was also defeated. These events show that local resistance to Abbasid authority persisted in regions of Iran for decades after the conquest, and that it had religious as well as political dimensions.

The conflict with Babak shows how seriously the Abbasid state took the threat. The revolt began in the region of Azerbaijan, where Babak established a fortified base at Badhdh, and for years Abbasid armies failed to dislodge him. The caliph al-Muʿtasim eventually committed one of his most trusted commanders, Afshin, a prince from Ushrusana in Central Asia, to the campaign. Afshin took Badhdh in 837; Babak, who had fled the fortress, was captured and handed over, and he was taken to Samarra and executed in January 838. Afshin's own later fate is revealing. In about 840 he was tried on charges that included heresy and the retention of Zoroastrian customs, along with political disloyalty, and he died in prison in 841. Hostile accounts of the trial make religious accusations central, but their use in what was clearly a political purge shows how accusations of heresy could serve rivals at court. The episode is useful to students because it demonstrates that the language of religious deviance was a political instrument as well as a statement of belief.

Al-Muqannaʿ offers a case study in how a regional movement could be remembered. His revolt centered on the district of Kish in Sogdiana and extended toward Bukhara, and Narshakhi, a local historian, gives an account of it, describing the veiled leader and the fortress from which he was besieged. His personal name is given variously in the sources, which reflects the fact that the written record is later and mostly hostile. The accounts agree that his movement drew support from local people, including some members of the Bukharan ruling family, and that it was suppressed after a siege, but they differ on the date of its end, which different sources place between about 779 and 783. The movement can be read as a local resistance in a region where Muslim rule was relatively recent and where tax and political grievances were real, as well as a religious movement, and the labels of heresy supplied by Muslim authors tell us what their opponents wished readers to believe.

Reading these movements requires attention to the sources. Almost everything known about them comes from later Muslim chroniclers who were hostile to the rebels and who tended to describe them in terms of heresy, immorality, and imposture. Such sources may exaggerate the strangeness of the rebels' beliefs, apply stereotyped labels, and obscure social and political causes, including fiscal grievances, regional autonomy, and loyalty to local leaders. The religious claims of the participants themselves, as opposed to their opponents' descriptions, are mostly unrecoverable. Historians therefore treat the movements as evidence of continuing religious and political contestation, while recognizing the limits of what the sources reveal. They show that the new order did not command universal acceptance and that Iranian responses to it included resistance as well as accommodation. They should not be read as simple nationalist revolts against Arabs, nor as proof of a general Zoroastrian revival.

Section 32.17

Being Muslim and Iranian: Language, Custom, Memory, and Belonging

Iranian Muslims maintained and adapted a range of inherited cultural practices. The Iranian languages continued to be spoken, and the Arabic word ʿajam, meaning non-Arab and often applied to Iranians, was part of how Arabic speakers categorized them. Customs of the Iranian calendar continued, notably the festival of Nowruz at the spring equinox, and Mihragan in the autumn. Abbasid caliphs and officials took part in the receipt of Nowruz gifts, and the fiscal calendar was tied to the Iranian solar year, with a calendar reform under the caliph al-Muʿtadid in the late ninth century adjusting the date of the tax year. These facts show that Iranian customs were not only preserved in private but integrated into Islamic public life, though the details of how Nowruz was observed varied by place and time.

Nowruz and the fiscal year illustrate how an Iranian institution could be incorporated into the Islamic state. The pre-Islamic Iranian solar calendar had marked the new year at the spring equinox, and the fiscal administration continued to collect land revenue on a schedule linked to the agricultural year. Because the Persian calendar was not corrected with intercalary days in the way that the agricultural cycle demanded, the official new year drifted away from the seasons, which created practical difficulties for tax collection. In 895 the caliph al-Muʿtadid ordered a reform that moved the beginning of the tax year, a measure known as the Nowruz of al-Muʿtadid. The reform was a response to administrative need, and it shows that the officials of an Islamic state were maintaining and managing an Iranian calendrical institution. It also shows that Nowruz had both a fiscal and a festive existence, which helps explain its survival.

Memory of the pre-Islamic past also continued. Stories of Sasanian kings and the ancient Iranian heroes circulated in oral and written form, in Middle Persian texts and their Arabic translations, and educated Iranians preserved genealogies and traditions of their ancestors. Chapter 42 examines how this memory was reconstructed in epic poetry, and this chapter notes only that the memory existed and was available as a resource for identity.

The ways in which families remembered their ancestors illustrate the point. Local dynasties of the eastern and northern regions emphasized their descent from Sasanian or earlier Iranian figures: the Samanids, whose rule is discussed in Chapter 35, traced their lineage to the Sasanian-era general Bahram Chobin, and other families preserved genealogies connecting them to the ancient kings. Such claims should be treated as assertions of status, since their historical accuracy is usually unverifiable, but what matters for the present argument is that Muslim families of Iranian origin continued to value and display an Iranian ancestry. Many Muslim families also continued to give their children Persian names, and the survival of such names in the biographical dictionaries is part of what complicates the use of naming patterns as a measure of conversion, as Section 32.4 explained.

The response of Arabic-writing authors to Iranian self-assertion reveals how seriously the question of belonging was taken. Writers of the ninth century, among them al-Jahiz (d. 868 or 869) and Ibn Qutayba (d. 889), composed works that addressed the claims of non-Arabs and defended the merits of the Arabs, and they treated the controversy as a serious intellectual matter. Their arguments, and the literary expression of the opposing position, belong to the intellectual history of Chapter 33. For this chapter, the controversy is evidence that Iranians were present in the Muslim community in sufficient numbers and with sufficient confidence to raise the question of their standing within it, and that the answer was not settled by force or by decree.

Historical Vocabulary
Shuʿūbiyya

Definition: Shuʿūbiyya is the name given to a controversy, mainly literary, of the eighth to tenth centuries, in which writers of non-Arab background argued for the merits of their own peoples against claims of Arab superiority within the Muslim community. The word derives from the Arabic shuʿūb, "peoples" or "nations," which occurs in the Quran (49:13).

Historical context: The debate appears in Arabic literature, where authors defended or attacked the idea that Arabs held a special status. Iranian writers were prominent among the participants, but they were not the only ones, and the positions taken varied.

Why it matters: It shows that Muslim Iranians discussed identity in a religious and literary idiom, and that cultural pride and Muslim belonging could coexist.

Potential misunderstanding: The movement should not be read as an anti-Islamic Iranian nationalist movement. Modern scholars debate how organized it was and how to characterize it, and many of its participants were devout Muslims. The literary history of the controversy belongs to Chapter 33.

These facts support the chapter's central claim. A Muslim of Iranian background could take part in the prayer, law, and scholarship of Islam, speak an Iranian language at home, celebrate Nowruz, and take pride in the Iranian past, without contradiction. Identities were plural and situational. A person might be a Muslim, a native of Nishapur, a Persian speaker, a client of an Arab tribe, and a member of a legal school, and which identity was emphasized depended on context. There was no single Iranian identity shared by all, and regional, linguistic, and religious differences remained. Historians therefore speak of identities that were reworked and negotiated, not simply preserved or abandoned.

Section 32.18

Islamization without Arabization: Continuity, Transformation, and Iranian Identity

The chapter has argued that Iran's Islamization was a gradual and uneven process distinct from conquest and from Arabization. By the tenth and eleventh centuries, Muslims formed the majority in most Iranian regions, though minority communities persisted, and the process had taken place at different speeds in cities and villages, on the plateau and in the Caspian highlands, in Fars and in Transoxiana. Its causes were plural, including conviction, opportunity, family and community ties, fiscal pressure, and in some cases coercion. Its institutions, mosques, courts, and scholarly circles, grew alongside conversion, and Iranian Muslims became active contributors to Islamic scholarship and religious life.

At the same time, Iranian languages, customs, and memories continued within the Muslim framework. Zoroastrian priests wrote and defended their tradition, non-Muslim communities persisted, and Muslim Iranians observed Nowruz and recalled their pre-Islamic past. This continuity was not a simple survival. Religious practices were adapted, institutions were replaced, and identities were reinterpreted in new contexts. The Islamic and the Iranian were intertwined, not opposed, and the pattern of Islamization without Arabization set the stage for the cultural developments that follow.

Mechanisms: How Islamization Proceeded without Arabization

It is worth asking why Iran followed this path, because the contrast with neighboring regions is striking. In Iraq, Syria, and Egypt, Arabic gradually replaced Aramaic, Syriac, and Coptic as the language of daily life over a period of centuries. In most of Iran, Persian and the other Iranian languages persisted, and in the ninth and tenth centuries Persian began to be written in Arabic script as New Persian. The contrast cannot be explained by differences in literary development. Syriac had a rich literature of theology, history, and science, Coptic and Greek were established written languages in Egypt, and their declining prominence under Arab rule needs its own explanations, which lie outside this chapter. Historians have proposed several factors for the Iranian case, and none is sufficient alone. Arab settlement in Iran was concentrated in a few garrison cities and, even there, the settlers were drawn into the local linguistic environment. The Iranian lands were also politically fragmented after the conquest, with local rulers and landholding families keeping their own languages in administration and household life for long periods, as the Sogdian and Bactrian documents show. Persian had a continuing class of scribes and landholders who used it, and in the ninth and tenth centuries regional courts gave it new prestige, a development traced in Chapter 35. The Iranian countryside was governed through local elites, the dehqāns, who maintained local languages and customs while accommodating the new rulers. And Arabic found a function that did not require abandoning Persian: it became the language of religion, law, and learned prose, while Persian remained the language of home and village and, in time, of a new literature. Bilingualism, with Arabic for some domains and Persian for others, is the pattern that the evidence suggests for educated Muslim Iranians.

The mechanisms of religious change were similarly distributed. Conversion occurred through personal and family decisions, shaped by the opportunities and pressures described in Section 32.5, and it was channeled through institutions: the mawlā system, the mosque, the court, the scholarly circle, and the market. Local elites mediated between the rural population and the new order. Because the caliphate did not have a program of mass conversion and depended on non-Muslim taxpayers, the pace was set less by policy than by the accumulation of local choices. At the same time, the state shaped the environment in which those choices were made, by granting legal status, collecting taxes, appointing judges, and sometimes repressing dissent. Understanding Islamization requires attention to both levels, the choices of families and communities, and the framework of law and power in which they were made.

Consequences

The consequences of this pattern were lasting. First, Iranian Muslims became creators of Islamic religious culture, as the examples of the compilers of the major collections of the Prophet's sayings and the jurists and theologians of Khurasan show, without having to become Arab to do so. Second, regional diversity persisted: a Fars with a long Zoroastrian presence, a Caspian region with Zaydi Shiʿi rulers, a Khurasan with Hanafi and Shafiʿi scholars, and a Transoxiana whose conversion was drawn out and contested each preserved a distinct religious character. Third, minority communities, Zoroastrian, Christian, Jewish, and others, continued as part of the social fabric, with their own languages and institutions. Fourth, Iranian identities remained plural and negotiable, anchored in region, language, family, school of law, and religion, and it would be mistaken to see a single Iranian Muslim identity formed in these centuries, or to equate that identity with Shiʿism. Fifth, the cultural foundation was laid for the Persianate world discussed in later chapters, in which Persian became a language of literature, administration, and court across a wide area. None of these outcomes was inevitable. The Iranian plateau might have followed a different path if the caliphate had been more or less centralized, if Arab settlement had been denser, or if Persian had lacked its written tradition. The history of Islamization without Arabization is a record of contingent outcomes that historians can explain without treating them as predestined.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Islamization of Iranian societies was a gradual, uneven, and profoundly consequential transformation, and that becoming Muslim did not necessarily mean becoming Arab, abandoning Iranian languages, or surrendering inherited cultural identities. It began by separating three processes that are often merged: the political conquest of Sasanian territories in the seventh century, the adoption of Islam by individuals and communities over generations, and the spread of Arabic in administration and scholarship, which did not displace Iranian languages in everyday life.

The religious landscape that Islam entered was already plural. Zoroastrian priests and fire temples, the Church of the East, Jewish communities, Manichaeans, Buddhists, and regional cults coexisted within the Sasanian world. The new order introduced a ruling religion and a legal framework of dhimma, under which non-Muslims retained their communal life in exchange for taxation and legal inequality. Legal ideals, as expressed in works such as Abu Yusuf's treatise, must be distinguished from the practice, which varied by place and period and is difficult to reconstruct.

The chronology of conversion cannot be established from direct records. Historians rely on biographical dictionaries, local histories, chronicles, legal texts, Zoroastrian writings, and geographical works. Richard Bulliet's influential reconstruction proposed an S-shaped curve with rapid urban change in the ninth and early tenth centuries, but it depends on a selected population of urban scholars and on assumptions about naming that scholars have questioned. The safest conclusion is that conversion was prolonged, regionally differentiated, and faster in cities than in the countryside.

Explanations for conversion must be plural. Religious conviction, social and political opportunity, family and community ties, fiscal considerations, and pressure or coercion in particular circumstances all played roles, in different proportions for different people. The institution of mawlā clientage gave converts a route into Muslim society while preserving distinctions of status that were a source of grievance. No single factor accounts for the process.

Cities, with their mosques, courts, markets, and scholarly circles, were the main sites of early Islamization. Villages changed more slowly, with dehqān families serving as influential intermediaries. Households experienced religious change through marriage, inheritance, and daily contact, though sources for women and ordinary families are scarce and reconstructions must be labeled as such.

Regional variation was marked. Fars preserved Zoroastrian institutions and produced priestly literature; the Jibal included early Muslim centers such as Qom and diverse cities such as Rayy and Isfahan; the Caspian highlands of Tabaristan and Daylam resisted or limited caliphal authority and were Islamized later; Khurasan became a major Muslim center and a base for Hanafi and Murjiʾite thought, while Sistan had Kharijite communities and Transoxiana experienced a contested conversion that later narratives such as Narshakhi's partially record.

Non-Muslim communities adapted rather than vanishing. Zoroastrian priests compiled and defended their tradition in Middle Persian books in the ninth and tenth centuries, while Christians, Jews, Buddhists, and Manichaeans experienced different legal treatment and different fates. At the same time, Muslim religious life developed through mosques, judges, teachers, and scholarly networks, with Iranians prominent in the process.

The early Sunni and Shiʿi traditions must not be read as the later confessional establishments. Hanafi and Shafiʿi communities, Murjiʾite and Kharijite currents, and Alid loyalties coexisted, and Shiʿi communities were present in particular places such as Qom and the Caspian region without making Iran predominantly Shiʿi. Movements associated with Sunbadh, al-Muqannaʿ, the Khurramites, and Babak show continuing contestation, though they are known mainly through hostile sources whose descriptions require critical reading.

Iranian Muslims maintained languages, customs such as Nowruz, and memories of the pre-Islamic past, and debated identity in Arabic literary forms such as the Shuʿubiyya controversy. Identities were plural and situational, reworked rather than simply preserved. The chapter thus provides the religious and social foundation for Chapter 33's treatment of language and intellectual life, and for the later chapters on regional dynasties, religious institutions, and historical memory.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it corrects two opposite simplifications. One imagines Iran as swept into Islam by conquest and thereby severed from its past; the other imagines an unchanged Iranian culture surviving under a thin Islamic surface. The evidence shows something more interesting: a long, negotiated transformation in which Iranians became participants in the creation of Islamic religious life while reworking their inherited traditions.

The chapter also shows how historians work with scarce evidence. Legal prescriptions, later local histories, hostile chronicles, and clerical writings each reveal something and conceal something else. Learning to distinguish what authorities said should happen from what can be shown to have happened is a skill that applies far beyond this subject.

The patterns established here recur later in the textbook. The regional dynasties of Chapter 34 and the Samanids of Chapter 35 operated within a society that was increasingly Muslim yet culturally Iranian. The Sunni and Shiʿi communities of Chapter 40 grew from the plural beginnings described here, and the formation of a Twelver Shiʿi state, examined in Chapter 58, was a much later development, not an inheritance from the early centuries.

Chapter 33 now turns to the languages and intellectual life through which Muslim Iranians expressed their experience, including Arabic as a language of scholarship and New Persian as a language of literature. The religious and social foundations laid in this chapter make it possible to understand why that expression took the forms it did.
