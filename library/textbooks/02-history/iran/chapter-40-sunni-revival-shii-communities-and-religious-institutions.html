Sunni Revival, Shiʿi Communities, and Religious Institutions

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

Religion in medieval Iran was not simply a set of doctrines that rulers adopted or rejected. It was a working system of teachers, texts, courts, colleges, shrines, lodges, endowments, neighborhoods, and households, and between about 900 and 1200 both Sunni and Shiʿi communities built the institutions that would carry their traditions for centuries.

Late in 1072, in the city of Nishapur in Khurasan, the scholar and Sufi teacher Abu'l-Qasim al-Qushayri died after a long career in which he had taught students, composed a famous handbook of Sufi doctrine, and, a generation earlier, drafted a written complaint about the treatment of his own theological school. His life touched most of the subjects of this chapter. He was a Shafiʿi in law, an Ashʿari in theology, and a Sufi in devotion, and he moved among these identities without feeling that they were competing allegiances. He had been caught up, as a younger man, in a political campaign against the Ashʿari tradition under the first Seljuk administration, and he lived to see that campaign reversed under a new vizier. His funeral and the reputation of his teaching were part of the fabric of a city whose religious life was organized around mosques, study circles, endowed colleges, and communal lodges.

Far to the west, in the same decades, a very different scholar spent his later years in Najaf, beside the tomb honored by Shiʿi Muslims as the burial place of ʿAli ibn Abi Talib. Abu Jaʿfar al-Tusi, the leading Twelver Shiʿi jurist and theologian of his generation, had left Baghdad amid the disturbances of the mid-to-late 1050s, after the city's political order had changed and his own circle had suffered damage. At Najaf he continued to teach and to write, and the community of students who gathered around him helped make the shrine city a lasting center of Imami learning. His career illustrates that Shiʿi religious authority, like its Sunni counterpart, depended on institutions and personal networks, and that both could be reshaped by political upheaval.

This chapter asks how Sunni and Shiʿi communities in Iran and the surrounding Islamic world developed and defended distinct forms of religious authority during the Buyid, Ghaznavid, and Seljuk periods. The chapter's period runs approximately from 900 to 1200 CE, with its greatest concentration in the tenth through twelfth centuries. It examines religion as a lived institutional system and not only as a record of theological disagreement or of rivalry between dynasties. Legal schools, theological traditions, colleges, judges, Sufi lodges, shrines, endowments, public ceremonies, and everyday practices all come into view.

The chapter's title contains a term that requires scrutiny. "Sunni revival" is a conventional label for the intensified political and institutional support that Sunni scholarship received after the end of Buyid supremacy in Baghdad in 1055. The expression is useful but potentially misleading. Sunni Islam did not disappear during the Buyid century, and most people living under Buyid rule, including in Baghdad, remained Sunni. What changed was the pattern of patronage, the scale of endowed institutions, the relationship between scholars and rulers, and the public posture of the caliphate and the Seljuk sultans. Some historians have therefore preferred to speak of consolidation and reorganization. The chapter treats the question of terminology as a historical problem in its own right.

The chapter also treats Shiʿi history on its own terms. Medieval Iran was not predominantly Twelver Shiʿi, and the Iranian plateau remained largely Sunni until the Safavid transformation examined in Chapters 57 and 58. Yet Shiʿi communities of several kinds, Twelver (Imami), Zaydi, and Ismaili, were present and in some regions influential, and their scholars produced works that shaped later Shiʿi thought. These communities cannot be treated as a single movement or as a minority footnote to a Sunni narrative. They had their own scholarly traditions, legal methods, centers of learning, and disagreements.

Historians reconstruct this religious world from an uneven body of evidence. Biographical dictionaries record the careers of scholars. Chronicles describe riots, appointments, and political decisions. Legal works, theological treatises, creeds, and polemics preserve arguments. Geographical writers describe the religious composition of cities. Endowment records, inscriptions, and surviving buildings document patronage. Almost all of this evidence was written by educated men, usually scholars or officials, and it concentrates on cities and on prominent figures. Women, villagers, pastoralists, and the poor appear mainly through indirect traces, and this chapter attempts to read the sources with that limitation in view.

The chapter belongs to a division of labor within Volume III. The political history of the Buyids, Ghaznavids, and Seljuks belongs to Chapters 36 through 38, and the administration of Nizam al-Mulk and his foundations to Chapter 39. The Nizari Ismaili state, Hasan-i Sabbah, and the fortress of Alamut belong to Chapter 41, Persian poetry and the Shahnameh to Chapter 42, and philosophy, medicine, and science to Chapter 43. Here those subjects appear only as far as they explain the organization of religious authority.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the diversity of Islamic communities in Iran and Central Asia between about 900 and 1100, and explain why a ruler's affiliation did not define the beliefs of an entire population.
  2. Distinguish the Sunni legal schools from Sunni theological schools, and explain the regional distribution and mutual rivalry of the Hanafi and Shafiʿi traditions in Iran.
  3. Explain the principal positions of the Ashʿari, Maturidi, traditionalist, and Muʿtazili approaches to theology.
  4. Evaluate the idea of a "Sunni revival," distinguishing institutional consolidation and patronage from the supposed restoration of an extinguished tradition.
  5. Trace the development of mosque teaching, hadith transmission, madrasas, waqf endowments, and the Nizamiyya colleges without attributing the madrasa to a single founder.
  6. Analyze the roles of judges, muftis, and jurists, and the relationship between scholarly legal authority and political government.
  7. Interpret the growth of Sufi teaching and communal lodges as part of, not apart from, Sunni scholarly life.
  8. Compare the Twelver, Zaydi, and Ismaili traditions and explain how Twelver religious authority was organized during the Occultation.
  9. Assess the contributions of al-Mufid, al-Sharif al-Murtada, and al-Tusi to Imami law and theology, and the significance of Baghdad, Qom, Rayy, and Najaf.

  11. Compare the religious policies of Buyid and Seljuk rulers and identify instances of patronage, exclusion, negotiation, and coercion.
  12. Evaluate the intellectual careers of al-Juwayni and al-Ghazali as case studies in the relationship among law, theology, philosophy, and spiritual practice.

Historical Context

By the tenth century, Islam had been the dominant religion of much of the Iranian world for some two centuries, a process examined in Chapters 28 through 33. Conversion had been gradual and uneven. Cities and the plateau's administrative centers converted earlier than many mountain and rural districts, and Zoroastrian, Christian, Jewish, and Buddhist communities persisted in various regions, as Chapter 32 explained. What had emerged by about 900 was a diverse Islamic society in which the Qurʾan, the Arabic language of scholarship, and common ritual obligations bound Muslims together, while schools of law, theological currents, and devotional movements divided them in ways that mattered locally.

The political framework changed repeatedly across the period. The Samanids, examined in Chapter 35, ruled Khurasan and Transoxiana as Sunni governors of the Abbasid caliph. The Buyids, examined in Chapter 36, held Baghdad from 945 to 1055 and governed much of western Iran and Iraq while the Abbasid caliph remained a Sunni religious figurehead. The Ghaznavids, examined in Chapter 37, were Sunni military rulers in Afghanistan and eastern Iran. The Seljuks, examined in Chapters 38 and 39, took Baghdad in 1055 and ruled as Sunni sultans recognized by the caliph. Each dynasty had its own relationship to religious scholars, and none controlled the beliefs of the population it governed.

Religious scholarship in this period was not primarily a state activity. The ulama, the scholars of religious knowledge, formed a social class defined by learning, family ties, and reputation. They served as teachers, judges, preachers, jurists, and community leaders, and their standing derived from their command of texts and their place in chains of transmission as much as from official appointment. Rulers could patronize, appoint, dismiss, and sometimes persecute scholars, but they could not easily create religious authority. They needed scholars to confer legitimacy, to administer law, and to shape opinion in cities.

The geography of religion followed the geography of cities. The great urban centers of Baghdad, Nishapur, Rayy, Isfahan, Herat, Balkh, Bukhara, and Samarkand were also centers of scholarship, each with its own mosques, families, and teaching traditions. Chapter 1 introduced the plateau's pattern of discontinuous settlement, and religious life reflected it: scholarly networks linked distant cities through travel and correspondence, while the countryside between them often maintained local practices that urban authors recorded only occasionally. The shrine cities of Najaf, Karbala, Qom, and Mashhad, and the Caspian highlands, supported distinct Shiʿi communities.

The Abbasid caliphate stood in a peculiar position. It had lost most of its military power but retained the symbolic status of the leadership of the Sunni community, and its recognition of rulers mattered, as Chapters 36 through 38 showed. At the same time, a rival Ismaili caliphate, the Fatimids, ruled North Africa from 909 and Egypt from 969, and sent missionaries across the Islamic world. Religious allegiance was therefore tied to political competition, which gave rulers and caliphs reasons to take an interest in doctrine, and gave scholars reasons to seek patrons.

A final point of context concerns terminology. The words "Sunni" and "Shiʿi" were used in the period, but their boundaries were less fixed than they later became. Many scholars moved among teachers of different persuasions, some Shiʿi scholars studied with Sunni teachers and the reverse, and several theological movements, notably the Muʿtazila, were not identical with either camp. This chapter uses the labels as historical categories that were real but porous.

Section 40.1

Islamic Iran and the Religious Landscape, c. 900–1100

A map of political rule in the tenth and eleventh centuries would show Samanid, Buyid, Ghaznavid, and Seljuk territories succeeding one another. A map of religious life would look quite different. It would show cities with large Sunni majorities and Shiʿi quarters, districts of the Caspian highlands where Zaydi imams held authority, towns of central Iran where Imami communities were strong, oasis cities with Hanafi and Shafiʿi families in competition, and rural areas whose practices are barely recorded. Political affiliation of the ruler offers only a rough guide to what people believed and did.

Most Muslims of Khurasan and Transoxiana were Sunni, and in the east the Hanafi school of law was dominant among the Turkic and local ruling elites. In Nishapur and much of western Khurasan, Shafiʿi scholarship was strong. In Baghdad, the largest city of the Islamic world, Hanafi, Shafiʿi, and Hanbali communities existed alongside a significant Shiʿi population, concentrated in quarters such as Karkh, as Chapter 36 described. In Rayy and Isfahan, Sunni and Shiʿi communities lived together, with rivalry among Sunni groups also recorded. The central Iranian town of Qom was a distinctive center of Imami Shiʿism. The mountains south of the Caspian sustained Zaydi communities. Fars and Kirman had their own mixtures, including Zoroastrian communities that retained religious institutions.

The relationship between the ruling dynasty and the population was never simple. The Buyids were Shiʿi in affiliation, but most of their subjects in Baghdad and in Iran were Sunni, and they governed through Sunni judges and soldiers. The Samanids, Ghaznavids, and Seljuks were Sunni, but their territories contained Shiʿi communities, Ismaili missionaries, and Muʿtazili scholars. The Seljuk ruling family followed the Hanafi school, while its vizier Nizam al-Mulk, as Chapter 39 noted, supported Shafiʿi and Ashʿari scholars. Rulers' preferences mattered for patronage, but they did not determine popular belief.

Non-Muslim communities also formed part of the religious landscape. Zoroastrians remained numerous in Fars, Kirman, and Yazd. Jewish communities lived in Isfahan, Hamadan, and other cities, and Christians of the Church of the East and other churches lived in Iraq, Khuzestan, and parts of Iran. This chapter concentrates on Sunni and Shiʿi institutions, but these communities contributed to the plural character of urban life and shaped the conditions under which Muslim scholars worked.

Evidence for the geography of belief is uneven. Geographical writers such as al-Muqaddasi described the religious character of cities, often through the lens of local factional conflict. Biographical dictionaries record where scholars taught and studied. Chronicles mention riots and appointments. None of these sources gives population figures for sects, and statements that a city was "mostly Sunni" or "largely Shiʿi" are impressions, not statistics. Students should hold such generalizations loosely, and ask whose observation lies behind them.

Section 40.2

The Sunni Schools of Law: Hanafi, Shafiʿi, Maliki, and Hanbali Traditions

Sunni Islamic law was organized into schools, called madhhabs, each named after an early jurist but developed over generations by his followers. By the tenth century four schools had come to be recognized as the principal Sunni traditions: the Hanafi, associated with Abu Hanifa (d. 767) and strongest in Iraq, Central Asia, and later among Turkic rulers; the Maliki, associated with Malik ibn Anas (d. 795) and dominant in North Africa and al-Andalus; the Shafiʿi, associated with al-Shafiʿi (d. 820) and strong in Egypt, Iraq, and Khurasan; and the Hanbali, associated with Ahmad ibn Hanbal (d. 855) and centered in Baghdad. These schools differed in how they weighed sources of law, such as the Qurʾan, the hadith, consensus, and analogical reasoning, and they developed different interpretive habits and bodies of case law.

Historical Vocabulary
Madhhab and Ijaza

Definition: A madhhab is a school of legal interpretation, a tradition of method and doctrine transmitted among scholars. An ijaza is a license, usually written, by which a teacher authorized a student to transmit a text or to teach a subject.

Historical context: The legal schools crystallized between the eighth and eleventh centuries as scholars compiled commentaries, developed theories of legal method, and organized teaching around founders' doctrines. Licenses of transmission linked students to chains of teachers extending back to authoritative figures.

Why it matters: Schools and licenses show that religious authority was exercised through networks of personal relationships, not through a single central institution. A scholar's standing depended on his lineage of teachers and on recognition by peers.

Potential misunderstanding: A madhhab was not a separate sect. Adherents of different Sunni schools prayed together, recognized one another as Sunni, and often studied with one another, though rivalry among schools could become intense locally.

In Iran and Central Asia, the Hanafi and Shafiʿi schools predominated. The Hanafi school enjoyed particular favor among the Turkic ruling families of the Ghaznavids and Seljuks and among the elites of Transoxiana, where it flourished alongside the Maturidi theological tradition discussed in Section 40.3. The Shafiʿi school was strong in Nishapur, Rayy, and Isfahan and among much of the scholarly class of Khurasan. The Maliki school was largely absent from Iran, and the Hanbali school, though important in Baghdad and with a presence in Herat, was a minority tradition on the plateau. The four schools were therefore not equally represented in the region, and students should not imagine a balanced distribution.

The schools' methods differed in ways that mattered to jurists and, occasionally, to ordinary believers. Hanafi jurists gave considerable weight to reasoned opinion and analogy, and developed refined techniques for dealing with cases not covered by explicit texts. Shafiʿi jurists emphasized a systematic theory of legal sources, giving priority to the Qurʾan and authenticated hadith, and produced influential works on legal method. Hanbali jurists emphasized strict adherence to transmitted texts and mistrusted speculative theology. Such differences influenced rules on contracts, family law, and ritual, but a great deal of legal practice overlapped among schools.

Scholarly networks tied jurists across regions. Students traveled from Khurasan to Baghdad, from Bukhara to Nishapur, and from the plateau to Mecca and Medina, and returned with licenses and reputations. A scholar's school identity was established by his teachers and by the books he taught. Families often preserved school allegiance across generations, and local religious leadership could pass within families. In cities, the head of a school might serve as an informal leader of a large part of the community, representing it before the ruler and arbitrating among its members.

Relationships with rulers varied. A ruler's own school could confer advantages: Hanafi qadis predominated in Ghaznavid and Seljuk judicial appointments in many regions, while Shafiʿi scholars received prominent patronage from Nizam al-Mulk. The rivalry between Hanafis and Shafiʿis in several Iranian cities was sometimes intense, a subject taken up in Section 40.14. Rulers could exploit or try to restrain it, but its roots lay in competition for local leadership, teaching posts, endowments, and judicial office as much as in doctrine.

Section 40.3

Sunni Theology: Ashʿarism, Maturidism, Traditionalism, and Religious Debate

Theology, called kalam, addressed questions about God's nature and attributes, the status of the Qurʾan, human responsibility, the grounds of ethical judgment, and the nature of faith. It was distinct from law, which dealt with practice, and it is a common error to identify each legal school with a single theology. The relationship was looser. Within a given legal school, scholars could hold different theological positions, and the same theological position could be defended by jurists of different schools.

Several currents competed in the tenth and eleventh centuries. The Muʿtazila, a rationalist movement that emphasized divine justice and the unity of God and held that the Qurʾan was created, had been influential in the earlier Abbasid period and retained a following among scholars, including the chief judge of Rayy, ʿAbd al-Jabbar (d. 1025), who worked under the patronage of the Buyid vizier al-Sahib ibn ʿAbbad, as Chapter 36 noted. Traditionalists, many of them Hanbalis, rejected speculative theology and insisted on accepting the texts of the Qurʾan and hadith without asking how they could be true. Their opponents regarded such insistence as a danger to reasoned belief.

Between these positions two Sunni theological schools took shape. The Ashʿari school, named for Abu'l-Hasan al-Ashʿari (d. c. 935), who had left the Muʿtazila, used rational argument to defend traditional doctrines, including that God's attributes are real, that the Qurʾan is God's uncreated speech, and that God creates human acts while humans acquire responsibility for them. The school found a home among Shafiʿi scholars, and prominent figures such as al-Baqillani (d. 1013) and Ibn Furak (d. 1015) taught in Baghdad and Nishapur. The Maturidi school, named for Abu Mansur al-Maturidi (d. c. 944) of Samarkand, took a related but distinct approach, giving somewhat greater scope to human reason in recognizing good and evil. It became associated with Hanafi scholars of Transoxiana and the east.

Disagreements were real and sometimes politically charged. Traditionalist critics distrusted Ashʿari methods as concessions to rationalism, while Ashʿari scholars regarded their method as the best defense of tradition. In Khurasan in the 1040s and 1050s, the Seljuk administration under its vizier al-Kunduri is reported to have ordered the public cursing of Ashʿari scholars, and several leading figures, including al-Qushayri and al-Juwayni, left or were forced to leave the region. Al-Qushayri wrote a written complaint about the persecution, which is among the evidence for the episode. The policy was reversed under Alp Arslan and Nizam al-Mulk, as Chapters 38 and 39 noted.

The theological debates took place in several settings: in scholars' study circles, in public disputations before rulers and viziers, in treatises written against rivals, and in creeds issued by caliphs and rulers. The caliph al-Qadir's creed, discussed in the Source Window below, is an example of an attempt to define acceptable doctrine by authority. Such statements reflect the desire to draw boundaries, but they did not end disagreement, and scholars continued to debate under different patrons.

Primary Source Window I

The Qadiri Creed and the Definition of Sunni Doctrine

Creator: Doctrinal documents and books issued under the Abbasid caliph al-Qadir (r. 991–1031), with scholars of his circle · Date: documents of 1017–1019 and books of 1029; a shorter recension reaffirmed under al-Qaʾim in 1040–1041 · Type and survival: Arabic caliphal creedal texts; the original documents have not survived, and a concise form is preserved by the later chronicler Ibn al-Jawzi

Context: Al-Qadir reigned during the Buyid period, when the caliphs lacked military power but retained religious prestige. The creed was part of a broader effort to define the caliphate's Sunni identity against rival claims, including those of the Fatimids, Shiʿi scholars, and Muʿtazili theologians. It circulated in Baghdad and was reportedly read to scholars and notables.

Paraphrase of the later chronicle recension (not a quotation from an original caliphal document): The surviving concise formulation affirms the unity of God and the reality of His attributes, holds that the Qurʾan is God's speech and uncreated, defines faith as comprising speech and action and as capable of increase and decrease, and states the order of excellence of the early Companions of the Prophet, placing Abu Bakr first. It rejects positions associated with the Muʿtazila and with Shiʿi preference for ʿAli over earlier caliphs. Students should consult a published translation before quoting wording.

Analysis: The creed shows a caliph, under political supervision, asserting a defined doctrinal position that drew on traditionalist teaching. Its emphasis on the Companions and the uncreated Qurʾan answered contemporary controversies. It also illustrates how doctrine could serve institutional purposes: a statement that scholars were expected to accept marked boundaries of acceptable belief.

Limitations: The original caliphal documents of 1017–1019 and books of 1029 are not extant. The concise recension reported by Ibn al-Jawzi is a later textual witness, not a verified copy of every original statement. It cannot establish how widely particular doctrines were accepted, how consistently they were enforced, or what ordinary believers made of them. The surviving chronicles also reflect partisan theological interests.

Perspective: The creed represents the caliph's circle and traditionalist scholars. Shiʿi and Muʿtazili scholars, whose positions it rejects, are present only as targets.

Questions for students:

  1. What doctrines does the creed affirm, and which does it reject?
  2. Who issued it, and under what political circumstances?
  3. What purposes might a caliph with little military power have had for issuing a creed?
  4. What can historians learn from it about the Sunni identity of the caliphate?
  5. What can they not establish from it alone about the beliefs of ordinary Muslims?
Section 40.4

What Was the Sunni Revival? Abbasid Legitimacy and Seljuk Patronage

The entry of the Seljuk sultan Tughril into Baghdad in December 1055, described in Chapter 38, ended Buyid supremacy over the caliphate and brought to power a dynasty that presented itself as the defender of Sunni Islam. Historians have long described the following decades as a period of Sunni revival, in which Sunni scholarship, institutions, and political influence recovered from a period of decline associated with Shiʿi Buyid dominance. The description captures real changes, but it also embeds assumptions that deserve scrutiny.

Several developments are well documented. The Seljuk sultans, as Chapter 38 showed, sought caliphal recognition and championed Sunni Islam. They supported judges, preachers, and jurists, founded or endowed mosques and colleges, and in some periods used their power against rival groups, among them Ismailis and certain Shiʿi communities. Their viziers, especially Nizam al-Mulk, built an extensive network of colleges, as Chapter 39 described. The caliphs, freed from direct Buyid control, recovered a degree of religious and ceremonial authority, and in the twelfth century some of them, notably al-Mustarshid and al-Nasir, built substantial political bases of their own.

At the same time, Sunni Islam had never disappeared. During the Buyid century, Baghdad remained the home of a vigorous Sunni scholarly community. Hanafi, Shafiʿi, and Hanbali scholars taught and wrote, the caliph retained his religious functions, and most of the city's inhabitants were Sunni. In the east, Samanid and Ghaznavid Sunni patronage had supported scholarship without interruption. The Buyids governed through Sunni officials and judges and did not attempt to convert their subjects. What the Buyid period lacked was a Sunni dynasty with military power willing to give Sunni institutions large-scale political backing in the capital.

The term "revival" therefore describes a change in the structure of patronage and public posture rather than a return from extinction. Institutions that existed earlier, such as madrasas, Sufi lodges, and legal study circles, expanded and became more closely tied to the state. The caliphate and the sultanate cooperated in presenting Sunni Islam as the public religion of the state. And a sharper sense of Sunni identity, defined against Fatimid and Ismaili challenges and against Shiʿi and rationalist rivals, took shape in doctrinal statements and political rhetoric.

The revival was also not a simple victory for a single Sunni position. The patronage that Ashʿari and Shafiʿi scholars received from Nizam al-Mulk was resented by Hanafi and Hanbali rivals. The caliphal creed reflected a traditionalist outlook that Ashʿari scholars did not entirely share. Seljuk policy changed from ruler to ruler. The result was a contested field in which several Sunni groups competed for the favor of the new rulers, and in which rulers could also turn against a group that had previously enjoyed support.

Historical Debate

Was There a "Sunni Revival" in the Eleventh and Twelfth Centuries?

The question: Did the Seljuk era witness a revival of Sunni Islam after a period of decline, or a reorganization and consolidation of institutions that already existed?

Interpretation A — A revival of Sunni Islam: Older scholarship, influenced by the narratives of Sunni chroniclers, depicted the Buyid century as a period of Shiʿi ascendancy and Sunni weakness, followed by a recovery under the Seljuks. On this view, the creed of al-Qadir, the Nizamiyya colleges, and the Seljuk campaign against Ismailis mark the restoration of Sunni religious and political leadership.
Interpretation B — Consolidation, not revival: Other historians, including Jonathan Berkey, have questioned the revival narrative, pointing out that Sunni scholarship remained vigorous throughout and that the main changes were in the scale and political backing of institutions. On this view, "revival" exaggerates the earlier weakness and overstates the Seljuks' role as originators.
Interpretation C — Reorganization of religious authority: A third approach emphasizes that the period saw new relationships among scholars, rulers, and institutions, including the rise of endowed colleges and a sharper definition of orthodoxy. Whether one calls this revival matters less than understanding which groups gained or lost influence.

Evidence: Historians draw on biographical dictionaries, chronicles, creeds, endowment records, and the careers of individual scholars. The evidence for Sunni vitality during the Buyid period comes from the same sources that describe the later flourishing.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns terminology and emphasis more than basic facts. Few dispute that the Seljuks patronized Sunni institutions. The debate concerns how to characterize the preceding century and how much change followed.

Why it matters: The question affects how the Buyid period and the Seljuk period are compared, and whether religious change is explained mainly by political patronage or by developments within scholarly communities.

Division II · §§40.5–40.8 Religious Learning, Law, and Institutional Authority
Section 40.5

Mosques, Scholars, and the Transmission of Religious Knowledge

Before there were large endowed colleges, religious knowledge passed from person to person. The ordinary setting was the congregational mosque or a smaller neighborhood mosque, where a teacher sat beside a pillar and students gathered around him in a circle. Children learned to recite the Qurʾan from local teachers, often in a mosque annex or a private house. Older students studied the Arabic language, the sayings and deeds of the Prophet transmitted as hadith, the principles of law, and theology, usually by reading a text aloud with a teacher who explained it. The method required a skilled teacher and a body of books, not a special building.

Hadith transmission shaped the culture of scholarship. A hadith was valued according to its chain of transmitters, and a scholar who had heard a report directly from a respected authority held a kind of intellectual capital. Students therefore traveled, a practice called the rihla, to hear hadith from famous teachers in Nishapur, Baghdad, Isfahan, Cairo, and the Hijaz. The Nishapur scholar al-Hakim al-Nishapuri (d. 1014) is one example of a hadith authority whose reputation drew students and whose works became standard references. Such journeys created networks of acquaintance that crossed political boundaries, since a student from Bukhara and one from Baghdad might study with the same teacher in Nishapur.

The teaching license, the ijaza, formalized these relationships. A teacher who judged a student competent to transmit a work or to teach a subject wrote a license naming the text, the teacher's own chain of transmission, and the student's qualifications. Licenses did not confer a state office, and they were not degrees issued by an institution. They represented a personal judgment by a recognized scholar, and their value depended on the teacher's reputation. A scholar's standing was thus built through a record of teachers, a body of students, and recognition by peers.

The ulama formed a distinct social group, though one with permeable boundaries. Many came from families in which learning passed from father to son, and certain families held teaching posts, judgeships, or the leadership of a legal school over several generations. Others rose from modest origins through talent and patronage. They earned their living in several ways: stipends from endowments, fees as judges or notaries, gifts from patrons, income from trade or landholding, and, for some, support from families that valued their service. Scholars were also preachers who addressed the community on Fridays and at festivals, and their sermons were one of the main channels by which religious ideas reached the broader public.

This system meant that religious authority could develop outside direct state supervision. A rulers' decision to appoint a judge or endow a college mattered, but the reputations that gave scholars influence rested on teaching, writing, and community recognition. A scholar who lost official favor could continue to teach in a mosque or private house, and could move to another city where his license and reputation would be recognized. This independence had limits. Rulers could dismiss judges, close or confiscate endowments, expel scholars, and in extreme cases imprison or execute them. The episode of the Ashʿari scholars in Khurasan, described in Section 40.3, shows both the vulnerability of scholars to political pressure and the ability of scholarly networks to survive it, since the exiled scholars taught elsewhere and returned later with their prestige intact.

The evidence for this world comes largely from biographical dictionaries, which record where scholars studied, whom they taught, and what they wrote, and from the works of the scholars themselves. These sources emphasize male scholars of the urban elite. Women also transmitted hadith and some held licenses, and women taught children and other women in households, but they are recorded much less fully. Rural religious teachers, village prayer leaders, and local holy persons appear mostly in passing. Students should treat the scholarly world described in the sources as a prominent part of religious life and not as the whole of it.

Section 40.6

Madrasas, Nizamiyya Colleges, and Religious Endowments

The madrasa is often described as the characteristic educational institution of medieval Islam, and the Seljuk vizier Nizam al-Mulk is often called its founder. Both statements need qualification. A madrasa was an institution in which law and related subjects were taught, usually in a dedicated building supported by an endowment, with salaried teachers and, often, lodging or stipends for students. Institutions of this kind existed in Khurasan and Transoxiana before the Seljuks. Nishapur had endowed teaching institutions connected with prominent scholars in the tenth and early eleventh centuries, and other eastern cities had similar foundations. Teaching also continued in mosques, private houses, and libraries after the spread of the madrasa, so that the new institution was added to existing settings and did not replace them. Chapter 39 described the Nizamiyya colleges and their administration, and this section concentrates on their place in the wider history of religious institutions.

What the Seljuk period contributed was scale, political backing, and an increasingly close tie between teaching posts and public careers. Nizam al-Mulk's colleges at Baghdad, Nishapur, and other cities, together with foundations by other viziers, commanders, and members of the dynasty, multiplied endowed positions for teachers and students. The Baghdad Nizamiyya, inaugurated in 1067, became the most famous example. The colleges gave a stable institutional home to scholars of the Shafiʿi school and Ashʿari theology, as Chapter 39 explained, and the ties between a patron and the scholars he supported strengthened that patron's influence among the learned class.

The legal instrument that made such institutions possible was the waqf, an endowment in which property was permanently dedicated to a pious purpose. The founder specified the purposes, such as paying a teacher, supporting students, maintaining a building, or giving alms, and appointed an administrator, a mutawalli, to manage the property and distribute its income. The endowed property, which might include shops, mills, baths, farmland, or urban buildings, could not in principle be sold or inherited. Endowments gave institutions a durable income that did not depend on a ruler's continuing favor, and they gave founders a way to preserve wealth and influence, since the founder or his descendants often kept the right to administer the waqf and to name teachers.

Waqfs also created conflicts. Administrators could mismanage or misappropriate income. Rulers or officials could pressure endowments or reassign their revenues in times of need. Founders' families could treat the administration as a hereditary property. Rival scholars competed for the posts that endowments funded, and the stipulations of a founder regarding the legal school to be taught could be disputed after his death. Evidence for these problems comes from biographical dictionaries and chronicles, which record disputes over appointments, but systematic records of endowments from this period are scarce, and students should not assume that surviving descriptions represent the typical case.

Teachers and students lived within this system in different ways. A senior teacher held a salaried chair, lectured, and presided over disputations in which students defended and attacked legal positions according to set conventions. Assistant teachers repeated lessons, and advanced students argued cases. Students from a distance might receive lodging and a stipend, which allowed them to study full time. Many graduates became judges, preachers, notaries, tutors, or administrators, so that the colleges served as routes into public service. Others returned to their home towns to teach.

The spread of madrasas should not be read as the replacement of independent scholarly life by state-controlled institutions. Many scholars taught outside colleges, and many colleges were founded by private patrons and scholars rather than by rulers. The relationship between scholars and the state was a matter of negotiation. Scholars sought endowed posts for security and prestige, while rulers and viziers sought scholars for legitimacy and trained personnel. The tension between patronage and independence appears in the story, preserved in biographical dictionaries and discussed in Chapter 39, that the Shafiʿi jurist Abu Ishaq al-Shirazi hesitated to take the Baghdad chair because of doubts about the sources of the endowment. The anecdote cannot be verified, but it expresses a concern that contemporaries evidently shared.

Section 40.7

Judges, Jurists, Fatwas, and the Practice of Islamic Law

Islamic law in the period was administered and interpreted by several kinds of officials and scholars. The qadi, or judge, heard disputes, recorded contracts and testimony, administered inheritance, and oversaw endowments and the property of orphans. Qadis were appointed by rulers, in the Abbasid tradition formally by the caliph or on his authority, and the chief judge of a major city held a position of high prestige. A mufti was a jurist who issued legal opinions, called fatwas, in response to questions from judges, rulers, or private persons. A mufti was not a state official and needed no appointment. His standing rested on learning and on the trust of those who consulted him.

The relationship between the two roles shaped legal practice. A qadi might consult muftis on difficult cases, and a litigant who disliked a ruling might seek an opinion from a respected jurist. Fatwas were written answers, often brief, that stated the legal position on a hypothetical or real case, and collections of them circulated among jurists as sources of precedent. Because opinions came from scholars outside the state, the law was never entirely a creature of the government. At the same time, rulers could and did choose which scholars to appoint as judges, which affected the application of law in practice.

Judicial appointments reflected political and sectarian considerations. In the Buyid period, the chief judges of Baghdad were generally Sunni, as Chapter 36 noted, even though the ruling family was Shiʿi in affiliation. Under the Seljuks, judges were mostly Hanafi or Shafiʿi, depending on the region and the patron, and local factions competed for the posts. In some eastern cities, leading families of a school held the chief judgeship or related offices over generations, such as the Hanafi family known as the Burhani sadrs of Bukhara in the later period. Appointment of a judge from a rival school could provoke protest, and in some cities it was a source of riots.

The scope of law was broad. Courts dealt with contracts, property, marriage and divorce, inheritance, debts, criminal accusations, and the administration of endowments. They did not constitute the only legal forum. Rulers and their officials heard complaints in the tradition of mazalim justice, discussed in Chapter 39, particularly where powerful persons were involved. Local officials called market inspectors, muhtasibs, supervised weights and measures and enforced standards of public conduct. Customary practice also governed many matters in villages and among pastoralists, where the formal courts were distant. The law as written by jurists coexisted with these other mechanisms.

Public morality was a particular area of contention. Scholars and officials debated what rulers and communities should do about practices regarded as sinful, such as drinking wine, music, or certain festivals. Preachers and some traditionalist groups called for enforcement, and occasionally crowds acted on their own to break wine jars or attack gatherings. Rulers who wished to claim religious legitimacy sometimes issued edicts against such practices, while others tolerated them in practice. The actual enforcement varied by ruler, city, and period, and the sources, which record the edicts and the disturbances, say less about the daily negotiation that probably took place.

The relationship between scholarly legal authority and political government was thus reciprocal and tense. Jurists such as al-Mawardi (d. 1058), a Shafiʿi jurist of Baghdad who wrote a treatise on the rules of government, described the offices of caliph, vizier, governor, and judge in legal terms and sought to fit the realities of military rule into a juridical framework. Rulers needed this legitimation and used it. Jurists also needed the protection of rulers and the posts they could provide. The law thereby bound rulers and scholars together without making either wholly subordinate to the other.

Historical Debate

Religious Institutions and Political Power: Instruments of the State or Autonomous Communities?

The question: Did the expansion of endowed colleges, judgeships, and Sufi lodges in the eleventh and twelfth centuries make religious scholars dependent on rulers, or did scholarly communities retain real autonomy?

Interpretation A — Institutions as instruments of the state: Some historians stress that rulers and viziers founded colleges, appointed judges and teachers, and used endowments to attract scholars who would support the regime's legitimacy and supply trained officials. On this view, the madrasa was a tool of political patronage, and scholars who held endowed posts were bound to their patrons.
Interpretation B — Autonomous scholarly communities: Others emphasize that scholarly authority rested on learning, transmission, and communal recognition, that many colleges were founded by private patrons, that teaching continued in mosques and homes, and that scholars could move among cities and patrons. On this view, rulers could support but not control religious authority.
Interpretation C — Mutual dependence: A third position holds that both claims describe parts of a reciprocal relationship. Rulers needed scholars for legitimacy and personnel, scholars needed rulers for security and resources, and the balance varied with the ruler, the city, and the scholar.

Evidence: The evidence includes biographical dictionaries, chronicles of appointments and expulsions, endowment stipulations where they survive, and the careers of scholars who moved when patronage changed.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns emphasis and interpretation. Few historians deny that patronage existed or that scholars had independent reputations. They differ over which feature best characterizes the relationship.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians understand the long-term role of the ulama in Islamic societies, including their later capacity to act as intermediaries between rulers and populations.

Section 40.8

Sufism, Spiritual Discipline, and Religious Communities

Sufism, the tradition of spiritual discipline and inner devotion within Islam, had roots in early ascetic movements that emphasized renunciation, constant remembrance of God, and moral self-examination. By the tenth and eleventh centuries it had acquired a body of literature, a vocabulary of spiritual stations and states, and a growing number of recognized teachers. Its most active centers in the Iranian world were in Khurasan, Baghdad, and Transoxiana, and its development there was closely connected with the scholarly life of the cities.

A Sufi teacher guided disciples through a course of discipline that might include fasting, vigils, repeated recitation of God's names, spiritual retreat, and obedience to the teacher's direction. The relationship of master and disciple was personal, and a chain of teachers linking a master to earlier authorities became a source of legitimacy, comparable to the chains of hadith transmission. Teaching took place in many settings: in a mosque corner, in a private house, in a small hermitage, or in a communal lodge.

Historical Vocabulary
Khanaqah and Tariqa

Definition: A khanaqah (Persian; also khanqah) was a communal residence or lodge where Sufis lived, practiced devotion, and received guests. A tariqa (Arabic, "path") refers to a spiritual method or discipline, and, in later centuries, to an organized Sufi order tracing its lineage to a founding master.

Historical context: Regulated communal Sufi life was established in parts of Khurasan by the eleventh century, with lodges supported by donors and, in some cases, by endowment. Organized orders in the later sense of the word tariqa developed mainly after the twelfth century, so the term should be used cautiously for the period of this chapter.

Why it matters: The lodge gave Sufism a physical and institutional base, and linked spiritual teaching to charity, hospitality, and the support of travelers.

Potential misunderstanding: Sufis were not a separate religion or a sect apart from Sunni Islam. Many were jurists and scholars, and Sufi teaching was usually carried on within the framework of Sunni law and theology.

Several figures illustrate the range of the tradition. Abu'l-Qasim al-Qushayri (d. 1072), whom this chapter has already met, was a Shafiʿi jurist and Ashʿari theologian who composed in 1045 a treatise on Sufism, the Risala, which presented Sufi teaching as consistent with Sunni orthodoxy and gathered the biographies and sayings of earlier Sufis. It became a standard handbook. Abu Saʿid ibn Abi'l-Khayr (967–1049), from the village of Mayhana in Khurasan, was a charismatic teacher who gathered disciples in a community in Mayhana and Nishapur and who became the subject of a later hagiographical tradition. Earlier, Abu ʿAbd al-Rahman al-Sulami (d. 1021) of Nishapur compiled biographical and doctrinal works on Sufism, and, in Herat, ʿAbd Allah al-Ansari (d. 1089), a Hanbali jurist and Sufi, taught a combination of strict traditionalism and mystical devotion. The Persian treatise Kashf al-mahjub by ʿAli al-Hujwiri, who lived and died in the Ghaznavid world and is associated with Lahore, shows that Sufi writing in Persian was also developing in the east.

Sufi lodges in Khurasan were supported by donors, including merchants, scholars, and officials, and some received endowments. They offered lodging and food to travelers, held sessions of recitation and sermons, and served as local centers of charity and instruction. Lodges associated with the Karramiyya, a devotional and ascetic movement strong in Nishapur and elsewhere in Khurasan, were prominent and sometimes controversial, and Karrami teachers had their own doctrinal positions that Ashʿari and other scholars criticized. These examples show that regulated communal Sufi life had several origins and was not the product of a single movement.

Sufism's relationship to Sunni scholarship was close. Many leading jurists were also Sufis, and many Sufis were trained in law and hadith. At the same time, there were tensions. Some jurists suspected Sufi claims of direct experience of God as a threat to the authority of the law and of the scholars who interpreted it. Some Sufi practices, such as ecstatic gatherings with music, were criticized as innovations. Others among the Sufis themselves criticized scholars who they felt had neglected inner sincerity for outward display. The existence of such criticism within both groups shows that the boundary between them was porous, and the interplay between legal learning and spiritual discipline, taken up in the life of al-Ghazali in Section 40.17, was a central feature of the period.

The social reach of Sufism extended beyond the learned elite. Teachers drew disciples from artisans, merchants, and rural families, and shrines of holy persons, whether living teachers or deceased saints, were visited by ordinary people seeking blessing and help. The sources, which are mostly hagiographies and biographical dictionaries composed by followers, present idealized pictures of teachers and miracles, and historians treat them as evidence of how communities remembered and valued their teachers more than as records of events. They still show that Sufi institutions provided a form of religious community accessible to people who were not scholars.

Division III · §§40.9–40.12 Shiʿi Communities, Scholarship, and Religious Identity
Section 40.9

Shiʿism and the Imamate: Twelver, Zaydi, and Ismaili Traditions

The term Shiʿi derives from shiʿat ʿAli, the "party of ʿAli," the early supporters of ʿAli ibn Abi Talib, the Prophet's cousin and son-in-law, as the rightful leader of the Muslim community after the Prophet's death in 632. Shiʿi allegiance was rooted in a conviction that leadership belonged to ʿAli and to certain of his descendants through his wife Fatima, the Prophet's daughter. Disputes over succession to the leadership of the community, over the legitimacy of the early caliphs, and over the death of ʿAli's son al-Husayn at Karbala in 680, which became a defining memory, gave Shiʿi identity its historical shape. By the tenth century, however, Shiʿism was not a single movement. It comprised several communities that disagreed about who the legitimate leaders, the imams, were and what authority they possessed.

Historical Vocabulary
Imamate and Occultation

Definition: The imamate is the doctrine of leadership of the Muslim community by an imam, understood by Shiʿi Muslims as a divinely sanctioned position held by descendants of ʿAli and Fatima. The occultation (ghayba) is the Twelver doctrine that the twelfth Imam is alive but hidden from the public.

Historical context: Shiʿi communities differed over the line of imams and the nature of their authority. The Twelvers recognized twelve imams, the Zaydis recognized any qualified descendant who rose in arms, and the Ismailis recognized a different line after the sixth Imam.

Why it matters: The meaning of the imamate shaped each community's approach to law, politics, and religious guidance.

Potential misunderstanding: The Sunni use of imam for a prayer leader or a distinguished scholar differs from the Shiʿi doctrinal sense of the term. Students should note which meaning a source intends.

Three traditions were important in and around Iran. The Twelver or Imami community recognized a line of twelve imams descending from ʿAli, the last of whom, Muhammad al-Mahdi, was held to have gone into concealment. Twelvers believed that the imams possessed special knowledge and were divinely guided, and they preserved the imams' teachings in collections of reports. Because the twelfth Imam was absent, the community's practical guidance passed to scholars who transmitted and interpreted the imams' teachings. The Twelver tradition developed its characteristic institutions in the tenth and eleventh centuries, as Sections 40.10 and 40.11 explain.

The Zaydi tradition, associated with Zayd ibn ʿAli, who led a revolt in 740, held that the imamate belonged to any qualified descendant of ʿAli and Fatima who rose in arms against unjust rule and who possessed religious learning. Zaydis therefore recognized a succession of imams who established themselves by action and were not designated by a line of descent from father to son. Their legal and theological positions were closer to those of some Sunni schools and the Muʿtazila than were those of the Twelvers. Zaydi communities were established in Yemen and, as Chapter 36 noted, in the Caspian highlands of Daylam and Tabaristan.

The Ismaili tradition followed a different line of imams, recognizing Ismaʿil, the son of the sixth Imam Jaʿfar al-Sadiq, or his son Muhammad, as the rightful successor. Ismaili thought developed a distinctive doctrine in which religious texts had an inner meaning known to the imam and those he instructed. The Fatimid dynasty, which ruled North Africa and then Egypt and claimed the caliphate, represented the political form of Ismailism in this period, and sent missionaries across the Islamic world, including Iran. Chapter 41 treats the Ismaili movement in detail, and here only its relationship to the wider Shiʿi landscape needs to be noted.

These communities disagreed with one another as well as with Sunnis. Twelvers rejected Zaydi views on the imamate and Ismaili claims, and Zaydis rejected the doctrine of a hidden imam. Within each tradition further divisions occurred. The evidence for their differences comes from heresiographical works, theological treatises, and polemics, which often present rivals in unflattering terms, and students should therefore be cautious about taking one community's description of another at face value. It would be a serious error to treat "Shiʿism" in this period as a unified bloc, or to infer from the presence of Shiʿi rulers that their subjects shared a single Shiʿi doctrine.

Section 40.10

Twelver Shiʿism, the Occultation, and the Formation of Scholarly Authority

According to Twelver tradition, the eleventh Imam, al-Hasan al-ʿAskari, died in 874 at Samarra, the Abbasid capital north of Baghdad, where he had lived under official supervision. His death created a crisis, because the sources indicate that he left no publicly acknowledged son. The community faced the question of who would lead it and whether the line of imams had ended. The answer that came to prevail was that he had a son, Muhammad, who had been hidden from the authorities and who remained alive in concealment.

Twelver tradition divides the concealment into two periods. In the first, the Lesser Occultation, the Hidden Imam was believed to communicate with the community through a series of four agents, called deputies. This period is traditionally dated from 874 to about 941, when the fourth deputy died without naming a successor. The Greater Occultation that followed was understood as a period in which the Imam could not be reached directly and would return at the end of time to establish justice. The traditional dates and the account of the four deputies come from later Twelver historical writing, and modern historians treat the details with caution, though they accept that the doctrine took shape during these decades and that the community underwent a real crisis of leadership.

The doctrine of the Hidden Imam changed the nature of religious authority. If the Imam was unreachable, the community still needed guidance on law, ritual, and doctrine. The answer developed over the tenth and eleventh centuries was that scholars who knew the teachings of the imams and who could interpret them held the responsibility of guiding the community during the absence. This authority was never absolute in the way the Imam's was believed to be, and Twelver scholars disagreed about how far reasoned interpretation could go beyond transmitted reports. But the scholar emerged as the community's guide.

The textual foundations of this scholarship were laid in the early tenth century. Muhammad ibn Yaʿqub al-Kulayni (d. 941), a scholar associated with Rayy and Baghdad, compiled a large collection of reports from the imams arranged by subject, known as al-Kafi, which became one of the principal Twelver hadith collections. Muhammad ibn ʿAli ibn Babawayh (d. 991), a scholar from Qom who later worked in Rayy, compiled several works, among them a legal handbook for those without access to a jurist, Man la yahduruhu al-faqih. Ibn Babawayh, often called al-Shaykh al-Saduq, also wrote on theology and on the doctrine of the occultation. These collections preserved the imams' teaching in written form and allowed scholars to refer to a common body of material.

Scholars of this early generation generally emphasized the transmission of reports from the imams and were cautious about extensive use of rational argument in law and theology. Their successors in the late tenth and eleventh centuries, based in Baghdad, took a more systematic and rationalist approach, as the next section shows. This development reflected both internal debate and the intellectual environment of Baghdad, where Muʿtazili and other theologians practiced rigorous argument. It is a mistake to treat Twelver thought as fixed in the form it took in the early tenth century, since it changed substantially in the following two generations.

The community's organization during the Occultation relied on networks rather than a central hierarchy. Scholars taught students, issued opinions, collected religious dues in some places on behalf of the Imam, and mediated disputes within the community. Wealthy lay members supported them. The Buyid period, which provided a favorable environment in Baghdad and Rayy, allowed these networks to operate more openly. After the Seljuk takeover, they continued, though with less political protection, which prompted relocation to Najaf and Hilla. The survival of Twelver institutions thus depended on scholarly networks and communal support, not on state sponsorship, a point that helps explain how the community persisted through changes of rulers.

Section 40.11

Imami Scholarship in Baghdad, Qom, Rayy, and Najaf

Imami scholarship in the tenth and eleventh centuries flourished in several centers, each with its own character. Qom, a town on the plateau south of modern Tehran, was among the earliest Shiʿi scholarly centers in Iran, associated with families of hadith transmitters and with the tradition of collecting reports from the imams. Ibn Babawayh came from Qom. Rayy, the great city of northern Iran, hosted Imami scholars, including al-Kulayni and Ibn Babawayh, alongside Sunni and Muʿtazili communities. Baghdad, the largest city of the Islamic world, was where a new generation of Imami scholars developed a more rationalist and systematic scholarship. And Najaf, increasingly prominent in the mid-eleventh century, developed further as a scholarly center around the shrine of ʿAli.

The Baghdad generation of the late tenth and early eleventh centuries produced three figures of lasting importance. Muhammad ibn Muhammad al-Mufid (d. 1022), known as al-Shaykh al-Mufid, was the leading Imami theologian and jurist of his time. He studied with Ibn Babawayh's circle and with Muʿtazili teachers in Baghdad, and he engaged in public disputations with scholars of other persuasions. He wrote on theology, law, and history, including a biography of the imams, al-Irshad. His method gave greater scope to rational argument than that of the earlier transmitters of reports, and he criticized positions taken by Ibn Babawayh, as the Source Window below illustrates. His disagreements with the earlier tradition, and with some of his own students, show that Imami thought was developing through debate.

His student al-Sharif al-Murtada (d. 1044), a member of a distinguished family of descendants of the Prophet, held the post of naqib of the Talibids, the office described in Chapter 36, and was a prolific writer. He developed a theory of legal reasoning, set out in a work on the foundations of law, in which he argued for a restrictive view of the reliability of single transmitted reports and for the role of reasoned principles and consensus. He also wrote a major work on the imamate and was a defender of Muʿtazili-influenced Imami theology. His brother al-Sharif al-Radi (d. 1015), a poet and the compiler of a famous anthology of sayings and sermons attributed to ʿAli, belonged to the same circle.

Abu Jaʿfar Muhammad ibn al-Hasan al-Tusi (d. 1067), known as Shaykh al-Ta'ifa, "the shaykh of the community," studied with both al-Mufid and al-Murtada, and became the leading figure of the following generation. He wrote prolifically on law, theology, and hadith. Two of his works, Tahdhib al-ahkam and al-Istibsar, became among the foundational Twelver hadith collections, and he also wrote a work on the principles of law, a legal compendium, a commentary on the Qurʾan, and a catalogue of Shiʿi authors and their works, al-Fihrist, which is a major source for the history of the tradition. His work consolidated Imami legal methodology and gave later scholars a systematic framework.

The relationship of these scholars to political power varied with circumstance. Specialist accounts differ on whether his move occurred around 1055 or 1057. His move helped establish Najaf as a scholarly center, a development that outlasted him.

Cause and Effect

Why Imami Scholarship Moved Toward Najaf

Long-term causes

The development of Imami scholarly networks around the shrines of the imams; the existence of pilgrimage routes to Najaf; the Occultation's emphasis on scholars as community guides.

Short-term causes

The end of Buyid protection in Baghdad after 1055; hostility and disorder in the capital; damage to the property of Imami scholars in the later 1050s.

Immediate context

Al-Tusi's departure from Baghdad and his settlement at Najaf, where he continued to teach and to write.

Transformation

Najaf began to function as a lasting center of Imami teaching, supported by students who gathered around al-Tusi and by the presence of the shrine.

Immediate effects

Imami scholarship continued under conditions less dependent on the favor of the Baghdad rulers; students and teachers formed a community in Najaf.

Long-term consequences

Najaf and, later, Hilla became centers of Imami learning for centuries; the tradition's institutional base shifted from a politically protected capital to a shrine city.

Historical complexity: Political change prompted the move, but it required existing networks, a revered shrine, and a community willing to support a scholar. The later prominence of Najaf cannot be attributed to a single decision.

Section 40.12

Zaydi Communities, Ismaili Networks, and Regional Diversity

The Twelvers were only one part of the Shiʿi world. In the highlands south of the Caspian Sea, the geographic isolation described in Chapter 1 allowed a distinctive Zaydi tradition to flourish. A descendant of ʿAli, al-Hasan ibn Zayd, established a Zaydi state in Tabaristan in the 860s, and the Zaydi missionary al-Hasan ibn ʿAli al-Utrush worked among the Daylamites and Gilites at the turn of the tenth century, as Chapter 36 noted. Zaydi imams continued to claim leadership in the Caspian regions for generations, and Zaydi scholars wrote on law, theology, and the imamate.

The tenth and early eleventh centuries produced important Zaydi scholars in the region. Brothers Abu'l-Husayn Ahmad al-Muʾayyad bi'llah al-Haruni (d. 1020s) and Abu Talib al-Natiq bi'l-Haqq (d. 1033) held the imamate in Daylam at different times and developed a legal and theological tradition that drew on Muʿtazili thought. Their writings, preserved and transmitted in Zaydi communities, including those of Yemen, show that the Caspian tradition formed part of a wider Zaydi intellectual network. The Zaydi tradition's emphasis on a qualified imam who must rise against unjust rule gave it a political edge that distinguished it from the quietist Twelver position of the same period.

The relationship between Zaydi and Twelver scholars was one of both contact and dispute. They shared devotion to the family of ʿAli and a body of reports, but disagreed about the doctrine of the Hidden Imam and about the legitimate line of imams. In practice, scholars moved among traditions to some extent. The Caspian region was also home to Sunni communities, and the Zaydi imams' authority was local and often contested. Students should resist treating the Caspian Zaydis as a marginal curiosity. They were a living community with institutions, scholars, and a political tradition that continued for centuries.

The Ismaili challenge was of a different kind. The Fatimid caliphate, established in North Africa in 909 and in Egypt in 969, organized a network of missionaries who sought converts and political loyalty in many regions, including Iran, Iraq, and Central Asia. Fatimid agents had been active in Khurasan and Transoxiana in the tenth century, and the Samanid and Ghaznavid authorities viewed them with hostility, as Chapter 37 noted for the Ghaznavid campaign against Ismaili Multan. Ismaili communities existed in parts of Iran, though their size and organization are difficult to establish from the hostile sources that describe them. The Fatimid claim to the caliphate directly challenged the Abbasids, and it gave Sunni rulers and caliphs reasons to emphasize a defined Sunni identity, as the Qadiri creed of Section 40.3 illustrates.

The emergence of the Alamut movement belongs to the final stage of this chapter's period. In 1090 a group led by Hasan-i Sabbah seized the mountain fortress of Alamut in the Alborz, as Chapters 38 and 39 noted. At that time the movement was part of the wider Ismaili mission, and it was not yet a distinct Nizari state. The division of the Ismaili community into Nizari and Mustaʿli branches followed in 1094, on the death of the Fatimid caliph al-Mustansir, when rival claimants to the succession divided the community. Chapter 41 treats Hasan-i Sabbah, the development of the Nizari movement, and its state. For the present chapter, the essential points are that Alamut was seized in 1090, that the 1094 division should not be projected backward onto the earlier years, and that the Ismaili challenge shaped the religious politics of the Seljuk state.

The regional diversity of the Shiʿi world in Iran was therefore considerable. Twelver communities were strong in Qom, parts of Rayy, and Baghdad's Karkh quarter. Zaydi communities held the Caspian highlands. Ismaili networks operated clandestinely in several regions. And all these existed alongside a Sunni majority in much of the plateau, which remained predominantly Sunni until the Safavid transformation examined in later chapters. Treating medieval Iran as already Shiʿi would be anachronistic, and treating its Shiʿi communities as a single minority would be equally misleading.

Section 40.13

Ashura, Ghadir Khumm, Shrines, and Public Religious Identity

Religious identity was not only a matter of law and theology. It was also performed in the streets, at tombs, and on days of the calendar that a community marked and its neighbors might ignore or resent. For Shiʿi Muslims, two annual occasions and a network of burial shrines gave public form to the doctrine of the imamate described earlier in this chapter. The first was Ashura, the tenth day of the month of Muharram, which commemorates the death of al-Husayn ibn ʿAli, grandson of the Prophet, and his companions at Karbala in the year 61 of the Islamic calendar (680 CE). The second was the observance of Ghadir Khumm on the eighteenth of Dhuʾl-Hijja, recalling a gathering at a place of that name on the return from the Prophet Muhammad's last pilgrimage, where, according to Shiʿi tradition, he proclaimed ʿAli as his successor.

It is important to separate what the two events mean to different communities. Almost all Muslims regard the killing of al-Husayn as a tragedy, and Sunni authors of the period wrote about it with grief. What differed was the way it was marked. In the Shiʿi tradition it became an occasion for public lamentation, recitation of the story of Karbala, and visits to the tomb. Ghadir Khumm was more sharply divided. Sunni scholars generally accepted that the Prophet had spoken at that place and had expressed affection for ʿAli, but they did not understand his words as designating ʿAli as imam in the Shiʿi sense. Shiʿi scholars, by contrast, treated the event as the central proof of ʿAli's designation. The same report was therefore evidence in one tradition and a point of friendly dispute, or of open polemic, in another.

The best-documented moment at which these observances moved from private devotion into public politics comes from Buyid Baghdad. The chronicle tradition reports that in the early 960s the Buyid amir Muʿizz al-Dawla ordered that markets be closed and that public mourning be held on Ashura, and that the festival of Ghadir be celebrated with lights and festivity. Our knowledge of these orders comes from later historians writing in Arabic, who were mostly Sunni, and who report them as provocative innovations. What the sources agree on is that the observances led to disorder, and that Sunni crowds responded with rival commemorations of their own and with violence. Historians therefore read the episode as a case in which the public ritual calendar became a way of claiming urban space, and not simply as a record of piety.

Shrines worked in a similar way. The tomb of ʿAli, according to Shiʿi tradition, lay at Najaf, and that of al-Husayn at Karbala, both in Iraq. The tombs of the seventh and ninth Imams, Musa al-Kazim and Muhammad al-Jawad, stood at the Kazimayn in the western part of Baghdad. The eighth Imam, ʿAli al-Rida, was buried at Tus in Khurasan, at the place that later grew into the city of Mashhad, and his sister Fatima al-Maʿsuma was buried at Qom. Qom's association with her tomb helped strengthen its identity as a scholarly and devotional center. These places drew pilgrims, attracted endowments, supported local scholars, and sometimes became objects of rulers' patronage. The Buyid amir ʿAdud al-Dawla is reported to have supported building at the shrine of ʿAli at Najaf in the later tenth century, a gesture that combined personal piety with the display of Buyid status.

Shrines were also vulnerable. Because they were identified with a particular community, they could be damaged in riots or neglected by rulers who did not favor that community. Sources for the eleventh century report fires and looting in Baghdad's Shiʿi districts and damage to tomb complexes, and the shrine at Mashhad suffered neglect and later restoration. The details of individual episodes are not always secure, since chroniclers differed on dates, causes, and responsibility. The general pattern is clear enough: sacred sites were also political sites.

Sunni Muslims had their own sacred places and their own pilgrimage practices, which should not be left out of the account. The tombs of companions of the Prophet, of early scholars such as Abu Hanifa in Baghdad and al-Shafiʿi in Egypt, and of Sufi saints drew visitors in the same period, and visiting graves, ziyara, was a widespread practice that Hanbali traditionalists and some others viewed with suspicion. The Shiʿi shrines were therefore one part of a larger landscape of visited tombs, but they carried the additional meaning of the imamate.

Section 40.14

Urban Sectarian Relations: Quarters, Factions, and Coexistence

Medieval Iranian and Iraqi cities were commonly organized into quarters, and in many of them religious affiliation was one of several things that held a quarter together, along with trade, kinship, and neighborhood loyalty. Baghdad is the best-known example. The district of Karkh, in the south-west of the city, was associated with a large Shiʿi population and with commercial life, while Hanbali traditionalists were strong in other districts and drew popular support. Clashes between the two are recorded repeatedly in the tenth and eleventh centuries, and they often followed the calendar described in the previous section. But the same sources also report that members of different communities shared markets, intermarried in some cases, and cooperated in time of crisis. The record of violence exists because chroniclers noticed it, and quiet coexistence was less likely to be written down.

In Iranian cities the most visible fault line was often not between Sunni and Shiʿi but between the two largest Sunni law schools. In Isfahan, Rayy, Nishapur, and other cities, followers of the Hanafi and Shafiʿi schools formed rival groups with their own scholars, mosques, and sometimes their own neighborhoods. The rivalry could be intense. Sources report street fighting, burning of quarters, and appeals to the authorities, especially in the twelfth century. It combined real doctrinal disagreement with competition over teaching posts, endowments, and local prestige, and it is a reminder that the 'Sunni' community was neither unified nor automatically at peace with itself. Theological affiliation added another layer, since many Shafiʿis were Ashʿaris and many Hanafis in the east were Maturidis, while Hanbali traditionalists distrusted both.

The cities also contained non-Muslim communities. Zoroastrians remained numerous in Fars, Kerman, and parts of the central plateau, and the fire temples and priestly families that sustained them are attested in the sources, even as the community gradually shrank over the centuries. Jews lived in Isfahan, Hamadan, Rayy, and many other places, with their own scholarly and communal institutions. Christians, mainly of the Church of the East, were found in the western and northern regions and in Baghdad. Under Islamic law, Jews, Christians, and Zoroastrians were treated as protected communities, dhimmis, who paid a poll tax and were subject to certain restrictions while enjoying legal protection and communal autonomy. How consistently these rules were applied varied with the ruler, the city, and the moment. Some non-Muslims served in administration and medicine, while in other periods they were subject to restrictions or popular hostility. Historians cannot reduce the situation to either idyllic tolerance or constant persecution.

The patterns of relations between communities also reflected the work of religious leaders. Scholars sometimes mediated disputes, preachers attacked rivals from the pulpit, and the heads of important families used their influence to protect their own communities. The office of naqib, a leader of the descendants of the Prophet or of ʿAli, gave certain families formal standing in Baghdad and elsewhere, and, as noted earlier in this chapter, the Sharif al-Murtada held such a post. Local officials, market inspectors, and judges were also part of the social structure that kept disputes within limits or failed to do so.

Five Views of Religious Life in an Eleventh-Century Iranian City

A ruler and his officials: Religion was a source of legitimacy and a problem of order. A ruler wanted the backing of respected scholars, the recognition of the caliph, and quiet streets. A Zoroastrian, Jewish, or Christian household: Religion defined a legally recognized community with its own leaders, courts, and rituals, but also a subordinate position. Security depended on payment of taxes, on the good will of local authorities, and on avoiding provocation. Members of these communities watched disputes among Muslims with an interest that was not merely academic.
A Sufi shaykh and his followers: Religion was a path of discipline and closeness to God that might cross the boundaries between law schools and, in some cases, between Sunni and Shiʿi devotion. Such figures were respected by many ordinary people, and they could be admired by rulers, but they also attracted suspicion from some jurists who considered certain practices improper.
Section 40.15

Rulers, Legitimacy, Toleration, and Coercion

Rulers in this period used religion in several ways at once. They sought legitimacy by presenting themselves as protectors of Islam, they built or supported institutions that served their purposes, and they sometimes acted against groups they regarded as dangerous. None of these actions followed automatically from a ruler's own beliefs, and the record is mixed.

The Buyids offer the clearest example of complexity. They were of Daylami origin and Shiʿi in affiliation, yet they did not abolish the Abbasid caliphate. They kept a Sunni caliph in Baghdad as a source of legitimacy, while ruling in his name and controlling his finances. Most of their subjects were Sunni, and the Buyids appointed Sunni judges as well as Shiʿi officials. Their patronage of Shiʿi observances, discussed above, was real but selective. The Ghaznavids, who ruled from Ghazna in what is now Afghanistan, were Sunni and Hanafi, and Mahmud of Ghazna cultivated the image of a champion of Sunni Islam against Ismailis and other groups. Sources report that when his forces took Rayy in 1029 they acted against Muʿtazili and Shiʿi scholars and seized or burned books. At the same time, he acted against Ismaili communities in Multan, and he sought caliphal recognition in return. These actions reflected religion, but they also served political aims, because Mahmud's enemies included the Buyids and the Fatimids, rulers whose legitimacy rested on competing claims.

The caliph himself took an active part in defining orthodoxy during this period. Al-Qadir (r. 991–1031) sponsored doctrinal documents in 1017–1019 and further books in 1029, associated with the Qadiri Creed; a concise form was publicly reaffirmed under his successor al-Qaʾim in 1040–1041, as the Primary Source Window earlier in this chapter explained. Its significance lay as much in who issued it as in what it said: the caliph, who had little military power under the Buyids, could still claim doctrinal authority. The jurist al-Mawardi (d. 1058) produced a theoretical account of government in his work on the rules of governance, in which the caliph was the legitimate head of the community, and rulers who held actual power could be recognized as exercising delegated authority. Historians continue to debate how far al-Mawardi described existing practice and how far he tried to preserve a role for the caliphate in a world of strong sultans.

The Seljuks, as Chapter 38 explained, entered Baghdad in 1055 as Sunni champions and Hanafi in affiliation. Their early reign also shows the ruler's religious policy shifting with the vizier in office. According to the sources, the early vizier of Tughril, al-Kunduri, supported a campaign in Khurasan during which preachers were instructed to curse certain groups from the pulpit, among them Shiʿis and the Ashʿaris. Ashʿari scholars in Nishapur, including al-Juwayni, left the region for a time. The campaign is recorded in several sources, though its scope and duration remain uncertain. Nizam al-Mulk, whose role in sponsoring the Nizamiyya madrasas is the subject of Chapter 39, later reversed the practice and brought Ashʿari scholars back, an action historians read as a decision about political coalition-building rather than a straightforward reflection of doctrine.

Toleration, in the sense of principled acceptance of religious difference, was not the usual language of the period, and the word should be used carefully. What historians observe instead is a range of practical accommodations that depended on circumstance. Protected non-Muslims enjoyed a defined legal position. Different Sunni schools and, to a lesser degree, Shiʿi communities were generally allowed to maintain their own institutions, though not always without hostility. Ismailis, by contrast, were regarded by many Sunni authorities as a political and religious threat, especially after the Fatimid challenge became a matter of state policy, and they faced active suppression. Coercion existed alongside accommodation, and the balance changed over time.

Section 40.16

Everyday Religious Life: Ritual, Calendar, Preaching, and Piety

Most of the people who lived in the Iranian world in the tenth and eleventh centuries were farmers, herders, artisans, and small traders, and most had little contact with the great scholars whose works this chapter has discussed. For them, religion was first of all practice: the daily prayers, the fast of Ramadan, the payment of alms, the burial of the dead, the marking of marriages and births, and for some the long journey to Mecca on the pilgrimage. The mosque was the place where the community gathered, especially for the Friday congregational prayer, and the sermon delivered on that day was a public occasion through which rulers and religious leaders addressed the population.

Popular preachers, storytellers, and exhorters formed another channel. Preachers delivered sermons in the mosques and in the open, recounting stories of the prophets and early Muslims, urging repentance, and warning of the afterlife. Learned scholars sometimes disapproved of them for including unreliable stories, and Sufi shaykhs and itinerant preachers appealed to wider audiences than the scholars. For a population that was largely non-literate, such oral performance was a primary means of learning about religion, and we know of it chiefly from the criticisms of educated writers, which means that the preachers' own viewpoint is poorly preserved.

The ritual year was also not purely Islamic in its observances. The Iranian solar calendar, with its festival of Nowruz at the spring equinox and the autumn festival of Mihragan, remained in use for agricultural and administrative purposes and was celebrated at courts, even though the Islamic lunar calendar governed prayer and fasting. Zoroastrian communities continued their own cycle of observances, as noted above. The coexistence of calendars reflects the way that earlier customs persisted in Islamic society, and it should not be read as a sign that people were unconvinced of their Islam. Many practices that scholars debated, such as the use of amulets, visits to graves, and requests for the intercession of saints, were part of everyday religion that did not fit neatly into the categories used by scholars.

Women took part in religious life in several ways. They prayed, fasted, and visited graves, and in some cities they attended gatherings for preaching and Sufi meetings. The biographical dictionaries that record scholars also mention a number of women who transmitted hadith and issued certificates of transmission, usually within scholarly families, though such women are less often recorded than men and we know them mainly through the male authors who listed them. Their presence shows that women's religious knowledge could carry recognized authority in some settings, while access to formal teaching posts and judgeships was closed to them.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Friday in a Provincial Iranian City

The Friday prayer was one of the few occasions that brought a large part of an urban Muslim population into a single space. Merchants closed their shops for the congregational prayer, the preacher invoked blessings on the ruler as well as on the caliph, and the form of the invocation was itself a statement about political loyalty. Mentioning a ruler's name from the pulpit, or ceasing to do so, was one of the ways a city announced a change of allegiance.

Around the mosque, the day also had a social and economic side. Water carriers, beggars, booksellers, and scribes gathered nearby, and endowments that supported the mosque, a school, or a bath provided work for caretakers, teachers, and servants. A boy might learn to recite the Qurʾan in a neighborhood school, and a few of the more gifted might go on to study law or hadith. Women's access to the mosque varied with place and custom. Non-Muslim neighbors observed the same day in other ways, with Jewish households marking the Sabbath on Saturday and Christians gathering on Sunday. Historians reconstruct such scenes from chronicles, biographical dictionaries, legal texts, and the occasional document, and they recognize that these sources describe urban and literate life much better than rural life, where evidence is thin.

Division V · §§40.17–40.20 Theology, Orthodoxy, and the Institutions That Endured
Section 40.17

Al-Juwayni and al-Ghazali: Two Scholars in a Changing Religious World

Two scholars of Khurasan illustrate how the institutions and debates of this chapter shaped individual careers. The first, ʿAbd al-Malik al-Juwayni (1028–1085), was a Shafiʿi jurist and Ashʿari theologian of Nishapur, known by the title Imam al-Haramayn, 'Imam of the Two Sanctuaries,' which he gained after a period in Mecca and Medina. He had left Khurasan during the campaign against the Ashʿaris described in the previous section, and he returned after the policy was reversed. He taught at the Nizamiyya madrasa in Nishapur, where he trained a large number of students. His works include a systematic statement of Ashʿari theology, a major treatise on the principles of Islamic jurisprudence, and a study of political theory written for a ruler. He was known for a careful, argumentative style and for his willingness to revise the earlier positions of his school.

The second, Abu Hamid Muhammad al-Ghazali (1058–1111), was born at Tus in Khurasan and studied in Nishapur, where he became one of al-Juwayni's students. After his teacher's death he joined the circle around Nizam al-Mulk, and in 1091 he was appointed to teach at the Nizamiyya in Baghdad, one of the most prestigious posts in the Sunni world. In Baghdad he wrote on law and on theology and produced two works that responded directly to contemporary challenges. One was a critique of the philosophers, the Tahafut al-Falasifa, usually translated 'The Incoherence of the Philosophers,' written around 1095. In it he criticized a number of positions of the Aristotelian philosophers, among whom he singled out Ibn Sina, and he identified three of them as incompatible with Islam: the eternity of the world, the claim that God does not know particulars, and the denial of the bodily resurrection. The second was a set of writings against the Ismailis, often called the Batiniyya, in which he argued against their claim that a hidden infallible teacher was needed. One of these, the Mustazhiri, was written at the request of the Abbasid caliph al-Mustazhir.

In 1095, according to his own later account, al-Ghazali experienced a spiritual and physical crisis that left him unable to teach. He left Baghdad, spent years in travel and retreat that included time in Damascus, Jerusalem, and the Hijaz, and devoted himself to the practice of Sufism. During this period he composed his large work Ihya' ʿUlum al-Din, 'The Revival of the Religious Sciences,' a guide to ritual, ethics, and spiritual discipline that joined the study of law with inner devotion. He later returned to teaching at Nishapur at the urging of Nizam al-Mulk's son, and he ended his life in Tus in 1111. His influence in later centuries was very large, in Sunni thought and among scholars of other traditions. His own period was narrower in its reception: contemporaries debated his positions, and some criticized the Ihya' for its treatment of hadith and for its Sufi elements. Historians therefore avoid describing him as the single figure who 'ended' philosophy or 'reconciled' law and Sufism, since both claims overstate the evidence.

Primary Source Window III

Al-Ghazali, al-Munqidh min al-Dalal ('The Deliverer from Error')

Creator: Abu Hamid al-Ghazali (d. 1111) · Date: written near or after his return to teaching around 1106; precise date debated (often placed c. 1106–1109) · Type: Arabic intellectual and spiritual autobiography with an account of four groups of seekers

Context: The work is a retrospective account of the author's search for certainty, written some years after the events it describes and after his return to teaching. It was composed for readers who wanted to understand his career, and it is also a defense of the choices he made.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): Al-Ghazali describes how, as a young man, he examined the competing claims of those who sought truth, and divided them into four groups: the theologians, the Batiniyya who relied on an authoritative teacher, the philosophers, and the Sufis. He reports that he found the theologians' arguments useful for defending the faith but not sufficient to give him certainty, rejected the claims of the teaching authority, accepted parts of the philosophers' learning while criticizing others, and concluded that the way of the Sufis, based on direct experience and practice, offered what he was seeking. He also tells of his decision to leave his teaching post and his spiritual crisis. Students should consult a published translation before quoting any wording.

Analysis: The source shows that a leading Sunni scholar of the late eleventh century saw the intellectual landscape as a field of competing approaches, among which kalam, Ismaili teaching, philosophy, and Sufism were distinct options. It also shows a prominent teacher moving from a career in the state-supported madrasa system to a life of spiritual discipline, and it suggests that the two worlds of law and Sufism could be joined within one life.

Limitations: The text is a retrospective autobiography, and its narrative of crisis and resolution is shaped by the author's later purposes. Historians cannot assume that every detail, including the schematic division of seekers, reproduces the actual sequence of his development. It also says little about the politics of his departure from Baghdad, a subject on which scholars have offered several explanations.

Perspective: The source represents the viewpoint of a Sunni jurist-theologian who became a Sufi. The perspectives of the philosophers and Ismailis whom he criticizes are known mainly through his descriptions, and the work does not directly record the views of ordinary believers.

Section 40.18

Faith, Reason, Tradition, and the Boundaries of Orthodoxy

The careers of al-Juwayni and al-Ghazali formed part of a longer debate over the relation of faith, reasoning, and inherited tradition. The scholars of the period did not divide neatly into rationalists and traditionalists. Muʿtazili thinkers such as ʿAbd al-Jabbar (d. 1025), who worked in Rayy under Buyid patronage, argued that reason could establish many truths about God and justice. Ashʿari theologians used rational argument but placed greater emphasis on divine power and on revelation, and Maturidis in the east did something similar with their own emphases. Hanbali traditionalists distrusted speculative theology and preferred to affirm the statements of the Qurʾan and the early community without asking how. Philosophers such as Ibn Sina, treated in Chapter 43, pursued a different method again, drawing on Greek and Arabic philosophical sources. Sufis claimed an experiential knowledge that was not confined to any of these schools.

Each of these groups claimed to represent the true faith, and the question of who counted as an authentic member of the community was itself contested. The phrase ahl al-sunna wa'l-jamaʿa, 'the people of the tradition and the community,' was claimed by Hanbalis, Ashʿaris, and Maturidis, who disagreed with one another about what it implied. The issue of takfir, declaring another Muslim an unbeliever, was a serious matter. Al-Ghazali's late work on the subject, the Faysal al-Tafriqa, cautioned against hasty accusations of unbelief and tried to limit the grounds on which they could be made, even though he had himself named three philosophical positions as unbelief. Debates of this kind show that scholars recognized the danger of treating differences of opinion as grounds for exclusion, and they also show that the practice was not unknown.

The word 'orthodoxy' therefore requires care. In Christian history it often refers to doctrines defined by councils, but Islam had no comparable central body. What functioned as orthodoxy at a given time and place was the outcome of competition among scholars, schools, and sometimes rulers. The caliph's creed, the Nizamiyya curriculum, and the opinions of widely respected scholars all helped to define a mainstream, but the boundaries remained porous. Sunni scholars also drew on one another's work across school lines, and Shiʿi scholars participated in the same debates over reasoning and tradition, as al-Mufid's treatise showed. This chapter's evidence suggests that no single party won the argument in this period. The position that later became dominant in much of Sunni Islam, which combined Ashʿari theology with Shafiʿi or Hanafi law and a measure of Sufi practice, emerged gradually and was never universal.

Section 40.19

Religious Institutions after Seljuk Fragmentation

The unity of the Seljuk state did not long survive the death of Malikshah in 1092. Nizam al-Mulk had been killed a few weeks earlier, and Malikshah's death was followed by competing claims among his sons and relatives, as Chapters 38 and 39 described. From then on, the Seljuk realm was divided among rival branches and, increasingly, among local commanders and atabegs, military guardians of young princes who acquired regional power. Sanjar, who governed Khurasan from the late 1090s and held the title of sultan from 1118, maintained a prestigious but limited authority until his defeat by the Qara-Khitai in 1141 and his capture by Oghuz groups in 1153. In the later twelfth century the Khwarazmshahs and others rose to power in the east, and the Abbasid caliph al-Nasir (r. 1180–1225) rebuilt caliphal authority in Iraq.

The religious institutions that the Seljuks and their predecessors had supported survived this fragmentation, and in some ways they spread. Madrasas continued to be founded by local rulers, officials, and wealthy individuals, because the model offered prestige and secure endowments. The waqf system, whose legal form did not depend on any particular dynasty, kept many schools and mosques going. The networks of scholars formed by chains of teachers and students, discussed earlier in this chapter, continued to link cities that were politically divided. A student could still travel from Nishapur to Baghdad or Rayy and find teachers and fellow students who recognized his credentials.

Urban rivalries remained intense, and in some places they sharpened as central authority weakened. The conflicts between Hanafi and Shafiʿi factions in cities such as Isfahan and Rayy are mostly documented for the twelfth century, and they sometimes drew in local rulers. Imami scholarship continued, especially in Rayy and its region and in Khurasan. The Imami exegete al-Tabrisi (d. 1153), who worked in Sabzavar, produced a major commentary on the Qurʾan, and a Shiʿi author of Rayy composed in Persian, in the middle of the twelfth century, a polemical work known as Kitab al-Naqd in reply to a Sunni attack on the Shiʿa. That work indicates both the continued presence of Shiʿi communities in Iranian cities and the continued rivalry with their Sunni neighbors. The Ismailis, whose fortress at Alamut and subsequent history are treated in Chapter 41, remained a powerful presence in the Alborz and elsewhere.

Sufism also developed institutionally. Khanaqahs spread, and the figures who became associated with later organized brotherhoods began to teach. Ahmad-i Jam (d. 1141) in Khurasan was a prominent Sufi shaykh with a large following, and in Baghdad ʿAbd al-Qadir al-Jilani (d. 1166), a Hanbali preacher and teacher, drew many followers; a brotherhood later took his name, though the organization of the order came after his lifetime. Abu'l-Najib al-Suhrawardi (d. 1168), also in Baghdad, taught a form of Sufism that later became the basis of another brotherhood. The Kubrawiyya, associated with Najm al-Din Kubra, belongs mainly to the following century. These are examples of a general trend toward the institutionalization of Sufi teaching, but it would be a mistake to describe the twelfth century as the point at which Sufi orders in their later form already existed.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Religious Institutions, c. 900–1200

What Changed

  • Madrasas multiplied and became a regular route into judgeships, teaching posts, and administrative careers, especially after the mid-eleventh century
  • The Ashʿari-Shafiʿi alliance gained institutional backing in many cities, and Sufism acquired a more organized, lodge-based form
  • Imami scholarship developed a more systematic legal and theological tradition, with centers in Baghdad, Qom, Rayy, and, later, further regions
  • Political authority fragmented after Malikshah's death, while religious institutions continued under many local patrons

What Stayed the Same

  • The Sunni majority across most of the plateau, alongside minority Shiʿi, Zaydi, Ismaili, and non-Muslim communities
  • The dependence of scholars on endowments, patrons, and networks of teachers and students
  • Rivalry among law schools and theological currents, and the use of public ritual to claim urban space
  • The ordinary rhythms of prayer, fasting, preaching, saint visitation, and the festivals of the calendar

Why the pattern matters: The period shows institutions changing more slowly than politics. Dynasties rose and fell within a few decades, while the schools, endowments, and scholarly networks that carried religious life persisted, which is one reason that this chapter treats the 'Sunni revival' as a reorganization rather than a sudden turn.

Section 40.20

Synthesis and Review: Religious Institutions and Communities, c. 900–1200

The preceding sections have followed the religious life of the Iranian world from the tenth century to the twelfth. Three conclusions emerge. First, Islam was well established across much of the Iranian world by 900, although conversion remained uneven and non-Muslim communities persisted. The period examined here was centrally one of internal organization, rivalry, and institution-building within Islam, even as religious change continued. Second, the communities within Islam were plural: Sunni schools of law and theological currents, Twelver Shiʿis, Zaydis, Ismailis, Sufis, and a variety of non-Muslim minorities. Third, institutions such as madrasas, endowments, and scholarly networks, far from being the invention of a single dynasty or vizier, grew out of earlier practice and were supported by many patrons with different interests.

The section also invites students to think of the 'Sunni revival' as a historical problem and not a settled label. The evidence shows Sunni institutions growing in scale and political backing, particularly under the Seljuks, but also shows continuities with earlier centuries and a continuing diversity within the Sunni community. The Shiʿi communities, meanwhile, developed a systematic scholarly tradition in the same period and sometimes under the protection of rulers who were not themselves Shiʿi. The two stories are connected and should be read together.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined the religious communities and institutions of the Iranian world between about 900 and 1200. It began from the point that the region was by then a predominantly Muslim society in which Islam had developed many forms of law, theology, devotion, and community. The study of this period therefore concerns the organization and rivalry of communities within Islam, and the place of non-Muslim minorities beside them, and not the arrival or spread of a new faith.

Among Sunni Muslims, the four surviving schools of law, the Hanafi, Shafiʿi, Maliki, and Hanbali, were distinct from the theological currents of the period, which included the Ashʿari, Maturidi, traditionalist, and Muʿtazili. The chapter emphasized that the two classifications cross: Hanafi law, for example, was associated with Maturidi theology in much of the east, and Shafiʿi law with Ashʿari theology in many centers. The Hanafi school was dominant in much of the east, and the Shafiʿi school was strong in western Iran and Khurasan, so that a city's religious life often reflected competition between them.

The 'Sunni revival' was presented as a historical problem. Sunni Islam did not originate with the revival, and the evidence for growth in Sunni institutions in the eleventh century is strong, but historians debate whether the process is best described as a revival, a consolidation, or a reorganization. The chapter argued that the evidence supports continuity with earlier developments and attention to the political circumstances, including the Buyid period, the Fatimid and Ismaili challenge, and the Seljuk need for legitimacy.

Institutions were central to this story. Mosques, informal teaching circles, and the certification of transmission through the ijaza long preceded the madrasa, and endowments, waqf, supported a range of religious and charitable functions. The madrasa, which was not a Seljuk invention, grew in scale and prestige with the support of the Seljuk state and Nizam al-Mulk, whose Nizamiyya schools were among the best known, although not the only ones. Judges, jurists, and muftis administered law and gave opinions, and their relationship to political power was a continuing subject of negotiation and debate.

Sufism was also not a Seljuk creation. Ascetic and mystical piety existed from the early centuries of Islam and developed in the tenth and eleventh centuries into teaching circles and lodges, with texts such as al-Qushayri's Risala presenting Sufism as compatible with the law. Figures such as Abu Saʿid ibn Abi'l-Khayr drew large followings in Khurasan, and the lodge, khanaqah, became a recognizable institution. The later brotherhoods took shape after the period covered here.

The Shiʿi communities were distinct and must not be conflated. Twelver Shiʿism, with its doctrine of twelve imams and the Occultation, developed a systematic body of law and theology in the works of al-Kulayni, Ibn Babawayh, al-Mufid, al-Murtada, and al-Tusi, in centers such as Baghdad, Qom, and Rayy, and later Najaf. Zaydis maintained a state and communities in the Caspian highlands, and Ismailis, linked to the Fatimid caliphate, organized a missionary network. Medieval Iran was not predominantly Twelver, and the seizure of Alamut in 1090 and the division of 1094 belong to the end of the period and to Chapter 41.

Public religious life, including Ashura, Ghadir Khumm, and shrine visitation, gave form to community identity and sometimes led to conflict. Urban quarters, factions, and rival schools shaped relations among Muslims, and Zoroastrian, Jewish, and Christian communities lived under a framework of protection and limitation. Rulers used religion for legitimacy and order in ways that included patronage, accommodation, and coercion, and the Buyids, Ghaznavids, and Seljuks acted in different ways according to their circumstances.

Al-Juwayni and al-Ghazali showed how these institutions and debates shaped individual lives. Al-Ghazali's critiques of the philosophers and the Ismailis, his crisis and his later writings on the religious sciences were products of his position in the madrasa system and of the intellectual options open to him. The broader debate over faith, reason, and tradition did not produce a single winner, and the idea of orthodoxy was contested.

After the fragmentation of the Seljuk state, religious institutions persisted under local patrons, and Sufi teaching, Imami scholarship, and urban rivalries continued. The pattern of the period is one of institutions changing more slowly than politics, and of a religious landscape that was plural throughout.

Key Terms

Madhhab
A school of Islamic law, named for an early jurist and defined by a method and a body of rulings; the Sunni schools of this period were the Hanafi, Shafiʿi, Maliki, and Hanbali.
Fiqh
Islamic jurisprudence, the scholarly effort to derive rulings concerning worship and social life from the Qurʾan, the Prophet's example, and legal reasoning.
Kalam
Theology, the rational discussion and defense of doctrine concerning God, creation, and human responsibility.
Ashʿari
A theological school named for al-Ashʿari (d. c. 935) that used rational argument to defend traditional doctrine, emphasizing divine power and the authority of revelation.
Maturidi
A theological school named for al-Maturidi (d. c. 944) of Samarkand, widely followed among Hanafis in the east, giving somewhat greater scope to reason.
Muʿtazila
A rationalist theological movement that emphasized divine justice and unity and the capacity of reason to know God's attributes.
Traditionalist (ahl al-hadith)
A broad current that gave priority to transmitted reports and was suspicious of speculative theology; Hanbali scholars were prominent in it.
Hadith
A report of the sayings or actions of the Prophet Muhammad, transmitted through a chain of narrators and evaluated by scholars.

A certificate in which a teacher authorizes a student to transmit a text or teach a subject, a basic form of scholarly credentialing.

A school for the study of the religious sciences, especially law, usually supported by an endowment, that became an important institution in this period.

The name given to madrasas associated with Nizam al-Mulk, the best known of which were in Baghdad and Nishapur.
Waqf
A legal endowment in which property is dedicated to a religious or charitable purpose, and whose income supports an institution.
Qadi
A judge who applied Islamic law, appointed by a ruler or his officials.

A broad tradition of ascetic and mystical piety within Islam, which in this period developed teaching circles, texts, and lodges.

A lodge for Sufi gatherings, teaching, and hospitality, supported by endowments or gifts.

The doctrine of leadership of the Muslim community; in Shiʿi traditions it refers to a divinely guided line of imams descended from ʿAli and Fatima.

The branch of Shiʿism that recognizes twelve imams, the last of whom is believed to be in occultation.

In Twelver belief, the concealment of the twelfth Imam, divided in the tradition into a lesser and a greater occultation.
Zaydi
A Shiʿi tradition that holds that the imamate belongs to any qualified descendant of ʿAli and Fatima who rises in arms; its main communities were in the Caspian highlands and Yemen.

A Shiʿi tradition that follows a different line of imams from the Twelvers, linked in this period to the Fatimid caliphate and its missionary network.

The tenth of Muharram, on which the death of al-Husayn at Karbala in 680 is commemorated, especially in Shiʿi communities.

The visiting of a tomb, whether of a prophet, an imam, a scholar, or a saint, a widespread practice that some scholars criticized.
Dhimmi
A member of a protected non-Muslim community, primarily Jews, Christians, and Zoroastrians, with defined rights and obligations under Islamic law.
Takfir
The declaration that a Muslim has fallen into unbelief, a grave accusation that scholars sought to limit.
Batiniyya
A label used by opponents, especially Sunni writers, for groups, particularly Ismailis, said to seek a hidden inner meaning in scripture.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how religion in the Iranian world was lived through institutions, communities, and public practice as well as through belief. Madrasas, endowments, shrines, lodges, and scholarly networks carried religious life across political changes, and the study of them explains why religious communities survived the rise and fall of dynasties.

The chapter also demonstrates the value of resisting simple labels. 'Sunni revival' and 'orthodoxy' are useful starting points, but the evidence shows continuity with earlier centuries and contest within each community. Similarly, the Shiʿi world was a set of distinct traditions in different places, and the medieval Iranian plateau was not predominantly Twelver. Keeping these distinctions in view helps students avoid reading later outcomes into earlier periods.

Later chapters build on what has been set out here. Chapter 41 turns to Ismailism and the Nizari state, and Chapters 42 and 43 take up literature and the sciences, where the figures and institutions of this chapter appear in a different light. The religious landscape that the chapter describes, with its plural communities and long-lived institutions, forms part of the background to the larger transformations that follow in the textbook.
