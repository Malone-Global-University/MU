The Kushan Empire and the Silk Roads

Indian History: South Asia from the Indus Civilization to the Present

Chapter Opening

Chapter 20 left the northwestern subcontinent as a crossroads inhabited by a shifting succession of Indo-Greek, Shaka, and Indo-Parthian rulers, each ruling substantial but comparatively fragmented territories, each issuing coinage that blended Greek, Iranian, and South Asian conventions, and none able to weld the entire zone into a single durable empire. This chapter asks how one further group of newcomers, arriving from the Central Asian steppe by way of Bactria, eventually succeeded where these predecessors had not — and what kind of empire resulted when they did.

A gold coin, struck at an uncertain mint somewhere between the Oxus and the Indus, shows a bearded king standing at a fire altar, his Bactrian-language legend proclaiming him "King of Kings." On the coin's reverse stands a deity whose iconography draws simultaneously on Iranian fire cult, Greek visual convention, and a name transliterated from a Central Asian language now largely lost to us. A stone inscription, discovered by chance in northern Afghanistan in 1993, records a king's decision to build a sanctuary and traces his ancestry back through three generations of predecessors, naming Indian cities deep in the Gangetic world that had come under his authority. A limestone relief from Gandhara depicts the Buddha in a standing posture, robed in something resembling a Mediterranean toga, his hair arranged in wavy locks that would not look out of place on a Hellenistic philosopher, while a red sandstone image from Mathura depicts a very different Buddha, seated, robed close to the body, radiating an entirely distinct visual idiom rooted in older South Asian sculptural traditions. These four objects — a coin, an inscription, and two sculptures — belong to a single historical phenomenon: the Kushan Empire, the subject of this chapter.

The Kushans have posed an unusual challenge to historians precisely because their empire does not fit comfortably inside any single national or regional historical tradition. Chinese chroniclers preserve fragments of their Central Asian origins. Bactrian inscriptions, written in a Greek-derived alphabet, alongside Prakrit inscriptions in Brahmi and Kharoshthi, preserve fragments of their royal ideology. Roman geographers and merchants occasionally mention peoples and goods connected to their world. Buddhist literary tradition, composed centuries after the events it claims to describe, preserves a heavily elaborated memory of one Kushan ruler in particular. And an enormous body of coinage, preserved in enormous quantities across a very large territory, preserves perhaps the single richest body of contemporary evidence for how Kushan kingship represented itself to its own subjects. No one of these bodies of evidence, read alone, could reconstruct Kushan history responsibly. Read together, with appropriate care for what each can and cannot establish, they allow historians to reconstruct an empire that connected South Asia to Central Asia, Iran, and the wider Eurasian commercial and cultural world.

This chapter's central argument can be stated directly. The Kushan Empire was not simply another dynasty that happened to conquer part of northwestern India. It emerged from a specific Central Asian political world — the fragmented and reconsolidating Yuezhi confederation examined in Section 21.1 — and it built its imperial system by absorbing, adapting, and building upon institutions already present among the Greek, Shaka, and Indo-Parthian rulers examined in Chapter 20, rather than sweeping them aside and starting anew. The resulting empire linked Bactria, the Kabul valley, Gandhara, the Indus system, the Punjab, and the western Gangetic world, including the great city of Mathura, into a single, if unevenly integrated, political system, one whose commercial reach extended toward Central Asian and Iranian trade networks and, through them, toward the broader Eurasian world associated with the phrase "Silk Roads." Along the way, this chapter will examine how Kushan coinage communicated imperial authority to a religiously and culturally diverse population, how Kushan patronage intersected with the development of Buddhism without making the Kushans a "Buddhist dynasty" in any simple sense, and how Gandharan and Mathuran artists, working under the wide umbrella of Kushan political stability, produced two of the most historically significant artistic traditions in early South Asian history.

Students should resist, from the outset, two tempting oversimplifications. The first treats the Kushans as simply "Indian," a dynasty whose Central Asian origins are an exotic footnote to what is really an episode of Indian history. The second treats the Kushans as simply "foreign conquerors," briefly ruling parts of India before disappearing without leaving any lasting mark on South Asian civilization. Neither framing survives contact with the evidence. The Kushans built an empire whose political center of gravity moved between Bactria and northern India, whose coinage addressed several distinct cultural and religious communities simultaneously, and whose patronage helped shape South Asian Buddhist art, architecture, and religious life for centuries afterward, even as the dynasty itself eventually fragmented and gave way to successor states examined in later chapters.

By the close of this chapter, students should understand not only the sequence of Kushan rulers and the extent of Kushan territory, but why historians increasingly describe the Kushan Empire as a genuinely transregional Eurasian polity — one whose history cannot be written from within the borders of any single modern nation, and whose significance lies precisely in how thoroughly it connected South Asia to the wider ancient world.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace the Yuezhi confederation's displacement from Central Asia and its settlement in Bactria, while evaluating the limitations of Chinese historical sources for this process.
  2. Explain how the Kushan (Guishuang) branch of the Yuezhi rose to dominance over rival Yuezhi principalities under Kujula Kadphises.
  3. Describe the sequence of early Kushan rulers — Kujula Kadphises, Vima Taktu, Vima Kadphises, and Kanishka — and the evidentiary basis, including the Rabatak inscription, for reconstructing this succession.
  4. Evaluate the evidence for Kanishka's chronology, including the distinction between the Kushan era beginning around 127/128 CE and the separate Saka era of 78 CE.
  5. Describe the territorial extent and multiple political centers of the Kushan Empire, from Bactria through the Kabul valley and Gandhara to Mathura, while resisting a hard, modern-style territorial boundary.
  6. Explain the Kushan Empire's place within long-distance Eurasian commercial networks without treating "the Silk Road" as a single, continuously traveled highway.
  7. Analyze Kushan coinage as evidence for imperial ideology, religious pluralism, and administrative continuity with earlier Indo-Greek, Shaka, and Indo-Parthian issues.
  8. Assess the relationship between Kushan royal patronage and Buddhist institutions, distinguishing contemporary epigraphic and numismatic evidence from later Buddhist literary tradition.
  9. Compare Gandharan and Mathuran artistic traditions as related but historically and stylistically distinct centers of Kushan-era art.
  10. Explain how Kushan rule embedded parts of South Asia within a wider, multidirectional Eurasian exchange system involving goods, people, religious traditions, and artistic forms.
  11. Synthesize the overall historical significance of the Kushan Empire as a transregional polity connecting South Asia, Central Asia, and Iran.
  12. Distinguish secure evidence from reconstructed or debated claims throughout Kushan chronology, geography, religion, and art.

Historical Context

Chapter 20 traced the sequence of Indo-Greek, Shaka, and Indo-Parthian rulers who governed portions of the northwestern subcontinent following the fragmentation of Mauryan authority and the earlier Greco-Bactrian kingdoms examined in that chapter's opening sections. That chapter emphasized several themes this chapter now builds upon directly: that the northwest functioned as a genuine crossroads rather than a passive frontier; that political authority in the region was frequently regional and overlapping rather than unified under a single ruler; that coinage provides an unusually rich body of evidence for reconstructing political history in the absence of continuous narrative sources; and that Hellenistic, Iranian, Central Asian, and South Asian populations, institutions, and visual traditions had already been interacting in this region for more than a century before the Kushans arrived on the scene.

The Kushans did not appear in a political vacuum. By the time the Kushan branch of the Yuezhi confederation began asserting supremacy over neighboring principalities, the northwestern subcontinent had already experienced Achaemenid administration, examined in Chapter 14, Alexander's invasion and its aftermath, Mauryan incorporation, the Greco-Bactrian kingdoms, a succession of Indo-Greek rulers, Shaka (Indo-Scythian) states including the Western Kshatrapas whose fuller conflict with the Satavahanas is examined in Chapter 22, and Indo-Parthian rule associated with rulers such as Gondophares. Each of these political formations left behind institutions, administrative practices, coinage conventions, religious communities, and artistic traditions that later rulers could inherit, adapt, or reject. The Kushans, this chapter argues, were unusually effective at absorbing this inherited institutional landscape rather than destroying it.

At the same time, the Kushans brought something genuinely new to this landscape: a political and dynastic origin rooted not in the Hellenistic successor-kingdom tradition of Alexander's generals, nor in the Iranian steppe world of the Shakas and Parthians alone, but in a specific Inner Asian confederation, the Yuezhi, whose displacement from the eastern steppe and eventual settlement in Bactria are known chiefly through Chinese historical sources compiled for reasons entirely unconnected to South Asian history. This chapter must therefore begin, in a way none of the preceding chapters in this Part have needed to do, by traveling well beyond South Asia's traditional geographic frame, into the Central Asian steppe and the Chinese historiographical tradition that recorded, however imperfectly, the origins of the dynasty that would eventually rule from Bactria to Mathura.

Dating this material poses genuine and persistent difficulties. Unlike the reasonably secure Mauryan chronology anchored by Ashoka's inscriptions, examined in Chapter 16, Kushan chronology depends on a combination of numismatic sequencing, a small number of dated inscriptions, comparison with better-documented neighboring chronologies, and inference from the layered, sometimes contradictory testimony of Chinese sources compiled well after the events they describe. This chapter presents Kushan chronology, and especially the pivotal question of when Kanishka's reign began, as an actively debated matter of ongoing scholarly reconstruction rather than settled fact, while adopting the chronology best supported by current epigraphic and numismatic evidence, consistent with the chronological framework already established in Chapter 20.

Section 21.1

Yuezhi Origins

The story of the Kushan Empire does not begin in South Asia. It begins on the eastern steppe of Inner Asia, in a region historians can locate only approximately, among a confederation of peoples whom Chinese sources call the Yuezhi. This chapter must open, therefore, well outside the geographic frame this textbook has used since Chapter 1, and it must rely, for its earliest evidence, not on South Asian inscriptions or archaeology but on Chinese historical writing compiled for reasons that had nothing to do with the eventual Kushan Empire in India.

According to Chinese historical tradition, chiefly preserved in later Han-dynasty compilations describing earlier events, the Yuezhi were displaced from their original territory, likely in the vicinity of the Gansu corridor in what is now northwestern China, by a rival steppe confederation, generally identified as the Xiongnu, during the second century BCE. This displacement, students should understand, is known to us only through an external source, composed by an imperial bureaucracy with its own political interests in narrating steppe history, transmitted through a chain of textual copying, and describing events among a non-Chinese-speaking population whose own perspective on these events does not survive independently. This textbook applies to these Chinese sources the same caution it has applied throughout to other external observers, from Al-Biruni in Chapter 1 to the Greek writers who accompanied Alexander in Chapter 14: valuable, often irreplaceable evidence, but evidence produced from outside the community it describes, shaped by that outside observer's own concerns, and not to be treated as a neutral or complete record.

Displaced westward, the Yuezhi are described as passing through the region of the Ili valley and Sogdiana before eventually crossing the Oxus River (the Amu Darya) and settling in the fertile region of Bactria, corresponding roughly to northern Afghanistan and parts of southern Uzbekistan and Tajikistan. This textbook resists presenting this migration as a single, tidy, dominoing chain of displacement — steppe peoples pushing other steppe peoples in an unbroken sequence across the whole of Inner Asia — a simplified model that Chapter 20 already cautioned against in its own discussion of Shaka movements into the Iranian and South Asian world. The evidence instead suggests a more complicated, poorly documented process of displacement, negotiation, and settlement, of which the westward Yuezhi movement into Bactria is simply the best-attested strand from a Chinese vantage point.

Once settled in Bactria, the Yuezhi confederation did not immediately form a single unified state. Chinese sources describe the Yuezhi as organized into several principalities, each under its own chief, sometimes rendered in later scholarship using the Central Asian title yabghu (a title also associated more broadly with various Inner Asian steppe polities of this general era). This fragmented arrangement should sound familiar to students of Chapter 20: much like the Indo-Greek, Shaka, and Indo-Parthian polities examined in that chapter, the early Yuezhi settlement in Bactria consisted of multiple, regionally based centers of authority rather than a single centralized kingdom.

The pivotal development for this chapter's narrative is the eventual rise to supremacy of one particular Yuezhi principality: the one Chinese sources render as Guishuang, a name modern scholarship generally connects to the dynastic name "Kushan." According to Chinese historical tradition, the chief of the Guishuang principality eventually subordinated the chiefs of the other Yuezhi principalities, unifying them under his own authority and thereby laying the foundation for what would become the Kushan state, examined directly in Section 21.2. This textbook presents this consolidation as a genuine and historically significant development, while acknowledging that the surviving Chinese textual tradition offers only a schematic outline of a process that surely involved considerably more local political complexity, negotiation, and conflict than a brief external chronicle could record.

It is worth pausing, before moving south into South Asian territory proper, to note what this section's evidence does and does not establish. It establishes, with reasonable confidence, a Central Asian steppe origin for the Yuezhi confederation, a westward displacement into Bactria, an initial period of political fragmentation among several Yuezhi principalities, and the eventual rise of the Guishuang/Kushan principality to a position of supremacy among them. It does not establish precise dates for any of these developments with the confidence historians can bring to, for example, Ashokan chronology; nor does it allow historians to reconstruct Yuezhi society, religion, or internal political organization in any detail beyond what Chinese observers chose, or were able, to record. Readers should carry this evidentiary humility forward into Section 21.2, where the narrative crosses, for the first time, into territory and evidence more familiar from this textbook's earlier chapters.

Section 21.2

Formation of the Kushan State

The transition from a dominant Yuezhi principality in Bactria to a durable imperial state extending deep into South Asia occurred across several generations of rulers, whose sequence and relationships historians reconstruct primarily from coinage and from a single, exceptionally important inscription discussed at length below. This section traces that sequence: Kujula Kadphises, Vima Taktu, Vima Kadphises, and Kanishka, the last of whom receives separate, fuller treatment in Section 21.3.

Kujula Kadphises, identified by Chinese tradition as the Guishuang chief who subordinated the other Yuezhi principalities, is generally regarded by modern scholarship as the founding figure of the Kushan dynasty proper. Coinage attributed to Kujula is particularly revealing for understanding how the Kushan state formed: rather than issuing an entirely new coinage type imposed uniformly across his territory, Kujula's coins in several regions deliberately continued the types, weights, and visual conventions already established by the Indo-Greek and Indo-Parthian rulers whose territory he was absorbing. This pattern is excellent evidence for a process of political incorporation rather than wholesale institutional replacement: Kujula appears to have taken over existing administrative and monetary systems piece by piece, adapting his own coinage to match regional expectations rather than immediately imposing a single new imperial standard. This same insight — that conquest and consolidation in this region tended to proceed through absorption of existing institutions rather than their destruction — echoes a theme Chapter 20 developed regarding the Shaka and Indo-Parthian rulers who preceded Kujula, and it will recur again in this chapter's treatment of Kushan coinage in Section 21.6.

Kujula's expansion carried Kushan authority southward from Bactria across the Hindu Kush into the Kabul valley and toward Gandhara, territory previously under various combinations of Indo-Greek and Indo-Parthian control examined in Chapter 20. The precise military and political mechanisms of this expansion are not well documented; historians infer the process chiefly from the geographic distribution of Kujula's coinage and from the gradual disappearance of independent Indo-Parthian coin issues in the affected regions, rather than from any surviving narrative account of specific campaigns.

The succession from Kujula to his descendants, and the internal genealogy of the early Kushan dynasty, remained genuinely uncertain to modern scholarship until the 1993 discovery of the Rabatak inscription in northern Afghanistan, examined in full in this chapter's Primary Source Window. This Bactrian-language inscription, composed during Kanishka's reign, provides Kanishka's own genealogy: it names Kujula Kadphises as Kanishka's great-grandfather, Vima Taktu as his grandfather, and Vima Kadphises as his father. This single inscription resolved, at a stroke, a great deal of earlier scholarly uncertainty about early Kushan succession, though students should note that even this evidence leaves considerable ambiguity about the precise dates of each ruler's reign and about events during each reign beyond what coinage and this one inscription can establish.

Vima Taktu, the generation between Kujula and Vima Kadphises, is securely named in the Rabatak genealogy and in a small number of other inscriptions and coins bearing his name outright; a much more abundant anonymous copper coinage bearing the title "Soter Megas" ("Great Savior") is commonly, though not uncontroversially, associated with his reign, with some scholars proposing alternative attributions for at least part of this large series. Considerably less is known about the specific events of Vima Taktu's reign than about either his grandfather Kujula or his grandson Kanishka. His reign appears nonetheless to represent a period of continued consolidation of the territory Kujula had begun to acquire, bridging the founding generation to the more thoroughly documented reigns that followed.

Vima Kadphises, Kanishka's father, is considerably better attested, chiefly through an important body of coinage, including substantial gold issues that mark a significant moment in the monetary history of this region, examined further in Section 21.6. Vima Kadphises's coinage and inscriptions suggest further territorial consolidation extending Kushan authority more firmly into northern India, laying the direct territorial and dynastic foundation from which his son Kanishka would build the empire examined in the remainder of this chapter.

Taken together, this four-generation sequence — Kujula Kadphises, Vima Taktu, Vima Kadphises, and Kanishka — represents the process by which a single Yuezhi principality, having first achieved supremacy over its Bactrian neighbors, gradually absorbed the fragmented Indo-Greek and Indo-Parthian political landscape south of the Hindu Kush and welded Bactria and northwestern South Asia into a single dynastic state. This is precisely where Chapter 20's narrative properly hands off to this chapter: the political world Chapter 20 described as fragmented among competing Greek, Shaka, and Parthian-descended rulers is, by the end of the sequence traced in this section, substantially incorporated under a single Kushan dynasty, setting the stage for the empire's fullest territorial and institutional development under Kanishka.

Section 21.3

Kanishka

Kanishka, generally referred to in modern scholarship as Kanishka I to distinguish him from later Kushan rulers of similar name, stands as the most historically significant, and most heavily mythologized, of the Kushan emperors. This section treats him as a historically documented imperial ruler whose reign can be reconstructed through inscriptions and coinage, while carefully separating that documented history from the considerably more elaborate portrait later Buddhist literary tradition would eventually construct around him, a distinction examined more fully in Section 21.7.

The most fundamental historical question surrounding Kanishka's reign is chronological: in what year did it begin? This question matters far beyond simple date-keeping, because Kanishka inaugurated a new dating era, used in subsequent Kushan inscriptions and continued by his successors, and establishing the starting point of this "Kushan era" is essential for dating every other event this chapter and later chapters discuss in relation to Kushan history. For many decades, some scholars proposed that Kanishka's era began in 78 CE, a date that has the complicating feature of being identical to the starting point of a separate, better-documented calendar, the Saka era, used later in Indian history for entirely different purposes. This coincidence produced considerable historical confusion, and this textbook, consistent with the chronological framework already adopted in Chapter 20, treats the Kushan era and the Saka era of 78 CE as two distinct calendrical systems that should not be conflated. Based on more recent epigraphic and numismatic analysis, this textbook adopts a working chronology placing year one of Kanishka's era at approximately 127 or 128 CE, used here as the approximate chronological anchor for his accession, while acknowledging that the era's first year need not coincide exactly with the literal first year of his reign, and that Kushan chronology more broadly remains an area of active scholarly reconstruction, with alternative reconstructions, including earlier dates, continuing to be argued in the specialist literature.

The Rabatak inscription, introduced in Section 21.2, provides this chapter's richest single source for Kanishka's own self-presentation and territorial claims. Beyond recording his genealogy, the inscription describes Kanishka's decision, in an early year of his reign, to construct a sanctuary, and it lists a series of Indian cities understood to have come under his authority: Saketa, Kausambi, Pataliputra, and Sri-Champa — cities whose earlier history this textbook has already examined in connection with the Mauryan and post-Mauryan periods. The inscription itself was found in Bactria, while this particular passage concerns Kanishka's claims within India; Mathura, though not named among these specific cities, is independently and securely placed within the Kushan political sphere by other epigraphic and archaeological evidence, examined further in Section 21.4. Historians must exercise appropriate caution in interpreting this list: royal inscriptions of this kind commonly assert maximalist territorial claims intended to communicate the scale of a ruler's authority and ambition, and the presence of a city's name in such a list does not necessarily mean that city was subject to continuous, day-to-day Kushan administration in the same sense as territory closer to the empire's core. Nonetheless, the inscription's very existence, naming cities across such an enormous geographic range, is itself significant evidence for the scale of the political claims Kanishka's court was prepared to make, and for the genuine reach of Kushan influence into the heart of the Gangetic world.

The Rabatak inscription also provides evidence for Kanishka's royal ideology. It affirms his authority using titles that draw on Iranian and Central Asian royal traditions, including formulations generally translated as "King of Kings," a title with a long prior history among Iranian dynasties and one that Kanishka's inscription and coinage, examined in Section 21.6, deploy alongside other titles reflecting the composite, multi-traditional character of Kushan kingship. This composite royal ideology — drawing simultaneously on Iranian, Central Asian, Greek administrative convention, and eventually South Asian religious patronage — is a theme this chapter will return to repeatedly, because it is central to understanding how a dynasty of Central Asian steppe origin came to rule so effectively over such a religiously and culturally varied population.

Later Buddhist literary tradition, composed well after Kanishka's actual reign, associates him with the convening of a major Buddhist council, sometimes described as the "Fourth Buddhist Council," at which an authoritative body of Buddhist commentary was said to have been compiled. This textbook does not treat this later tradition as secure contemporary history. No contemporary inscription or Kanishka-era document independently confirms such a council in the specific terms later tradition describes, and the account, as it survives, reflects the concerns and literary conventions of a much later Buddhist historiographical tradition invested in establishing Kanishka's authoritative place within Buddhist sacred history. This does not mean the later tradition is worthless as evidence; it demonstrates, at minimum, that Kanishka's reign left a durable and significant impression on later Buddhist collective memory, a point developed further in Section 21.7. But historians should distinguish clearly between that later memory and what contemporary evidence can actually establish about Kanishka's specific religious activities and decisions during his own lifetime.

Section 21.4

Imperial Geography

What kind of empire, precisely, did the Kushans rule? This section addresses that question directly, resisting the temptation to draw a single, hard, modern-style boundary line around "the Kushan Empire" on a map, as though its territory possessed the same kind of continuous, uniformly administered extent that a modern state's borders imply.

At the empire's core lay Bactria, the region north of the Hindu Kush centered on the upper Oxus valley, corresponding to territory in present-day northern Afghanistan, southern Uzbekistan, and Tajikistan. Bactria was not merely the dynasty's point of origin; it remained central to Kushan political identity and, as the Rabatak inscription's Bactrian-language text demonstrates, to Kushan administrative and inscriptional culture throughout the empire's history. Kushan rulers did not treat Bactria as a peripheral frontier once their attention turned toward South Asia; rather, the empire functioned with multiple significant centers, of which Bactria was one of the most important.

South of the Hindu Kush, the Kabul valley and Gandhara formed a second crucial zone, bridging the Bactrian core to the Indus system and serving as a major center of Kushan political authority, urban life, and, as Section 21.8 will examine in detail, artistic production. The city of Purushapura, corresponding to present-day Peshawar, emerged as one of the empire's most important urban centers within this zone, closely associated with Kanishka's patronage.

Further east and south, Kushan authority extended across the Indus system and into the Punjab, territory whose earlier Indo-Greek, Shaka, and Indo-Parthian history Chapter 20 examined in detail, and which the Kushans, as Section 21.2 described, incorporated gradually rather than through a single decisive campaign. From the Punjab, Kushan influence and, in some periods, direct administrative authority extended into the western Gangetic world, reaching the great city of Mathura, which functioned as a major southern political and, especially, artistic and religious center of the empire, examined further in Section 21.8.

Students should resist imagining this geography as a solid, uniformly colored block on a historical atlas. The evidence instead suggests an empire whose intensity of control varied considerably by region and by period: core territories close to Bactria, the Kabul valley, and Gandhara likely experienced more direct and continuous administration, while more distant claims, including some of the cities named in the Rabatak inscription's territorial list, may have reflected looser forms of influence, tribute relationships, or symbolic assertion of authority rather than day-to-day governance identical across the entire claimed territory. This pattern of variable, graduated authority radiating outward from several distinct political centers — rather than uniform sovereignty within a single fixed boundary — is consistent with how many pre-modern transregional empires, including several examined earlier in this textbook, actually operated.

The essential point this section establishes, and which the rest of this chapter builds upon, is that the Kushan Empire should be understood as a genuinely cross-regional empire with several political centers — Bactria, the Kabul valley and Gandhara, and Mathura chief among them — rather than as an Indian empire that happened to possess a distant Central Asian frontier, or as a Central Asian empire that happened to possess a distant Indian frontier. Both framings distort the historical reality by privileging one region as the "real" center and demoting the others to peripheral status. The Kushan Empire's genuine historical significance lies precisely in how thoroughly it integrated these regions, whatever the practical variations in day-to-day administrative intensity, into a single political and commercial system.

Section 21.5

Silk Road Commerce

The phrase "Silk Road," or "Silk Roads," names a modern historiographical concept describing the network of overland (and, in a broader sense, maritime) routes across which goods, people, and ideas moved across Eurasia in the ancient and medieval periods. This section examines the Kushan Empire's place within this wider commercial world, while immediately cautioning against a common and historically misleading image: that of a single, continuously traveled highway running in an unbroken line from Rome to China, along which individual merchants and their goods journeyed the entire distance from one end to the other.

The reality documented by archaeology, numismatics, and surviving textual references was considerably more fragmented and regionally organized. Rather than a single road, historians describe a network of overlapping regional trade systems, linked at various points of intersection, through which goods typically passed from one regional network to the next through a series of intermediary merchants, markets, and political jurisdictions, rather than through a single continuous journey. Bactria, positioned at a crucial junction between Central Asian routes leading toward the Tarim Basin and, eventually, Chinese territory; Iranian routes leading westward through Parthian and, later, Sasanian territory toward Mesopotamia and the Mediterranean world; and South Asian routes leading southward across the Hindu Kush toward Gandhara, the Indus system, and, ultimately, the Arabian Sea and Indian Ocean commercial world examined more fully in Chapter 25 — occupied an unusually advantageous position at the intersection of several of these regional systems.

The Kushan Empire's importance to this wider commercial world derived substantially from its control over territory through which these regional networks intersected, rather than from any claim that Kushan rulers directly controlled trade along the network's entire length. Goods moving between, for example, Han China and the Roman Mediterranean world typically passed through multiple political jurisdictions and changed hands among multiple sets of merchants along the way; no single ruler, Kushan or otherwise, controlled the entire route, and Central Eurasia offered multiple alternative corridors alongside maritime routes that also carried substantial long-distance commerce. What the Kushans did control, at least within their core territories, was a set of important nodes and passages — routes through Bactria, the Kabul valley, and Gandhara — that formed important corridors within this cross-continental exchange, giving the empire genuine commercial significance and, very plausibly, creating opportunities for tolls, taxation, and commercial revenue alongside the general prosperity such trade generated.

Archaeological evidence for this commercial world includes coin hoards found far from their places of minting, including Kushan coins recovered in Central Asian contexts and Roman coins recovered in South Asian contexts; luxury goods, including textiles, semi-precious stones, and glassware, moving across considerable distances; and, more indirectly, the cosmopolitan character of Kushan-era art examined in Section 21.8, which drew visual and iconographic conventions from Hellenistic, Iranian, Central Asian, and South Asian traditions in ways that reflect sustained cultural as well as commercial contact across this network.

This chapter deliberately does not attempt to reconstruct the entire early Eurasian trade system in comprehensive detail; that broader synthesis belongs to Chapter 25, which examines trade, religion, and cultural exchange across early historic South Asia as a whole. This section's more limited task is to explain how commerce helps make the Kushan state itself historically intelligible: why Bactria, the Kabul valley, and Gandhara were worth consolidating into a single political system, why the empire's rulers had strong incentives to maintain secure routes and stable regional order, and why the empire's coinage, examined next, needed to communicate authority effectively to merchants, religious communities, and political elites operating across a very wide and culturally varied commercial and political space.

Section 21.6

Kushan Coinage

Chapter 20 taught students how to read coins as historical evidence in general terms: what a coin's weight, metal, imagery, and legend can and cannot establish about political authority, economic conditions, and cultural exchange. This section applies those same methods to a different, though related, historical question: what does Kushan coinage reveal about how the empire integrated its territories and communicated its authority to an unusually diverse population?

Kujula Kadphises's early coinage, as Section 21.2 noted, deliberately continued regional coin types already familiar to the populations he was incorporating, a pattern of monetary continuity that eased the practical and psychological transition from Indo-Greek or Indo-Parthian rule to Kushan rule. Over subsequent generations, however, Kushan coinage developed its own increasingly distinctive character. Vima Kadphises issued substantial gold coinage, a development of real economic significance: gold coinage on this scale suggests both access to substantial gold reserves, likely connected to the empire's commercial position within the trade networks examined in Section 21.5, and a deliberate assertion of imperial prestige, since gold coinage in this period carried strong associations with major, wealthy, and militarily secure states.

Kanishka's coinage, the most extensively studied body of Kushan numismatic evidence, displays several features of particular historical importance. His coins typically bear a Bactrian-language legend, written in a script adapted from the Greek alphabet, reflecting the continued administrative and cultural importance of the Bactrian language and of Greek scribal conventions inherited from the earlier Hellenistic presence in the region examined in Chapter 20. Kanishka's coins also display the distinctive Kushan tamgha, a dynastic emblem or symbol functioning somewhat like a royal seal or heraldic mark, appearing consistently across his coinage as a visual marker of Kushan royal authority.

Most historically striking, however, is the extraordinary range of deities depicted on the reverse of Kanishka's coins. Museum collections, including examples held by the British Museum, preserve Kanishka-era coins depicting deities identified through their Bactrian-language names as, among others, Mao (a moon deity with roots in Iranian and Central Asian religious tradition), Athsho (a fire deity reflecting Iranian religious influence), Wesho (a deity whose iconography and name have been connected, with some scholarly debate, to Shiva within South Asian religious tradition), and, on a smaller number of surviving issues, the Buddha himself, depicted with a name legend and an increasingly recognizable set of visual conventions.

This chapter must state directly what this remarkable numismatic diversity should, and should not, be taken to demonstrate. It should not be read as evidence that Kanishka personally believed in, or practiced devotion to, every deity that appeared on his coinage, as though the emperor's private religious convictions could be reconstructed simply by cataloguing his coin types. It should instead be read as evidence of a deliberate and sophisticated imperial strategy: Kushan kingship, ruling over a population that included worshippers of Iranian, Central Asian, Greek-influenced, and South Asian religious traditions, including a substantial and growing Buddhist community, communicated its authority by presenting itself as patron and protector of this entire religious landscape, rather than by favoring a single tradition to the exclusion of others. The coinage, in other words, is powerful evidence for Kushan imperial ideology and administrative strategy, addressed to a genuinely plural population, rather than a straightforward window into any individual ruler's personal theology.

Section 21.7

Buddhism and Royal Patronage

No aspect of Kushan history has attracted more sustained attention, or more historical embellishment, than Kanishka's relationship to Buddhism. This section addresses that relationship critically, distinguishing three distinct levels of evidence that this textbook has already flagged in Section 21.3: contemporary inscriptions and coins; archaeological evidence for Buddhist institutions; and later Buddhist literary tradition composed well after Kanishka's own lifetime.

At the level of contemporary evidence, Section 21.6 has already established that Kanishka's coinage includes a small but historically significant number of issues bearing a Buddha image and legend, alongside a much larger number of issues depicting Iranian, Central Asian, and other deities. This is genuine and valuable contemporary evidence that Buddhist imagery had achieved sufficient prominence and royal recognition, by Kanishka's reign, to appear on official coinage — a development of real historical significance regardless of how one interprets Kanishka's personal beliefs. Archaeological evidence from Kushan-era Gandhara and Mathura, examined further in Section 21.8, documents substantial investment in Buddhist monastic institutions, stupas, relic deposits, and image production during this period, indicating that Buddhist institutions flourished and expanded considerably under the broader conditions of Kushan-era political stability and commercial prosperity, whether or not every specific instance of patronage can be traced directly to the emperor's own treasury.

Later Buddhist literary tradition goes considerably further than this contemporary evidence permits. Composed generations, and in some cases centuries, after Kanishka's actual reign, this tradition presents Kanishka as an enthusiastic royal patron of Buddhism, associates him with the construction of major stupas and monasteries, and, as Section 21.3 noted, credits him with convening an important Buddhist council at which authoritative commentarial literature was compiled. This textbook does not treat these later claims as established historical fact in the way it treats the contemporary coin and inscriptional evidence. The later tradition reflects the concerns of Buddhist communities writing at a later date, invested in constructing an authoritative historical memory that placed a powerful, historically real emperor at the center of their own institutional and doctrinal history — a pattern of retrospective religious memory-making this textbook has already examined in other contexts, including the elaborated later traditions surrounding Ashoka in Chapter 16.

This does not mean the later tradition is without historical value, nor does it mean that direct evidence for Kanishka's own Buddhist patronage is limited to his coinage alone. Independent evidence, including references to a monastery at or near Purushapura that carried Kanishka's name and was associated with his foundation, together with an inscribed reliquary connected to his reign, provides more direct support for specific acts of royal Buddhist patronage than the later council tradition alone could offer. This evidence still falls well short of establishing Kanishka as a doctrinally exclusive "Buddhist emperor," but it strengthens, rather than merely gestures toward, the case that his patronage of Buddhist institutions was real and not simply a later invention. Historians can reasonably conclude that Kanishka's reign coincided with, and likely contributed to, a period of significant Buddhist institutional growth and geographic expansion, including the acceleration and expansion of Buddhist networks through Central Asia and toward the Tarim Basin along the commercial routes examined in Section 21.5 — networks along which Buddhist communities were already present in Bactria and parts of Central Asia well before Kanishka's reign, but which his period's political stability and commercial prosperity helped extend and institutionalize further, a development of major long-term significance for the history of Buddhism across Asia.

Particular caution is warranted regarding the relationship between the Kushan period and the development of Mahayana Buddhism, a major tradition within Buddhism whose fuller doctrinal development is not this chapter's primary subject. Older popular accounts sometimes credited Kanishka directly with founding, or single-handedly patronizing into existence, the Mahayana tradition. This textbook rejects that formulation as far too simple. Mahayana Buddhist ideas and textual traditions appear to have been developing gradually, across multiple regions and communities, over a period both preceding and extending well beyond Kanishka's reign, and no contemporary evidence supports crediting a single ruler with founding this broad and internally varied doctrinal development. What can be said more responsibly is that the Kushan period, with its political stability, extensive commercial connections, and substantial investment in Buddhist institutions, provided historically favorable conditions within which various Buddhist textual, doctrinal, and artistic developments, including strands that would later be identified with Mahayana tradition, continued to unfold and to spread outward along the trade networks the empire helped sustain.

Students should conclude this section understanding that the Kushans were not a "Buddhist dynasty" in any simple, exclusive, confessional sense. Kushan royal ideology, as Section 21.6 demonstrated, addressed a genuinely plural religious landscape. What the Kushan period unambiguously provided was an unusually favorable political and commercial environment within which Buddhist institutions, art, and, very plausibly, doctrinal development flourished and began spreading well beyond South Asia's traditional geographic frame.

Section 21.8

Gandharan and Mathura Art

Chapter 20 deliberately withheld extended treatment of Gandharan and Mathuran artistic development, reserving fuller discussion for the period of greatest artistic flourishing, which occurred during the period of Kushan political ascendancy and extensive religious and elite patronage. This section provides that fuller treatment, examining Gandhara and Mathura as distinct but historically connected centers of South Asian art during the Kushan period, sustained not by the state alone but by a combination of royal, monastic, merchant, and private patrons.

Gandharan art, produced chiefly in workshops located in the Peshawar basin and surrounding territory, developed a visual idiom that historians once described, somewhat reductively, as "Greek art applied to Buddhist subjects." This textbook resists that oversimplified formula. Gandharan sculpture, overwhelmingly Buddhist in subject matter, drew on Hellenistic visual conventions inherited from the region's Indo-Greek past examined in Chapter 20 — including naturalistic drapery folds, contrapposto stance, and certain facial and hairstyle conventions reminiscent of Greco-Roman sculpture — while also incorporating Iranian and Central Asian decorative elements reflecting the region's Kushan-era political and commercial connections, and while operating throughout within a deeply local Buddhist religious and institutional environment that determined its subject matter, iconographic program, and devotional purpose. Gandharan workshops produced narrative relief panels depicting episodes from the Buddha's life and previous lives, freestanding Buddha and bodhisattva images, and extensive architectural sculpture adorning stupas and monastic complexes.

Mathuran art, produced using the region's characteristic red sandstone, developed along a related but visually distinct trajectory, rooted more directly in earlier South Asian sculptural traditions already visible in pre-Kushan Indian art, including the treatment of the human body, drapery, and facial modeling. Mathuran sculpture served Buddhist, Jain, and Brahmanical religious patrons alike, reflecting the city's position as a major religious and artistic center serving multiple religious communities simultaneously, consistent with the religiously plural imperial environment examined in Sections 21.6 and 21.7.

One of the most historically significant developments associated with this period is the emergence of anthropomorphic images of the Buddha — sculptural representations depicting the Buddha in human form, rather than through the earlier South Asian Buddhist convention of representing his presence symbolically, through devices such as an empty throne, a wheel, footprints, or a bodhi tree, examined in earlier chapters' treatment of early Buddhist art and practice. This textbook treats the question of where and when the anthropomorphic Buddha image first developed as a genuine historical problem rather than a settled fact awarding priority to either Gandhara or Mathura. Both centers produced significant early Buddha images during the Kushan period, and the surviving evidence does not permit historians to award confident chronological priority to one center over the other; indeed, some scholars argue that related developments may have occurred at both centers roughly concurrently, drawing on distinct but comparably rich artistic and religious resources, rather than one center simply inventing the image and the other subsequently copying it.

What can be said with more confidence is that Kushan-era political stability, commercial prosperity, and the substantial investment in Buddhist institutions examined in Section 21.7 created conditions extraordinarily favorable to a major expansion and transformation of South Asian religious art. Gandharan and Mathuran workshops, drawing on related but distinct artistic vocabularies, together produced a body of Buddhist visual culture whose influence would extend, in subsequent centuries, across Central Asia, into the Tarim Basin, and eventually toward China, carried along the commercial and religious networks examined in Sections 21.5 and 21.9. This artistic legacy stands, alongside the empire's political and commercial achievements, among the most historically consequential outcomes of Kushan rule.

Section 21.9

India in Eurasian Exchange

This section draws together the preceding material into this chapter's central conceptual argument: under Kushan rule, portions of South Asia became deeply embedded within a much larger Eurasian system of exchange, without thereby losing their own distinct regional character or historical development. Chapter 20 established the northwestern subcontinent as a crossroads region; this chapter has shown how Kushan consolidation transformed that crossroads into the connective tissue of a genuinely transcontinental political and commercial system.

This embeddedness operated across several interlocking dimensions examined throughout this chapter. Politically, the Kushan Empire's multiple centers, examined in Section 21.4, linked Bactria, the Kabul valley, Gandhara, and Mathura into a shared, if unevenly integrated, imperial framework. Commercially, as Section 21.5 explained, Kushan territory occupied crucial junctions among overlapping regional trade networks connecting South Asia to Central Asia, Iran, and, indirectly, to more distant Mediterranean and Chinese markets. Numismatically, as Section 21.6 demonstrated, Kushan coinage drew on Greek, Iranian, and Central Asian conventions while addressing an audience that included South Asian religious communities. Religiously, as Section 21.7 showed, Kushan-era conditions supported the substantial expansion of Buddhist institutions and their eventual spread into Central Asia. And artistically, as Section 21.8 demonstrated, Gandharan and Mathuran visual traditions, developed under Kushan patronage, drew upon and synthesized artistic vocabularies from across this same wide geographic range.

It is essential, however, that students avoid a subtly distorting conclusion that could be drawn carelessly from this material: the notion that South Asia was merely the passive recipient, or endpoint, of outside "influence" flowing inward from Greece, Iran, or Central Asia. The evidence instead supports a genuinely multidirectional pattern of exchange. Buddhist ideas, texts, artistic conventions, and, quite plausibly, actual missionary monks moved outward from South Asia into Central Asia and, over subsequent centuries, into the Tarim Basin and China, carried along the same commercial routes that had earlier carried Hellenistic and Iranian influence into Gandhara. South Asian goods, including textiles and luxury items, moved into Central Asian and, indirectly, Mediterranean markets. Sanskrit and Prakrit linguistic and literary influence extended outward along these same networks. This textbook resists any framing that would make Kushan-era South Asia a mere recipient of a one-way "Silk Road influence" originating elsewhere; the historical reality, consistent with a broader theme this textbook has developed since Chapter 1's discussion of South Asia's connectivity to the wider world, is one of sustained, reciprocal exchange in which South Asian societies were active participants and, in the specific case of Buddhist religious and artistic transmission, significant exporters as well as recipients.

This multidirectional exchange should not be romanticized into a simple picture of harmonious cosmopolitan blending. Exchange of this kind occurred within a political framework structured by conquest, taxation, tribute, and unequal power relationships among the empire's various regions and populations, themes this chapter's Multiple Perspectives feature below examines directly. Cosmopolitan visual and religious culture coexisted with the ordinary realities of imperial rule: administrative hierarchy, military garrison, taxation of agricultural and commercial wealth, and, very plausibly, considerable variation in how directly different populations experienced Kushan authority depending on their proximity to the empire's core territories.

Section 21.10

Review and Historical Synthesis

This chapter has traced the formation, consolidation, and historical significance of the Kushan Empire, from its origins among the Yuezhi confederation on the Central Asian steppe through its eventual position as a transregional power linking Bactria, Gandhara, and the Gangetic world within a wider Eurasian commercial and cultural system. Several threads developed separately across the preceding nine sections converge here into a single interpretive foundation for Chapter 22 and for this textbook's continuing treatment of early historic South Asia.

The origins argument of Sections 21.1 and 21.2 established that the Kushan dynasty emerged from a specific Central Asian steppe confederation, the Yuezhi, whose westward displacement and settlement in Bactria are documented chiefly through external Chinese sources requiring the same source-critical caution this textbook has applied to other outside observers throughout the series. The rise of the Guishuang/Kushan principality to supremacy over its Yuezhi rivals, and the subsequent four-generation sequence from Kujula Kadphises through Vima Taktu and Vima Kadphises to Kanishka, reconstructed chiefly from coinage and from the pivotal Rabatak inscription, demonstrated a pattern of political consolidation achieved substantially through the absorption and adaptation of existing Indo-Greek and Indo-Parthian institutions rather than their wholesale destruction — directly continuing a theme Chapter 20 had already introduced regarding this region's earlier political transitions.

The chronological and biographical argument of Section 21.3 established Kanishka as a historically documented emperor whose era, on this textbook's adopted chronology, began around 127/128 CE — used as an approximate anchor for his accession rather than a certainly exact date, and carefully distinguished from the separate Saka era of 78 CE — and whose royal ideology, recorded in the Rabatak inscription, drew on Iranian, Central Asian, and eventually South Asian religious and political vocabulary to assert authority across an enormous claimed territory.

The geographic argument of Section 21.4 established the Kushan Empire as a genuinely cross-regional polity with multiple significant political centers — Bactria, the Kabul valley and Gandhara, and Mathura — rather than an Indian empire with a distant frontier or a Central Asian empire with a distant Indian appendage. The commercial argument of Section 21.5 explained why this particular geographic configuration mattered: Kushan territory occupied crucial junctions among overlapping Eurasian trade networks, giving the empire genuine commercial significance without implying that any single power controlled trade across the network's entire length.

The numismatic argument of Section 21.6 demonstrated that Kushan coinage, and especially Kanishka's remarkably diverse divine imagery, reflected a deliberate imperial strategy of addressing a religiously and culturally plural population rather than a simple record of any individual ruler's personal beliefs. The religious argument of Section 21.7 extended this caution to Kanishka's relationship with Buddhism specifically, distinguishing contemporary coin and inscriptional evidence, archaeological evidence for institutional growth, and considerably more elaborate later Buddhist literary tradition, while resisting the oversimplified claim that Kanishka personally founded Mahayana Buddhism.

The artistic argument of Section 21.8 examined Gandharan and Mathuran sculpture as related but historically and stylistically distinct traditions, both flourishing under Kushan-era stability and patronage, both contributing to the historically significant emergence of anthropomorphic Buddha imagery, and neither properly understood as simply derivative of the other. Finally, the exchange argument of Section 21.9 drew this material together to demonstrate that Kushan-era South Asia became deeply embedded within a wider, genuinely multidirectional Eurasian system of commercial, religious, and artistic exchange, contributing outward as much as it received influence inward, while never losing its own distinct regional historical development.

Students should carry forward from this chapter a recognition that imperial "cosmopolitanism" of the kind the Kushan Empire displayed did not erase political hierarchy, taxation, military garrison, or uneven distributions of power among the empire's regions and populations; sophisticated cultural synthesis and the ordinary machinery of imperial rule coexisted throughout. Chapter 22 now shifts this textbook's geographic focus southward and eastward, away from the Kushan-centered northern and transregional world examined in this chapter and Chapter 20, toward the Satavahana state and the independent political, commercial, and religious development of the Deccan during these same general centuries.

Primary Source Window

The Rabatak Inscription

Text: Bactrian-language inscription, written in a script adapted from the Greek alphabet · Discovery: Found in 1993 near Rabatak in northern Afghanistan · Date of composition: Early in Kanishka's reign, 2nd century CE · Type: Royal inscription recording genealogy, territorial claims, and religious dedication

Context: The inscription was discovered by chance, removed from its original context before controlled excavation, which limits what archaeologists can establish about its precise original architectural setting. Its text, however, survives well enough to be read and translated, providing historians with contemporary evidence for early Kushan dynastic history that had previously been reconstructed only indirectly from coinage.

Paraphrase: The inscription records that Kanishka, in an early year of his reign, ordered the construction of a sanctuary. It traces his descent through Vima Kadphises (his father), Vima Taktu (his grandfather), and Kujula Kadphises (his great-grandfather), and it names Saketa, Kausambi, Pataliputra, and Sri-Champa as Indian cities that had come under his authority. It also employs royal titles reflecting Iranian and Central Asian traditions of kingship.

Analysis: This inscription is historically significant on several levels: it resolves genealogical questions about early Kushan succession that coinage alone could not settle; it provides evidence for the scale of Kanishka's territorial claims, even though such claims should be read with appropriate caution regarding how uniformly they translated into actual administration; and it demonstrates the continued importance of the Bactrian language and Greek-derived script within Kushan administrative and religious culture even after the dynasty's political center of gravity had shifted substantially toward South Asia.

Historical Debate

Was Kanishka a Buddhist Emperor?

The question: Should Kanishka be described as a committed Buddhist ruler whose reign marked a decisive royal sponsorship of Buddhism, or should his relationship to Buddhism be understood more cautiously, as one strand within a deliberately plural imperial religious strategy?

Interpretation A — Kanishka as a major Buddhist patron: Later Buddhist literary tradition presents Kanishka as an enthusiastic royal patron who convened an important Buddhist council, sponsored major stupas and monasteries, and helped Buddhism expand into Central Asia. The presence of Buddha imagery on his coinage, together with substantial archaeological evidence for Buddhist institutional growth in Gandhara and Mathura during his reign, supports the view that his patronage was historically significant and specifically favorable to Buddhism among the empire's many religious communities.
Interpretation B — Kanishka as a religiously plural imperial patron: Kanishka's coinage overwhelmingly depicts Iranian, Central Asian, and other non-Buddhist deities, with Buddha images forming only a small proportion of his surviving issues. The later Buddhist council tradition lacks confirmation in any contemporary source and reflects a later Buddhist historiographical tradition invested in claiming an authoritative imperial patron. On this view, Kanishka's actual religious policy is better described as broadly inclusive patronage of a plural religious landscape, within which Buddhism was one significant beneficiary among several, rather than a specifically or primarily Buddhist royal program.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on overlapping evidence — coinage, the Rabatak inscription, and archaeological remains from Gandhara and Mathura — but they weigh the later Buddhist literary tradition differently and reach different conclusions about how central Buddhism was to Kanishka's own self-understanding as a ruler.

Why it matters: This textbook favors a version of Interpretation B, consistent with Section 21.7's argument, while acknowledging that Kanishka's reign coincided with, and likely contributed to, real and historically important Buddhist institutional growth, regardless of how one characterizes his personal religious commitment.

Student task: Using the evidence summarized in Sections 21.6 and 21.7, explain which interpretation the contemporary numismatic and epigraphic evidence more strongly supports, and explain what kind of additional evidence might help resolve the question further.

Historical Vocabulary
Yabghu, Tamgha, and Imperial Titulature

Yabghu is a title associated with chieftainship among various Inner Asian steppe confederations, used in modern scholarship to describe the leaders of the several Yuezhi principalities that settled in Bactria before Kushan unification, examined in Section 21.1. The Kushan tamgha refers to a distinctive dynastic emblem appearing on Kushan coinage and other official objects, functioning as a visual marker of royal authority comparable in some respects to a heraldic device or royal seal, examined in Section 21.6. Kushan rulers, beginning prominently with Kanishka, also adopted grander titulature, including formulations generally translated as "King of Kings," drawing on a long tradition of Iranian royal titles used earlier by Achaemenid and Parthian rulers, examined in Section 21.3.

Together, these terms illustrate a broader pattern this chapter has emphasized throughout: Kushan kingship drew deliberately on multiple prior political traditions — Central Asian steppe, Iranian, Hellenistic administrative, and eventually South Asian religious — combining them into a composite royal identity suited to ruling an unusually diverse imperial population.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Fragmented Successor States to Kushan Consolidation

What Changed

  • Political authority in the northwest shifted from multiple competing Indo-Greek, Shaka, and Indo-Parthian rulers, examined in Chapter 20, to a single Kushan dynasty controlling a much larger, if unevenly integrated, territory
  • The empire's political center of gravity expanded to include Bactria, the Kabul valley, Gandhara, and Mathura as linked centers rather than separate regional kingdoms
  • Coinage developed increasingly distinctive Kushan dynastic features, including the tamgha and an unusually wide range of religious imagery, while still drawing on earlier regional conventions
  • Buddhist institutions and artistic production expanded substantially in scale and geographic reach, extending outward into Central Asia

What Stayed the Same

  • Coinage remained the central tool of political communication and economic administration, continuing patterns established under earlier Indo-Greek and Indo-Parthian rule
  • The northwest remained a genuine crossroads region, connecting South Asia to Central Asia and Iran, rather than becoming a sealed or isolated territory
  • Political authority continued to vary in intensity by region rather than applying uniformly across an entire claimed territory
  • Multiple religious traditions continued to coexist and receive various forms of patronage rather than being replaced by a single state religion

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both continuity and change shows that the Kushan Empire represents a genuine consolidation of the northwestern political landscape rather than a total rupture with what came before, consistent with this textbook's recurring caution against describing major historical transformations as sudden, total breaks with the past.

Cause and Effect

Why Could the Kushans Build a Larger Empire?

Long-term causes

Centuries of prior political, commercial, and cultural interaction in the northwest, examined in Chapter 20, had already produced a region accustomed to absorbing and adapting outside rule, along with well-developed monetary and administrative conventions a new dynasty could inherit.

Short-term causes

The Guishuang/Kushan principality's specific success in subordinating rival Yuezhi chiefs, examined in Section 21.1, and Kujula Kadphises's strategy of absorbing rather than replacing existing Indo-Greek and Indo-Parthian institutions, examined in Section 21.2, gave the dynasty an unusually strong initial foundation.

Effect

Successive rulers extended and consolidated this foundation across four generations, producing, under Kanishka, an empire spanning Bactria to Mathura with substantial commercial, religious, and artistic significance.

This textbook's response

This chapter treats Kushan success as the outcome of a specific combination of inherited institutional advantages and effective political strategy, rather than attributing it to any single ruler's exceptional genius or to inevitable historical destiny.

Historical complexity: The same variable, graduated pattern of authority that allowed the Kushans to expand so effectively, examined in Section 21.4, also made the empire potentially vulnerable to fragmentation once central authority weakened, a vulnerability that materialized in the empire's eventual decline from the third century CE onward.

Everyday Life and Social History

Ordinary Life within a Transregional Empire

For most inhabitants of Kushan territory, daily life almost certainly continued to center on agriculture, craft production, local religious practice, and regional exchange, largely unaffected in its basic rhythms by which dynasty happened to claim distant sovereignty. Villagers in the Punjab, artisans in Mathura, monastic communities in Gandhara, and pastoral or agricultural populations in Bactria each experienced Kushan rule primarily through more immediate and local structures: taxation, market relationships, religious institutions, and local elites, rather than through direct contact with the emperor or his court.

At the same time, the commercial and religious networks this chapter has examined created real opportunities and pressures for ordinary people: merchants and caravan workers whose livelihoods depended on secure trade routes; artisans in Gandharan and Mathuran workshops whose skilled labor met sustained demand from religious patrons; and monks and lay Buddhist communities whose institutions expanded with the empire's general prosperity. Historians must be careful, as throughout this textbook, not to assume that the cosmopolitan, wealthy imperial culture visible in coinage, inscriptions, and monumental art was equally experienced, or equally beneficial, across every social group and region within this vast and unevenly integrated empire.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the formation and historical significance of the Kushan Empire, from its origins among the Yuezhi confederation of the Central Asian steppe through its development into a transregional power connecting Bactria, Gandhara, and the Gangetic world within a wider Eurasian commercial and cultural system. It has argued throughout that the Kushan Empire cannot be understood as either simply an episode of Indian history or simply a foreign interlude, but must instead be recognized as a genuinely cross-regional Eurasian polity.

The chapter began in Central Asia, tracing, through the necessarily cautious lens of external Chinese historical sources, the Yuezhi confederation's westward displacement and settlement in Bactria, and the subsequent rise of the Guishuang/Kushan principality to supremacy over its Yuezhi rivals. It then traced the four-generation sequence of early Kushan rulers — Kujula Kadphises, Vima Taktu, Vima Kadphises, and Kanishka — reconstructed chiefly through coinage and, decisively, through the 1993 discovery of the Rabatak inscription, emphasizing throughout that Kushan expansion proceeded substantially through the absorption and adaptation of existing Indo-Greek and Indo-Parthian institutions rather than their wholesale replacement.

The chapter examined Kanishka's reign in detail, adopting a working chronology placing year one of his era around 127/128 CE — an approximate anchor for his accession rather than a certainly exact date — and carefully distinguishing the Kushan era from the separate Saka era of 78 CE. It used the Rabatak inscription to establish both Kanishka's genealogy and the scale of his territorial claims, while cautioning against treating maximalist inscriptional claims as straightforward evidence of uniform administrative control.

The chapter then examined the empire's geography, arguing that the Kushan state should be understood as a genuinely cross-regional empire with several significant political centers — Bactria, the Kabul valley and Gandhara, and Mathura — rather than as an Indian empire with a distant frontier. It examined the empire's place within long-distance Eurasian commerce, cautioning against the misleading image of a single continuous "Silk Road" and explaining the empire's significance as controlling crucial junctions among overlapping regional trade networks.

The chapter's numismatic analysis showed how Kushan coinage, and especially Kanishka's remarkably diverse religious imagery, reflected a deliberate imperial strategy addressed to a religiously plural population rather than a simple record of personal royal belief. Building on this foundation, the chapter examined Kanishka's relationship to Buddhism critically, distinguishing contemporary evidence from later, considerably more elaborate Buddhist literary tradition, and rejecting the oversimplified claim that Kanishka personally founded Mahayana Buddhism.

The chapter's treatment of Gandharan and Mathuran art examined these traditions as related but historically and stylistically distinct centers of artistic production, both contributing to the historically significant emergence of anthropomorphic Buddha imagery during this period, neither properly understood as simply derivative of the other. Finally, the chapter's treatment of Eurasian exchange argued that Kushan-era South Asia became deeply embedded within a genuinely multidirectional system of commercial, religious, and artistic exchange, exporting as well as receiving influence, while never losing its own distinct regional historical development.

Throughout, this chapter has emphasized that sophisticated imperial cosmopolitanism coexisted with the ordinary machinery of conquest, taxation, and unevenly distributed power. The Kushan Empire's eventual fragmentation from the third century CE onward, addressed briefly here, would open the way for new regional powers examined in subsequent chapters, even as the empire's commercial, religious, and artistic legacies continued to shape South Asian and wider Eurasian history for centuries afterward.

Key Terms

Yuezhi
A Central Asian confederation, known chiefly through Chinese historical sources, displaced westward from the eastern steppe and eventually settled in Bactria, from among whom the Kushan dynasty emerged.
Guishuang
The Chinese rendering of the Yuezhi principality generally connected by modern scholarship to the dynastic name "Kushan," whose chief rose to supremacy over rival Yuezhi principalities.
Yabghu
A title associated with chieftainship among Inner Asian steppe confederations, used to describe the leaders of the several Yuezhi principalities before Kushan unification.
Bactria
The region north of the Hindu Kush centered on the upper Oxus valley, serving as the Kushan dynasty's original political base and remaining central to Kushan identity throughout the empire's history.
Kujula Kadphises
Chief of the Guishuang Yuezhi principality, generally identified as the founding ruler of the Kushan dynasty, who subordinated rival Yuezhi chiefs and began southward expansion.
Vima Taktu
Kushan ruler, son of Kujula Kadphises and grandfather of Kanishka according to the Rabatak inscription, associated with continued consolidation of Kushan authority.
Vima Kadphises
Kushan ruler, son of Vima Taktu and father of Kanishka, associated with important gold coinage and further territorial expansion.
Kanishka (Kanishka I)
The most historically prominent Kushan emperor, whose era, on this textbook's adopted chronology, began around 127/128 CE (used as an approximate anchor for his accession), documented through the Rabatak inscription, extensive coinage, and later Buddhist tradition.
Rabatak Inscription
A Bactrian-language inscription discovered in 1993 in northern Afghanistan, recording Kanishka's genealogy and territorial claims, providing the chapter's central epigraphic evidence.
Kushan Era
The dating system Kanishka introduced, whose year 1 is placed at approximately 127/128 CE and is used here as an approximate anchor for his accession; it need not coincide exactly with the first year of his reign, and is carefully distinguished in this chapter from the separate Saka era of 78 CE.
Saka Era
A separate Indian calendrical system beginning in 78 CE, historically confused by some earlier scholars with the beginning of Kanishka's Kushan era, but treated in this chapter as a distinct dating system.
King of Kings
A royal title with roots in Iranian dynastic tradition, adopted by Kanishka and other Kushan rulers as part of a composite imperial titulature.
Bactrian Language
An Iranian language written in a script adapted from the Greek alphabet, used in official Kushan inscriptions including the Rabatak inscription and on much Kushan coinage.
Purushapura
A major Kushan urban and political center in Gandhara, corresponding to present-day Peshawar, closely associated with Kanishka's patronage.
Mathura
A major city in the western Gangetic world serving as a key southern political, religious, and artistic center of the Kushan Empire.
Kushan Tamgha
A distinctive dynastic emblem appearing on Kushan coinage, functioning as a visual marker of royal authority comparable to a heraldic device or seal.
Silk Roads
A modern historiographical term describing the overlapping network of Eurasian trade routes connecting South Asia, Central Asia, Iran, and, indirectly, the Mediterranean and Chinese worlds; not a single continuous highway.
Mahayana Buddhism
A major Buddhist tradition whose gradual development, both preceding and extending beyond Kanishka's reign, should not be attributed to a single royal founder.
Anthropomorphic Buddha Image
A sculptural representation of the Buddha in human form, as opposed to earlier symbolic representations, whose emergence at both Gandhara and Mathura during the Kushan period is treated as a genuine historical problem rather than a settled priority claim.
Gandharan Art
A Buddhist sculptural tradition centered on the Peshawar basin, drawing on Hellenistic, Iranian, and Central Asian visual conventions within a locally rooted Buddhist religious environment.
Mathuran Art
A sculptural tradition centered on Mathura, using local red sandstone, rooted more directly in earlier South Asian sculptural conventions and serving Buddhist, Jain, and Brahmanical patrons.
Kushano-Sasanians
A successor political formation associated with Sasanian Persian influence, emerging as Kushan central authority fragmented from the third century CE onward.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to look beyond South Asia's own borders in order to understand one of its most historically consequential ancient empires. The Kushans cannot be understood by looking only at northern India, and they cannot be understood by looking only at Central Asia; their history requires holding both regions, and the networks connecting them, in view simultaneously.

The developments traced in this chapter — Yuezhi displacement and settlement in Bactria, the gradual consolidation of Kushan authority through the absorption of earlier institutions, the composite religious and political ideology visible in Kushan coinage and inscriptions, and the flourishing of Gandharan and Mathuran art under conditions of imperial stability — together demonstrate that some of the most historically significant developments in early South Asian history occurred precisely at the points where South Asia connected most intensively with the wider Eurasian world.

Students who understand the Kushan Empire as a genuinely transregional polity, who can distinguish contemporary evidence from later religious tradition regarding Kanishka's reign, and who recognize that cultural and commercial exchange under Kushan rule moved in more than one direction, are well prepared to carry this chapter's central lesson into the remainder of this textbook: South Asian history has never been a sealed, self-contained story, and some of its most important transformations occurred precisely where South Asia met the wider world.
