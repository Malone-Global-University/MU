Arabic, New Persian, and the Formation of an Islamic-Iranian Intellectual World

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

Between about 700 and 1000 CE, the lands of the Iranian plateau and Khurasan did not exchange one culture for another. Their scholars, secretaries, poets, priests, merchants, and teachers built a world of learning that worked in several languages at once, in which Arabic became the shared language of scholarship and Persian, written in a new script and enriched by new vocabulary, slowly acquired new literary work to do.

Consider a bookseller's shop in Baghdad in the later tenth century. Its owner, Ibn al-Nadim, the author of a great catalogue of books known as the Fihrist, completed his work around 987. The title itself is a Persian word taken into Arabic, and the catalogue records works written in Arabic, Pahlavi, Syriac, Greek translation, and Persian. In its pages, translated advice literature from the Sasanian court stands beside grammar, law, poetry, medicine, and history. The shop, the catalogue, and the paper on which the books were copied belong to a single world. It was an Arabic-writing world whose scholars came from Basra, Kufa, Fars, Khurasan, and Transoxiana, and it was far from a world of Arabs alone.

Now travel far to the east, along the roads of Khurasan, to Bukhara or Merv in the same century. Here, in the middle of the tenth century, scholars were being asked to turn two of the largest works of Arabic learning, a world history and a commentary on the Quran, into Persian for readers who understood Persian more easily than Arabic. A geographer wrote an entire description of the world in Persian. A governor gathered learned men to compile a prose book of the Iranian kings. At the same time, a great deal of ordinary legal and religious scholarship in the same cities continued to be written in Arabic. The people who worked on these Persian projects were not recovering a dead language. They were extending a living one into genres that had not previously been written in it, using a script borrowed from Arabic and a vocabulary that now included many Arabic words.

This chapter asks how such a world came to exist. Chapters 28 through 32 have already described the Arab-Muslim conquest, the Abbasid Revolution, the Iranian elites who served the caliphate, and the long process of Islamization. A change of government and a change of religion did not by themselves change what people spoke at home, what priests wrote in their sacred books, or how a village scribe kept accounts. Language history moves along its own timetable, driven by schooling, administration, trade, religion, migration, and prestige. The central argument of this chapter is that the Islamic-Iranian intellectual world was neither a replacement of Persian culture by Arabic culture nor a simple revival of a dormant Persian tradition. It was a new creation, produced through the interaction of Arabic, Iranian languages, inherited traditions, and institutions of learning.

The chapter has four connected tasks. It first describes the linguistic landscape of early Islamic Iran and explains how Arabic became the language of administration and, especially, of scholarship. It then shows that Iranians were not merely recipients of Arabic learning. Grammarians, historians, translators, secretaries, and scholars of Iranian origin helped shape Arabic scholarship and Arabic prose, and the translation of Sasanian books transformed Iranian memory into Arabic literature. Third, it traces the emergence of New Persian, from spoken Persian and its Middle Persian ancestry to the earliest written evidence in Hebrew and Arabic scripts, and to the first poetry and prose. Finally, it explains how Arabic and Persian came to serve different but overlapping purposes, and how scholars, books, and patrons moved across the region.

The evidence for these developments is uneven, and this chapter will repeatedly draw attention to that fact. Some of it is contemporary: papyri and documents, inscriptions, coins, and a small number of early manuscripts. Much more is later: bibliographies such as the Fihrist, biographical dictionaries, anthologies compiled centuries after the poets they preserve, and chronicles that tell of the eighth and ninth centuries from the vantage point of the tenth, eleventh, or thirteenth. Students must learn to ask of every statement about this period who recorded it, how long afterward, and with what purpose.

Three common mistakes shape the chapter's method. First, writing in Arabic does not establish that an author was ethnically Arab; the identity of each writer has to be established from evidence, not inferred from language. Second, New Persian did not emerge from a complete linguistic vacuum, because spoken Persian continued throughout the period while its writing systems and literary registers changed. Third, early literary traditions are frequently preserved in later sources, so a later attribution to a ninth-century poet, or a later account of a ruler's patronage, cannot be treated automatically as contemporary documentation.

Finally, this chapter marks a boundary of responsibility within the textbook. It establishes the linguistic and intellectual foundations of the period. It leaves the political histories of the Tahirids and Saffarids to Chapter 34, the Samanid court and the fuller flowering of New Persian culture to Chapter 35, the later development of religious institutions to Chapter 40, the Shahnameh and Firdawsi to Chapter 42, and the detailed achievements of science, medicine, and philosophy to Chapter 43. What belongs here is the formation of the intellectual environment in which all of those later developments became possible.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why political conquest and religious change did not automatically produce linguistic change in early Islamic Iran.
  2. Distinguish among Middle Persian, Parthian-derived varieties, Sogdian, Bactrian, Khwarazmian, Arabic, and early New Persian, and between literary, administrative, spoken, and regional languages.
  3. Trace the Arabization of administration and the growth of Arabic as the principal language of scholarship from the later seventh to the tenth century.
  4. Distinguish writing in Arabic from Arab ethnic identity, using specific authors as examples.
  5. Analyze the contributions of Iranian scholars to Arabic grammar, lexicography, adab, translation, and historical writing.
  6. Assess how Middle Persian books, especially advice literature and royal history, were transmitted into Arabic and transformed in the process.
  7. Interpret the Shuʿubiyya controversy as a set of debates over genealogy, culture, and status rather than as a unified nationalist movement.
  8. Explain the linguistic continuity and change that connect Middle Persian to early New Persian, and the debates over the meaning of the term Dari.
  9. Compare the scripts used to write Persian, including Pahlavi, Hebrew, Arabic, and others, and explain why the Arabic script became dominant.
  10. Evaluate the strengths and limits of the evidence for the earliest New Persian poetry and prose.
  11. Describe the circumstances in which authors, scholars, and communities chose among Arabic, Persian, and other languages.
  12. Connect the movement of scholars, books, and patronage across Baghdad, the plateau, Khurasan, and Transoxiana to the formation of a shared intellectual world.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did the political and religious transformation of Iran not immediately transform the languages its inhabitants spoke and wrote?
  2. How should historians determine the identity of an Arabic-writing scholar when language alone does not reveal ancestry, community, or self-understanding?
  3. In what sense were Iranian scholars shapers of Arabic learning rather than merely its recipients?
  4. What happens to a body of inherited stories, ethical teachings, and royal histories when it is translated, and what is preserved, adapted, or lost?
  5. Why were the debates known as the Shuʿubiyya less a nationalist movement than a quarrel about status, and why does that distinction matter?
  6. What does it mean to say that New Persian was neither invented from nothing nor merely the restoration of an ancient language?
  7. What are the strengths and weaknesses of evidence that survives mainly in later collections, anthologies, and manuscripts?
  8. Why did Persian expand in a multilingual environment, and what happened to the other Iranian languages it met?
  9. How did authors and communities decide between Arabic and Persian, and what do their choices reveal about audience, prestige, and purpose?
  10. Why is the relationship between Arabic and Persian better described as creative interaction than as competition?

Historical Context

When the armies of the early caliphate entered Iran in the middle decades of the seventh century, examined in Chapter 28, they encountered a society whose languages were varied and whose written culture was concentrated in particular institutions. At the apex of the Sasanian state, Middle Persian, written in the Pahlavi script, served as the language of royal inscriptions, the chancery, and the Zoroastrian priesthood. Its prestige reached beyond Fars, and its scribal tradition shaped record-keeping across the empire. But Middle Persian was not the only language of a large and diverse region. In Mesopotamia, Aramaic and Syriac were widely used and carried the literature of the Church of the East. In the northeast, Sogdian merchants and communities, Bactrian administrators, and Khwarazmian-speaking farmers used their own Iranian languages. In the mountains and along the Caspian coast, local dialects flourished with little or no written form.

The conquest altered the political framework in which these languages operated. The caliphate's rulers spoke and wrote Arabic, and the language was carried to the garrison cities of Basra and Kufa in Iraq and to new settlements in Khurasan, among them Merv. The administrative practices examined in Chapter 31 brought Iranian bureaucrats into the service of the caliphate and gradually moved record-keeping into Arabic. The processes of conversion, minority life, and religious demography discussed in Chapter 32 meant that an expanding population of Persian-speaking Muslims now needed Arabic for religious learning, even while continuing to use Persian for most daily purposes.

Writing, however, did not disappear. The Zoroastrian priesthood continued to copy, compose, and compile Middle Persian books, and a remarkable body of Pahlavi literature, including encyclopedic religious compilations and theological arguments, was produced under Islamic rule in the ninth and tenth centuries. Jewish and Christian communities wrote in their own scripts. In the northeast, local rulers and their secretaries kept records in Bactrian and Sogdian for several decades after the conquest. This is an essential background to everything that follows: the Islamic-Iranian intellectual world was assembled in a society that already possessed rich, plural, and competitive traditions of writing.

Two further conditions shaped the period. The first was technological and institutional: paper became increasingly available in the eighth and ninth centuries, and with it came a trade in copied books, a profession of copyists and booksellers, and a culture of private libraries. The second was political. The Abbasid revolution of 750, examined in Chapter 30, shifted the center of the caliphate eastward to Iraq and brought Khurasani supporters and Iranian administrators into positions of influence, while the gradual weakening of caliphal control in the eastern provinces, examined in Chapters 34 and 35, created regional courts that wanted scholars, poets, and secretaries of their own. In this combination of circumstances, Arabic scholarship expanded with extraordinary energy, and Persian began to be written, first in small ways and then in more ambitious ones, for new purposes.

Division I · Section 33.1

From Islamic Conquest to Intellectual Transformation

Historians who study conquests often assume that a change of rulers produces a change of culture on roughly the same timetable. The history of Iran in the first centuries of Islamic rule shows why that assumption fails. By the time the caliphate's authority was established across the plateau, the new rulers had changed the tax system, the coinage, and the religious framework of public life. Yet the great majority of people in Fars, the Jibal, and Khurasan continued to speak the languages of their parents. A farmer in the Fars countryside, a muleteer on the Khurasan road, and a village landholder who kept his estate accounts for the tax collector did not become Arabic speakers when the governor's seal changed. Arabic spread unevenly, through some channels with great force and through others hardly at all.

The reasons lie in how languages actually move. A language spreads when large numbers of people have strong reasons to use it: to obtain employment, to be understood by an official, to join a prayer, to trade with a neighbor, to marry into a family, to teach a child. Different languages attract different kinds of loyalty and serve different tasks. Under early Islamic rule, Arabic offered extraordinary advantages in the domains of religion, law, scholarship, and, increasingly, official correspondence. For daily speech among communities that already shared a language, it offered fewer. The Iranian-speaking majority therefore tended to acquire Arabic as a second language for particular purposes, while maintaining Persian and other Iranian languages in the home, the market, and the village. The pattern differed from the one in Egypt or Syria, where Arabic eventually displaced Coptic and Aramaic as the principal vernaculars, and understanding that difference is one reason why Iranian history takes the particular shape that this volume describes.

This chapter's principal question can now be stated precisely. How did Iranians participate in Islamic intellectual culture, a culture that was conducted largely in Arabic, while at the same time developing a Persian-language tradition that eventually produced one of the great literatures of the world? The two halves of that question are not competing alternatives. The same cities, and sometimes the same individuals, nurtured both. A secretary trained in Arabic composition might commission a Persian translation. A Persian-speaking poet might write verse in Arabic as well. A scholar of the Quran might teach in Arabic and explain the meaning of a verse to his students in Persian. A Zoroastrian priest in Fars might compose a long work in Middle Persian in the same decades that an Arabic grammarian of Persian ancestry in Basra fixed the rules of Arabic.

What the Chapter Covers and Why It Is Organized as It Is

The chapter's nineteen sections fall into five divisions. Division I, Sections 33.1 through 33.4, establishes the linguistic landscape and the conditions that made the coexistence of Arabic and Persian possible. Division II, Sections 33.5 through 33.10, examines the participation of Iranians in Arabic intellectual culture: the cities and institutions of learning, grammar and lexicography, adab, translation, historiography, and the debates about identity. Division III, Sections 33.11 through 33.15, turns to New Persian itself, its relationship to Middle Persian, its geography, its scripts, its vocabulary, and the problem of the earliest poetry. Division IV, Sections 33.16 through 33.18, examines Persian prose and translation, the bilingual world of the later tenth century, and the networks through which scholars and books moved. Section 33.19 draws the threads together and explains how these developments prepared the ground for Chapters 34 and 35.

Two cautions shape the approach. The first concerns the vocabulary of identity. Words such as Arab, Persian, Iranian, and Muslim sometimes appear in modern discussion as though they were mutually exclusive. In the period studied here they were not. A person could be a Muslim, an Arabic-writing scholar, a native speaker of Persian, a descendant of a Sasanian landholding family, and a client of an Arab tribe, all at once, and the sources very often tell us only some of these facts. The second concerns the direction of explanation. It is tempting to describe the period as a competition between Arabic and Persian in which Persian eventually won or Arabic eventually lost. The evidence supports a different picture, in which two languages expanded their roles together, in distinct but overlapping spheres, and in which many of the most creative achievements depended on movement between them.

Division I · Section 33.2

The Linguistic Landscape of Early Islamic Iran

To understand how a new written language could emerge, students must first dispel the image of a single standardized "Persian" confronting a single "Arabic." The linguistic landscape of Iran and its eastern neighbors at the time of the conquests was composed of many languages, each tied to particular regions, communities, and functions. Historians distinguish usefully among four categories. Literary languages were those in which texts of religion, law, history, or poetry were composed and copied. Administrative languages were those used to keep accounts, issue orders, and record transactions. Spoken languages were the idioms people used in daily life, often unwritten or only occasionally written. Regional languages and dialects were varieties tied to particular valleys, cities, or mountain districts. A single language could occupy several of these categories at once, and the same community often moved among them.

Middle Persian, the descendant of Old Persian and the language of the Sasanian court and priesthood, is the best-documented of the Iranian languages on the eve of Islam. It was written in the Pahlavi script and survives in royal inscriptions, seals, coins, administrative documents, and a large body of Zoroastrian religious literature. Its prestige extended across the Sasanian realm, and it served as an administrative language in many provinces. It was, however, a written language of state and religion rather than a uniform spoken language of the whole population, and the Persian actually spoken in homes, markets, and villages varied from region to region. As the term is used in this textbook, Middle Persian denotes the language attested in Sasanian and early Islamic Pahlavi texts, while New Persian denotes the later language written mainly in the Arabic script, with the understanding, developed in Section 33.11, that the two are connected by continuous spoken development.

Other Iranian languages occupied different territories and roles. Parthian, the language of the Arsacid court, had left descendants and a distinctive literary legacy, and medieval Arabic writers used the word Fahlavi for the dialects of western and northwestern Iran, a usage that differs from the modern scholarly term Pahlavi (see the Historical Vocabulary box in Section 33.12). Sogdian was the language of the merchants and cities of Transoxiana, spoken from Samarkand and Bukhara through the Zarafshan valley and written in a script derived from Aramaic; it carried Buddhist, Manichaean, and Christian texts and a lively commercial correspondence across Central Asia. Bactrian, written in an adapted Greek script, had served the administrators of Bactria and the Kushan realm and was still used for documents in the seventh and eighth centuries. Khwarazmian was spoken in the region of the lower Oxus, and it persisted for centuries after the conquest, as later glossaries show. Farther west and north, Kurdish dialects, the languages of the Caspian coast, and Iranian dialects in Azerbaijan, later displaced there by Turkic speech, completed a mosaic that no single label can capture.

Iran's population also included speakers of non-Iranian languages. In Iraq and Khuzestan, Aramaic and Syriac were the languages of many villagers, of the Church of the East, and of scholars whose works would shortly be translated into Arabic. Arabic itself was present from the beginning, spoken by garrison troops and tribal settlers, especially in Khurasan, where soldiers and their families from Basra and Kufa settled in Merv and other towns. The numbers in the sources are uncertain, but the settlement of Arabic speakers in the northeast was substantial enough that Arabic became a language of one segment of local society even before it became a language of scholarship. Over time, many descendants of these settlers adopted Persian as their language of daily life, a reminder that language shift moved in more than one direction.

Early Islamic Iran was multilingual in every region, and the future of New Persian was neither predetermined nor limited to a single place of origin. It would grow out of one of these varieties of speech, in interaction with the others and with Arabic, and under particular historical circumstances that the following sections examine.

Division I · Section 33.3

Arabic Becomes the Language of Administration and Learning

The conquered territories inherited administrative systems that operated in languages other than Arabic. In Syria the registers were kept in Greek, in Egypt in Greek and Coptic, and in Iraq and Iran largely in Middle Persian. For several decades after the conquests the rulers allowed these systems to continue, retaining experienced local scribes and tax officials, because the new state needed the skills that they possessed. Surviving coins reflect this continuity. Arab governors in the east struck silver coins modeled on Sasanian dirhams, bearing the portrait of a Sasanian king and Pahlavi legends, with Arabic phrases added in modest form, and similar issues continued in some regions well into the eighth century. Local rulers in the northeast and in Tabaristan issued coinage of their own in older styles.

The shift to Arabic in the central administration came in stages. Later historians, notably al-Baladhuri, recorded that under the Umayyad caliph ʿAbd al-Malik, around the end of the seventh century, the keeping of registers was moved from Greek into Arabic in Syria and from Persian into Arabic in Iraq, the latter attributed to the governor al-Hajjaj and his secretary Salih ibn ʿAbd al-Rahman. The caliph also introduced a coinage with purely Arabic inscriptions in the same decades. These reports come from sources compiled well after the events, and the precise dates and sequence remain debated, but the general direction is not in doubt. What the accounts describe is the transfer of the core fiscal registers of the center into Arabic and the progressive requirement that officials use Arabic in their work. The story carries an implication that the traditions themselves often stress: Iranian scribes, whose Persian skills had been indispensable, now had to learn Arabic to keep their positions, and many did so with distinction.

The northeast followed a different timetable. Khurasan, a frontier province with Arabic-speaking garrisons in a largely Persian-, Sogdian-, and Bactrian-speaking environment, retained its Persian-language registers longer. Reports associate the transfer of Khurasan's registers to Arabic with the early 740s. Documentary evidence from the east supports the picture of continued local writing. Bactrian documents from the conquest period record transactions in the local administrative language, and Sogdian documents found at Mount Mugh, from the early eighth century, show a local ruler of the Zarafshan valley issuing and receiving correspondence in Sogdian in the decades of the Arab advance. Evidence of this kind reminds students that Arabization was not an instantaneous imposition but a process shaped by local circumstances, by the interests of local notables, and by the needs of the rulers.

Administrative Arabization is a subject of Chapter 31, which describes the Iranian secretaries and the Abbasid system. The present chapter concerns the consequences for language and learning. Two should be distinguished. The first was the growth of Arabic as an official language of the chancery, which required officials across the empire to acquire written Arabic and created a large class of Arabic-literate bureaucrats, many of them of Iranian background. The second was the growth of Arabic as the language of religious and scholarly authority. The Quran was recited and studied in Arabic. The collections of the sayings and practices of the Prophet, which became the basis of Islamic law and ethics, were preserved and discussed in Arabic. The emerging schools of law, theology, and Quranic exegesis conducted their arguments in Arabic. To participate in these fields at the highest level, a person needed command of the language, and scholarly communication across the vast territories of the caliphate required a shared medium. Arabic provided it.

These two developments reinforced one another but were not identical. A scribe might know enough Arabic to draft a letter without being a scholar of grammar, and a scholar might spend his life in Quranic study without holding any official post. Neither required that one abandon the language of one's family. What the combined effect produced was a society in which Arabic was the language of advancement, of high culture, and of religious learning, and in which Persian and other Iranian languages remained the languages of kinship, daily life, and local identity. This arrangement, which linguists call diglossia, or more accurately a multilingual division of labor, set the conditions for everything that followed.

Division I · Section 33.4

Arabic as a Shared Intellectual Language

Why did people of such different backgrounds write in Arabic? The simplest answer is that Arabic became, within a few generations, the only language in which a very large audience of readers could be reached. A scholar in Nishapur who wrote in Arabic could expect to be read in Baghdad, Damascus, Cordoba, or Samarkand. A scholar who wrote in Persian could be read by those who understood Persian, a smaller if still substantial public. Arabic carried scriptural authority, a developing technical vocabulary, a standardized grammar, and, above all, a large community of fellow readers. For many purposes, the choice of Arabic was a choice of audience as much as of identity.

It follows that writing in Arabic cannot be taken as evidence of Arab ancestry. The scholars whose names fill the biographical dictionaries came from many backgrounds. Al-Khalil ibn Ahmad, the Basran philologist discussed in Section 33.6, belonged to an Arab tribal family. His pupil Sibawayh was of Persian origin. Al-Jahiz, one of the most celebrated Arabic prose writers of the ninth century, was of African ancestry and grew up in Basra. Hunayn ibn Ishaq, the greatest translator of Greek medical and philosophical works into Arabic, was a Christian from Hira in Iraq. Thabit ibn Qurra, a mathematician and translator, belonged to the Sabian community of Harran. Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ was a Persian from Fars, raised as a Zoroastrian, who adopted Islam, and the historian al-Tabari was a native of Tabaristan on the Caspian coast. None of these men became "Arab" in an ethnic sense by writing in Arabic, and the sources often speak of their ancestry, tribal affiliations, and religious communities with considerable precision.

One institution deserves special attention in this connection: the status of mawla (plural mawali), a client attached to an Arab tribe or family by a bond of patronage. In the first Islamic century, non-Arab converts commonly entered Muslim society through such ties. Many Iranians became clients of Arab tribes, took the Arabic names of their patrons, and wrote in Arabic as full participants in their scholarly communities. The term appears in the biographies of scholars, grammarians, and secretaries, and it carried social meaning, sometimes of inferiority in the eyes of those who valued Arab descent. The history of the status, and the tensions it produced, are a subject for Chapter 32. Its significance here is that the Arabic scholarly tradition developed within a society in which membership was defined by Islam, by allegiance, and by learning rather than by ancestry alone.

Arabic connected scholars across great distances. A young man from Khurasan might travel to Iraq or the Hijaz to study with famous teachers, copy their works, and return home with a store of knowledge and a network of teachers and fellow students. The travel for the sake of hadith, in particular, became a recognized form of scholarly life, and some of the most celebrated collectors of prophetic traditions came from Iranian lands: Muhammad al-Bukhari from Bukhara, Muslim ibn al-Hajjaj from Nishapur, al-Tirmidhi from Termez, al-Nasaʾi from Nasa, Abu Dawud from Sistan, and Ibn Maja from Qazvin. All of them wrote in Arabic, and their compilations became part of the foundations of Sunni scholarship, a subject to which Chapter 40 returns. Their work illustrates a point that this chapter makes again and again: the Arabic-language intellectual world was not an Arab possession that Iranians approached from outside. It was a shared field of work, and scholars from Iranian lands were among its builders.

Other communities used other languages for their own purposes. Zoroastrian priests wrote their books in Middle Persian. Jews in Iran and Iraq wrote religious commentary in Hebrew and Aramaic and, increasingly, in Arabic written in Hebrew letters, a practice known as Judeo-Arabic, while those in Iran also wrote Persian in Hebrew letters. Christians used Syriac in the liturgy and for theology while many of their scholars, like Hunayn, worked in Arabic. The result was a world in which one language was shared across communities for many purposes and in which each community retained its own languages for others. Understanding this pattern helps explain why the rise of Arabic scholarship did not extinguish the other languages of Iran.

Division II · Section 33.5

Cities, Scholars, Books, and the Institutions of Learning

Scholarship in this period was an urban activity. It required teachers, students, patrons, copyists, and, increasingly, paper and books, all of which were concentrated in cities. The principal centers of Arabic learning in the eighth century were the Iraqi garrison cities of Basra and Kufa, founded in the period of the conquests as camps for the Arab-Muslim armies and soon transformed into centers of linguistic, legal, and religious scholarship. Both attracted Arab tribesmen who preserved poetry and speech, converts of Iranian and Aramaic background, and scholars who sought to systematize the new religious and cultural order. After the foundation of Baghdad in 762 by the caliph al-Mansur, the new capital became the dominant center, drawing scholars from across the empire to its mosques, courts, and markets.

In the Iranian lands themselves, several cities became important centers of learning. Merv, the administrative capital of Khurasan, was the home of a mixed Arab and Iranian society and, in the early ninth century, the residence of the Abbasid ruler al-Maʾmun before he moved to Baghdad. Later writers celebrated its libraries, though the descriptions come from authors who wrote long after its heyday and should be read with caution. Nishapur, the leading city of western Khurasan in the ninth and tenth centuries, was a center of hadith scholarship and law. Rayy, near modern Tehran, was a major city of the Jibal, associated with scholars of medicine, theology, and philosophy. Bukhara, Samarkand, and Balkh, farther east, became leading centers of both Arabic and Persian writing, and Bukhara in the tenth century housed a royal library that later accounts describe in glowing terms.

Teaching Circles, Libraries, and Booksellers

The institutions of learning in this period were more informal and personal than those of later centuries. Teaching took place in the mosque, in the homes of scholars, in the courts of governors and caliphs, and in the shops of booksellers. A typical arrangement was a circle of students gathered around a teacher, who recited or explained a text and allowed students to take notes or copy. A scholar's authority rested on the reputation of his teachers and the chain of persons through whom he had received a text, and many biographical entries list teachers and students in great detail. Students might spend years traveling from city to city to hear a particular scholar, and the reputation gained through such study was portable across the Islamic world.

Students should be careful about the word madrasa. The formal, endowed college with a regular curriculum, salaried teachers, and a physical campus, associated especially with the Nizamiyya colleges of the eleventh century, was a later development. Later sources assign to tenth-century Nishapur institutions that they describe as early madrasas, but the nature of these foundations is debated, and it would be anachronistic to project the fully formed college back onto the eighth or ninth century. Projecting back the later institutional forms would also obscure the real strength of the earlier system, which depended on personal relationships between teachers and students and on the portability of knowledge itself.

The institution often called the House of Wisdom (Bayt al-Hikma) in Baghdad has been the subject of much popular writing. Its existence as a library or translation center associated with the caliphs is attested, and individuals such as Sahl ibn Harun, of Persian origin, are linked to it in the sources. Historians disagree, however, about its scale, organization, and function, and about the extent to which later authors idealized it. A cautious reading treats it as one important example of a wider culture of caliphal and elite patronage of books and translation rather than as a single research university in the modern sense. The central point is that the translation of works from Greek, Syriac, and Middle Persian into Arabic was supported by the households of caliphs, viziers, and wealthy notables, and that Persian-speaking scholars and secretaries were prominent among both the patrons and the practitioners.

Books themselves were a decisive factor. The adoption of paper, which spread westward from China through Central Asia and was produced in Samarkand and Baghdad, allowed manuscripts to be made more cheaply and in greater quantity than on parchment or papyrus. Later tradition associates the spread of papermaking with Samarkand in the aftermath of the battle of Talas in 751 and attributes the first paper mills in Baghdad to the 790s, but the details of these stories rest on later accounts and historians treat them with caution; papermaking probably reached the Islamic world through several routes. What is clear is that by the ninth century the book trade had become a major business. The warraqun, copyists and booksellers, ran shops in Baghdad's markets, and these shops functioned as meeting places for scholars. Ibn al-Nadim, the author of the Fihrist, belonged to this profession, and his catalogue is itself a product of the bookseller's world. Arabic prose benefited especially, as authors wrote for a growing reading public in a variety of genres.

Division II · Section 33.6

Iranian Scholars and the Study of Arabic Language

Why would the study of the Arabic language itself become a major scholarly discipline? Part of the answer lies in the Quran. As a revealed text in Arabic, it required correct recitation, interpretation of its vocabulary, and understanding of its grammar. As Islam spread to peoples who did not speak Arabic from birth, the need to teach the language to new Muslims and to preserve its forms from corruption grew pressing. Part of the answer also lies in culture and politics. Arabic poetry and the speech of the Bedouin were regarded as a standard of eloquence, and scholars of Basra and Kufa devoted their lives to recording, analyzing, and explaining them. Part of it, finally, lies in careers. Mastery of the language was the key to employment in the chancery and in the courts, and a grammarian or lexicographer could gain patronage and status.

The central figure in this field was Sibawayh, whose book al-Kitab, "The Book," became the foundation of Arabic grammatical study for centuries. According to the biographical tradition, he was born in a village in Fars, of Persian ancestry, and moved to Basra, where he studied with leading scholars, including al-Khalil ibn Ahmad, whose own work on the sounds, forms, and meters of Arabic made him one of the great founders of the discipline. Sibawayh died young by most accounts, in the later eighth century or around its end, though sources differ on his dates and on the place of his death. The details of his life, like those of many early scholars, rest on anecdotal and often legendary reports compiled long afterward, and students should treat the stories about his career, including accounts of rivalries with scholars in Baghdad, as part of a later biographical tradition rather than as established fact.

The achievement of al-Kitab is better documented than the life of its author. It is a comprehensive description of Arabic as it was spoken and composed by those whom the grammarians regarded as authoritative speakers, organized by syntactic function and supported by extensive examples from poetry, the Quran, and the speech of the Bedouin. It treats morphology, syntax, phonology, and the relationship between form and meaning with a rigor that shaped all later Arabic grammatical writing. That a man of Persian origin produced the work that later scholars treated as the "Qurʾan of grammar" is a striking illustration of the theme of this chapter. It does not mean that Sibawayh was motivated by Persian pride, or by any Persian project, or that he approached Arabic as an outsider. His own teachers and many of his colleagues were Arab, he worked in a community of Arabic scholars, and the work belongs to the Arabic tradition. It means that scholars of Iranian origin were fully at the heart of the intellectual life of the language.

Later grammarians and lexicographers came from the Iranian lands in considerable numbers. Compilers of the great Arabic dictionaries include scholars from Herat, Farab in Transoxiana, and Hamadan, and the scholars of Rayy and Nishapur wrote on Arabic literature as well as religion. Their works, together with those of Basran and Kufan scholars, built the apparatus that allowed Arabic to function as a language of scholarship for speakers of many native tongues. Modern historians have questioned the neat distinction between rival "schools" of Basra and Kufa that later writers constructed, noting that the later tradition probably systematized what were in fact more fluid and overlapping scholarly practices. The question for this chapter is not which school was right, but what the discipline accomplished: it described and stabilized a language that had spread far beyond the community that first spoke it, and it did so through the work of people of many origins.

The impact on Persian was indirect but significant. Scholars trained to analyze Arabic acquired the analytical tools of grammar, lexicography, and philology, and these methods influenced how later scholars would describe and discuss Persian. The first Persian dictionaries, which appeared in the eleventh century, drew on lexicographical methods developed in Arabic, and the habit of illustrating a word's meaning with verses of poetry was borrowed from the Arabic tradition. The study of Arabic thus became a school of linguistic self-awareness that benefited the study of other languages, including the one that was about to acquire a written literature.

Division II · Section 33.7

Adab, Secretaries, and the Culture of Educated Writing

If grammar described the language, adab defined how an educated person used it. The word originally meant custom, good conduct, and the manners that a person acquired from a parent or teacher. Over the eighth and ninth centuries it acquired the broader meaning of a body of literary cultivation, ethical knowledge, history, poetry, and practical learning that a refined person was expected to possess, and eventually the term came to describe a literary genre as well. Adab was never a fixed curriculum. Different authors emphasized different elements, and the content changed as new materials, including translated Persian and Greek works, entered the Arabic repertoire. What remained constant was the ideal of a person of broad knowledge and polished expression, equipped to advise a ruler, to compose an eloquent letter, and to converse in a gathering of the learned.

Historical Vocabulary
Adab and the Kātib

Definition: Adab denotes, in the period of this chapter, the cultivated learning, refined conduct, and literary skill that an educated person was expected to possess, and by extension the literature that taught or displayed those qualities. A kātib (plural kuttāb) is a secretary, a person trained in writing who served in the chancery, in provincial administration, or in the household of a ruler or notable.

Historical context: The secretarial profession was one of the principal channels through which Iranians entered Arabic literary life. In the Sasanian empire a trained class of scribes had maintained the administrative records in Middle Persian, and under the caliphate, scribes of Iranian background learned Arabic and continued to serve. Their education combined correct Arabic style, calligraphy, arithmetic, the lore of earlier kings and administrators, and knowledge of poetry and history. Chapter 31 examines the political roles of these secretaries; here the focus is on the culture they produced.

Why it matters: The secretary's education explains how a body of knowledge once concentrated in Sasanian court circles could become a part of Arabic literature. It also explains why so much early Arabic prose was written by people who were not native speakers of Arabic in the home.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not translate adab simply as "literature" or "etiquette." It combined both, along with ethics and history, and the word's meaning shifted during the period. Nor should they assume that every kātib was Iranian: Arab, Christian, and other secretaries also served, and the profession was shaped by many traditions.

The secretaries had special reasons to value adab. A kātib's career depended on his ability to draft correct, graceful, and persuasive documents, to advise officials, and to represent his patron to others. Manuals of the profession, among them Ibn Qutayba's Adab al-Katib (The Secretary's Manual) in the ninth century, set out the knowledge that such a person needed: orthography, grammar, the vocabulary of administration, and the conventions of correspondence. The secretarial class also served as patrons and audiences of literature. They collected poetry and anecdote, read histories, and commissioned translations. Their social position rested on skill with the pen, which provided a route of advancement for talented men of modest origin, including converts and clients, in a society in which birth often mattered.

The relationship between adab and court culture was close but reciprocal. Courts and households of caliphs, governors, and viziers sustained writers by providing salaries and gifts, and those writers in turn provided entertainment, instruction, and prestige. The prose of the ninth century, exemplified in the works of al-Jahiz, drew upon a wide range of materials, including anecdotes, natural observation, theological argument, and humor, and its wide-ranging curiosity depended on a reading public of officials, scholars, and merchants. Adab literature also served pedagogical purposes: it taught how a person should behave toward a ruler, a colleague, a friend, or a servant, using stories and maxims, many of them drawn from Iranian sources, to illustrate its lessons.

The historical question is how to describe the Iranian share in all this without exaggeration. The old accounts that attribute the whole of Arabic prose style to Persian secretaries cannot be sustained, because Arabic prose drew on the Quran, on the rhetoric of the tribal orators, and on Greek and Syriac models as well as Persian ones. But it is equally untenable to treat Arabic adab as a purely Arab creation. Iranian secretaries brought to it their traditions of royal counsel, their taste for ethical sayings attributed to ancient kings and sages, and their ideal of the cultivated administrator. Translators rendered Pahlavi books of advice into Arabic, and those books shaped later Arabic writing about government and conduct. The next section examines how that translation process worked.

Division II · Section 33.8

Translation and the Transmission of Iranian Knowledge

The most direct way in which the Sasanian past entered the Islamic world was through translation. In the eighth and ninth centuries, scholars rendered into Arabic a range of Middle Persian works, including political instruction, collections of moral sayings, tales and fables, and historical writings, along with works on astronomy, astrology, and medicine that Chapter 43 treats in connection with the sciences. Because very few of the Middle Persian originals survive, the Arabic versions, and the later Persian versions that derived from them, are often the only evidence for what the originals contained. This situation calls for careful reasoning. A translation is never a transparent window onto an original; it is a work of selection, adaptation, and interpretation shaped by the translator's choices and by the expectations of his audience.

The outstanding example is Kalīla wa-Dimna, a collection of animal fables arranged within a framing narrative and designed to teach the arts of statecraft and conduct. According to a tradition recorded in the introductory chapters of the Arabic text and in later Arabic and Persian sources, the work originated in India, where a Sanskrit collection of fables, now usually associated with the Panchatantra, was brought to the court of the Sasanian king Khusraw I in the sixth century by a physician named Burzoy, who translated it into Middle Persian. The Middle Persian version has not survived, but a Syriac translation made in the sixth century does, and an Arabic version, composed in the middle of the eighth century by Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ, became one of the most widely read and imitated works in Arabic literature. From the Arabic version, translations spread in turn into many other languages, including later Persian, Greek, Hebrew, and eventually European tongues. The story of the book's travels from India through Iran to the Arabic-speaking world and beyond is one of the best illustrations of the long-distance transmission of narrative literature.

The role of Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ in this transmission needs to be understood carefully. This chapter does not repeat the account of his life and career as a secretary, given in Chapter 31. The question here concerns what he and others did with texts. Because the Middle Persian text is lost, scholars cannot determine exactly how much of the Arabic version is faithful rendering and how much adaptation. Some chapters of the Arabic work seem to reflect Islamic and Arabic contexts, and the style is that of a skilled Arabic prose writer rather than a literalist. The result is a work that belongs to the history of Arabic literature as surely as to the history of Iranian and Indian narrative. This double belonging is typical of the translation movement as a whole: the translated works became part of the Arabic tradition by being transformed into it.

Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ and other translators were also credited by later bibliographers with Arabic versions of Sasanian royal historical writing and political counsel. The most important historical text was the Middle Persian Khwadāy-nāmag, "Book of Lords" or "Book of Kings," a chronicle of the Iranian kings from mythical origins to the end of the Sasanian dynasty. Arabic versions, often called Siyar al-Muluk or Siyar muluk al-ʿajam ("The Lives of the Kings" or "The Lives of the Kings of the Persians"), circulated in the eighth and ninth centuries, and several translators are named in the later tradition. Other works of advice and political thought, including testaments attributed to Sasanian kings and a text known as the Letter of Tansar, entered the Arabic repertoire as well. The Letter of Tansar survives in a Persian version that its thirteenth-century transmitter says was translated from an Arabic version by Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ, which was in turn rendered from Middle Persian. Such chains of transmission, running from Middle Persian through Arabic to New Persian, show both the continuity of the tradition and the number of transformations it underwent.

What Translation Preserved, and What It Changed

Three consequences of the translation movement deserve emphasis. First, it preserved. Without Arabic translation, much of what is known about Sasanian ideas of kingship, administration, and history would have been lost, because the Middle Persian originals were copied only occasionally and many have vanished. Second, it adapted. The Arabic versions rendered Iranian ideas into an Islamic framework, and later users read them as part of a common ethical and political literature that Muslim writers could employ without difficulty. A maxim attributed to a Sasanian king could be quoted in a work of Islamic advice beside a saying of the Prophet or a verse of poetry. Third, it selected. Translators and patrons chose some works and not others. Texts of court ethics and statecraft, of obvious use to secretaries and rulers, were favored, while others, particularly those tied to Zoroastrian ritual and cosmology, remained in the care of the priests who continued to copy them in Middle Persian.

The genre known as the mirror for princes, a work of advice for rulers, illustrates how translated material shaped later literature. Arabic and, later, Persian writers on government drew on the translated Sasanian counsel in producing their own works on the proper conduct of kings, viziers, and officials. These works are covered in later chapters in their historical settings. The essential point for the present chapter is that the Sasanian inheritance was not recovered by Persian revivalists. It was transmitted largely by Arabic-writing scholars, many of them of Iranian background, who judged it useful to the intellectual life of the Islamic world and who made it available in the language that the widest range of readers could understand.

Division II · Section 33.9

Arabic Historiography and the Preservation of Iranian Memory

Arabic historical writing developed in the eighth and ninth centuries from several sources: reports of the early community and the conquests, genealogical and tribal traditions, and, increasingly, narratives of earlier peoples, including the kings of Persia. The Iranian material entered the Arabic historical tradition partly through the translations just described and partly through oral accounts transmitted by local notables, including the dihqans, who preserved stories of the Iranian past in their families and communities. The result was that Arabic-language historians of the ninth and tenth centuries wrote about the Sasanians, and about older Iranian rulers whom the tradition called the Pishdadids and Kayanids, using material that was first formulated in Middle Persian but had been reshaped by successive tellers.

Two historians in particular illustrate the possibilities and the problems. Al-Dinawari (died around the end of the ninth century; the precise date is variously given) came from Dinawar in the Jibal and wrote, among much else, a work of history known as al-Akhbar al-Tiwal, "The Long Reports." It is distinguished by its narrative style and by the extent of its attention to the pre-Islamic Iranian kings and their stories, which seem to draw on traditions less familiar from other Arabic sources. Al-Tabari (born at Amul in Tabaristan in 839 and died in Baghdad in 923) composed a monumental history from the Creation to his own time, as well as a major commentary on the Quran. His history, arranged in annalistic form for the Islamic period, includes a long preliminary section on the prophets and kings of earlier ages, among them the kings of Iran down to the fall of the Sasanian dynasty. Both historians wrote in Arabic for a general Muslim readership, and both treated Iranian history as part of a world history that included the Biblical prophets, the Arabs, and the Romans.

Students should recognize that al-Dinawari and al-Tabari did not set out to preserve a distinctively "Iranian" memory in an ethnic or national sense. Their frame was universal and Islamic. They integrated the history of the Iranian kings into a worldview in which God governed history, in which earlier peoples were instances of moral and political lessons, and in which the Islamic community was the culmination. The preservation of Iranian tradition was a consequence of this inclusive approach, rather than an aim in itself. That does not diminish its significance. Because Arabic histories included Sasanian narratives, those narratives survived the disappearance of the Middle Persian books that first carried them, and later Persian writers, including the Persian translators of al-Tabari and eventually Firdawsi (Chapter 42), drew on the same stock of material.

Problems of Reconstruction

Using these Arabic histories as evidence for the pre-Islamic past requires critical attention to the chain of transmission. Much of the Iranian royal history in al-Tabari derives from earlier Arabic writers, who in turn relied on translations or oral reports of Middle Persian materials. At each stage, details could be altered, abridged, rationalized, or harmonized with Islamic and Biblical chronology. Hamza al-Isfahani, who in the tenth century wrote a history of the kings of Iran, noted that the Arabic versions of the royal history differed from one another and that the discrepancies reflected the work of the translators, a perceptive comment on the instability of the tradition. The Middle Persian Khwadāy-nāmag is lost, and what scholars reconstruct of its content is inferred from comparison of its Arabic and Persian descendants, a process of editorial reconstruction that involves judgment and uncertainty.

The reliability of the narratives varies by period. The accounts of the early mythic kings are legendary and reflect Iranian epic tradition, not documented history. For the Sasanian period, the Arabic and Persian narratives can be compared with contemporary evidence: royal inscriptions, coins, seals, and the testimony of Greek, Roman, Syriac, and Armenian writers. Such comparison shows that the narrative tradition preserved accurate information on some matters, such as the sequence of many kings, while distorting or embellishing others, notably the stories of court intrigue and the career of individual rulers, which reflect storytelling conventions and later attitudes. Historians must therefore treat the Arabic accounts of Iran's past as valuable but secondary evidence, interpreted through the interests and perspectives of the Islamic-era authors who produced them.

Finally, the Arabic histories were themselves shaped by their time. The image of the Sasanian monarchy as a model of justice and good government, which appears in many Arabic works, reflected the ideals of secretaries and rulers who found in the Sasanian past a useful precedent for their own concerns about order and administration. This is a form of retrospection rather than documentation. The Iranian past was recorded in Arabic, but it was recorded by people with views of their own, and the memory that survives is not a neutral transcript but an interpreted heritage.

Division II · Section 33.10

Shuʿubiyya, Cultural Prestige, and Debates over Arab and Iranian Identity

The integration of Iranians into Arabic culture did not proceed without friction. In the eighth and ninth centuries, scholars, poets, and officials argued about the relative standing of different peoples within the Islamic community, about whether Arab descent conferred privilege, and about the merits of the cultures that had preceded Islam. These debates are known collectively by the term Shuʿubiyya, and they must be understood as a series of intellectual controversies carried on in Arabic, mostly in written form, rather than as an organized political party or a modern nationalist movement.

Historical Vocabulary
Shuʿubiyya

Definition: The name given to a set of intellectual and literary controversies, chiefly of the eighth to tenth centuries, concerning the standing of Arabs relative to non-Arab peoples within the Islamic community. The term derives from shuʿub, "peoples" or "nations," a word that appears in a Quranic verse (49:13) which describes the division of humankind into peoples and tribes so that they might know one another, and which declares the most honored in God's sight to be the most pious.

Historical context: The debates arose from the tensions of a society in which Arab descent carried political and social prestige, in which many Muslims were non-Arab clients, and in which scholars and secretaries of non-Arab origin competed for position. Some participants invoked Quranic ideas of equality, while others claimed that non-Arab peoples possessed distinguished ancestors, histories, and achievements. Opponents responded by defending Arab eloquence, genealogy, and the Arab role in the rise of Islam. The term was often used by critics of those they accused of belittling the Arabs.

Why it matters: The Shuʿubiyya shows that the place of Iranian heritage within the Islamic world was contested in the open and in writing, and it reveals how scholars used history, language, and genealogy as arguments about status.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not regard the Shuʿubiyya as an early Persian nationalism. Participants included Persians, but also other non-Arabs, and their arguments were varied. Many Iranian scholars wrote in praise of Arabic and Islam. Modern nationalist writers sometimes read the controversies as a patriotic Persian resistance to Arab domination, an interpretation that goes beyond the evidence.

The arguments ranged widely. At one end stood a line of thinking that stressed the equality of believers, supported by Quranic and prophetic statements that piety, not lineage, determined worth. At another were those who asserted the superiority of the non-Arab peoples, pointing to the achievements of the ancient kings of Persia, their sciences, their statecraft, and their literature, and criticizing the supposed crudeness of the Arab tribes before Islam. Opposing authors responded with works defending the Arabs. Ibn Qutayba wrote on the excellence of the Arabs and replied to critics, and his writings drew on genealogies, poetry, and religious evidence while also making use of much Iranian learning in his adab works. Al-Jahiz, in his writings, treated the claims of different peoples with a mixture of satire, learning, and balance, and he recognized the contributions of the Persians to administration and culture even as he defended Arabic eloquence. Sahl ibn Harun, a scholar of Persian origin who served as a librarian under the caliphs Harun al-Rashid and al-Maʾmun, is described by later writers as a defender of non-Arab claims, though what survives about him is limited.

Poets, too, took part. Bashshar ibn Burd, a poet of Persian descent active in Iraq in the eighth century, composed verse celebrating his non-Arab ancestry; later accounts tell of his death at the hands of the authorities. His case, like that of other poets of the period, shows both the vitality of Iranian pride and the risks it could entail, though the evidence comes largely from later biographical anthologies. At the same time, large numbers of poets and scholars of Iranian origin wrote in Arabic with evident devotion to the language and its literary traditions. The same person might praise the glory of the ancient Persian kings and compose a flawless Arabic ode.

The participants in these controversies were not limited to Persians. The Aramaic-speaking peasantry of Iraq, sometimes called Nabataeans in the sources, and the Copts of Egypt were also the subject of discussion, which suggests that the question concerned the position of non-Arabs generally rather than a single national group. Moreover, the controversies were shaped by social and political circumstances. They flared in times of competition for office, and the terms of argument changed as the caliphate's character changed. The Abbasid state, which drew heavily on Khurasani and Iranian support, was less committed than its Umayyad predecessor to Arab privilege, and by the tenth century the intensity of the debates had declined as Arabic literary culture became the shared property of many peoples.

Historians have long debated how best to interpret the Shuʿubiyya, a point taken up again in the discussion of the revival of Persian later in this chapter. The evidence is limited, because many of the original Shuʿubi writings are lost and survive only in quotations and refutations by opponents, who naturally presented them in an unflattering light. For the purposes of this chapter, the principal lesson is that the formation of the Islamic-Iranian intellectual world involved negotiation about identity and prestige, and that Iranians took part in that negotiation using the Arabic language, in the Arabic literary tradition, and in terms provided by Islam itself.

Division III · Section 33.11

From Middle Persian to Early New Persian

The emergence of written New Persian must be understood against the long history of the language it grew from. Persian, in its successive stages, descends from the Old Persian of the Achaemenid inscriptions, through the Middle Persian of the Sasanian period, to the New Persian of the Islamic era. These labels designate stages in a continuous development, not separate languages that replaced one another at a stroke. Historians of language use them to mark differences in grammar, vocabulary, and sound, and to organize the surviving texts, but the people who spoke the language at any given time did not experience a sudden transition. Their speech changed gradually, generation by generation, and the written forms of the language changed more slowly and for different reasons.

The differences between Middle Persian and early New Persian are real and appreciable. In grammar, Middle Persian had already lost most of the case inflections of Old Persian, and New Persian simplified further. Changes in the verbal system produced forms familiar from the later language: for example, the progressive marker that appears in early New Persian as hamī or mī is generally understood as a development of the Middle Persian particle hamē. In sound, many words changed form between the two stages: the Middle Persian wuzurg, "great," appears in New Persian as buzurg. In vocabulary, New Persian retained a very large part of the Middle Persian lexicon, so that a great many everyday words are shared, but added many Arabic words, which Section 33.14 examines.

One feature of this development is easily misunderstood. Persian was not extinct in the early Islamic centuries, and its renewal as a written language was not a resurrection. It continued as the spoken language of millions in Fars and in much of the east. Documents and testimony show that Persian speakers kept speaking Persian through the conquests, through the Arabization of the chancery, and through the Islamization of the population. What changed was the writing of the language. Under the Sasanians, written Persian meant Middle Persian in the Pahlavi script, a conservative, priestly and administrative medium that preserved older spellings and used Aramaic-derived writing conventions that obscured the spoken form. Under Islam, as Sections 33.13 and 33.15 show, speakers began to write the spoken language as it was actually pronounced, in scripts more convenient than Pahlavi, and for purposes that the old scribal tradition had not served.

The relationship of the spoken language to the written one therefore explains why historians speak of continuity and change together. There was continuity in the underlying language, in many words and grammatical structures, and in an unbroken chain of speakers. There was change in the sounds and forms of speech, in the writing system, in the registers appropriate to different genres, and in the language's cultural prestige and functions. The scholars who study the earliest New Persian texts find in them features that distinguish them from the Middle Persian of the Zoroastrian books and that reflect the speech of the time. The two stages also coexisted: Middle Persian continued as a religious language of Zoroastrians for some centuries after New Persian began to be written, so that in the ninth century one could find Pahlavi books and early New Persian verses produced in the same broad region.

Division III · Section 33.12

Dari, Khurasan, and the Geography of Literary Persian

When did spoken Persian become a prestigious written language, and where? The question brings into view a term that has generated long argument: Dari. Early Arabic writers refer to a language they call Dari, distinguishing it from other varieties of Iranian speech. Later Persian authors often call the language of their poetry Parsi-yi Dari, and in the twentieth century the name was adopted for the standard form of Persian in Afghanistan. The historical meaning of the term in the early Islamic period, however, differs from its modern uses, and its origins are debated.

Historical Vocabulary
Pahlavi and Dari

Definition: In modern scholarly usage, Pahlavi denotes Middle Persian as written in the Pahlavi script, the language of Sasanian inscriptions and of Zoroastrian books. In the usage of many medieval Arabic writers, however, Fahlavi designated the dialects of the western and northwestern regions, derived from the old Parthian lands (Pahlav), and not the written Middle Persian of the priests. Dari is a medieval term for a variety of Persian that early sources associate with the royal court and the east. Later it became the label of the written literary Persian and, in the twentieth century, of the official form of Persian in Afghanistan.

Historical context: The source discussed in the Primary Source Window below preserves an early classification of the languages of Iran in which Fahlavi, Dari, Parsi, Khuzi, and Syriac are distinguished by region and by social setting. Other tenth-century writers offer comparable lists. The proposed origin of the word Dari from the Persian word for "court" (dar, a gate or court) is widely discussed, though not universally accepted.

Why it matters: Without understanding the shifting meanings of these words, students will misread medieval sources and modern discussions alike. The name "Pahlavi" is attached to a script, a language, and a dynasty of the twentieth century, and each has a different history.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that the Dari of the tenth century is simply the modern Dari of Afghanistan, or that "Pahlavi" in an Arabic geography means the same thing as it does in a modern grammar. Always ask who uses the term, when, and about which language.

Scholars have advanced several interpretations. One approach emphasizes the early reports that associate Dari with the royal court of the Sasanians at Ctesiphon and with the cities of the east, understanding it as a prestige variety of Persian that carried courtly usage into Khurasan. Another stresses the eastern setting in which written New Persian first flourished, regarding the literary language as especially tied to the speech of Khurasan and Transoxiana, where it was used as a common language among Muslims of diverse origins. A third approach emphasizes the descent of New Persian from the Persian of Fars, the Parsi of the sources, and the broader southwestern Iranian dialect group to which Middle Persian belonged. These views are not necessarily exclusive. A widely held reconstruction sees written New Persian as descended from a southwestern Iranian variety closely related to the Middle Persian of Fars, but developed and standardized in the northeast, where it absorbed features of local speech and acquired courtly and literary status. How much weight to give each element remains open, and the evidence does not permit certainty.

Persian in the Northeast and Its Neighbors

The geography of written Persian is a matter of Khurasan and Transoxiana. By the ninth and tenth centuries, Persian had become a common language among the Muslim population of many northeastern cities, and the evidence points to a process of spread rather than the simple continuity of a native tongue. In cities such as Bukhara and Samarkand, where Sogdian had long been the dominant language, Persian gradually became the usual language of urban Muslims, courts, and administration, while rural populations retained Sogdian for longer. Sogdian did not vanish at once. A descendant survives to the present day in the Yaghnobi language of a valley in the upper Zarafshan, and records show that Sogdian was still used in some places for generations after the conquest. Bactrian, however, fades from the record in the later eighth and ninth centuries, and Khwarazmian persisted in its homeland longer, as glossaries of the later medieval period demonstrate.

The spread of Persian into these regions was not culturally neutral. It involved changes in status, patronage, and power: a language that served as the medium of rulers, officials, and the learned acquired advantages over languages that did not. Conversion to Islam, urbanization, movement of Persian-speaking settlers and soldiers, commerce, and the interest of local rulers in an administrative and literary language that they and their subjects could understand all played roles. Persian also served as a common language between Muslims of different local origins in a region where Arab settlers, local Iranian-speaking elites, and later Turkic soldiers coexisted. The expansion of Persian therefore meant, in some places, the retreat of other Iranian languages. Students should not see this as a natural or inevitable process, nor as a simple triumph, but as a historical outcome of particular social and political circumstances, one that carried losses as well as gains.

The northeast was not the only region where Persian was spoken and written. Fars remained a stronghold of the language, with its Zoroastrian priests continuing to write Middle Persian and its general population speaking forms of Persian that were closely related to the language of the earlier period. Sistan produced its own early tradition. The western Iranian regions continued to speak the dialects described as Fahlavi. The emergence of literary Persian was thus not a matter of a single dialect radiating from a single region but of interaction among several regions and speech communities, of which Khurasan and Transoxiana became the most prominent literary centers.

Division III · Section 33.13

Writing Persian: Arabic, Pahlavi, Hebrew, and Other Scripts

One of the most important changes of the period was not linguistic but graphic. Persian came to be written in several scripts, associated with different religious and cultural communities, and the eventual dominance of the Arabic script shaped the whole course of the language's literary history. To understand this development, students must first appreciate the character of the Pahlavi script used for Middle Persian. Derived from Aramaic writing, it used a limited set of letters that each represented several sounds, and it retained a system of heterograms, Aramaic words that were written but read aloud as the Persian equivalents. The result was a script that served the needs of trained scribes, who knew the conventions, but that was difficult to learn and ambiguous to read for those without such training.

Writing Persian in other scripts solved some of these difficulties. Zoroastrian priests, alarmed at the possibility of corruption in the sacred texts, had developed in the late Sasanian period a script, the Avestan alphabet, that represented sounds with precision, and it was used both for the Avesta itself and for rendering Middle Persian in a clearer form known as Pazand. Jewish communities in Iran, who knew Hebrew letters from religious education, used them to write Persian, producing a body of texts now called Judeo-Persian. Christian and Manichaean communities in Iran and Central Asia used their own scripts for the Middle Iranian languages they wrote. The variety shows that written Persian was not the property of a single community.

The Arabic script presented a different kind of solution. It was already known to a very large population of Muslims, taught in the schools and mosques, and the standard script of the chancery and the book trade, and paper made it cheap to copy. Its letters, written with connected strokes and with marks for vowels and distinctions between similar consonants, represented the sounds of Arabic well. Persian, however, has sounds that Arabic lacks, notably the sounds of p, ch, zh, and g. To write them, Persian writers adapted the script by adding dots and strokes to existing letters, which resulted in the additional letters used in Persian. The adaptation was gradual, and early manuscripts show variation in how these letters were written, and sometimes they were not distinguished from their Arabic counterparts at all. The Arabic script was not perfectly suited to Persian, especially in its representation of vowels, but it was good enough, widely available, and, above all, the script of the dominant literate community.

The reasons for its success were practical and social rather than inherent in the script itself. Schooling for Muslim children began with the Quran, and literacy in the Arabic script was therefore widespread among those who received any education. Scribes and secretaries wrote in the script as a matter of course. As the use of Persian in writing grew in Muslim circles, the Arabic script was the obvious choice. The consequence is that by the later tenth century, the great majority of New Persian texts known from Khurasan and Transoxiana are written in Arabic letters, while Zoroastrians continued to use Pahlavi for religious works and Jewish communities continued to use Hebrew letters for Persian.

The Earliest Evidence: Judeo-Persian

Students may be surprised to learn that the earliest dated evidence for Persian of the New Persian type does not come from poems or books in the Arabic script but from Hebrew letters. A Judeo-Persian business letter found at Dandan-Uiliq, an oasis site in the Khotan region of Central Asia, is usually dated by comparison with Chinese records to about 718. A short Persian inscription in Hebrew letters carved on a rock at Tang-i Azao, in what is now Afghanistan, is usually dated to 752 or 753. Other Judeo-Persian documents and manuscripts from the ninth century and later survive. These sources are important because they were written for practical and religious purposes, by ordinary members of a community, in a form close to spoken Persian. They demonstrate that the language was written in the Islamic period, in the east, some time before the Arabic-script literary record becomes abundant.

These early documents should be handled with care. They are few, they are short, and their dates depend on indirect evidence and scholarly judgment. They show that Persian could be written in the eighth century in a form recognizable as New Persian, not that all Persian speakers did so, or that Jewish writers invented the literary language. They do point to a wider fact: the written form of Persian was developing through the efforts of multiple communities, and the Arabic-script literature that later came to dominate was one stream in a broader movement.

Division III · Section 33.14

Arabic Vocabulary, Literary Models, and the Reshaping of Persian

Persian absorbed an enormous number of Arabic words, and no account of its development can avoid the fact. The borrowings were concentrated in particular domains. Religious vocabulary was borrowed in large part, though Persian also retained or created its own terms. Words for law, administration, learning, science, medicine, and abstract thought came in great numbers, because these were the fields in which Arabic provided the vocabulary of educated discussion. Early Persian prose, such as the translations of the tenth century, uses a relatively modest proportion of Arabic loanwords, and later centuries show a steadily higher proportion in learned writing, a pattern that scholars of the language have documented, even if exact measurements require careful method.

The process of borrowing did not turn Persian into Arabic. Persian retained its basic grammar, its sentence structure, its pronouns, its numbers, most of its everyday vocabulary, and the essential character of its verbal system. Arabic words were fitted into Persian patterns: a borrowed noun could be combined with a Persian verb to form a compound expression, and borrowed words took Persian suffixes and plurals. The coexistence of Persian and Arabic elements gave the written language a distinctive and flexible character, allowing writers to choose between a more Persian-based and a more Arabic-based diction according to the subject and the audience. Persian also created or kept its own words for Islamic concepts: the everyday terms for prayer, fasting, God, angel, and heaven in Persian-speaking Muslim communities are Iranian, not Arabic, in origin. This adaptation of the language to Islam is discussed again in Section 33.17.

Borrowing flowed in the other direction as well. Arabic took many words from Persian, often through administrative and commercial contact: examples include terms for gardens, gems, fruits, and the administrative catalogue itself, as the title Fihrist shows. The linguistic exchange was thus two-way, though the weight of borrowing differed, and the prestige of Arabic as a scholarly language meant that the Arabic contribution to Persian was greater than the Persian contribution to Arabic.

Prosody and Literary Conventions

The adaptation of Arabic models also affected Persian poetry. Arabic verse followed a quantitative system of meters, codified in the eighth century by al-Khalil ibn Ahmad and others in the science called ʿarud, based on patterns of long and short syllables, and used a system of rhyme in which every line of a poem ended with the same sound. Persian poets writing in the Islamic period adopted much of this apparatus. The use of quantitative meters, of end rhyme, and of forms such as the ode and the short rhymed poem allowed Persian poetry to be integrated into the wider literary culture of the Islamic world, and the audience for it thus included readers who knew the Arabic conventions well.

Scholars disagree about how far Persian poetry owes its forms to Arabic and how far to older Iranian traditions. Middle Iranian languages had their own verse traditions, apparently based on the number of syllables or stresses rather than on quantity, and some surviving Parthian and Middle Persian poems attest to them, while Sasanian minstrels are mentioned in later sources. The adaptation of Arabic quantitative meters to Persian, and the continuity or discontinuity with the older verse, remain subjects of discussion, and the evidence is limited. The most cautious statement is that Persian poets of the Islamic period created a new poetic practice drawing on Arabic prosody and rhyme as well as on inherited Iranian traditions of performance and storytelling, rather than simply continuing a pre-Islamic form or copying an Arabic one. The point about grammar is equally important: Arabic prosody and conventions were adapted to Persian speech and did not impose Arabic grammar upon it.

Middle Persian, Early New Persian, and Arabic Writing

What Changed

  • The writing system: from the ambiguous Pahlavi script with heterograms to the Arabic script (and, for some communities, Hebrew letters), written in a way that more closely followed pronunciation
  • The vocabulary: a large influx of Arabic words in religion, law, learning, and administration
  • The grammar and sound of the spoken language, which continued to change from Middle Persian forms toward those of early New Persian
  • The social setting of written Persian: from a language of the Sasanian state and the priesthood to a language used by Muslim secretaries, poets, and, increasingly, authors of prose
  • The status of Arabic: from the language of conquerors to the common language of scholarship and administration

  • The continuous spoken use of Persian by large populations across the plateau
  • The core grammar and everyday vocabulary of the language, shared across Middle and New Persian
  • The multilingual character of Iran, with Persian coexisting with other Iranian languages and with Arabic and Aramaic
  • The role of Zoroastrian communities in preserving Middle Persian literature
  • The importance of storytelling, advice, and ethical reflection in written culture

When and how change occurred: Change was gradual and uneven, faster in the written forms than in the spoken language, and faster in the northeast than in other regions. Secretaries, scholars, and poets experienced it most directly. Villagers and herders who spoke Persian at home experienced more continuity.

Why the pattern matters: It prevents two opposite errors: treating New Persian as a rebirth of an ancient language after centuries of silence, and treating it as a new language created by the conquest. It was a development of a living language, in new circumstances.

Division III · Section 33.15

The First New Persian Poetry and the Problem of Early Evidence

New Persian poetry is the best-known product of early written Persian, and it is also the most difficult to study with confidence. The earliest verses are attributed by later tradition to poets of the ninth century, many of them associated with the eastern regions of Khurasan, Sistan, and Transoxiana. Before examining the claims, students must understand how little of this early poetry survives in its original form. The poems were composed and often performed orally, and few were written down in manuscripts contemporary with their authors. What survives is mostly quotation: verses cited in later anthologies, biographical dictionaries of poets, chronicles, and dictionaries compiled in the eleventh through thirteenth centuries.

Later writers name several individuals as among the earliest Persian poets. Hanzala of Badghis, in what is now northwestern Afghanistan, is said to have flourished in the ninth century, and verses are attributed to him. Abu Hafs of Sogdiana, from the Samarkand region, is named in some traditions as an early composer. A poet named Muhammad ibn Wasif, a secretary at the court of the Saffarids of Sistan, appears in the local Tarikh-i Sistan. The anthologist Muhammad ʿAwfi, writing in the early thirteenth century, collected notices on these and other early poets, and the eleventh-century lexicon of Asadi Tusi, which cites early verses to illustrate the meanings of words, preserves some quotations. None of these sources is contemporary with the poets it describes. The poets' lives, careers, and even dates are known mainly from such later reports, and some details may reflect the later legends and expectations of the anthologists.

One famous story illustrates the problem. The Tarikh-i Sistan, a chronicle compiled in the eleventh century and continued later, reports that when a court poet praised the Saffarid ruler Yaʿqub ibn al-Layth in Arabic verse, the ruler asked why he should be addressed in a language he did not understand, and that this led to the composition of Persian verse for his court. The account has been widely repeated and is often cited as showing the origins of Persian court poetry. As a piece of evidence, however, it must be weighed carefully. It was recorded long after the event, it reflects the later interest of Sistan and of Persian-language literary culture in claiming an early and distinguished origin, and it cannot be confirmed from contemporary sources. It may preserve a memory of a real development, namely the appearance of Persian verse in the eastern courts, or it may be a later story that explains that development in a memorable way. The detailed political history of the Saffarids, and the account of their rise, belongs to Chapter 34.

Source Comparison

Early Judeo-Persian Evidence and Later Arabic-Script Persian Literary Traditions

Source set A: early Judeo-Persian evidence. The Dandan-Uiliq letter (usually dated to about 718), the Tang-i Azao inscription (usually dated to 752 or 753), and other early Judeo-Persian documents are contemporary or nearly contemporary records, written in Hebrew letters by members of Jewish communities in the east. They are short and practical in character: a business letter and an inscription, not an anthology of verse.

Source set B: later Arabic-script literary traditions. Accounts of the first Persian poets come mainly from the Tarikh-i Sistan (eleventh century), the lexicon of Asadi Tusi (eleventh century), and the anthology of ʿAwfi (early thirteenth century), among others. These sources are far more extensive and literary, and they name individuals, but they were compiled long after the poets they describe and survive in manuscripts of still later date.

Comparison: The first set is close in time to the events and was produced for non-literary purposes, so it is less likely to be shaped by literary tradition. It is, however, narrow in subject and cannot tell us about poetry or courts. The second set is rich in names, stories, and verses, and invaluable for understanding what later Persian readers believed about their literary origins, but each of its claims must be treated as a later tradition unless corroborated. The two sets complement one another: the first shows that Persian was being written in the eighth century, while the second shows how a later literary culture remembered the beginnings of Persian poetry.

What cannot be established from either set: The exact dates of the earliest Persian verses, the order in which the first poets composed, the reliability of the stories of patronage, or the extent of oral composition before the first written texts.

Another source-critical problem concerns attribution and linguistic form. Because early poems were transmitted orally and copied repeatedly, their language was often modernized by later copyists, and verses attributed to different poets sometimes reflect the conventions of a later period. The dating of individual poems on the basis of language, meter, or content is therefore uncertain. What can be said with reasonable confidence is that Persian poetry was being composed in the eastern Iranian lands in the ninth century, that it was connected with the courts and households of local rulers, and that it made use of the rhyming forms and, in some measure, the meters of the wider Islamic literary world. The mature flowering of that tradition, in the tenth century and under the Samanids, and the career of its best-known poets, belongs to Chapter 35.

The historical significance of this early poetry lies less in the specifics of individual careers than in what it reveals about the formation of a literary Persian. The first poets did not write in Middle Persian, nor did they follow the verse forms of the Sasanian minstrels in any simple way. They wrote in the spoken Persian of the east, in the Arabic script, with the rhyme and many of the meters of Arabic poetry, and for audiences that included rulers, officials, and educated townspeople. In this practice the elements of the new tradition can already be seen: inherited language, borrowed script and form, and the support of regional courts. It was a transformation, not a restoration, and the evidence indicates that its beginnings were modest and regional before it became a literary tradition of wide influence.

Division IV · Section 33.16

The Beginnings of New Persian Prose, Translation, and Historical Writing

Persian prose is a later and, in some ways, more revealing development than Persian verse. Verse had a ready-made audience in courts and a performance tradition behind it. Prose required a different kind of social infrastructure: authors, scribes, patrons, and readers who needed information, explanation, or narrative in Persian rather than Arabic. The first large-scale prose works in New Persian appear in the middle and later tenth century, in the eastern regions, and they tend to be translations, compilations, or adaptations of works that already existed in Arabic or in earlier Iranian traditions. This is a significant fact about the character of early Persian prose: it began less as an original literature than as a vehicle for making existing knowledge available to Persian-speaking audiences.

Several works illustrate the range. In 957 or 958, according to the preface that survives, a governor of Tus named Abu Mansur gathered scholars to compile a prose Shahnama, a book of the Iranian kings based on earlier traditions, including those descending from the Middle Persian Khwadāy-nāmag. The compilation itself is largely lost, but its preface survives, and the work is important as evidence of an effort to assemble Iranian historical memory in Persian prose, and as a source of the material that the epic poet Firdawsi used later, a subject treated in Chapter 42. In the 960s, associated with the Samanid ruler Mansur ibn Nuh, Persian versions of two of the largest works of Arabic scholarship were produced: a Persian rendering of al-Tabari's history, attributed to the vizier Balʿami, and a Persian rendering of al-Tabari's commentary on the Quran. Both are not literal translations. The Persian history abridges and rearranges the original and adds material from other sources, and the commentary was adapted for Persian readers.

The preface to the Persian commentary, as it is usually understood, describes how the ruler asked the learned men of Transoxiana about the permissibility of rendering the Quranic commentary into Persian, and how they ruled that it was permitted. It is not clear how reliable the account is in every detail, but it points to a real question: whether Persian could properly carry religious explanation. The Quran itself remained in Arabic, and the commentary rendered its meaning, not its words, for readers who did not understand Arabic well. The legal discussion of the use of Persian in religious contexts was not new. A view associated with the Hanafi school of law, whose influence was strong in Transoxiana and Khurasan, was that a person unable to recite in Arabic might be permitted to recite in Persian in certain circumstances, though this view was contested by other jurists. The Samanid-era translations should be understood against this background of discussion about language, religion, and accessibility. The detailed role of the Samanid court in sponsoring such works belongs to Chapter 35.

Persian prose also extended beyond history and religion. The geographical work Hudud al-ʿAlam ("The Limits of the World"), composed in 982 or 983 by an author whose name is not known, describes the regions of the world in Persian and was dedicated to a local ruler in the northeast. A treatise on pharmacology, the Abniya, written in Persian by a physician of Herat in the later tenth century, survives in a manuscript copied in 1055, among the oldest dated Persian prose manuscripts. The existence of such works shows that Persian was beginning to be used for technical and descriptive writing as well as narrative and religious explanation. The language of these early prose works is also instructive. It tends to use a relatively small number of Arabic words by the standard of later Persian, and to keep a syntax close to the spoken language, in contrast to the more elaborate and Arabic-influenced prose of later centuries.

Historical Debate

Was New Persian a Cultural Revival, a Transformation, or Both?

The question: Historians and general writers have long described the emergence of written New Persian in the ninth and tenth centuries as a "revival" or "renaissance" of Persian culture after centuries of Arab rule. Others argue that the term misleads, because it implies that the language and its culture had been dormant. The question concerns how to characterize the relationship between the new Persian literary culture and the Iranian past.

Interpretation A, revival: Some scholars, especially in older and nationalist writing, emphasize the recovery of pre-Islamic Iranian themes: the epic memory of the kings, the ethical counsel of the Sasanian court, the pride of Persian identity expressed in the Shuʿubiyya, and the patronage of rulers who claimed Iranian descent. On this view, the new literary language expressed an Iranian cultural identity that reasserted itself once political circumstances permitted.
Interpretation B, transformation: Others stress that spoken Persian never disappeared, that the language was written in new scripts with new vocabulary and borrowed poetic forms, that its authors were Muslims working in an Islamic framework, and that its major projects, such as the translations of al-Tabari, were adaptations of Arabic scholarship. From this standpoint, the new literary culture was an innovation produced by the Islamic world rather than a restoration of the Sasanian one.
Interpretation C, selective recovery within a new synthesis: Many recent historians combine elements of both views. They see authors and patrons selectively drawing on Iranian traditions of kingship, narrative, and ethics, within an Islamic, bilingual world, and creating something new from the combination. The memory of the pre-Islamic past was real and politically useful, but it was recalled in new forms and for new purposes.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on the same evidence: the continuous use of spoken Persian; the Judeo-Persian documents; the Arabic-script poetry and prose; the Arabic transmission of Sasanian royal tradition; the Pahlavi books of the Zoroastrians; the prefaces that describe the aims of patrons; and the Shuʿubiyya texts.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and terminology rather than basic facts. It turns on how much weight to give continuity versus novelty, on whether patrons' stated aims reflect real motives, and on what the word "revival" implies about the preceding period.

Why it matters: The choice of description affects how the whole period is understood. A narrative of revival can imply that Iranian identity was suppressed and then liberated, while a narrative of pure innovation can understate the role of inherited memory and language. The more careful description of a creative, selective transformation fits the evidence best, and it avoids reading modern national categories into the tenth century.

Division IV · Section 33.17

A Bilingual Intellectual World: Arabic and Persian in Different Settings

By the later tenth century, educated people in Khurasan, Transoxiana, and the Iranian plateau routinely moved between languages, choosing according to subject, audience, and purpose. A scholar writing a treatise on the sayings of the Prophet or a point of law wrote in Arabic, because that was the language of the field and its audience. A teacher explaining the meaning of a Quranic verse to a local congregation might do so in Persian. A secretary drafting an official letter to the caliph's court used Arabic, while a poet composing for a regional ruler might use either, or both. This was a stable system with distinct domains, and individuals moved fluidly among them.

Bilingual scholars are an index of this world. Many of the greatest figures of the age wrote in Arabic though Persian was their mother tongue, and some wrote in both. A well-known case is that of the scholar al-Biruni, a native of Khwarazm, whose first language was Khwarazmian and whose works are overwhelmingly in Arabic; in the mid-eleventh century he is reported to have remarked in one of his prefaces that Arabic was better suited to scientific writing than Persian. The statement postdates the period of this chapter and is paraphrased here from a later source, but it reflects an attitude held by many scholars of the tenth century and after. Other scholars of the same era, such as the physician and philosopher Ibn Sina, who was born near Bukhara in 980, wrote principally in Arabic, but also produced works in Persian for local audiences. These choices were not signs of conflict between loyalty to one language and loyalty to another. They reflected the practical question of what language would best reach the intended readers.

The adaptation of Persian to Islamic concepts shows another aspect of this world. Persian-speaking Muslims developed vocabulary for their religious life that was drawn from Persian roots rather than Arabic, such as namāz for the ritual prayer, rūza for fasting, khudā for God, farishta for angel, and bihisht and dūzakh for paradise and hell. Alongside these terms, Arabic loanwords were also used. The result was a religious vocabulary in Persian that was more distinctive than a simple translation of Arabic would have been, and it shows how Persian absorbed Islamic content while preserving its own resources. Local religious instruction, preaching, and storytelling in Persian also contributed, though much of this activity was oral and left little direct trace in the written record.

Multiple Perspectives

Four Ways of Using Language in the Tenth Century

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives that synthesize patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any named historical actor, and the groups did not have equal power or freedom of choice.

From the perspective of an Arabic-writing Iranian scholar in Nishapur or Baghdad: Arabic is the language of the Quran, of law, and of the learned community across the whole Islamic world. Writing in it brings access to the widest audience and to the highest forms of scholarly recognition. Persian remains the language of family and home, and its use for scholarship is a matter of convenience for readers who need it rather than a rival to the language of learning.
From the perspective of a Persian-speaking court poet in an eastern city: A ruler who understands Persian, and an audience who delight in it, provide a livelihood and an opportunity to compose in the spoken language of the court and the town. The Arabic prosody and rhyme offer a prestigious set of forms, and the use of Persian allows the poet to be heard directly by patrons. The poet's position depends on the favor of the ruler and is far from secure.
From the perspective of a Zoroastrian priestly scribe in Fars: Middle Persian and the Pahlavi script remain the proper vehicles for the sacred tradition, for the compilation of religious knowledge, and for defending the faith in argument with Muslims, Christians, and others. The writing of Persian in the Arabic script and the translation of Arabic scholarship are developments in the Muslim community, which the priests observe from a minority position under pressure to preserve their texts.
From the perspective of a book merchant and copyist in a major city: The profit is in books that people want to read, and the market is increasingly one of Arabic works, but also of Persian ones for those who prefer them. The merchant is concerned with paper, copyists, and customers, and cares less for the ideological meaning of language choices than for the demand of scholars, officials, and patrons.
Cause and Effect

Why Arabic Achieved Scholarly Prestige and Why Written Persian Developed New Functions

Long-term causes

The Quran and its Arabic text; the political authority of the caliphate; the movement of Arabic-speaking settlers into garrison cities; the existence of administrative and scribal traditions in Iran that could be adapted to Arabic; and a Persian-speaking majority with its own languages and literary memory.

Short-term causes

The Arabization of the chancery; the growth of schools of law and hadith; the spread of paper and the book trade; the Abbasid patronage of translation; and the emergence of regional rulers in the east who wanted courts and cultural prestige of their own.

Immediate context

In the ninth and tenth centuries, scholars across the Islamic world needed a common language, while regional courts in Khurasan and Transoxiana sought poets, secretaries, and histories that their rulers and townspeople could read directly.

Effects

Arabic became the shared language of scholarship, and Iranian scholars shaped it. Persian developed written forms for poetry, history, religious explanation, geography, and technical writing, adopting Arabic vocabulary and models while keeping its structure and its role as the spoken language of the region.

Historical complexity: Neither outcome was inevitable. The prestige of Arabic depended on religious, political, and institutional conditions that could have developed differently, and the rise of written Persian depended on regional political circumstances, the choices of individual patrons, and the work of many scribes and scholars. The two developments were not opposites. Arabic prestige created the infrastructure of schooling and script that Persian then used, and the expansion of Persian was possible because of, not in spite of, the Islamic scholarly world.

Division IV · Section 33.18

Intellectual Networks, Patronage, and the Circulation of Knowledge

The bilingual world described above depended on networks that carried scholars, books, and ideas across great distances. Scholars traveled between Baghdad, the cities of the plateau, Khurasan, and Transoxiana, seeking teachers and patrons. Students gathered in the major centers and returned to their home cities with notebooks and copied manuscripts. Letters, gifts, and introductions linked scholars in different regions. The routes of trade and administration were also the routes of knowledge: the road through Rayy, Nishapur, and Merv toward Bukhara, the Zagros routes to Iraq, and the river and desert tracks of Sistan all carried books as well as merchandise. The result was a continuing exchange in which a work written in Baghdad could be read in Bukhara within a generation, and in which scholars from Transoxiana could take up residence in Iraq.

Patronage supported this exchange. Caliphs, viziers, provincial governors, local rulers, wealthy merchants, and landowners paid scholars, commissioned books, and maintained libraries. The motives for patronage varied: religious devotion, the desire for prestige, the need for skilled administrators and advisers, and genuine intellectual interest. A later account of the physician and philosopher Ibn Sina, drawn from his autobiography, describes a visit to the royal library at Bukhara in the late tenth century, with its rooms of books arranged by subject. The account is a later report and should be treated with caution, but it conveys the impression of the scale that library culture could reach in Samanid Bukhara. The detailed treatment of Samanid patronage is reserved for Chapter 35. Here, it is enough to note that regional courts competed with the caliphal center in attracting scholars, and that this competition helped sustain intellectual life outside Baghdad.

Book production relied on a trade in paper, ink, and copyists. Works were copied by professional scribes, by students for their own use, and by scholars themselves. The circulation of a work depended on the availability of copies and on the reputation of its author, and often on the formal process by which an author or teacher authorized others to transmit his book. A book could be recited and corrected in a teaching circle, and the resulting copy might carry a certificate noting that it had been read and verified. Such practices helped ensure accuracy but also limited access to those who could attend the sessions or afford the copies. The sources for this book culture, including the Fihrist and biographical dictionaries, are products of the same world and tell us most about those who stood near its center.

Everyday Life and Social History

What Literacy, Book Access, and Multilingual Communication Meant for Different Social Groups

For most inhabitants of the Iranian lands, the story told in this chapter was experienced indirectly, if at all. The majority of the population were farmers, herders, laborers, and artisans, who lived in villages and small towns and spoke Persian or another Iranian language at home. Most could not read or write. Their contact with Arabic came through the Friday sermon, the call to prayer, the recitation of the Quran, and dealings with officials or tax collectors, while many carried on with their work in the languages of their families. A villager might know enough Arabic phrases to participate in prayer and ordinary religious practice without being able to speak the language fluently. Preachers and teachers who explained religious matters in the local language were the intermediaries between the written tradition and the village.

For secretaries, merchants, and artisans in the cities, multilingualism was a practical skill. A merchant in Bukhara might speak Persian and Sogdian, read Arabic for commercial documents and legal contracts, and use Persian in everyday dealings. A scribe needed Arabic to work in the chancery. Children of the learned classes received instruction in the Quran and in Arabic writing, and some went on to study grammar, poetry, and law, while others learned a craft or a trade through apprenticeship. Literacy was thus a graded and uneven attainment: many people could read simple texts or sign their names, fewer could compose, and still fewer could pursue advanced study.

The evidence for women's participation in this book culture is limited. Biographical dictionaries record women as transmitters of religious traditions in some periods and places, and later accounts mention women poets and patrons, including a tenth-century poet traditionally identified as Rabiʿa of Balkh or Quzdar, whose historical details are difficult to establish and whose reputation was shaped by later legend. Most of what is known about women's literacy and learning in the eighth through tenth centuries is filtered through male authors and concerns elite households. Non-elite readers, who may have listened to stories and poetry in public places rather than reading them, left almost no records. Historians must therefore infer their experience from indirect evidence and acknowledge that large parts of the intellectual world described in this chapter are known only from the viewpoint of the educated men who wrote about it.

Division V · Section 33.19

The Formation of an Islamic-Iranian Intellectual World: Continuity, Exchange, and Transformation

The preceding sections have followed several developments that, at first sight, seem separate. Iranian languages continued in use, Arabic became the language of scholarship, Iranian scholars shaped Arabic grammar, prose, and history, Sasanian books were translated into Arabic, a new written Persian emerged in several scripts, and networks of scholars and patrons linked the plateau to Baghdad and Transoxiana. The task of this concluding section is to show how these developments were connected, because their connection is the principal historical result of the period.

The first connection runs through institutions. The same conditions, the spread of paper, the book trade, the schools of the mosque and the court, and the patronage of rulers, supported both Arabic scholarship and the new Persian writing. The Arabic script and the schooling that taught it gave Persian a medium, and the habits of copying, collecting, and commenting developed in Arabic scholarship were applied to Persian texts. The second runs through people. Secretaries and scholars of Iranian background mastered Arabic and shaped it, and they also provided the first authors, translators, and audiences of written Persian. The same classes that produced an Arabic grammarian also produced the patrons of a Persian translation. The third runs through memory. The Sasanian past reached the Islamic world by translation into Arabic, and the same stock of narratives later informed Persian prose and poetry, so that Arabic transmission was the channel through which much Iranian memory reached the Persian literature of the tenth century and after.

These connections show why the simple alternatives, replacement or revival, do not describe what happened. Arabic did not replace Persian, because Persian continued to be spoken and then written, and because Arabic's role was a specific one. Persian was not simply revived, because it had not died, and because its written forms were new in script, vocabulary, and literary models. What emerged was a world in which individuals and communities could draw on several linguistic and scholarly traditions, and in which the relationship between them was largely cooperative and creative. Competition and tension existed, as the Shuʿubiyya debates show, but they occurred within a shared intellectual world, and many of the participants wrote in both languages.

The limits of the evidence should not be forgotten. The picture drawn here depends heavily on later sources for the earliest Persian poetry and for the translation movement, and on the viewpoint of educated, urban, Muslim, male authors. The experience of Sogdian-speaking farmers, of Zoroastrian villagers, of women, and of the poor remains largely hidden. The processes described also varied by region: the northeast was not Fars, and Iraq was not Khurasan. The reconstruction offered here is therefore provisional, as all historical reconstruction is, and it should be revisited as new manuscripts and documents are discovered and studied.

The developments of this chapter prepared the ground for those that follow. The regional dynasties of the ninth and tenth centuries, the Tahirids and Saffarids of Chapter 34 and the Samanids of Chapter 35, did not create the Islamic-Iranian intellectual world from nothing. They inherited a multilingual society with trained secretaries, a book trade, an established tradition of Arabic scholarship, an emerging written Persian, and a store of Iranian historical and ethical memory. Their patronage allowed these elements to combine and flourish, as the following chapters show. Later chapters examine their long-term consequences: the gathering of Iranian historical memory in verse (Chapter 42), the achievements of science, medicine, and philosophy in Arabic and Persian (Chapter 43), and the spread of Persian beyond Iran as a language of literature and administration in the wider Persianate world.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Islamic-Iranian intellectual world of the eighth through tenth centuries was neither the replacement of Persian culture by Arabic culture nor the simple revival of a dormant Persian tradition. It was a new creation, built through the interaction of Arabic, Iranian languages, inherited traditions, and institutions of learning. The conquest changed the government and the religious framework of Iran, but it did not by itself change the languages that most people spoke. Language history followed its own timetable, shaped by schooling, administration, trade, religion, and prestige.

The linguistic landscape of early Islamic Iran was multilingual. Middle Persian, written in the Pahlavi script, was the language of the Sasanian chancery and the Zoroastrian priesthood, but it coexisted with Parthian-derived dialects, Sogdian, Bactrian, Khwarazmian, Kurdish and Caspian languages, Syriac and Aramaic, and the Arabic of garrison settlers. No single standardized Persian existed, and the future of written New Persian was neither predetermined nor limited to one region.

Arabic became the language of administration through a process that was uneven in time and place, beginning with the central registers of Iraq around the end of the seventh century and reaching Khurasan later, while local languages such as Bactrian and Sogdian continued in documentary use for some decades. Arabic also became the shared language of scholarship because it carried the Quran, the law, the sciences of tradition, and a vast community of readers. Writing in Arabic did not make an author an Arab. Scholars of Arab, Persian, African, Christian, and Sabian background all wrote in it, and the status of the client (mawla) shows that membership in the Muslim community was defined by more than descent.

Iranians were builders of Arabic scholarship. The grammar of Sibawayh, the lexicography of scholars from Herat, Farab, and Hamadan, the hadith collections of scholars from Bukhara, Nishapur, Termez, Nasa, Sistan, and Qazvin, and the adab culture of secretaries all drew on people of Iranian origin working within the Arabic tradition. Cities such as Basra, Kufa, Baghdad, Merv, Nishapur, Rayy, and Bukhara became centers of learning organized around teaching circles, courts, libraries, and booksellers, supported by the spread of paper, not by formal colleges, which belong to a later period.

Translation transformed the Sasanian inheritance. Middle Persian works of advice, storytelling, and royal history, among them Kalīla wa-Dimna and the Khwadāy-nāmag, were rendered into Arabic, preserved in Arabic literature, and adapted to Islamic frameworks. Historians such as al-Dinawari and al-Tabari incorporated Iranian royal tradition into universal histories. Because the Middle Persian originals are lost, these Arabic histories are valuable but secondary evidence, shaped by chains of transmission and by the concerns of their authors. The Shuʿubiyya controversies reveal that the standing of Iranian heritage within the Islamic world was debated in writing, but they were not an early Persian nationalism.

New Persian emerged from the continuous development of spoken Persian, written in new scripts and enriched by Arabic vocabulary. The earliest dated evidence is Judeo-Persian, in Hebrew letters, from the eighth century, and the Arabic script, adapted with additional letters, became dominant because of its place in schooling, administration, and the book trade. Debates about the meaning of Dari reflect the multiple origins of the literary language, which seems to have descended from southwestern Iranian speech and been shaped in the northeast. The spread of Persian in Khurasan and Transoxiana came at the expense of Sogdian and Bactrian, a reminder that it was not a culturally neutral process.

The earliest Persian poetry is known mostly through later collections, anthologies, and chronicles, so reconstructed careers and dates must be handled cautiously. What can be said is that Persian verse was being composed in the east in the ninth century, adapting Arabic prosody and rhyme to the Persian language without importing Arabic grammar. In the mid-tenth century, Persian prose appeared in the form of a prose Shahnama, Persian renderings of al-Tabari's history and commentary, geography, and technical writing, often as translation or adaptation. Arabic remained central to many scholarly fields.

By the later tenth century, Iran possessed a bilingual intellectual world in which authors, readers, and communities chose among Arabic, Persian, Middle Persian, Judeo-Persian, and local languages according to audience, purpose, and tradition. Networks of scholars, books, and patrons connected Baghdad, the plateau, Khurasan, and Transoxiana. The evidence we have reflects mainly the educated, urban, male, Muslim elite, and it says little about villagers, women, and many minorities.

These developments created the foundations for what followed. The regional dynasties of Chapters 34 and 35 inherited and patronized a world that had already assembled trained secretaries, scholarly institutions, a written Persian, and a store of Iranian memory. The emergence of New Persian was not a return to the Sasanian past, and the use of Arabic did not prevent Iranian cultural development. Both were parts of the same historical creation.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a society absorbed a profound political and religious transformation without losing the ability to create. Iran after the conquests did not become a copy of the Arabic-speaking heartlands, and it did not return to the Sasanian world. Its scholars, secretaries, poets, priests, and merchants worked in several languages, and from that work came both the participation of Iranians in building Arabic scholarship and the beginnings of a written Persian literature. The case is a reminder that cultural history rarely follows the simple scripts of replacement or recovery.

The chapter also reveals larger processes that recur across the textbook: the importance of institutions, such as schools, courts, and the book trade, in shaping what languages can do; the role of translation as a creative act of selection and adaptation; and the way later memory reshapes earlier events. The reliance on later sources for the earliest Persian poetry and for the translation movement is a lesson in critical reading that applies to many periods. Students who learn to ask who recorded an event, how long after, and for what purpose have acquired a habit of mind that will serve them throughout the textbook.

The developments described here shaped what followed. The regional dynasties of the ninth and tenth centuries built their courts on this foundation, and the Persian language that emerged became a medium of literature, administration, and learning across a wide area, the Persianate world introduced in Chapter 1 and explored in later chapters. The continuing life of Arabic in scholarship, and the bilingual habits of educated people, also persisted: for centuries, major writers in Iranian lands wrote in both languages, and the relationship between them remained an unusually productive one.

The chapter connects the Iranian plateau to Iraq, Central Asia, and the wider Islamic world, and it connects the Sasanian past to the Islamic present through the long chain of transmission from Middle Persian to Arabic to Persian. It contributes to the larger textbook by showing that continuity and transformation were not opposites in Iranian history, and by preparing the way for the political and literary developments of the chapters that follow. The Islamic-Iranian intellectual world was not given by the conquest or recovered from antiquity. It was made, over several centuries, by people working with the languages and traditions they had.
