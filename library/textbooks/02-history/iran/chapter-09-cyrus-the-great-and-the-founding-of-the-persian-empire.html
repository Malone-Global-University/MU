Cyrus the Great and the Founding of the Persian Empire

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

Within roughly two decades, a ruler of Anshan in southwestern Iran became the king of Media, the conqueror of Lydia, and the king of Babylon. What cannot be reconstructed with equal confidence is how, in what order, and with what intentions.

In 1879 excavators at Babylon recovered a barrel-shaped clay cylinder inscribed in Babylonian cuneiform. It is now in the British Museum. Written after the conquest of Babylon in 539 BCE, it presents a new king, Cyrus, as the chosen restorer of Babylonian order. It was composed in Babylonian language and scribal tradition for a Babylonian political and religious context, presumably under Cyrus's authority. That fact is the key to this chapter: the founder of Persian imperial power is known first through the words and institutions of the peoples he conquered.

Chapter 8 asked what kind of political order the Medes actually formed and concluded that military power and regional dominance should not be equated automatically with a bureaucratic empire. Chapter 9 asks the next question. How did Cyrus II turn the Persian kingship of Anshan and Fars into a power that spanned Media, Anatolia, Babylonia, and much of the Iranian world? The chapter treats this as a transformation with several parts: military victory, political incorporation, legitimation in the eyes of very different audiences, and the gradual emergence of a new imperial polity.

The chapter is also about evidence. The best-documented events of Cyrus's career come from Babylonian sources: a year-by-year chronicle and a royal inscription. Much fuller narratives come from Greek authors writing a century or more later, including Herodotus and Xenophon, and these give us Cyrus's supposed childhood, dreams, and death. They are vivid, and they frequently conflict. Students will learn to ask of every claim who recorded it, when, and for whom.

This chapter stops short of the later standardization and reorganization of imperial government. Cyrus already ruled through governors, inherited administrative institutions, and provincial arrangements, but the more systematically documented fiscal, territorial, road, and administrative structures associated especially with Darius belong to Chapters 10 and 11. Here the subject is conquest, legitimation, incorporation, and the passage from kingdom to empire under Cyrus, closing with his death around 530 BCE and the succession of Cambyses II.

Two modern myths need addressing at the outset. The first is that Cyrus captured Babylon without a fight and issued the first declaration of human rights. The British Museum itself notes that reading the Cylinder as a human-rights charter is anachronistic. The second is that "Achaemenid" was Cyrus's own dynastic label. Section 9.2 shows why that is more complicated than it looks.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Cyrus II moved from the kingship of Anshan to rule over Media, Anatolia, Babylonia, and eastern regions.
  2. Distinguish earlier Babylonian documentary and chronicle traditions from later Greek narrative traditions about Cyrus.
  3. Analyze the Nabonidus Chronicle's account of the defeat of Astyages and assess Herodotus's competing story.
  4. Evaluate whether Cyrus "inherited" a Median empire, given the uncertainties established in Chapter 8.
  5. Assess the chronology of the Lydian conquest and explain why it is uncertain.
  6. Describe the sequence of Opis, Sippar, and Babylon in 539 BCE and explain why the conquest was not bloodless.
  7. Interpret the Cyrus Cylinder as a Babylonian legitimation text rather than a modern rights charter.
  8. Explain how incorporation, not only conquest, made Persian rule durable, without attributing Darius's institutions to Cyrus.
  9. Evaluate Pasargadae as evidence and distinguish Cyrus-period architecture from later inscriptions.
  10. Compare interpretations of whether Cyrus created a new style of empire.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did Cyrus II transform a regional Persian kingship rooted in Anshan into a multinational empire?
  2. How can historians separate the securely documented Cyrus from the conqueror and ideal ruler constructed by later traditions?
  3. What does the fall of Astyages reveal about the nature of Median power?
  4. Why is the chronology of the Lydian and eastern campaigns so much less certain than that of Media and Babylon?
  5. What does the Cyrus Cylinder tell us about Cyrus's policies, and what does it tell us about how conquerors legitimized themselves?
  6. In what sense was Cyrus's rule "incorporative," and what were the limits of that description?
  7. Why do inscriptions attached to Cyrus's own capital not necessarily record his own words?
  8. How did conquered peoples experience Persian conquest differently from Persian rulers?

Historical Context

By the mid-sixth century BCE, western Asia was organized around several powers. Babylonia, ruled from 556 BCE by Nabonidus, held the Mesopotamian lowlands and extended influence toward Syria and northern Arabia. Lydia, under Croesus, dominated much of western Anatolia and its Greek cities. Egypt remained independent. In western Iran, Median rulers held Ecbatana, and, as Chapter 8 argued, the extent and organization of their authority are debated. Farther southeast, in the region examined in Chapter 5 as the highland heart of Elam, Anshan in Fars was governed by a Persian royal line whose members had absorbed Elamite administrative and cultural traditions.

The Neo-Assyrian empire, which had dominated the Near East for centuries, had collapsed in the late seventh century BCE. Its administrative practices, royal ideology, and deportation policies nonetheless formed part of the political inheritance of every successor state. Cyrus emerged into a world that already possessed vocabularies of imperial kingship. What was new was the scale of the polity that he assembled and the speed of its assembly.

Two structural conditions matter. First, as Chapter 1 showed, power on the plateau had to integrate regions with different ecologies and populations, and Fars, Media, and Khuzestan were connected by the Zagros corridors. Second, Cyrus operated in a politically diverse landscape of local rulers, regional elites, settled communities, and mobile populations. The surviving evidence does not allow historians to reconstruct his early recruitment system or the precise balance between royal command, elite cooperation, and local military obligations.

Section 9.1

From Anshan to Empire

At Astyages's court, according to Chapter 8's reading of the evidence, Median authority rested on alliances with regional lords and on military leadership rather than on a fully articulated bureaucratic state. In Anshan, a Persian dynasty ruled a smaller but well-resourced highland and lowland region with deep Elamite roots. Within about a generation, this regional balance was overturned.

The central transformation was not simply "Persia replaces Media." A single king came to combine several roles at once: Persian king of Anshan, successor to Median authority in the highlands, ruler of Lydia and its Greek subjects, and king of Babylon. Each role required a different kind of legitimacy, addressed to a different audience. The empire that resulted was assembled through conquest and then held together, in significant part, through politically intelligible incorporation.

Section 9.2

Cyrus before the Conquests: Anshan, Persia, and Royal Descent

Cyrus's own Babylonian titulary, preserved in the Cylinder, calls him king of Anshan, son of Cambyses, and descendant of Teispes, all kings of Anshan. This is the strongest contemporary statement of his lineage. Anshan was an old Elamite name for the highland region around modern Tal-e Malyan in Fars (see Chapter 1 and Chapter 5), and its use shows that Persian rulers claimed a place within an established political geography rather than inventing one.

Greek tradition adds that Cyrus was the grandson of Astyages through his mother Mandane. That genealogy comes from Greek narratives, not from Cyrus's own inscriptions, and it may reflect later efforts to present Persian rule as continuous with Median rule. It should be treated as tradition rather than established fact.

Historical Vocabulary
Achaemenid

Definition: The dynastic name (Old Persian Haxāmaniš, Greek Achaimenes) later applied to the Persian royal house from Cyrus to Darius III.

Historical context: The Cyrus Cylinder does not describe Cyrus as an Achaemenid. The familiar Pasargadae inscriptions naming "Cyrus, an Achaemenid" are probably later, associated with Darius's reign, when the dynasty's descent was being defined in a way that linked Darius's line to Cyrus's.

Why it matters: "Achaemenid" is a retrospective label for the whole dynasty. Using it for Cyrus's own self-understanding risks projecting Darius's dynastic claims backward.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume Cyrus "was an Achaemenid" in the sense that he himself used that term.

Section 9.3

How Do We Know Cyrus? Sources and Their Problems

The Nabonidus Chronicle, a Babylonian text recording events year by year, is the most useful Babylonian chronicle tradition for reconstructing Cyrus's conquests of Media and Babylon. The surviving tablet, BM 35382, is a much later copy, probably dating to the third or second century BCE, although at least part of the chronicle tradition it preserves is generally thought to derive from Cyrus's period. It is terse, partly damaged, and written from a Babylonian scribal perspective. The Cyrus Cylinder is a royal inscription composed to legitimate Cyrus's rule and is therefore a source for ideology rather than neutral report. Babylonian administrative and economic documents show how ordinary institutions, including temples, dated their records to the new king. Archaeology at Pasargadae supplies architecture and landscape.

Herodotus, writing roughly a century later, offers the fullest narrative of Cyrus's birth, rise, and death, but he drew on oral traditions and Greek assumptions and often conflates or dramatizes. Xenophon's Cyropaedia is a philosophical work about ideal rule, not a biography, and it even gives Cyrus a different route to power in Media. Later classical writers repeat, adapt, and add to these traditions.

Historical Method

Which Cyrus?

Nabonidus Chronicle (a later copy of an earlier chronicle tradition): Cyrus as a political and military actor in dated events (Astyages, Opis, Babylon). Cannot tell us his motives or inner policy.

Cyrus Cylinder: Cyrus as represented through Babylonian scribal and royal traditions within a Babylonian political and religious context. Cannot tell us how he was seen elsewhere or what he privately intended.

Babylonian administrative evidence: Continuity of institutions and dating conventions. Cannot tell us about battles.

Pasargadae: Royal self-presentation in Fars. Cannot alone tell us about conquests, and some of its inscriptions are later.

Herodotus and Xenophon: Greek ideas about Cyrus, some possibly preserving Persian or Median traditions. Each claim must be tested against other evidence.

Student task:

  1. Which of these sources could tell us whether Cyrus's soldiers fought at Opis? Which could not?
  2. Why might a Greek author admire Cyrus, and how might that shape his account?
Section 9.4

Cyrus and Astyages: The Overthrow of Median Rule

The Nabonidus Chronicle records that in 550/549 BCE Astyages mustered his army against Cyrus, king of Anshan. The army revolted, seized Astyages, and delivered him to Cyrus, who then marched into Ecbatana and carried off its silver, gold, and other possessions to Anshan. This is short but firm: Median rule ended through mutiny and capture, and Ecbatana's treasure was taken to the Persian homeland.

Herodotus tells a longer story of a dream, an infant Cyrus saved by a herdsman, a banquet at which the general Harpagus is served his own son, and a revolt organized by Harpagus. These tales belong to a widespread folkloric pattern of a prophesied ruler rescued in infancy and should not be used as the factual backbone of the narrative. They are best used as evidence for how Greeks, and perhaps some Median or Persian informants, remembered and explained the change of dynasty.

Section 9.5

Conquest or Succession? Incorporating Media

Cyrus's victory did not obliterate Media. Median elites and personnel continued to appear prominently under Persian rule. Greek writers' later tendency to use "Mede" broadly for Persians additionally shows the persistence of Median terminology and prestige in Greek perceptions, but does not by itself prove administrative or personnel continuity.

Yet historians should not say that Cyrus simply "inherited the Median Empire." Chapter 8 showed that the structure and boundaries of Median authority are uncertain. It is safer to speak of a transition from Median power to a Persian-led conquest state and expanding imperial polity, and only later to a mature Achaemenid imperial system. The phrase "liberated Persia from Median domination" likewise belongs to later narrative tradition rather than to documented history.

Section 9.6

Lydia, Croesus, and the Conquest of Anatolia

According to Herodotus, Croesus crossed the Halys river into Cappadocia and fought an indecisive battle at Pteria, withdrew to Sardis, and was then pursued by Cyrus, who defeated him and took the city. The traditional date is 547 or 546 BCE. The Nabonidus Chronicle passage often used to support 547 depends on a damaged geographical name whose restoration as "Lydia" is disputed, so the exact year should be qualified.

Persian rule was then extended over the Greek cities of western Anatolia, a process usually associated with Cyrus's commanders after Cyrus himself turned east. The later consequences of this Aegean frontier belong to Chapter 13.

Section 9.7

Cyrus in the East: Expansion beyond the Iranian Plateau

Persian rule later clearly included Parthia, Hyrcania, Bactria, Sogdiana, Arachosia, and Gandhara, and tradition credits Cyrus with extending power into these regions. But there is little contemporary documentation of when and how each was incorporated, and the evidence for Cyrus's Central Asian campaigns is especially scarce. By Darius's accession, eastern lands including Parthia, Bactria, Sogdiana, Arachosia, and Gandhara belonged to the Persian imperial sphere, and Cambyses is not known to have conducted eastern wars. Their incorporation is therefore generally associated with Cyrus, but the order, dates, mechanisms, and exact boundaries of Cyrus's eastern expansion remain poorly documented.

The lesson of this section is uneven documentation. The empire's reach to the east is not in doubt, but the sequence, campaigns, and exact boundaries usually cannot be reconstructed with confidence. Students should resist any tidy year-by-year "eastern tour."

Section 9.8

Nabonidus and the Road to Babylon

Nabonidus took the Babylonian throne in 556 BCE, and Babylonian sources show that he spent about a decade at the oasis of Tayma in northern Arabia. His religious policy, in particular his devotion to the moon god Sin, is presented by hostile texts as neglect of Marduk and of the New Year (Akitu) festival. Yet these accounts largely come from Persian-era texts, including the Cylinder, that had reasons to depict Nabonidus as an unfit ruler. They should not be repeated as neutral fact.

What is clear is that Babylonia was a wealthy and prestigious prize, and that Cyrus's growing power in the north and east posed a serious threat. Whether internal tensions within Babylon weakened Nabonidus, or whether such tensions were exaggerated after the fact, is difficult to judge.

Section 9.9

539 BCE: Opis, Sippar, and the Capture of Babylon

In autumn 539 BCE, Cyrus's forces defeated a Babylonian army at Opis on the Tigris. Sippar fell soon after, apparently without a fight. Babylon was then occupied, Nabonidus was captured, and Cyrus entered the city and took up Babylonian kingship. The Chronicle states that Ugbaru, governor of Gutium, entered Babylon with Cyrus's forces before Cyrus himself entered the city. A later line uses the form Gubaru for the official who appointed district governors; these forms are often identified as the same Gobryas, though the text itself uses the two spellings.

The city could therefore be taken without a major battle within its walls, but the wider conquest was not bloodless. Fighting had already occurred at Opis, and an army had been defeated. Correcting this point removes one of the most common Cyrus myths.

Section 9.10

The Cyrus Cylinder: Conquest, Legitimation, and Babylonian Kingship

The Cylinder was deposited after 539 BCE, is written in Babylonian language and script, and uses traditional Babylonian royal ideology. It represents Cyrus as chosen by Marduk, condemns Nabonidus, and describes the restoration of cult images and the return of displaced populations to their sanctuaries.

Primary Source Window

The Cyrus Cylinder

Creator: Babylonian scribal composition issued under Cyrus's authority · Date: after 539 BCE · Type: Royal foundation inscription in Babylonian cuneiform on a clay cylinder, found at Babylon in 1879, now in the British Museum

Context: Composed after the occupation of Babylon, in a genre of foundation deposits used by Mesopotamian kings.

Paraphrase (not a direct quotation): The text describes Marduk as turning away from Nabonidus and seeking a righteous ruler. Marduk names Cyrus, who enters Babylon peacefully as a restorer, respects the city's cults, and returns gods and people to their sanctuaries.

Analysis: The Cylinder shows how Cyrus's regime framed the new king through Babylonian language, Marduk, and established Babylonian royal formulas. It shows Cyrus's regime understood that Babylon's temples and priesthood were essential political actors.

Limitations: It is a legitimation text. It cannot establish Cyrus's private religious beliefs, how other conquered peoples were treated, or how fully its claims about restoration were carried out. Its claim of peaceful entry does not address Opis.

Perspective: The perspective is that of the conquering court and its Babylonian scribes. Voices of ordinary Babylonians, supporters of Nabonidus, and other conquered peoples are missing. Modern reading of the Cylinder as a "declaration of human rights" is anachronistic; it says nothing about rights in the modern sense.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the source say or record?
  2. Who created it and under what circumstances?
  3. What purposes shaped it?
  4. What can historians learn from it?
  5. What can historians not establish from it alone?
Section 9.11

Governing by Incorporation: Continuity, Religion, and Local Elites

Cyrus combined political incorporation with inherited and developing administrative structures. The evidence is clearest in Babylonia, where existing scribal, temple, legal, and governing institutions continued under Persian rule; the empire-wide system is much less fully documented for Cyrus than it later becomes under Darius. He appeared in locally intelligible royal forms, used existing elites and scribal networks, restored selected cults and images, and integrated existing political communities rather than trying to make them culturally uniform. Some displaced populations were permitted to return home.

This should not be turned into the slogan that "Cyrus invented religious tolerance." The evidence supports pragmatic and sophisticated accommodation within ancient imperial kingship, not modern pluralism. Restoration of cults and sanctuaries could help secure cooperation from local religious institutions and present the new king as a legitimate restorer. The later standardization of taxation, labor obligations, and provincial government belongs to Chapters 10 and 11.

Historical Vocabulary
Titulary

Definition: The set of titles and epithets a ruler uses in official texts.

Historical context: Cyrus's Babylonian titulary includes "king of Anshan" and the titles of Babylonian kingship, showing that he adapted his self-presentation to each audience.

Why it matters: Titles show how rulers wished to be understood, and change with audience and date.

Section 9.12

Pasargadae and the Making of Persian Royal Identity

Pasargadae, in Fars, was Cyrus's dynastic capital and preserves the earliest monumental phase of Persian imperial architecture: palaces, gardens, a stone-terraced platform at Tall-e Takht, and a tomb traditionally identified as Cyrus's. UNESCO describes it as Cyrus's first dynastic capital and an early synthesis of traditions drawn from the empire's peoples, and it was inscribed as a World Heritage Site in 2004.

Yet the site poses a method problem. The Old Persian, Elamite, and Babylonian inscriptions declaring "Cyrus the king, an Achaemenid" are generally assigned to Darius's period rather than Cyrus's own. The buildings can belong to Cyrus while an inscription attached to them belongs to a later king reshaping Cyrus's memory.

Section 9.13

Cyrus's Death, Succession, and the Empire He Left

Cyrus died around 530 BCE. Herodotus has him killed fighting Tomyris and the Massagetae, but ancient accounts differ, and even the location of the Massagetae is uncertain. The manner of his death should not be presented as settled.

What is secure is that Cyrus left an enormous empire, that Cambyses II succeeded him, and that the Persian monarchy now ruled peoples with very different languages, religions, and political histories. Cyrus left an already functioning empire whose administration would be expanded, reorganized, and more systematically documented under Cambyses and especially Darius (Chapter 10).

Historical Debate

Did Cyrus Create a New Style of Empire?

The question: Was Cyrus's empire distinctively accommodating, or did it continue older Near Eastern imperial practice?

Interpretation A — A new style: Cyrus's emphasis on local traditions, elite continuity, and cult restoration produced an unusually durable multinational structure.
Interpretation B — Continuity with older kingship: Cyrus adopted Babylonian legitimation formulas, conquest remained violent where resisted, and restoration was a political act within long-standing Mesopotamian practice, not an unprecedented doctrine of tolerance.

Evidence: The Cylinder, the Chronicle, Babylonian administrative texts, and comparison with Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian royal inscriptions.

Points of disagreement: Mostly interpretation and scale: how far accommodation was new, and how far it was intended policy.

Why it matters: It shapes whether Cyrus is read as an innovator or as a masterful user of existing models.

Student task: Which additional evidence, for example from other conquered regions, would help decide?

Multiple Perspectives

The Fall of Babylon

These are analytical reconstructions, not quotations.

Cyrus's court: Presents the conquest as restoration of order under divine sanction.
Babylonian temple officials: Weighed continuity of cult and institutional position against the fall of the previous Babylonian regime.
Soldiers and villagers near Opis: Experienced the campaign as military disruption, with little say in the outcome.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Assyrian and Babylonian Kingship to Persian Empire

What changed: The scale of the polity and the range of its peoples; a Persian dynasty at the center; the beginnings of a distinct Persian royal identity at Pasargadae.

What continued: Babylonian royal ideology and temple institutions; existing elites and scribes; the conventions of foundation deposits.

Pace and experience: Change was abrupt at the level of rulership and gradual at the level of local institutions. Elites and temple staff saw more continuity; soldiers, deportees, and those on campaign routes saw more rupture.

Why it matters: Persian imperial identity was built by adapting existing forms, not by discarding them.

Cause and Effect

The Founding of the Persian Empire

Long-term causes

Collapse of Assyria; an Anshan kingship with Elamite and highland resources; the post-Assyrian regional balance among Media, Lydia, Babylonia, Egypt, and smaller powers.

Short-term causes and trigger

Unrest in Astyages's army; Herodotus's account that Croesus moved against Cyrus after the fall of Astyages; Nabonidus's long absence at Tayma and disputes surrounding his religious and political policies, whose precise contribution to Babylon's fall remains debated.

Immediate effects

Persian control of Ecbatana, Sardis, and Babylon; incorporation of Median and Babylonian elites.

Long-term consequences

An empire whose consolidation became the task of Cambyses and Darius.

Historical complexity: No single cause, neither Cyrus's ability nor his enemies' weakness, is sufficient.

Everyday Life and Social History

What Conquest Meant to Ordinary Communities

In some places, temple institutions and local administration carried on with the new king's name in the date formula. In others, campaigning could disrupt harvests, require supplies, and displace people. Taxation and labor obligations continued under new authorities, and some communities were forced or permitted to move. Accommodation at the level of kingship did not mean ordinary people ceased to experience conquest as coercive. Most sources come from elites and scribes, so the voices of farmers, herders, and laborers must be inferred from archaeology and administrative records.

Chapter Summary

Cyrus II began as ruler of Anshan, a Persian kingship in Fars with Elamite roots. Within about two decades he brought Media, Lydia, and Babylonia under his rule and extended Persian authority far to the east. This chapter has treated that transformation as a combination of conquest and political incorporation, not as the simple replacement of one empire by another.

Our evidence is uneven. The Nabonidus Chronicle tradition, preserved in a much later copy, provides important dated evidence for Astyages's defeat and the conquest of Babylonia, while the contemporary Cyrus Cylinder provides direct evidence for how Cyrus's regime framed and legitimized its Babylonian kingship. Greek authors give richer but later and less reliable narratives, and archaeology at Pasargadae speaks to royal identity but includes later inscriptions.

The defeat of Astyages in 550/549 BCE is well attested, but Cyrus should not be said to have simply inherited a Median empire. The Lydian date is uncertain, and eastern expansion is poorly documented. In 539 BCE, Babylon was entered without a major battle inside its walls, but the wider conquest included the fighting at Opis.

The Cyrus Cylinder is best read as a Babylonian legitimation text. Cyrus's rule was durable partly because it adapted to local kingship and institutions, but this was pragmatic imperial politics, not modern religious pluralism. Pasargadae shows the beginnings of a distinct Persian royal identity, while its later inscriptions show how Cyrus's memory was shaped by Darius's dynasty.

Cyrus died around 530 BCE in circumstances that remain uncertain. His son Cambyses II inherited an already functioning empire whose administration would be expanded, reorganized, and more systematically documented under Cambyses and especially Darius (Chapter 10).

Key Terms

Anshan
Highland region of Fars and old Elamite center whose name Persian kings, including Cyrus, used in their titles.
Achaemenid
Retrospective dynastic name for the Persian royal house, tied in inscriptions to Darius's period.
Titulary
Set of royal titles used in official texts, adapted to each audience.
Nabonidus Chronicle
Babylonian year-by-year text recording Astyages's defeat and the fall of Babylon.
Cyrus Cylinder
Babylonian clay foundation inscription presenting Cyrus as Marduk's chosen king.
Cuneiform
Wedge-shaped script used for Babylonian and other languages.
Legitimation
The strategies by which a ruler claims rightful authority.
Ecbatana
Median capital, whose wealth Cyrus carried off to Anshan.
Marduk
Chief god of Babylon, said in the Cylinder to have chosen Cyrus.
Akitu
Babylonian New Year festival central to kingship.
Foundation deposit
Inscribed object placed in a building or city by a king to record his works.
Incorporation
Integration of conquered elites and institutions into the new political order.
Opis
Tigris site of the Persian victory over the Babylonian army in 539 BCE.
Sippar
Babylonian city that fell to Persian forces soon after Opis.
Croesus
Lydian king known chiefly through Herodotus.
Tayma
Arabian oasis where Nabonidus spent years.
Pasargadae
Cyrus's dynastic capital in Fars, a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
Tall-e Takht
Stone-terraced platform at Pasargadae.
Cyropaedia
Xenophon's work on ideal rulership, not a reliable biography.
Massagetae
Steppe people linked by Herodotus to Cyrus's death; location uncertain.

Chapter Reflection

Cyrus's career shows that conquest alone does not explain imperial durability. His rule required making Persian kingship intelligible to Median nobles, Lydian and Greek subjects, and Babylonian priests, each in their own terms.

The chapter also shows that the founder of a state is often known through the words of others. Babylonian scribes, Greek writers, and Darius's inscription-makers all shaped his memory, and historians must read each against the others.

What Cyrus left was an already functioning empire. Chapter 10 turns to Cambyses II and Darius I, who consolidated, reorganized, and systematized its government.
