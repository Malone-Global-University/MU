The Seljuk Conquest and the Turkic-Iranian Political Synthesis

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

Within about two decades, Oghuz Turkic groups who had asked for pasture on the margins of Khurasan produced a ruling family that was recognized in Baghdad as the principal secular power of the Abbasid world. The Seljuks did not invent the Persianate Islamic political order they entered. They joined it to a different dynastic and military tradition, and the tensions inside that combination explain both the durability and the repeated breakdown of what they built.

In December 1055, a Turkic commander named Tughril entered Baghdad at the invitation, or at least with the eventual recognition, of the Abbasid caliph al-Qaʾim. The last Buyid ruler in the city was removed from power. The caliph, who had lived for more than a century under the supervision of Iranian military dynasties from the Daylami highlands, received a ruler whose family had been on the Syr Darya steppe only a few generations earlier, and whose followers still moved with their herds. Twenty years before, the same family had been little more than leaders of an Oghuz following that was seeking grazing land from the Ghaznavid governors of Khurasan. How a pastoral military following became a recognized sultanate in so short a time is the first problem of this chapter.

The second problem is what kind of order the Seljuks created. The standard summary is that Turkic warriors provided the swords and Persian viziers provided the pens. That summary contains a truth that earlier Muslim historians recognized themselves, but it is also misleading. Seljuk rule was built from three overlapping inheritances: the dynastic and military habits of the Oghuz, including shared family sovereignty and the delegation of command to relatives; the administrative and literary institutions of the Samanids, Buyids, and Ghaznavids, examined in Chapters 35 through 37; and the claim to Sunni Islamic legitimacy that came from supporting the Abbasid caliphate. None of these was static, and none belonged to one ethnic group. Turkic commanders learned to collect taxes, Iranian secretaries acquired armed followings and landed interests, and the sultans distributed power among relatives in ways that the Persianate bureaucracy had to accommodate rather than simply administer.

This chapter therefore studies a process of integration rather than a conquest followed by absorption. The Seljuks took over cities, roads, tax registers, mosques, and literate officials that already existed. They also changed how those things worked. Revenue assignments supported armies. Princes governed appanages, that is, territorial shares held by members of the ruling family. Atabegs, military guardians of young princes, became regional rulers in their own right. Royal women influenced succession and patronage. A Persianate court culture that had grown under the Samanids and the Ghaznavids acquired new patrons and new audiences from Iraq to Transoxiana, even as the sultans themselves often continued to move between winter and summer quarters rather than rule from one fixed capital.

The chapter follows the chronology from the Oghuz background and the migration into Khurasan beginning in 1035, through the victory at Dandanqan in 1040, the takeover of Baghdad in 1055, the campaigns of Alp Arslan and the battle of Manzikert in 1071, and the high point of Malik Shah's reign, to the crisis of 1092. It then follows the fragmentation of the empire: the civil wars of the early twelfth century, the long reign of Ahmad Sanjar in Khurasan, the defeat at Qatwan in 1141, the Oghuz revolt of 1153, and the end of the western Iranian Seljuk line in 1194 when the Khwarazmshah Tekish defeated Tughril III at Rayy. It also explains why the Seljuks of Rum in Anatolia, whose history is much longer, belong to a related but distinct story that this chapter touches only as far as the aftermath of Manzikert requires.

The evidence is uneven, and learning to read it is part of the chapter's work. Coins and inscriptions record the titles the Seljuks claimed and the names of the caliphs they acknowledged. Buildings such as the Friday Mosque of Isfahan survive as physical evidence of patronage and architectural change. The narrative record comes largely from Arabic and Persian chronicles, some written within a generation of the events and some compiled much later from earlier works that are now lost, along with Byzantine and Armenian historians who viewed the Seljuk advance from the other side. The Seljuk family's own account of its origins was shaped by dynastic tradition. A historian therefore has to ask not only what happened but who recorded it, when, and for whom.

Finally, the chapter belongs to a division of labor within Volume III. Chapter 37 treated the Ghaznavids and the earlier part of the Dandanqan story from their side; this chapter takes the Seljuk perspective on that campaign and does not repeat the Ghaznavid narrative. The career and writings of the vizier Nizam al-Mulk are reserved for Chapter 39, the detailed history of Sunni and Shiʿi religious institutions for Chapter 40, the Nizari Ismaili state for Chapter 41, Persian poetry and historical memory for Chapter 42, learning and science for Chapter 43, and the Khwarazmian empire and the approach of the Mongols for Chapter 44. Here those subjects appear only as far as they explain the Seljuk political order.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe Oghuz steppe society and explain why the Seljuk ruling family should be understood as a product of the political and economic world of Central Asia.
  2. Distinguish securely documented developments in the early Seljuk story from later dynastic tradition, and explain how the sources were transmitted.
  3. Explain why the Oghuz movement into Khurasan between 1035 and 1040 concerned pasture, taxation, recognition, and sovereignty together rather than any one of them alone.
  4. Trace the stages by which the Seljuks moved from Khurasan to the central and western plateau, Baghdad, the Caucasus, Syria, and Anatolia, and explain why Dandanqan was a beginning of conquest rather than its completion.
  5. Analyze the institutional settlement between sultan and caliph and compare it with the earlier Buyid arrangement.
  6. Evaluate the significance of the battle of Manzikert, distinguishing the battlefield result from the longer transformation of Anatolia.
  7. Assess the Great Seljuk empire at its height as a system of delegated, negotiated, and family-based authority rather than a uniformly centralized state.
  8. Explain how iqtaʿ assignments, military slaves, Turkmen cavalry, secretaries, and viziers operated together, and why the simple division between Turkic soldiers and Persian administrators is inadequate.
  9. Examine Seljuk religious and cultural patronage, including support for Sunni scholarship and Persian-language culture, without crediting the dynasty with inventing the institutions it supported.
  10. Interpret the political role of royal women, especially in the crisis of 1092, and of atabegs and regional commanders after it.
  11. Compare explanations for the fragmentation of the empire after 1092, including the Oghuz revolt of 1153, without reducing it to a single cause.
  12. Evaluate what changed in Iranian political and cultural history because of Seljuk rule and what continued from earlier Islamic-Iranian states.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did the Seljuks build an imperial political order across Iran and the wider Islamic world, and why did the combination of Turkic dynastic traditions, Iranian administration, and Islamic legitimacy produce both durable institutions and recurring instability?
  2. What can historians securely say about the origins of the Seljuk family, and how do later dynastic traditions complicate that reconstruction?
  3. Why was the conflict between the Oghuz and the Ghaznavids in Khurasan about more than a search for pasture?
  4. Did the Seljuks transform Abbasid government, or did they perpetuate under a new dynasty the military supervision of the caliphate that the Buyids had practiced?
  5. Why did the battle of Manzikert matter, and why is it misleading to treat it as the moment Anatolia changed hands?
  6. In what ways did the Great Seljuk empire rely on relatives, commanders, and negotiated subordination, and when did that reliance become a source of weakness?
  7. How did Turkic military practice and Iranian administrative practice change each other, and who is left out of the shorthand that treats them as separate?
  8. What did Seljuk patronage of Sunni Islam and Persian culture inherit, what did it extend, and what did it newly create?
  9. What roles did women, atabegs, and regional commanders play in the political history of the dynasty?
  10. Why did Seljuk political structures continue in modified forms after the original imperial system had broken apart?

Historical Context

By the 1030s the eastern Islamic world had passed through a succession of regional dynasties that had gradually replaced direct Abbasid administration. The Tahirids and Saffarids in the ninth century, examined in Chapter 34, and the Samanids in Transoxiana and Khurasan, examined in Chapter 35, had combined Islamic law, Arabic learning, and a growing use of New Persian at court. The Buyids, examined in Chapter 36, had come from the Daylam region south of the Caspian, taken Baghdad in 945, and thereafter supervised the Abbasid caliphs while governing much of western Iran and Iraq through a family confederation. The Ghaznavids, examined in Chapter 37, had risen from Turkic military commanders of the Samanids, taken Khurasan and the Indian frontier under Mahmud, and ruled as a military monarchy with an Arabic and Persian secretarial culture. This was the political world the Seljuks entered, and its institutions are the starting point for understanding what they did and did not change.

Central Asia was also changing. Turkic-speaking groups had been present on and beyond the northeastern frontier of the Islamic world for centuries as soldiers, traders, slaves, and neighbors. Turkic military slaves had served the Abbasids and Samanids, and the Ghaznavid dynasty itself had Turkic origins. The Qarakhanids, a Turkic dynasty that adopted Islam, took Bukhara from the Samanids at the end of the tenth century and divided Transoxiana with the Ghaznavids. Further north and west, along the Syr Darya and the Aral Sea steppe, lay the Oghuz, a Turkic-speaking confederation of pastoral groups whose political unity was loose and whose relations with settled neighbors included trade, raiding, service, and sometimes conversion to Islam.

The Iranian plateau and Khurasan offered a very different environment from the steppe. As Chapter 1 explained, settlement on the plateau was concentrated in oases, foothills, and river valleys separated by deserts, and political power depended on controlling a chain of cities and the water and roads between them. A steppe following that wished to enter this world needed to negotiate with city-based governors, cultivators who owed taxes, religious scholars, and merchants. It also needed pasture, and grazing land in Khurasan was not unclaimed. The interaction of pastoral demand and settled political order is one of the chapter's recurring themes, and it returns in the discussion of the Oghuz revolt of 1153.

In the west, the Abbasid caliphate still existed as a religious and ceremonial institution that Sunni Muslims recognized, while its practical power had long been exercised by military patrons. The Fatimid caliphate in Egypt, an Ismaili Shiʿi rival to the Abbasids, claimed the same title and had supporters across the Islamic world. The Byzantine empire held Anatolia and parts of the Caucasus and northern Syria, and had in the preceding decades extended its authority in Armenia. Georgian and Armenian principalities occupied the mountainous routes between the plateau and the Black Sea world. Any account of Seljuk expansion has to keep in view that these were all actors with their own interests, not empty space waiting to be occupied.

Finally, the Seljuk story must be read against a long scholarly habit of treating the arrival of Turkic dynasties as a rupture that ended an Iranian age. Historians now generally reject the idea of a sudden break. Persian literary culture, Sunni religious institutions, and bureaucratic practice continued, and in many respects expanded, under Turkic rulers. At the same time, the Seljuk period did introduce changes in political organization, military finance, and demography, particularly in the movement of Turkmen groups westward into Azerbaijan and Anatolia. This chapter tries to give both continuity and change their due.

Section 38.1

The Oghuz World: Steppe Society, Pastoralism, and Political Organization

The Seljuk dynasty emerged from the Oghuz, a Turkic-speaking confederation of pastoral groups whose grazing lands lay in the steppe and semi-desert country north and east of the Aral Sea and along the lower Syr Darya in the tenth and early eleventh centuries. The name was the one most commonly applied by Arabic and Persian writers to a cluster of groups that shared a language, a repertoire of tribal names, and a loosely recognized system of leadership, but that did not form a centralized state. Many Oghuz who later moved into Iran and Anatolia were called Turkmen in Muslim sources. The relationship between the two names is explained in this chapter's Historical Vocabulary feature, and it matters because the word "Turkmen" in eleventh-century sources often signals both ethnicity and a way of life.

Oghuz society rested on herding. Sheep supplied meat, milk products, wool for felt, and most of a family's wealth; horses supplied transport, warfare, and prestige; camels carried baggage on longer migrations. Families lived in tents made of felt over a wooden frame and moved between seasonal pastures, usually wintering in sheltered river valleys and sandy lowlands and moving to better-watered grassland in warmer months. Such a way of life was not static or purely self-sufficient. Pastoral groups needed grain, metalwork, textiles, and weapons, which they obtained by trade, by raiding, and by serving settled states as soldiers. The steppe edge was therefore a zone of constant exchange with the cities of Khwarazm, Transoxiana, and Khurasan, and Muslim merchants moved through it. Pastoralism and settled agriculture were interdependent, and this chapter returns to that interdependence in its treatment of conflict over pasture.

Political leadership among the Oghuz was both clan-based and military. Our best near-contemporary description of an Oghuz ruler comes from Ibn Fadlan, a member of an Abbasid embassy who crossed their country in 922 on the way to the Volga Bulghars. He described a society of tent dwellers led by a chief bearing the title yabghu, supported by subordinate leaders, in which authority was real but personal and negotiated. His account also records suspicion of outsiders and descriptions of customs that reflect the observations of a literate Muslim visitor with his own assumptions, and for that reason it must be read with the same care as any outsider's description. A later and valuable source is the eleventh-century Turkic lexicon of Mahmud al-Kashgari, which lists Oghuz tribal divisions and preserves Turkic vocabulary; it was compiled in the 1070s, after the Seljuks had risen, and so it reflects the world they had already changed.

The yabghu stood at the head of the confederation, but his power depended on the loyalty of tribal leaders who could, and sometimes did, withdraw with their followings. Military command rested with officers of the yabghu, and the sources attribute to the early Seljuk ancestor such a position, as discussed in Section 38.2. Because political authority was held within ruling families rather than by individuals alone, family members shared in rule, and rivalry among them was common. This inherited notion of shared family sovereignty is important for later chapters, because it explains why the Seljuks distributed authority among relatives, and why historians treat the practice as a feature of Seljuk rule rather than a defect that appeared only when the empire weakened.

The Oghuz also lived along a religious frontier. Many Oghuz followed older Turkic religious practice, often called Tengrism in modern scholarship, in which the sky and ancestral spirits played central roles, though our knowledge of its details comes largely from outsiders. Some were Muslims, some were influenced by Buddhist, Nestorian Christian, or Jewish communities in the region, and conversion to Islam usually proceeded through contact with merchants, Sufis, and nearby Muslim dynasties rather than through conquest. The family that became the Seljuks adopted Islam while living on this frontier, and sources describe early Seljuk fighting against non-Muslim Oghuz groups, which shows that Oghuz society was divided by religion as well as by clan.

Students should avoid two mistakes. The first is to treat the Oghuz as a primitive society whose entry into the Islamic world was a simple invasion by "barbarians." They were participants in long-standing economic and political relationships and had experience of settled states as soldiers, traders, and neighbors. The second is to imagine them as a unified people moving in a single wave. Oghuz groups moved at different times, under different leaders, for different reasons, and with different relations to the Seljuk family. When the sources speak of "the Turkmen," they are often describing a political and military constituency that formed around Seljuk leadership, not a single community with one will. That distinction becomes decisive in the analysis of the revolt of 1153.

Section 38.2

Seljuk ibn Duqaq, Jand, and the Conversion of the Ruling Family

The dynasty takes its name from Seljuk ibn Duqaq, a figure about whom later tradition says a great deal and earlier evidence says very little. Almost everything we know about him comes from narratives written generations after his lifetime, many of them dynastic histories composed for Seljuk courts or their successors, or compilations drawing on such works. These accounts agree on some points and diverge on others. They describe Seljuk as the son of Duqaq, who is sometimes given an epithet connected to a bow, and as a military leader in the service of the Oghuz yabghu. They place him at the yabghu's court or camp, describe a break between them, and have him lead his followers away toward the lower Syr Darya, to the region of Jand, a town and district on the border between the steppe and the Muslim lands of Transoxiana and Khwarazm.

The reasons given for the break differ. Some accounts present a personal quarrel at the yabghu's court; others suggest political rivalry over leadership or over the division of spoils. Historians generally accept that Seljuk left the yabghu's following and settled with his own group near Jand, because later events presuppose a distinct Seljuk following with its own leadership. But the accounts of the dispute itself, including its dramatic episodes, are typical of dynastic origin stories, in which the founder is shown acting from admirable motives against an arbitrary superior. The chapter's Primary Source Window treats these traditions as evidence about how later Seljuk courts understood their origins, not as dependable record of the details.

Jand was a good place for such a group to settle. It lay near the edge of the Muslim world, where Oghuz groups encountered merchants and other Muslims, where the Samanid state had maintained a frontier presence, and where, after the Samanids' fall at the end of the tenth century, the Qarakhanids and Ghaznavids competed for influence. The sources associate Jand with the family's conversion to Islam, and they describe Seljuk's followers as fighters against non-Muslim Oghuz neighbors. This conversion needs careful treatment. The date and circumstances cannot be fixed securely, and the sources' emphasis on a pious Muslim founder served the legitimacy of the dynasty later, when Seljuk sultans presented themselves as defenders of Sunni Islam. A reasonable reconstruction is that the family adopted Islam gradually, through contact with Muslim traders and neighbors and through service in frontier warfare, and that the later narratives concentrated this process into a single founding act.

Seljuk's descendants are better documented than Seljuk himself. The tradition names several sons, among them Arslan, later called Arslan Isrāʾil, Mikail, and Musa. Mikail's sons Tughril and Chaghri are the principal figures of the next stage of the story, and their cousin and nephew lines, as well as the sons of Arslan Isrāʾil and of other family members, would later provide the Seljuk branches that ruled in Kirman, Syria, and Anatolia. The family's genealogy therefore matters politically as well as historically. Because rule belonged to the family, the number and location of its branches determined where Seljuk authority could extend and where it could be contested.

The first part of the Seljuk story also illustrates how historians use traditional narrative. They look for episodes that more than one source records, for those that later events presuppose, and for details that seem to serve a later purpose. They distinguish a fixed date from a traditional one. They remember that the Seljuks became a ruling dynasty only after 1040, and that the writers who composed the dynasty's history did so for a court that had every reason to dignify the family's past. That caution does not make the origin stories useless. It makes them evidence of what the Seljuk elite believed, or wished others to believe, about their descent and religion.

Historical Vocabulary
Oghuz, Turkmen, Yabghu, Amir, Sultan, and Appanage

Definition: Oghuz names the Turkic-speaking pastoral confederation of the Central Asian steppe from which the Seljuks came. Turkmen is the term Muslim writers used for Turkic groups, especially Oghuz, who had entered or settled in the Islamic world; in Seljuk sources it often implies a nomadic or semi-nomadic way of life and a military following. A yabghu was an Oghuz paramount leader. An amir (Arabic amīr) was a commander or governor. A sultan (Arabic sulṭān) originally meant "authority" and became the title of a ruler who held military and political power, recognized by the caliph. An appanage is a territorial share assigned to a member of a ruling family to govern, usually with the expectation of military service to the senior ruler.

Historical context: The Seljuks used these terms in changing ways. Tughril's rank as sultan was fixed in the 1050s through recognition by the caliph, and it implied a relationship to the caliphate that the Ghaznavid sultans before him had also claimed, though in different circumstances. The appanage system grew out of the Oghuz idea that the ruling family collectively shared sovereignty. The term is a modern scholarly convention, not a Seljuk word, but it describes a reality that the chronicles record when they describe princes holding regions.

Why it matters: These terms signal the overlap between Turkic steppe practice and Islamic political vocabulary. The Seljuks became sultans in the Islamic sense while continuing to treat rule as a family matter.

Potential misunderstanding: "Turkmen" in the eleventh century is not the same as the modern nationality of Turkmenistan, and not every Turkic person in the Seljuk world was Turkmen. "Sultan" was not a unique Seljuk invention, and the Seljuk claim to it was one of recognition and use, not creation.

Primary Source Window I

Later Accounts of Seljuk Family Origins

Creators: Anonymous and named authors of Seljuk dynastic histories and later compilers, including the Persian works of Zahir al-Din Nishapuri and Ravandi and the Arabic chronicle of Ibn al-Athir · Dates: mostly later twelfth and early thirteenth centuries · Type: Dynastic narratives and chronicles

Context: No source written by Seljuk ibn Duqaq or by a contemporary of his survives in the form of a continuous narrative. The principal accounts of his career come from writers who served Seljuk courts or lived after the dynasty had risen. Some, including Ibn al-Athir, wrote in Arabic as universal chroniclers and drew on earlier works that are now lost. Others, such as Nishapuri and Ravandi, wrote in Persian within Seljuk court culture. The tradition they transmit varies in detail.

Thematic source-based summary (not a quotation or a paraphrase of one specific passage): The later narratives generally describe Seljuk as a military commander in the service of the Oghuz yabghu, record a falling out with the yabghu, and have Seljuk move with his followers to the Jand region, where he and his family became Muslims and fought against non-Muslim Oghuz. Details of the quarrel, the numbers of followers, and the dates differ among accounts, and students should check the paraphrase against published translations of the works named above before relying on specifics.

Analysis: The accounts agree on the basic outline, which suggests a shared tradition that took shape early, but they also share features of dynastic myth: a founder of unusual ability, a justified break with a bad master, and an early conversion that anticipates the dynasty's later role as defender of the faith. Historians treat them as evidence of Seljuk self-presentation and of the kinds of stories Seljuk courts preferred.

Limitations: The sources cannot establish dates for Seljuk's career, the real reasons for his departure, the size of his following, or the character of his early religious belief. Because the later authors wrote under dynastic patronage, they had reason to present the founders favorably.

Perspective: The texts reflect the viewpoint of Seljuk court and Muslim literary culture. They say little about the Oghuz groups that did not follow the Seljuks, the yabghu's perspective, or the experience of ordinary herding families.

Section 38.3

Arslan Isrāʾil, Tughril, Chaghri, and the Struggle for Survival

The generation after Seljuk lived in a political landscape that was both dangerous and full of opportunities. The Samanid state, which had long managed the Transoxianan frontier and had employed Turkic soldiers and allied with steppe groups, collapsed at the end of the tenth century. In its place the Qarakhanids, a Turkic dynasty, took Bukhara and divided Transoxiana with the Ghaznavids, whose power was growing under Mahmud. Between and around these dynasties, local commanders and neighboring rulers looked for allies among the Oghuz. For the Seljuk family, which commanded a following of fighters, this was a situation in which service, alliance, and conflict could shift quickly.

Arslan, Seljuk's son, who is usually named Arslan Isrāʾil, is the central family leader in the sources for the early eleventh century. The Arabic form of his second name reflects a tradition, preserved in the sources, that has been connected to Biblical and Qurʾānic names that Turkic leaders took up on conversion. His career was bound up with the Qarakhanids and the Ghaznavids. He appears as an ally of a Qarakhanid prince in Transoxiana, and in the 1020s his followers moved within the sphere of Qarakhanid and Ghaznavid conflict around Bukhara. The Ghaznavid sultan Mahmud saw him as a danger and as a possible instrument. In 1025, Mahmud, who had crossed the Oxus, took Arslan Isrāʾil prisoner, and the sources record that he was imprisoned in a fortress in India, where he died after years in captivity.

The capture of Arslan Isrāʾil had two consequences that students should keep distinct. First, it deprived the Seljuk family of an experienced leader and encouraged Mahmud's attempt to bring the Oghuz under supervision. Mahmud permitted, or ordered, some Seljuk followers to settle in Khurasan, in districts near Nasa and Farawa on the northern edge of the Kara Kum, where Ghaznavid officials could watch them and use them as soldiers. Second, it created a lasting grievance and a model of Ghaznavid policy toward the Oghuz that Seljuk leaders remembered. The settlement in Khurasan placed a large mobile following inside the Ghaznavid territory, with pasture needs that the local settled population and officials had to accommodate. The seeds of the conflict that followed lay in that arrangement, and the sources suggest that Ghaznavid officers regarded the Oghuz as unreliable and expensive, while the Oghuz regarded themselves as being exploited and restricted.

Within the family, leadership passed to younger men. Tughril and Chaghri, sons of Mikail, and, in the traditions, raised in the family after their father's early death, emerged as the principal commanders of the next generation. Their personal names in the sources, Tughril Beg and Chaghri Beg, carry the Turkic title beg, meaning lord or chief, and each had Muslim names as well: the sources identify Chaghri as Dawud. The brothers differed in temperament as the sources portray them, though such portraits come from later writers and must be handled with caution. What is clearer is that they cooperated. Tughril took the more political and diplomatic role, Chaghri the more directly military and eastern one, and the later division of command between them (Section 38.6) reflected a relationship that had been tested in the years of survival.

Other members of the family mattered too. Musa, known by the title Yabghu, held a senior position, and the Oghuz leaders associated with him appear alongside the brothers in the Khurasan negotiations. A half-brother of Tughril, Ibrahim Inal, who had a different father, led a large following that would later raid toward Byzantine Armenia and take part in western campaigns, and he would become a rival and a rebel in the 1050s. Qutalmish, a son of Arslan Isrāʾil, would later contest the throne in 1063. The family's strength and its vulnerability were the same thing: it had several capable and independent members, each with followers.

This section's main lesson is that the "Seljuk" movement was not a single march of one people under one commander. It was a family-based following that had survived pressure from Qarakhanids and Ghaznavids, had lost a leader, had been relocated by a hostile sultan, and was composed of relatives whose interests could converge or diverge. The Khurasan crisis of the 1030s grew from that set of circumstances.

Section 38.4

The Oghuz Migration into Khurasan, 1035–1038

Mahmud of Ghazna died in 1030, and his son Masʿud I came to the throne after a brief succession dispute, a story told in Chapter 37. Masʿud inherited an empire that stretched from Khurasan and the Iranian plateau to the Indian frontier, and a Khurasan whose officials were uneasy about the Oghuz groups settled near Nasa and Farawa. Those groups raided, demanded pasture, and, in the eyes of Ghaznavid officials, disrupted the settled population. Some Ghaznavid commanders fought them with partial success. By the mid-1030s, the situation in Khwarazm and on the Oxus was also unstable. The Ghaznavid-appointed ruler of Khwarazm had been killed, and political disorder there pushed more Oghuz and their leaders south and west.

In 1035, the Seljuk leaders, including Tughril, Chaghri, and Musa Yabghu, crossed the Oxus with their followers and presented themselves to Masʿud as supplicants who sought permission to graze their herds in Khurasan. The sources record an exchange of letters and embassies in which the Seljuks acknowledged Ghaznavid sovereignty and asked for land, while Masʿud's court debated how to respond. Ghaznavid advisers disagreed. Some wanted to treat the Oghuz as soldiers and settle them under supervision, others wanted to eliminate them as a threat, and a few urged conciliation. The Ghaznavid contemporary historian Abu'l-Fazl Bayhaqi, whose account is discussed in Chapter 37, preserves the viewpoint of Masʿud's court and its worries about Oghuz demands. Seljuk-side accounts come later, and there is no Seljuk contemporary narrative of the negotiations.

Between 1035 and 1038, the Seljuks and the Ghaznavid authorities alternated between agreements and clashes. The Oghuz were assigned pasture in districts of northern Khurasan; they raided and were attacked; Ghaznavid armies won some engagements and lost others; and Seljuk leaders consolidated their position by negotiating with local notables and by appealing to cities frightened by the disorder. Chaghri moved against Merv in 1037, and in 1038 Tughril entered Nishapur, the leading city of Khurasan, where the sources record that his name was proclaimed in the Friday sermon and that he took the throne in an assertion of sovereignty. Nishapur was a center of religious learning and commerce and a city whose notables had to decide whom to accept. The Seljuks did not hold every city permanently in these years, and Ghaznavid authority revived and receded, but the acquisition of Nishapur and Merv established the Seljuks as rulers with urban revenue and legitimacy rather than only as nomadic raiders.

Why the conflict was about more than pasture

It is tempting to summarize the crisis as a dispute over grazing land, but the sources show several issues at stake. Pasture was real: the Oghuz needed grass and water for their animals, and the Khurasan oases and steppe edges were limited and already used. Taxation was real: settled cultivators and townspeople were taxed, and a pastoral following that settled among them created questions of what it owed and what it could demand. Political recognition was real: the Seljuk leaders sought acceptance as legitimate commanders and, eventually, as rulers, and the Ghaznavid refusal to treat them as such pushed them toward open conflict. Regional sovereignty was real: Khurasan was a province whose governors, notables, and cities had interests of their own, and the Ghaznavid sultan's authority there depended on his armies' ability to protect them. The Seljuk victory came from addressing all four at once, offering protection and tax relief to cities and recognition to local elites while their cavalry denied the Ghaznavids control of the countryside.

The events of these years also show the importance of mobility and negotiation to Seljuk method. The Oghuz could withdraw into the desert, regroup, and return, while a heavy Ghaznavid army had to be supplied and could lose cohesion when it failed to bring the enemy to battle. The Seljuk leaders were adept at diplomacy, using embassies, promises, and offers of submission to buy time. They also benefited from Ghaznavid mistakes and from the unrest of the Khurasan population, many of whom had little reason to favor Ghaznavid officials. None of this made Seljuk success inevitable, and the sources give examples of near-defeat and of Ghaznavid victories. What the period shows is a contest whose outcome depended on logistics, local politics, and judgments by individuals on both sides.

Section 38.5

Dandanqan, 1040: The Defeat of the Ghaznavids

On 23 May 1040, the Seljuks defeated the army of Masʿud I of Ghazna at Dandanqan, a fortified place on the desert road between Merv and Sarakhs in northern Khurasan. This chapter reconstructs the battle from the Seljuk side, using the later Seljuk and Arabic narratives and the contemporary Ghaznavid account where it bears on Seljuk decisions. It does not retell the reign of Masʿud or the court politics of Ghazna, which Chapter 37 covers. Students should read the two chapters together, because the same events look different from the two camps.

The campaign that led to Dandanqan began after Masʿud realized that his earlier measures had failed to restore Ghaznavid control of Khurasan. In 1039 and early 1040, he led a large army, which included cavalry, infantry, elephants in the Ghaznavid tradition, and a train of officials and baggage, from Balkh toward Merv, seeking a decisive engagement with the Seljuk commanders. For the Seljuks, the situation required careful management. They could not match a full Ghaznavid army in a conventional pitched battle if the enemy was well supplied and fresh, and their leaders knew the terrain and the limits of Ghaznavid logistics.

Seljuk tactics exploited those limits. Light cavalry harassed the Ghaznavid columns, attacked foragers, and withdrew when pursued, and Seljuk forces moved to deny water and fodder in a region where wells and oases were widely spaced. The Ghaznavid army, advancing through the arid country east of Merv in the heat of late spring, suffered from thirst, fatigue, and the strain of repeated skirmishes. The sources also record dissension in Masʿud's army, including reluctance among some commanders and the defection of Turkmen soldiers who had been serving the Ghaznavids. When the decisive engagement came, the Ghaznavid army was exhausted and its cohesion had been weakened by weeks of attrition.

The battle itself, as the sources describe it, lasted a day, and its details vary. The Seljuk commanders, with Chaghri and Tughril prominent, used their cavalry to strike the Ghaznavid flanks and rear while holding the enemy's forces in the open. Ghaznavid units gave way or fled, and Masʿud, with a remnant of his army, withdrew from the field. Our sources do not provide reliable numbers for either army or for casualties, and students should treat any precise figures in later retellings with suspicion. What is clear is that the Ghaznavid defeat was decisive enough to end Masʿud's attempt to hold Khurasan.

The consequences were immediate and wide. Masʿud withdrew toward Ghazna and the Indian frontier. In the following months he was removed from power and killed, a story told in Chapter 37, and the Ghaznavid state turned its attention to its eastern territories. The Seljuks took Merv and Nishapur firmly and received the submission of many local notables. The sources record that the Seljuk brothers were acknowledged as rulers of Khurasan, and that after the battle Tughril was honored as the senior figure. From that position, the Seljuks began to define their relationship with the Abbasid caliph, a story that continues in Division III.

Dandanqan should not be read as the completion of Seljuk conquest. It gave the Seljuks Khurasan, where cities, revenues, and routes were now open to them, but it did not give them the plateau, Iraq, or the western frontier. Those areas had their own rulers, among them the Kakuyids at Isfahan and Hamadan, the Buyid branches in Fars and Iraq, the Kurdish and local dynasties of the Zagros and Azerbaijan, and each required a separate campaign or settlement. The Seljuks also had to consolidate their position within Khurasan itself, where the loyalty of cities was conditional and Ghaznavid forces still controlled parts of the east. The battle's significance lay in what it made possible, and the next decades of Seljuk history are the story of how that possibility was realized.

Military History Study

Dandanqan and the Seljuk Conquest of Khurasan

Strategic aims: The Seljuks sought to preserve their position in Khurasan and to deny the Ghaznavids a decisive victory. The Ghaznavids sought to restore control by destroying the Seljuk command.

Forces and organization: The Seljuks fielded mounted archers drawn from their Oghuz following, organized by family and tribal command. The Ghaznavids brought a larger, more heterogeneous army with infantry, heavy cavalry, and elephants, supported by administrative and supply structures. Our sources do not give dependable figures for either side.

Logistics and geography: The desert road between Merv and Sarakhs offered little water. A heavy army needed organized supply and a safe route, while light cavalry could live off the land, move quickly, and use local knowledge. The Seljuk strategy of harassment, mobility, and denial of water turned geography into an advantage.

Morale and politics: Dissension within the Ghaznavid army and the defection of some Turkmen soldiers show that the contest was political as well as military. Masʿud's decision to force a battle in unfavorable conditions has been criticized by modern historians, though his options were limited.

Consequences: The loss ended Ghaznavid control of Khurasan, led to Masʿud's overthrow, and brought the Seljuks recognition as rulers, while leaving further conquest to later campaigns.

Section 38.6

Tughril and Chaghri: The Distribution of Conquered Territory

After Dandanqan, the Seljuk leaders faced a problem that every conquering family confronts: how to share the fruits of victory without destroying the alliance that won it. The sources describe a division of responsibility in which Chaghri took charge of Khurasan, based at Merv, while Tughril, as the senior brother, turned west, first holding Nishapur and then extending his authority across the plateau. Other relatives received commands and territories, and the chronicles record the division of Seljuk holdings among family members in ways that varied in detail.

This was not an administrative convenience devised after the fact. It followed the family's own conception of sovereignty. Authority belonged to the ruling house, and its senior members each had a claim to a share and a following to support. By assigning regions to relatives, the Seljuk leaders reduced immediate conflict, secured loyalty by rewarding it, and extended their reach by delegating command to men who commanded their own soldiers. The risk was equally evident. A relative with a territorial base and an army could become an independent ruler or a rival, and Seljuk history is full of such cases.

Chaghri's command in Khurasan placed him on the frontier facing the Ghaznavids and the Qarakhanids. It was the family's original homeland in Iran and the main reservoir of Oghuz and Turkmen followers, and it required a commander who could manage relations with pastoral groups and with the settled population. Chaghri's line would later include Alp Arslan, who succeeded Tughril, and, through him, Malik Shah, which gave the eastern family a continuing claim to the senior position. Tughril's command in the west required different skills: negotiation with local dynasts, handling of cities, and engagement with Baghdad. The chronicles credit him with political caution and a willingness to accept submission rather than insist on conquest.

Other relatives held regional commands that shaped the empire's geography. Ibrahim Inal, Tughril's half-brother, led Turkmen forces in the Jibal and later on the northwestern frontier. Qavurd, a son of Chaghri, would take Kirman in the late 1040s and found a branch that ruled there for generations. Qutalmish, son of Arslan Isrāʾil, held a position of influence that would later produce conflict. These men did not simply execute the senior brothers' orders. They had their own following and interests, and the early Seljuk state is better described as a coalition of family commands than as a bureaucracy directed from a capital.

This arrangement also explains why the Seljuk polity did not have a single center in its first generation. Tughril's court moved, as did Chaghri's. The family used the cities they captured, Nishapur, Rayy, and later Isfahan, as bases and sources of revenue, but kingship remained mobile, and decisions had to be negotiated across distances. The fact that the sources report a coherent, relatively peaceful division of the early conquests is a mark of the brothers' political skill. It is also a reminder that the system that worked for two brothers who trusted one another would be tested when their children inherited it.

Section 38.7

The Conquest of Nishapur, Rayy, Hamadan, and Isfahan

From Nishapur, which Tughril made his base in 1038 and held more securely after Dandanqan, the Seljuks expanded across the northeastern and central plateau by a sequence of campaigns that mixed force and negotiation. The sources do not always agree on years, and historians generally give approximate dates for individual conquests. The overall pattern, however, is clear. Tughril moved from Khurasan to the Jibal, the highland country of western Iran, and through the major cities of the northern plateau toward Isfahan.

Control of cities mattered for several reasons. Cities were centers of taxation, markets, and the production of goods, and they held the registers and the staff of administrators whose knowledge allowed revenue to be collected. They were also nodes on the road system, so that holding Rayy, Hamadan, or Isfahan meant control of the routes between Khurasan and Iraq. They contained religious leaders, judges, and notables whose public support legitimized a ruler. And they were defended by walls and sometimes by garrisons, so that taking them required either siege or an agreement. The Seljuks used both. In some cases a city accepted Seljuk rule after negotiation, in return for confirmation of its leaders and protection from plunder. In others, resistance led to siege and harsher treatment.

Rayy, an old and important city near modern Tehran, came under Seljuk control in the 1040s, after the collapse of the local authority that had followed the Buyids and the Ghaznavid intervention of earlier decades. Hamadan, a key point on the road to Baghdad, was taken in the later 1040s. Isfahan was a more substantial prize. Its ruler, a member of the Kakuyid family who had governed from Isfahan and Hamadan, had accepted a relationship with the Seljuks and then resisted, and Tughril besieged the city for a long period before it submitted around 1050–1051. After the surrender, Tughril settled there and the city became one of the principal bases of Seljuk government, a role that would grow under Malik Shah (Section 38.20). The Kakuyid ruler was not eliminated but was given another territory, an example of the Seljuk practice of absorbing local dynasts into the new order rather than removing every previous ruler.

These campaigns brought into Seljuk service many of the personnel who had worked for the earlier dynasties. Secretaries, tax officials, judges, and local notables continued their functions under the new rulers, sometimes in the same offices. This continuity of personnel is one reason the Seljuks could govern cities quickly after they conquered them, and it is evidence for the argument that they inherited an administrative world rather than creating one. At the same time, the arrival of a Turkmen military following changed the relations between officials and soldiers. Revenue now had to support a large cavalry force, and the officials who had served settled dynasties had to adjust to the demands of mobile commanders.

The conquest of the plateau also had human consequences that the chronicles record with varying detail. Turkmen forces took pasture and sometimes plundered, and cultivators and townspeople in the paths of campaigns suffered losses. The sources report appeals to the Seljuk leaders to restrain their followers, and Tughril is sometimes credited with efforts to do so, but the evidence is thin and the writers had an interest in presenting a lawful ruler. The disruption also created opportunities. Local elites who submitted early could gain position, and merchants who needed security on the roads benefited from the establishment of a stable authority. The experience of conquest was therefore uneven, and the sources, written mainly by educated urban Muslims, provide only partial access to the rural majority.

Section 38.8

Azerbaijan, the Jibal, Kirman, and the Western Frontiers

While Tughril secured the central plateau, other Seljuk commanders extended authority to the west, northwest, and south. The pattern here was less one of centralized conquest than of multiple expeditions, often led by relatives and commanders with their own forces, that produced a patchwork of submission, tribute, and local survival.

In Azerbaijan, a region of mountain valleys and grazing land well suited to Turkmen herds, local dynasties such as the Rawwadids at Tabriz and the Shaddadids in the Caucasus lowlands accepted Seljuk suzerainty in the 1040s and 1050s, often after a show of force. The Seljuks did not remove them immediately. A ruler who recognized Seljuk authority, paid tribute, and supplied troops could keep his territory, and the arrangement saved the Seljuks the expense of direct administration. At the same time, the arrival of large numbers of Turkmen in Azerbaijan began a demographic change that would continue for centuries, transforming the pastoral economy and the linguistic landscape of the northwest. This process was gradual and uneven, and it did not simply displace the existing populations, but its long-term importance is clear in the later history of Azerbaijan and Anatolia.

Turkmen groups, including those under Ibrahim Inal, also pressed against the Byzantine and Armenian frontiers from the 1040s. The Byzantine and Armenian sources record raids into Armenia and Anatolia in these years and battles such as Kapetron in 1048, where a Byzantine and allied force confronted Seljuk-associated raiders. In 1054, Tughril himself led a campaign into the region and besieged Manzikert without taking it. These episodes belong to the background of Alp Arslan's later campaigns (Division IV). They show that the frontier was active long before 1071 and that Turkmen groups acted with a degree of independence from the central Seljuk leadership.

In the south, Kirman, a region of oases and trade routes between Fars and Khurasan, was taken by Qavurd, a son of Chaghri, in the late 1040s. His descendants established a Seljuk line in Kirman that lasted for generations, with its own court and its own relations with the senior sultans. Fars, farther west, passed through a more complicated transition: the Shabankara leader Fażlūya overthrew and killed the last Fars Buyid, Fulad Sutun, in 1062; Qavurd of the Kirman Seljuks defeated Fażlūya and entered Shiraz about 1063; and Alp Arslan asserted firmer authority over the province only in campaigns of 1067–1069. These campaigns show that Seljuk authority extended piece by piece, through members of the family who then established their own dynasties.

The interaction between military conquest, local dynasties, and negotiated submission is the essential point. Seljuk expansion was effective partly because many local rulers preferred accommodation to resistance. They saw that the Seljuks' cavalry was formidable and that the Buyid and Ghaznavid states that might have protected them were weak. For the Seljuks, accepting submission conserved forces and allowed the extension of influence across a wide area quickly. The cost was that authority remained indirect. Local rulers kept their armies and their own interests, and the relationship depended on the continued strength of the Seljuk center. When that strength weakened, as it did after 1092, the subordinate dynasties of the periphery became independent.

Section 38.9

The Eastern Settlement: Ghaznavids, Qarakhanids, and Frontier Diplomacy

While Tughril looked west, Chaghri and his sons managed the eastern frontier, where the Seljuks had to settle their relations with two established dynasties. After Dandanqan, the Ghaznavids under Masʿud's successors retained their territories in what is now Afghanistan and the Indian borderlands, and they continued to contest parts of Khurasan, particularly in the east around Herat and Balkh. The boundary between the two states shifted during the 1040s and 1050s, and the sources record battles, raids, and truces. By the later 1050s, the situation stabilized, and under the Ghaznavid sultan Ibrahim, who reigned from 1059 to 1099, the two dynasties reached a long accommodation, sealed by marriage ties and a recognized frontier. The Ghaznavids continued as a major power in the east, and this chapter does not follow their history further, which belongs to Chapter 37.

The Qarakhanids were a different kind of neighbor. They held Transoxiana, including Bukhara and Samarkand, and were divided into branches that competed with one another and with the Seljuks. The Oxus was a boundary that could be crossed by armies and was crossed repeatedly. For the Seljuks, Transoxiana was strategically valuable for several reasons: it was a source of horses, soldiers, and trade; it was the homeland of related steppe groups and the source of the threat of further migration; and it was a center of religious scholarship and Persian-language culture. For the Qarakhanids, it was the heart of their state. The result was a frontier in which both sides used force, diplomacy, and dynastic marriage to maintain advantage.

Seljuk policy toward the east involved a recognition that direct conquest was neither necessary nor always possible. The Seljuks accepted a settlement in which Khurasan lay within their sphere and Transoxiana remained in Qarakhanid hands, though under pressure. Later campaigns, by Alp Arslan in 1072 and by Malik Shah in 1089, would reassert Seljuk influence across the river, and they are discussed in Sections 38.18 and 38.21. These campaigns produced forms of overlordship, in which Qarakhanid rulers acknowledged Seljuk authority, paid tribute, and retained local power, and they exemplify the system of indirect control that marked the empire.

The stabilization of the east allowed the Seljuks to concentrate on the west and gave them a secure base in Khurasan. It also had a lasting cost. Because Khurasan was the Seljuks' original territory and the home of many Turkmen followers, the eastern frontier remained sensitive, and the political center of gravity of the empire moved west under Tughril and Malik Shah while the eastern branch under Sanjar retained its own character. The eventual division of the Seljuk world into an eastern and a western sultanate, discussed in Division VIII, had its roots in this early geography.

Division III · §§38.10–38.13 Baghdad and the Transformation of the Abbasid Political Order
Section 38.10

Baghdad after the Buyids: Caliphal Authority and Military Competition

By the early 1050s, Baghdad was a city whose political significance exceeded its practical strength. It remained the seat of the Abbasid caliph, the symbolic head of the Sunni Muslim community, and a major center of scholarship, trade, and religious life. It was also the capital of a Buyid ruler, al-Malik al-Rahim, who held the title of amir in Iraq and had little reliable power. The Buyid family, as Chapter 36 explained, had taken Baghdad in 945 and governed Iraq and western Iran through a confederation of related rulers. By al-Rahim's reign (1048–1055), the family's branches had become rivals, its treasury was strained, and its military support was divided and unreliable.

The soldiers on whom Buyid authority in Iraq depended were of several kinds, Daylami infantry from the Caspian highlands and Turkish cavalry commanders, many of them former military slaves. Both groups expected payment, often through assignments of revenue, and both made political demands. Iraq's agricultural revenue, once a pillar of Abbasid finance, had suffered from neglect of irrigation, local disorder, and the diversion of income to military assignments, and the Buyid ruler could not keep his troops paid. Unpaid soldiers mutinied, and powerful commanders pursued their own interests. Among them, the Turkish commander Arslan al-Basasiri rose to prominence in the 1050s, building a position of independent influence that alarmed both the Buyid ruler and the caliph's circle.

The caliph al-Qaʾim, who had succeeded in 1031 and would reign until 1075, was an observant Sunni ruler who sought to defend the dignity of his office. Under the Buyids, the caliphs had lost control of the army and much of the treasury, though they retained a household, estates, and, importantly, religious prestige. Al-Qaʾim and his vizier, who was a skilled political operator, tried to maintain the caliphate's position against both the Buyid rulers and unruly commanders. The caliph's circle was also aware of the Fatimid caliphate in Cairo, whose Ismaili Shiʿi claims challenged the Abbasids' universal religious authority and whose agents sought support in Iraq. Baghdad's population was divided between Sunni and Shiʿi neighborhoods, and the Buyid period had seen recurring communal violence in the city. A new patron who was firmly Sunni and militarily strong could look attractive to the caliph's party, even though no one could be sure how such a patron would behave.

For Tughril, Baghdad offered political and symbolic gains. Control of the caliph would give his rule a legitimacy that no military victory could provide on its own. Recognition by the Abbasid caliph, whose investiture of rulers had been a feature of the Islamic political order for centuries, would place the Seljuk sultan in the same line as earlier Muslim dynasties and set him above rival Turkic and Iranian commanders. Iraq itself was a rich if damaged province, and Baghdad's markets and revenue could support his army. The Seljuk leader also shared the caliph's wariness of Fatimid expansion. Our sources suggest that the caliph's circle and Tughril exchanged messages for several years before the takeover. The situation was one of opportunity for both sides, and the terms of the eventual relationship had still to be settled.

Section 38.11

Tughril's Entry into Baghdad, 1055

Tughril approached Baghdad in the autumn of 1055 with a large army, having announced his intentions in messages to the caliph. The Arabic chronicles, principally Ibn al-Athir, who wrote in the early thirteenth century using earlier sources, preserve a narrative of the Seljuk approach and entry. According to the accounts, Tughril presented himself as a loyal servant of the caliphate who had come to defend it and, in some versions, to continue to the pilgrimage, and he asked that his name be included in the Friday sermon in Baghdad. The caliph's party, conscious of the military balance, agreed to receive him. The Buyid ruler al-Rahim and the commanders who supported him had little means to resist.

Tughril entered the city in December 1055. The sultan's name was proclaimed in the Friday sermon, a statement of recognition that mattered greatly in Islamic political practice. Al-Rahim was removed from power and confined, and he is reported to have died in custody a few years later. The sources describe an audience in which the caliph received Tughril and conferred on him robes and titles that acknowledged his position. The Buyid dynasty's Iraqi line thus ended, though its branch in Fars survived until 1062; control of that province then passed through Shabankara and Seljuk struggles rather than immediately to Alp Arslan (Section 38.8). Arslan al-Basasiri, who had no reason to trust the new regime, withdrew from the center of events, but he remained in the region, and the story of his return is told in the next section.

The takeover was not a simple liberation of the caliph. Seljuk troops were quartered in and around the city, and the sources record friction with the population, including disputes about billeting, plunder, and the behavior of Turkmen soldiers. Baghdad's notables and the caliph's circle had to manage a large foreign army in a city whose institutions were not designed for it. Tughril took steps to maintain order, but he also made clear that military authority belonged to him. The caliph kept his household, his religious functions, and his ceremonial role, and the relationship that emerged is the subject of Section 38.13.

The political meaning of Tughril's assumption of authority was twofold. For the Seljuks, it was a decisive step in their transformation from a conquering family into a recognized dynasty of the Islamic world. The caliph's endorsement gave Tughril a title and a place within the legal and ceremonial order that other Muslim rulers could not easily dispute. For the caliphate, the change was a replacement of one military patron by another, but one whose religious alignment was closer to the caliph's own. How far this changed the real position of the caliph is a matter of historical debate, addressed below. What is clear is that the events of December 1055 are a turning point in both Iranian and Abbasid history, one that ended the era of Buyid supervision and began that of the Seljuk sultanate.

Historical Vocabulary
Khutba, Sikka, Investiture, and Caliphal Recognition

Definition: The khutba is the sermon delivered at the Friday congregational prayer; in political practice, naming a ruler in it, normally after the caliph, signaled that the community recognized his authority. The sikka is the coinage and, by extension, the right to strike coins in one's own name or in the name of the sovereign. Investiture refers to the formal conferral of office, titles, and insignia by a superior, here usually the caliph's grant of a diploma, robes, banners, and honorific titles to a ruler. Caliphal recognition is the combination of these acts by which the Abbasid caliph acknowledged a ruler's authority.

Historical context: From the ninth and tenth centuries, regional dynasties sought caliphal recognition to legitimize rule they had won by force. Buyid, Ghaznavid, and earlier rulers used these instruments, and the Seljuks continued the practice with particular emphasis. The sources show the importance of the order in which names appeared in the khutba and on coins, with the caliph's name first and the ruler's after it.

Why it matters: These practices provide evidence for the political relationships that chronicles describe. Coins and inscriptions survive, so that historians can check literary accounts against material evidence.

Potential misunderstanding: Recognition by the caliph did not mean that the caliph controlled the ruler, nor that the ruler obeyed the caliph in practice. It established a framework of legitimacy within which power was exercised.

Primary Source Window II

A Narrative of the Seljuk Takeover of Baghdad, 1055

Creator: Ibn al-Athir (d. 1233), al-Kāmil fī'l-taʾrīkh (The Complete History) · Date: early thirteenth century, about a hundred and seventy years after the events · Type: Arabic universal chronicle organized by year

Context: Ibn al-Athir wrote at Mosul and in Syria in a world that the Seljuks had shaped. He compiled his year-by-year narrative from earlier historians, some of whose works are lost, and he often does not name his sources. His account of 1055 is therefore a retrospective compilation resting on earlier testimony of unknown date. Contemporary or near-contemporary Baghdad sources, such as the historian Hilal al-Sabi, are in part lost or survive only in fragments, which is why later chronicles are so important and so difficult to evaluate.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): The chronicle narrates that Tughril approached Baghdad and communicated with the caliph and his officials, that the caliph's party accepted his entry, that the Friday sermon was delivered in the sultan's name, that the Buyid ruler was removed and confined, and that the caliph conferred honors on Tughril. The account also notes tension between Seljuk troops and the population and the discontent of the commander al-Basasiri. Students should compare this paraphrase with a published translation of the relevant year of the Kāmil before citing particulars.

Analysis: The account is useful for the sequence of events and for how a Sunni historian writing under later regimes understood the takeover: as a transfer of power in which caliphal recognition played a central role. Its emphasis on the khutba reflects the contemporary importance of that act.

Limitations: Because the chronicle is retrospective and compiled, it cannot show what each participant intended or said. It is written from a Sunni perspective with the benefit of hindsight about the Seljuks' later role, and it may smooth over uncertainty.

Perspective: The source reflects an educated Sunni Arabic-writing historian with ties to the Zangid court at Mosul. It gives little voice to the Buyid ruler, to Shiʿi residents of Baghdad, or to ordinary inhabitants who had to house Seljuk troops.

Section 38.12

The al-Basasiri Crisis and the Recovery of Baghdad, 1058–1060

The Seljuk settlement in Baghdad was tested within three years. This episode is easy to confuse with the takeover of 1055, so students should keep the two events distinct. In 1055 Tughril entered Baghdad with the caliph's recognition. In 1058–1060 the city was seized by a commander who defied the new order and recognized the Fatimid caliph, and the Seljuks had to reconquer it.

Arslan al-Basasiri, a Turkish commander who had been the most powerful military figure in Baghdad under the Buyids, was alienated by the Seljuk takeover and by his hostility to the caliph's vizier, who had worked against him. He moved to Syria and Iraq's northern frontier, where he allied with Arab and other local rulers and opened communication with the Fatimid court in Cairo. The Fatimids, whose caliph claimed legitimate authority over all Muslims, saw in him an opportunity to extend their influence to the Abbasid capital. In early 1058 Tughril, who had meanwhile received a formal investiture in Baghdad, left the city to deal with other problems. These included a revolt by his half-brother Ibrahim Inal, whose Turkmen followers were dissatisfied with their position, and the pressure of campaigns on other fronts.

In late 1058, with Tughril absent, al-Basasiri entered Baghdad with his followers and Arab allies. The Fatimid caliph was named in the Friday sermon, an extraordinary reversal that the sources describe as a shock to the Sunni establishment. The caliph al-Qaʾim was removed from his palace and held in custody by an Arab chieftain in a town on the Euphrates. For roughly a year, Baghdad was under al-Basasiri's control, and Fatimid claims were publicly asserted in the heart of the Abbasid world. The episode shows how contested the new settlement still was. The Seljuk sultan's recognition by the caliph had not created a stable order, and military power in Iraq could still be seized by a determined commander with external support.

The restoration of Seljuk authority required Tughril to deal first with the rebellion of Ibrahim Inal, whom he defeated in 1059, and then to return to Iraq. He reached Baghdad late in 1059, and Seljuk forces, together with those loyal to the caliph, retook the city. Al-Basasiri was pursued and killed in early 1060, and the caliph was restored to his palace. The Fatimid effort to take over the Abbasid capital had failed, partly because the Fatimid court did not commit sufficient resources and partly because Basasiri's support among the population was limited.

The crisis had lasting effects. It confirmed that the Abbasid caliph depended on the Seljuks for protection and that the Seljuks could not leave Iraq unwatched. It deepened Sunni hostility to the Fatimids and strengthened the Seljuks' claim to defend the caliphate. It also exposed the vulnerability of an empire whose ruler had to deal with family revolts and distant frontiers at the same time. These problems of distance and delegated authority would recur throughout Seljuk history.

Section 38.13

Sultan and Caliph: The Institutional Settlement

The relationship between the Seljuk sultan and the Abbasid caliph developed over several years and was never fully written down in a single instrument. Historians reconstruct it from the sequence of ceremonies, the practice of the Friday sermon and coinage, the conferral of titles, and the way in which authority was exercised in practice. It is best understood as a division of functions in which the sultan held military and fiscal power and the caliph held religious and legitimating authority, with significant overlap and negotiation at the edges.

Military and fiscal authority

The sultan commanded the armies, appointed governors and commanders, decided on war and peace, and controlled the main revenues of the empire through his officials and the assignments he made to his soldiers. The caliph did not command an army of his own comparable to those of the sultans, though he maintained a household guard and officials. His revenues came from estates and from allowances arranged with the sultan, and the sources record disputes over their adequacy. In practical terms, the caliph's power to act independently depended on the goodwill of the sultan and on the balance among Seljuk princes.

Ceremonial, religious, and judicial authority

The caliph conferred legitimacy through investiture, which in 1058 included a grand ceremony in Baghdad in which Tughril received the title often rendered "King of the East and West." The caliph's name stood first in the Friday sermon and on coins, followed by the sultan's. The caliph appointed or confirmed the chief judge and other religious officials in Baghdad, and he remained the symbol of Sunni unity. The caliph also retained a role as a religious arbiter, issuing statements of Sunni doctrine and sometimes intervening in disputes among scholars. Chapter 40 returns to this institutional and religious dimension.

Titles, marriage, and diplomacy

Marriage ties between the families were a tool of diplomacy. Tughril married into the Abbasid family in 1062–1063, a union the sources report the caliph accepted with reluctance. Later, in the 1080s, the caliph al-Muqtadi married a daughter of Malik Shah, and the couple's son became a focus of Malik Shah's efforts to influence the succession in the caliphate. Such marriages show that each side sought to bind the other, and that the caliph's family was not simply a passive object of Seljuk policy.

Comparing the Buyid and Seljuk arrangements

Under the Buyids, as Chapter 36 explained, Shiʿi-leaning military rulers governed a Sunni caliph. They took Persian royal titles, controlled the treasury and army, and treated the caliph as a ceremonial figure whose participation was useful to their legitimacy. The Seljuks, who were Sunni, shared the caliph's religious allegiance, claimed to restore and protect his dignity, and used the language of service to the caliphate. In both cases military power rested with a dynasty from outside the caliph's household. The differences lay in religious alignment, ceremonial presentation, and the degree to which the dynasty used the caliphate as a symbol of its own mission. Students should avoid claiming that the Seljuks immediately restored the political independence of the caliphs. That did not happen under Tughril or Malik Shah. Only later, after Seljuk power fragmented, did some caliphs, notably al-Mustarshid and al-Nasir in the twelfth century, build their own military and political bases in Iraq.

Historical Debate

Did the Seljuks Transform Abbasid Government or Perpetuate the Buyid Arrangement?

The question: When the Seljuks displaced the Buyids in Baghdad, did they change the character of the relationship between caliph and ruler, or did they continue, under a new dynasty and with a different religious alignment, the structure of military supervision that had existed since 945?

Interpretation A — Transformation: Some historians stress the change in religious and ceremonial context. The Seljuks were Sunni and presented themselves as defenders of the caliphate. Their patronage of Sunni scholarship and institutions, and the caliph's explicit endorsement of the sultan's title, created a partnership with a different political meaning than the earlier Buyid supervision of a Sunni caliph by Shiʿi-leaning amirs.
Interpretation B — Perpetuation: Others emphasize structural continuity. In both periods, a military dynasty from outside Baghdad held real power and controlled the caliph. The caliph's revenue, freedom of action, and security depended on the ruler, and the Seljuks, like the Buyids, intervened in succession and appointments. On this view, the main novelty was the sultan's greater legitimacy, not a change in the distribution of power.
Interpretation C — A new balance in a long process: A third approach sees both continuity and change. The sultan's supremacy remained, but the new alignment gave the caliphate a more defined religious role, which later caliphs could use when Seljuk power fragmented. The settlement shaped the conditions in which a partial caliphal revival became possible.

Evidence: Historians draw on chronicles, coin legends, accounts of ceremonies, records of titles and diplomas, and the later political history of the caliphate. The evidence is partial. Many sources are Sunni and retrospective, and the arrangement itself was never codified.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and emphasis more than the facts, and it turns on how much weight to give to religious legitimacy as compared with military control.

Why it matters: The question affects how historians understand the long-term development of Islamic political thought and practice, including the later theories of the relationship between rulers and the caliphate.

Student task: Identify the evidence that best supports each interpretation, and explain which features of the Seljuk settlement you consider most significant and why.

Division IV · §§38.14–38.18 Alp Arslan and the Expansion of Seljuk Power
Section 38.14

The Succession of 1063: Alp Arslan and the Contest for Authority

Tughril died in September 1063 at Rayy, without a surviving son. His death exposed a basic feature of Seljuk dynastic practice: the family had no rule of succession that all members accepted. A ruler could name an heir, and powerful officials and commanders could support his choice, but the claims of other family members remained alive, and each had followers. The succession of 1063 shows how the Seljuk system worked when the senior ruler died, and it explains why the eventual succession crises of the dynasty were not accidents of weak rulers but expressions of a structural problem.

The sources report that Tughril had designated a younger member of Chaghri's line, Sulayman, as his heir, and that his vizier, al-Kunduri, supported the choice. Against this stood Alp Arslan, Chaghri's son, who governed Khurasan and commanded the largest and most experienced body of Seljuk troops. A third claimant, Qutalmish, a son of Arslan Isrāʾil and thus a member of an older branch of the family, also asserted a claim. Each pursued his case by means available to a Seljuk prince: oaths from commanders, control of cities, and military action. Alp Arslan, with his base in Khurasan and the support of many commanders, moved west, secured recognition at Rayy and Nishapur, and had the khutba delivered in his name. Qutalmish rebelled, and in November–December 1063 Alp Arslan defeated his forces; Qutalmish escaped but died after falling from his horse, according to the historical accounts. His sons, who survived, would later play a part in the history of Anatolia (Section 38.17).

The new sultan also changed the administration. He dismissed al-Kunduri, who had backed Sulayman and who was executed according to the sources, and he appointed as vizier Nizam al-Mulk, a Persian administrator from Tus in Khurasan who had served in the Seljuk bureaucracy and had Alp Arslan's trust. Nizam al-Mulk's long tenure, which lasted until 1092, is among the most important features of Seljuk government, and Chapter 39 is devoted to him. The point to retain here is that the succession of 1063–1064 combined a military contest among relatives with a change of administrative leadership. It was won by the claimant who had the strongest army, the support of senior commanders, and a vizier who could manage the revenues.

The outcome also showed that family authority could be preserved through the negotiation of shares. Alp Arslan confirmed relatives in their regional commands, including those of Kirman, and he used delegated authority to extend his reach. Where relatives accepted his seniority, they were rewarded. Where they resisted, they were defeated. The system encouraged ambitious princes to seek advantage in every succession, and it required each Great Sultan to be both a commander and a negotiator. The theme returns at the end of Alp Arslan's reign and again, with greater force, in 1092.

Section 38.15

The Caucasus, Armenia, and the Byzantine Frontier

Alp Arslan's early campaigns turned to the northwest, the region of the Caucasus, Armenia, and the Byzantine frontier, where Turkmen groups had been active for two decades. This was a borderland of mountain valleys, high plateaus, and small states that had been contested by Byzantines, Armenians, Georgians, and Muslim rulers for centuries. By the 1060s, Byzantine authority there had been extended and then weakened. The Byzantine empire had annexed the Armenian kingdom of Ani in 1045, replacing local rule with imperial administration, and had dissolved much of the local Armenian military organization, leaving the frontier less well defended than before. Armenians, who were mostly Christians of a church that differed from the Byzantine Orthodox church, were not always loyal to the imperial government, and the combination of religious tension and weak defense made the frontier vulnerable.

In 1064 Alp Arslan led a campaign into Georgia and Armenia and captured Ani, a major commercial and religious center of the region. Ani had been the capital of the Bagratid Armenian kingdom and a city of churches and markets, and its fall was a major event. Seljuk, Armenian, and Byzantine writers describe the capture, and their accounts differ in tone. Muslim chroniclers present it as a victory over Christians, while Armenian sources describe slaughter and enslavement, though the numbers given in these sources are not reliable and should not be taken literally. The capture of Ani placed Seljuk power on the high Armenian plateau and opened routes toward Anatolia, Georgia, and the Caucasus.

The Georgian kingdom under Bagrat IV was another target. Alp Arslan campaigned in Georgia in 1064 and again later, and the Georgian ruler accepted a form of submission, at least for a time. The relations between the Seljuks and the Georgian and Armenian states were complex. Some local rulers allied with the Seljuks, some resisted, and others shifted according to circumstance. The Caucasus remained a region of political fragmentation, and Seljuk power there rested on a mixture of conquest, negotiation, and tribute.

Meanwhile, Turkmen raiding groups moved into the Byzantine provinces of eastern and central Anatolia. The sources record raids that reached far into the interior, including attacks on cities such as Caesarea in 1067 and others in the following years. These raids were conducted by groups with varying degrees of connection to the Seljuk center. Alp Arslan could claim their success and could direct some of them, but he did not control every band, and the Byzantine government was often responding to raiders who acted on their own initiative. The emperors of the period, Constantine X Doukas and then Romanos IV Diogenes, who took the throne in 1068, faced a military and political crisis. Romanos, a general, attempted to restore the frontier defense with campaigns against the raiders, but he was constrained by court factions, financial strain, and the poor state of the army.

Repeated frontier warfare had political consequences for all parties. For the Byzantines, it undermined confidence in the government and intensified internal conflict. For the Armenians and Georgians, it created choices between submission and resistance and drove migrations. For the Seljuks, it brought successes that enhanced the sultan's prestige and the problem of controlling mobile groups whose aims did not always match his. By 1071 the stage was set for a confrontation in which Alp Arslan's immediate aims lay elsewhere, but in which Byzantine decisions would bring the two powers into battle.

Section 38.16

The Battle of Manzikert, 26 August 1071

The battle of Manzikert, fought on 26 August 1071 near the town of Manzikert (modern Malazgirt) north of Lake Van, was one of the most consequential engagements in the history of the Middle East. The date is secure, and this chapter treats it as a fixed point. What the battle meant is the question that requires care, and the answer depends on separating the battlefield from the longer processes that followed.

Alp Arslan's immediate strategic concern in 1070 and 1071 was not Anatolia but Syria and Egypt, where the Fatimid caliphate was a rival. He campaigned in northern Syria, besieging Aleppo and its surroundings, and he sought to bring the Fatimids to heel. At the same time he made a truce with the Byzantine emperor, which both sides intended to hold for a time. Romanos, however, decided to launch a major campaign to restore Byzantine authority in the east. In 1071 he led an army into Armenia that combined Byzantine regular troops, foreign mercenaries including Normans and Turkic soldiers, and allied contingents, and which the sources describe as large though they give no reliable figures.

Romanos advanced to Manzikert, took the fortress, and divided his army, sending part of it away on other tasks, including a detachment to Khliat (Ahlat) and some of his mercenaries. The sources disagree about the reasons and wisdom of the division. When Alp Arslan, who was in northwestern Iran, learned of the Byzantine advance, he moved rapidly north with a smaller force, probably composed largely of cavalry and including Turkmen contingents. His army reached the vicinity of Manzikert before the emperor expected it. On 26 August, a Friday, the two forces met in the open country near the town.

Byzantine and Arabic narratives agree on the main shape of the battle, though they differ on details. The Seljuk horse archers harassed the Byzantine lines, attacked the wings, and feigned retreat to draw the Byzantines out of formation. As the day went on, the Byzantine army became strung out and disordered as it pursued an elusive enemy. The command of the rear guard, under Andronikos Doukas, a member of a family that was a rival of Romanos at court, withdrew from the field, and Byzantine sources, which are hostile to the Doukas faction, describe this as treachery. Whether it was deliberate betrayal or a fatal misjudgment cannot be settled. The Seljuks attacked the isolated center, and Romanos fought on until he was wounded and captured.

The capture of an emperor was extraordinary, and the sources record that Alp Arslan treated Romanos with considerable respect, releasing him after a negotiation. The terms involved a ransom, a truce, and arrangements concerning frontier towns and a marriage alliance, but the sources give differing accounts, and specific figures should be treated with caution. Romanos returned to find that his enemies at court had taken power. He was deposed, mutilated, and died in 1072. The Byzantine government, now under the Doukas family, was in no position to fulfill the agreement.

Historians evaluate the narratives with attention to their origins. Michael Attaleiates, a Byzantine judge and historian who accompanied the expedition or was well informed about it, provides a contemporary account sympathetic to the emperor. Michael Psellos and other Byzantine writers reflect the politics of the capital and the Doukas faction. Matthew of Edessa, an Armenian chronicler, provides a perspective from the frontier population. Arabic and Persian sources, among them Ibn al-Athir and later Seljuk histories, present the battle as a triumph of the sultan. Later Persian writers magnified its significance in a way that suited the dynasty. Taken together, the sources show that the battle was a major defeat for Byzantium, but they do not by themselves establish what it changed.

Military History Study

Manzikert: Battlefield Victory and Territorial Transformation

Strategic aims: Alp Arslan sought to deal with the Fatimid rivalry in Syria and keep the Byzantine frontier quiet. Romanos IV sought a decisive success to restore imperial authority and his own political position.

Forces and organization: The Byzantine army included regulars, mercenaries, and allied units under divided command. The Seljuk army was a mobile mounted force with Turkmen and professional contingents. The sources do not provide reliable numbers.

Tactics: Horse archers harassed, enveloped, and drew out their opponents, while the Byzantine army lost cohesion. The withdrawal of part of the Byzantine rear guard decided the outcome.

Immediate result: A decisive Byzantine defeat and the emperor's capture, followed by negotiations and the emperor's release.

Longer-term significance: The battle did not itself hand Anatolia to the Seljuks. Alp Arslan did not occupy the peninsula. The long-term effects came from the Byzantine political crisis that followed, the movement of Turkmen groups into Anatolia, and the establishment of new regional powers, as the next section explains.

Section 38.17

Manzikert and the Transformation of Anatolia

The widespread belief that Manzikert delivered Anatolia to the Turks in a single day is a misconception, and correcting it is one of the most important tasks of any account of the battle. Alp Arslan did not advance into the peninsula after his victory. He returned to the east, where his concerns lay, and he did not establish a Seljuk administration in Anatolia. What changed in the following years was the balance of power inside the Byzantine empire and the freedom of action of Turkmen groups on its frontier.

The Byzantine empire after 1071 entered a period of civil war. The Doukas family, which had removed Romanos, ruled under Michael VII, and factions of generals and nobles contested the throne. Commanders who had been sent against Turkmen raiders sometimes used their troops for their own ambitions, and several rebelled. Both sides in these conflicts hired Turkish soldiers, and some rebels invited Turkmen leaders into Byzantine territory. These Byzantine civil wars, which continued until Alexios I Komnenos took the throne in 1081 and for years afterward, drained the empire's resources and opened Anatolia to settlement by Turkmen groups. The process was not a single conquest but a long sequence of raids, alliances, and occupations.

Turkmen leaders established regional principalities in various parts of Anatolia and the borderlands. The Danishmendids held parts of central and northern Anatolia, with centers at Sivas and Malatya; the Mengujekids, Saltukids, and others held positions in the east. Sulayman, a son of Qutalmish, the rebel of 1063, established himself in western Anatolia and made Nicaea (Iznik) a political center by the late 1070s or early 1080s; he is securely attested there by 1078, although 1081 has traditionally been given for its emergence as the Seljuk capital. The name "Rum" refers to Rome, the Muslim term for the Byzantine lands. Sulayman's line, the Seljuks of Rum, was a separate branch of the dynasty with its own history. It lost Nicaea to the First Crusade and the Byzantines in 1097, moved its center to Konya, and survived in various forms into the early fourteenth century. This chapter describes the origins of the Rum Seljuks only because they follow from the aftermath of Manzikert. Their government and later history lie beyond this chapter's scope, and students should not confuse them with the Great Seljuks of Iran and Iraq.

Relations between Sulayman and the Great Seljuk court were often strained or hostile. Although Sulayman belonged to the wider ruling family, he did not simply govern Anatolia as Malik Shah’s obedient subordinate: his early Syrian alignments reportedly included allegiance to the Fatimids, and Malik Shah later sought to impose his own authority over the western branch. Sulayman also became involved in the politics of Syria, where he was killed in 1086 in a conflict with another Seljuk prince, a reminder that the family's branches competed with one another.

The transformation of Anatolia was gradual. It involved Turkmen migration, the adoption of pastoral and settled life in new regions, conversions and religious change, Byzantine counterattacks, and the creation of mixed populations. Greek and Armenian communities continued to live in Anatolia under various authorities, and the process by which Turkish language and Islam became dominant occurred over centuries. Manzikert was a major turning point because it broke the Byzantine army's power in the east at a time of internal weakness, but the outcome depended on what came after it. Historians therefore speak of the battle as a symbol and a catalyst, and avoid suggesting that it produced a Turkish Anatolia at once.

Section 38.18

Alp Arslan's Eastern Policy, Royal Authority, and Death in 1072

After Manzikert, Alp Arslan's attention turned to the east, where Khurasan and Transoxiana remained the foundation of Seljuk power. The Qarakhanids and the Ghaznavids were still rivals, and the family's Turkmen followers needed to be managed. The sultan's government, under Nizam al-Mulk, was also developing the administrative structures that would carry the empire through the following decades. Alp Arslan's reign, which lasted from 1063 to 1072, is often viewed through the lens of Manzikert, but the sources show that he spent much of his time on the eastern frontier and on managing relations among commanders and relatives.

His relations with provincial elites and commanders were governed by the practice of delegating authority. He confirmed his relatives in their positions, and he appointed trusted commanders to key cities. He also arranged the succession. Around the middle of his reign, the sources report, he designated his young son Malik Shah as heir and had the commanders and officials swear oaths to him. This act, which required the consent of many people, expressed an intention to create a smoother transfer of power than had occurred in 1063. Its success depended on the continued loyalty of the commanders and on the abilities of the heir, and the events of 1072–1073 would test it.

In late 1072, Alp Arslan led an army across the Oxus to campaign against the Qarakhanid ruler in Transoxiana. During the campaign, at a fortress where he was supervising the interrogation of a captured commander, the prisoner attacked and wounded him with a knife. The sultan died of the wound shortly afterward, in the closing weeks of 1072. The accounts of his death emphasize his piety and the arrangements he made for the succession, though they are retrospective and have the character of moral tales. The details of the incident differ among sources, and students should regard the story as a traditional narrative of a ruler's end.

Alp Arslan's death, only a year after Manzikert, ended a reign of consolidation and expansion. His reputation in the later tradition is that of a brave and just warrior-ruler, and he was buried at Merv. The more significant historical fact is the structure he left behind. The empire had been expanded to include Armenia and parts of Anatolia, Fars, and large parts of the eastern plateau. It rested on a coalition of family commands and Turkmen followers, administered by a vizier of exceptional ability. Whether that structure would hold under a young successor was the question that faced the empire in 1072.

Division V · §§38.19–38.23 Malik Shah and the Imperial Apex
Section 38.19

Malik Shah's Accession and the Consolidation of the Sultanate, 1072–1073

Malik Shah was a teenager when his father died. Alp Arslan had designated him as heir, and the commanders in the army on the Oxus, and the vizier Nizam al-Mulk, acted quickly to secure his succession. The vizier is credited with ensuring that the army and the officials recognized the young sultan and with bringing the news to the main centers of government. This action, which the sources present as decisive, is an early illustration of the vizier's importance. His broader career belongs to Chapter 39. Here it is enough to say that the succession of 1072–1073 depended on a combination of military support, administrative control, and prior designation.

The new sultan faced challenges from relatives. His uncle Qavurd, who had governed Kirman since the 1040s, was the senior surviving member of the family of Chaghri and had a claim of his own, and he advanced from the south against Malik Shah, asserting that the sultanate should pass to the senior male. This argument, based on seniority within the family, was consistent with older Turkic custom and with the Seljuk practice of the previous generation, and it was a serious threat. Malik Shah met Qavurd's forces near Hamadan in 1073 and defeated him. The Kirman line, however, continued under Qavurd's descendants, who remained part of the Seljuk family structure, a reminder that the Seljuk response to rebellion often involved retention of the rebel's region in the family. Other relatives and commanders also tested the new ruler, and the sources describe a series of conflicts and negotiations.

The consolidation of the sultanate also required attention to the frontier. Qarakhanid and Ghaznavid rulers watched events closely, and the Seljuk position in Khurasan and Transoxiana needed reinforcement. Malik Shah combined force with diplomacy, and by about 1073 the immediate threats to his rule had been contained. In the following years, the sultan's authority became more secure, and the empire reached its widest extent. The stability of the 1070s and 1080s depended on a continuing balance among the sultan, his vizier, and his commanders, and on the accumulated experience of a government that had by now operated for over thirty years.

It is important to see this consolidation as a political achievement and not a natural outcome. The succession of a youth after the death of a strong ruler, in a dynasty without a fixed succession rule, could easily have produced prolonged civil war. That it did not owes much to the preparations made by Alp Arslan, the skill of Nizam al-Mulk, and the interest of senior commanders in preserving a stable regime that guaranteed their assignments. The events of 1092, when a similar situation arose without the same conditions, show what might otherwise have happened.

Section 38.20

Isfahan and the Political Geography of the Empire

The Seljuk empire had no single permanent capital. The sultan and his court moved with the seasons, the needs of campaigns, and the requirements of governing a large territory. Winters might be spent in the milder climate of Iraq or the plateau's southern cities, summers in the cooler highlands of the Jibal or northern Iran. The movement allowed the ruler to be present at different points, to supervise commanders and officials, and to supply the court and army from different districts. It also reflected the heritage of a ruling family accustomed to seasonal movement. Kingship in this period was mobile, and students should not imagine the Seljuk sultan as a monarch sitting in one palace.

Even so, certain cities acquired special importance. Isfahan, which Tughril had made a base, became under Malik Shah a principal center of government and the site of major building programs. It lay in a fertile region of the central plateau, on routes connecting Khurasan, Fars, Iraq, and the north, and it had a long history as a center of administration and learning. The sultan and Nizam al-Mulk patronized building there, and the Friday Mosque of Isfahan received major additions in this period, as Section 38.32 explains. The city also became associated with scholarly and scientific activity, including an observatory and astronomical work in the 1070s that is discussed in Chapter 43.

Baghdad remained essential as the seat of the caliph and the principal city of Iraq. The sultans visited it, built or maintained palaces there, and intervened in its affairs, but they did not reside there continuously. Nishapur and Merv in Khurasan remained major centers, with Merv acting as the seat of the eastern branch and later of Sanjar. Rayy, Hamadan, Shiraz, and other cities were regional capitals and administrative nodes. The empire's political geography was therefore a network of cities linked by roads and by the sultan's movement among them, rather than a hierarchy centered on a single capital.

This arrangement has implications for understanding Seljuk power. Because the center was mobile, the sultan's authority depended on the presence of his court and army. When he was absent, provincial commanders and governors exercised power, and the vizier's offices handled the administration. The system worked well when the sultan was strong and the commanders loyal. It was vulnerable when the sultan died or was distracted, because there was no fixed administrative center to preserve continuity. The distribution of resources was also uneven. The sultan's revenue came from the cities and agricultural districts of the plateau and Iraq, and the army was supported by assignments across the empire. The mobile court acted as a means of redistributing resources and renewing loyalty through personal contact.

Section 38.21

The Eastern Empire: Transoxiana, Qarakhanids, and Frontier Sovereignty

Malik Shah's eastern policy aimed to secure Khurasan against the Qarakhanids and to extend Seljuk influence into Transoxiana without incurring the cost of permanent occupation. Transoxiana, with Bukhara and Samarkand, was a rich region and a center of Islamic scholarship and Persian culture, and its control had been a goal of earlier dynasties. The Qarakhanids who ruled it were divided into branches and often at odds, which gave the Seljuks opportunities.

In 1089, Malik Shah led a major campaign across the Oxus. He took Bukhara and Samarkand and captured the Qarakhanid ruler of the western branch, Ahmad Khan, and the sources report that his army advanced further east toward the regions of Kashgar and the Turkic steppe. The campaign established Seljuk overlordship over Transoxiana. Ahmad Khan was restored as a subordinate ruler who acknowledged Malik Shah's sovereignty, a pattern typical of Seljuk practice in frontier regions. In these arrangements, the sultan's name was recited in the Friday sermon and stamped on coins, the local ruler paid tribute and sent troops, and the Seljuks might station officials or garrisons, but local rule continued.

The distinction between direct control, tributary authority, and diplomatic influence is essential to understanding the empire's eastern frontier. Khurasan was governed directly or through Seljuk princes and commanders. Transoxiana was ruled by Qarakhanids under Seljuk suzerainty, a relationship that was maintained by force when necessary. Khwarazm, the oasis south of the Aral Sea, was placed under a governor appointed by the sultan, and the line of governors that began there, as Chapter 44 explains, would eventually produce the Khwarazmshahs, who became an independent power. Malik Shah thus created or confirmed a series of relationships of varying intensity along his eastern frontier, and the success of the system depended on his personal prestige and military strength.

The Ghaznavids to the southeast remained a separate state at peace with the Seljuks, and the relationship was managed through diplomacy and marriage. Seljuk power over the east was real but not uniform. In each region, the form of sovereignty differed, and subordinate rulers retained considerable autonomy. This flexible and negotiated sovereignty was an effective way of governing a wide territory, but it also meant that when central authority weakened, local rulers could assert independence with little difficulty.

Section 38.22

The Western Empire: Syria, Mesopotamia, and the Eastern Mediterranean

In the west, Seljuk expansion proceeded through commanders and relatives who operated with considerable independence. Syria and the Eastern Mediterranean were contested territories where the Fatimid caliphate, Arab dynasties, Byzantine garrisons, and local rulers competed, and where the arrival of Turkmen forces changed the balance of power.

In the early 1070s, a Turkmen commander named Atsiz ibn Uvaq, acting in the name of the Seljuk sultan, took Jerusalem from the Fatimids in 1071 and Damascus in 1076. He attempted to invade Egypt but was repulsed. These conquests detached much of Syria and Palestine from Fatimid control and established Turkmen rule in cities that had previously been under Arab or Fatimid authority. In 1078–1079, Malik Shah's brother Tutush was given Syria as an appanage, and he established himself in Damascus and extended his authority over other parts of the region. Tutush's position illustrates the Seljuk method: a member of the ruling family was assigned a region, expected to conquer or consolidate it, and acknowledged the sultan's seniority while governing largely on his own.

Northern Mesopotamia and the Jazira were controlled by Arab dynasties, including the Uqaylids of Mosul, who were subordinated after a series of campaigns in the 1080s. Aleppo, a major city in northern Syria, came under Seljuk authority in 1086 during a western expedition by Malik Shah himself, who reached the Syrian coast and reasserted his authority in the region. In the same period, Sulayman of Rum, as described above, captured Antioch from the Byzantines in 1084, and he was killed in a conflict with Tutush in 1086. These episodes show the interplay of family rivalry, regional ambition, and imperial supervision that characterized the western empire.

Relations with the Byzantines in these years were shaped by the settlement that followed Manzikert and the crisis of the Byzantine state. Under Alexios I Komnenos, the empire stabilized and began to resist. The Fatimids, weakened by internal difficulties and the loss of Syria, sought to recover Palestine and other regions when opportunities arose. This was the political landscape that the First Crusade would encounter in 1097–1099. By that time, Malik Shah had died, Tutush had been killed in a conflict with his nephew Barkiyaruq in 1095, and Syria was divided among competing Seljuk princes, Turkmen amirs, and local Arab rulers, as well as the Fatimids. The fragmentation of Syria after 1092 is therefore an important part of the context of the Crusades. This chapter does not narrate the Crusades, which belong to other histories, but it is essential to recognize that the Crusaders entered a region where Seljuk authority was already divided.

Section 38.23

An Empire at Its Height: Power, Delegation, and the Limits of Unity

Around 1090–1092, the Great Seljuk empire reached its greatest extent. It stretched from the borders of Central Asia and the Qarakhanid lands in the east to the Mediterranean coast in the west, and from the Caucasus and Anatolia in the north to Fars, Kirman, and the Persian Gulf region in the south. Yet this extent should not be mistaken for uniformity. The empire consisted of regions governed in different ways, and it is best understood as a structure of overlapping authorities with the sultan at its head.

Royal relatives governed or claimed many regions. Tutush held Syria, the Kirman Seljuks governed their territory, Sulayman's line held parts of Anatolia, and other princes and commanders held smaller territories. Subordinate rulers such as the Qarakhanids of Transoxiana and various local dynasts accepted Seljuk suzerainty. Military households, composed of commanders with their own troops and often their own slaves, held assignments across the empire. Provincial governors, many of them Turkic military men with Persian civil officials as subordinates, administered districts and collected revenue. The sultan's court dealt with all of them through a mixture of command, persuasion, and reward.

Sovereignty meant different things in different parts of the empire. In Khurasan and the central plateau, the sultan's officials collected taxes and made appointments. In Iraq, the caliph's presence required a distinct relationship. In Transoxiana and Syria, power rested on tributary relationships and on princes who ruled with considerable independence. In Anatolia, the Seljuk presence took the form of a separate branch with its own agenda. The sultan's claim to be the head of this structure was real, but he could not govern every part directly. His authority rested on military support, revenue, family negotiations, and a capable vizier. Malik Shah's achievement was to keep this structure working for two decades, and the weakness of the structure was its dependence on his personal authority.

The empire at its height was thus a system in which regional power was an essential feature, not merely a late symptom of decline. The appanage system, the iqtaʿ assignments that supported commanders, and the mobile court were integral parts of the design. They allowed the Seljuks to govern a vast territory with limited bureaucratic resources, and they created interests that were invested in the system's survival. They also created the conditions under which the system could break apart, because each member of the ruling family and each commander had both a stake in the whole and a base from which to pursue independent aims.

Section 38.24

The Great Sultan, Dynastic Authority, and Political Hierarchy

At the head of the Seljuk political order stood the Great Sultan, a title that distinguished the senior ruler from the other Seljuk princes and from the regional dynasties that accepted his overlordship. In inscriptions and on coins, the Great Sultan appeared with a series of honorific titles, many of them in Arabic and drawn from the vocabulary of religion and kingship, and with the name of the caliph recorded as the source of his legitimacy. These titles were a language of claims. They asserted that the sultan was just, victorious, and a defender of the faith, and they placed him within an Islamic political tradition that the dynasty adopted and adapted.

The practical authority of the Great Sultan depended on his relations with several groups. Subordinate Seljuk princes, who held appanages, owed him military service and recognition of his seniority, but they governed their territories largely on their own and could resist or rebel. Regional amirs, who commanded troops and held assignments of revenue, were the sultan's essential supporters and potential rivals. Provincial governors, many of them amirs, administered districts. The caliph and the religious scholars provided legitimacy, and the vizier directed the civil administration. The sultan's task was to balance these groups, to reward loyalty, to punish defiance without driving too many into opposition, and to keep the army paid.

Household politics were part of this balance. The sultan's wives, mothers, and children belonged to powerful families, and marriages created alliances with other dynasties and with commanders. Royal women, as Section 38.34 explains, were not passive figures. They held property, maintained their own households and followers, patronized buildings and scholars, and in some cases had their own officials and troops. The ruler's sons were often placed in the care of an atabeg, a senior commander who acted as guardian and tutor and who, as time passed, became a political figure in his own right. These institutions, described further in the vocabulary feature below, bound princes to the commanders and families that raised them.

Succession was the most dangerous feature of the system. The Seljuks did not follow primogeniture, the rule that the eldest son inherits. Different principles competed: the designation of an heir by the ruler, which could be confirmed by oaths from commanders; the seniority of the oldest male relative, an idea that fitted older Turkic custom; the claims of sons, who could rely on their mothers' families and on commanders who had raised them; and the practical test of military strength. The succession of 1063, as Section 38.14 showed, was resolved in favor of the candidate with the strongest army. The succession of 1072 was smoother because of the preparations Alp Arslan had made. The succession of 1092 would be an open struggle. The uncertainty was built into the political culture of the dynasty, and each succession was a negotiation as much as an inheritance.

Section 38.25

Persian Viziers, Secretaries, and Administrative Continuity

The civil administration of the Seljuk empire was carried out by a corps of officials who had served earlier dynasties and who came, in large part, from Iranian families with a long tradition of bureaucratic service. The administrative terminology of the Seljuk period shows this continuity. The central offices were called dīvāns, a term for both the office and the register that it kept, and the head of the civil administration was the vizier, a minister who directed these offices on behalf of the ruler. The vocabulary of Arabic and Persian administration, much of it inherited from the Abbasid and Samanid bureaucracies, was used in Seljuk chanceries.

The principal offices included the dīvān of the vizierate, which supervised finance and the civil bureaucracy; the chancery, which drafted and sent diplomas, letters, and decrees, and where skill in Arabic and Persian prose was essential; the offices responsible for taxation and the audit of accounts; the office that supervised the army's rolls and assignments; and the office of supervision, whose officials inspected provincial administration and gathered information for the court. The postal and intelligence network, which went back to the Abbasids and was maintained in various forms, reported on provincial affairs. These offices needed literate staff skilled in accounting, correspondence, and law. Most of them came from families of secretaries who passed their skills to sons and relatives, and who moved among courts as dynasties changed.

The vizier stood at the top. Tughril's vizier al-Kunduri was a Persian administrator from Nishapur who had a major role in the early government, and Alp Arslan's and Malik Shah's vizier, Nizam al-Mulk, directed the administration for almost thirty years. Nizam al-Mulk's career, his family's wide network of offices, his writings on government, and his role in founding schools are the subject of Chapter 39. For present purposes, the important point is that the vizierate was a position of great power and also of vulnerability. A vizier served at the sultan's pleasure and was often resented by commanders, who disliked civilian control of revenue, and by rival officials. Many viziers were dismissed, imprisoned, or killed.

It would be wrong to say that Seljuk administration was simply a continuation of earlier practice. It operated in a new context. The sultan's court moved, and administrators had to travel with it or operate in its absence. The appanage system meant that princes had their own courts and their own officials, so that there were several administrative hierarchies within the empire, each with a vizier and chancery. The iqtaʿ system tied revenue collection to military assignments, so that officials had to coordinate with commanders. Language practices were also changing. Persian was used increasingly in correspondence and in the keeping of accounts in the east, while Arabic remained the language of the caliph's chancery, of law, and of much formal correspondence.

The distinction between Turkic soldiers and Persian administrators was therefore an important feature of Seljuk government, but not an absolute one. Some secretaries held military command or iqtaʿ assignments. Some Turkic commanders learned to read and write and took part in political and administrative decisions. Families intermarried. Religious and regional loyalties, such as those linking officials from Khurasan, cut across ethnic lines. The relationship between the two groups was one of cooperation and rivalry, and it produced a political culture that historians describe as a synthesis, a view that Section 38.33 assesses in a debate feature.

Section 38.26

Turkmen Warriors, Military Slaves, Commanders, and Imperial Armies

The Seljuk army was not a single force. It combined groups with different origins, forms of recruitment, methods of payment, and loyalties, and the competition among these groups was a continuing feature of Seljuk politics. The most important distinction was between the Turkmen followers of the dynasty and the professional troops, many of them military slaves, who formed the sultan's household and the core of the standing army.

The Turkmen were tribal cavalry who served under their own leaders and who joined the Seljuk cause for reasons of kinship, loyalty, pasture, and plunder. They were skilled horse archers, mobile and effective in campaigns, and they had provided the force that won the early victories. But they were also difficult to control. They fought for their own leaders and interests, expected a share of the spoils, needed pasture for their herds, and could act independently, as the frontier raids into Byzantine territory showed. They did not necessarily fit the demands of administered territory, where cultivators and cities needed protection from soldiers who might plunder them.

The second group consisted of military slaves, called ghulams or mamluks in the sources. These were boys and young men, most of them of Turkic or other non-Muslim origin, purchased or captured, converted to Islam, trained in the military arts, and employed in the households of the sultan and of commanders. They were paid, usually in cash or revenue assignments, and they owed personal loyalty to their master. Military slavery was an institution of long standing in the Islamic world, used by the Abbasids, the Samanids, and the Ghaznavids, and the Seljuks adopted it as a means of creating a force that was less dependent on tribal leaders. Slave soldiers could rise to high rank and command, and some became governors and regional rulers. The terms ghulam and mamluk did not mean that these men were powerless. They could hold property, command forces, and exercise political influence.

Commanders, called amirs, were the pivotal figures. They led contingents of their own followers and slaves, held assignments of revenue to support them, and were responsible for providing troops when summoned. Some were members of the ruling family, some were Turkmen leaders, and some were former slaves who had risen to command. The palace guard, drawn from the sultan's household troops, protected the ruler and served as a rapid-reaction force. Provincial contingents provided garrisons and local order. Allied and auxiliary forces, including Kurdish, Arab, and other units, supplemented the main army. In warfare against fortified places, the Seljuks used siege engineers and the techniques developed in the Islamic world, including catapults and incendiary devices, and they also relied on their cavalry for mobility and on infantry for sieges and defense.

The political implications of this composition were significant. Commanders competed for the sultan's favor and for assignments. Turkmen leaders resented the rise of slave commanders and the civil officials who controlled revenue. Some historians argue that the policy of the sultans and their viziers in the later eleventh century favored the professional troops and encouraged the movement of Turkmen groups toward the frontier, where they could be useful as raiders, a policy that contributed to the settlement of Azerbaijan and Anatolia. This interpretation is debated, and the sources do not allow certainty about the intentions of rulers. What is clear is that managing the different military constituencies was a central problem of government, and that the sultan who could do so effectively had a decisive advantage.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Ghaznavid to Seljuk Military Government

What Changed

  • The ruling house moved from a dynasty of slave-soldier origin, centered on a court at Ghazna, to a ruling family of tribal origin with a mobile court and a large pastoral following
  • Authority was shared among relatives through appanages to a degree that the Ghaznavid sultanate had not practiced
  • The geographic center of power shifted west to the central plateau and Iraq, and the army had to deal with a larger and more varied territory
  • Tribal Turkmen cavalry formed a major part of the army alongside slave troops, creating a new problem of managing competing military groups

What Stayed the Same

  • Both dynasties were ruled by Turkic military elites who governed through a combination of slave troops, commanders, and a Persian-speaking bureaucracy
  • Both claimed legitimacy through recognition by the Abbasid caliph and championed Sunni Islam
  • Both relied on revenue from agricultural districts and cities, and on a corps of secretaries and viziers from established families
  • Both patronized Persian-language and Arabic literary culture

When and for whom: The transition was uneven. It occurred between the 1030s and the 1060s and was experienced most directly by the cities and cultivators of Khurasan and the plateau, who changed rulers, and by the officials who served new masters. Administrators experienced greater continuity than soldiers.

Why the pattern matters: The comparison shows that the Seljuks inherited and extended a model of military government. It also shows that their distinctive contribution lay in the dynastic and military organization built around a family and a tribal following, not in the invention of the system itself.

Section 38.27

Iqtaʿ, Revenue, Taxation, and the Fiscal Organization of the Empire

The fiscal system of the Seljuk empire rested on agricultural taxation, urban revenues, and the assignment of revenue to support the army. The most important institution was the iqtaʿ, a grant of the right to collect revenue from a district or other source in return for service, usually military. The system was not invented by the Seljuks. Assignments of revenue to soldiers had been used by the Abbasids and the Buyids, and the practice of paying troops through land revenue had developed over a long period. The Seljuks expanded and systematized it, and it became the basis of their military finance.

The central distinction is between a right to revenue and ownership of land. The holder of an iqtaʿ, called a muqtaʿ, was entitled to collect the taxes due from the cultivators and other taxpayers in a district, within limits set by law and custom, and to use them to support himself and his troops. He did not own the land or the cultivators, and in principle the assignment could be revoked or transferred. In practice, holders sought to make their assignments permanent and hereditary, and the tension between the ruler's interest in control and the holder's interest in security was a constant theme. The system encouraged commanders to extract as much as possible, and its abuses, such as excessive collection and neglect of irrigation, are criticized in contemporary and later writings.

Revenue came from several sources. The main one was the land tax, the kharaj, levied on agricultural land at rates that varied by region, crop, and the method of assessment. Non-Muslim subjects of protected status paid a poll tax, the jizya. Merchants and travelers paid duties on goods and tolls. Cities provided taxes from markets and crafts. The treasury received income from the sultan's own estates and from fines and confiscations. The Seljuk state taxed pastoralists as well, though the methods were less regular, and the interaction of the tax system with mobile populations produced friction.

Agriculture depended on irrigation. The plateau's cultivation relied on rivers, springs, canals, and underground channels called qanats, introduced in Chapter 1, and their maintenance required labor and investment. A muqtaʿ who neglected irrigation reduced future revenue, while one who invested in it might increase it. The sources suggest that the quality of administration varied widely. In regions of conflict or heavy assessment, land fell out of cultivation and peasants fled or protested. In others, the Seljuk period saw expansion of cultivation, especially where a strong governor invested in water works. The evidence is uneven and mostly comes from official and urban sources, which makes it hard to measure.

The treasury and the assignments were linked. A sultan who distributed too much revenue to commanders weakened his own finances, and one who withdrew assignments risked revolt. The vizier's office had the task of maintaining the balance, assessing the value of assignments, and ensuring that the army could be paid. These problems of finance were central to the stability of the empire. When revenue declined or the assignments were disputed, the military cohesion of the state suffered.

Historical Vocabulary
Iqtaʿ, Ghulam, Atabeg, Dīvān, and Vizier

Definition: Iqtaʿ was an assignment of the right to collect revenue, granted to a soldier or official in return for service. A ghulam (plural ghilmān) was a military slave, trained and employed by a ruler or commander. An atabeg (Turkic, roughly "father-lord") was a senior commander who served as the guardian and tutor of a young prince and who often acquired political power on that basis. A dīvān was an administrative office or the register it kept. A vizier (Arabic wazīr) was the chief minister who headed the civil administration.

Historical context: All five terms were older than the Seljuks except atabeg, which became characteristic of the Seljuk period and later. The institutions they describe interlocked: ghulams were paid by iqtaʿ and cash, commanders held iqtaʿ, atabegs guided princes who held appanages, and viziers directed the dīvāns that kept the registers.

Why it matters: Understanding these terms allows students to read the chronicles and to see how revenue, military power, and political authority were connected.

Potential misunderstanding: The iqtaʿ is not the same as a European fief. The holder had a right to revenue and not to the land, and the political and legal setting was different, though historians have long debated the comparison. Likewise, "slave" in this context does not describe the condition of an agricultural laborer. Military slaves could hold power and wealth.

Section 38.28

Nomads and Settlers: Agriculture, Cities, Commerce, and Social Conflict

The Seljuk conquests brought large numbers of pastoral people into regions of intensive agriculture and dense urban life. The meeting of these ways of life produced cooperation, competition, and conflict, and its consequences ran through the political and social history of the empire. Understanding the Seljuk period requires attention to the experiences of the groups involved, not only to the actions of the rulers.

Competition for land was the most obvious problem. Pastoral groups needed pasture, particularly winter pastures in lowlands and summer pastures in the highlands, and they needed water. In the plateau and Khurasan, the best-watered land was already cultivated or reserved for crops, and the extensive pastures were limited. When Turkmen herds crossed or grazed on cultivated fields, cultivators lost crops, and disputes followed. The sources record complaints against Turkmen soldiers and appeals to the authorities. The Seljuk government tried to manage the problem by assigning particular pastures to groups, by moving some groups to frontier regions such as Azerbaijan and Anatolia, and by restraining soldiers, with limited success.

Interaction was not always hostile. Pastoralists supplied animal products, transport, and military service, and settled communities supplied grain, cloth, metal goods, and markets. Merchants dealt with both. Caravan trade, which connected the cities of Khurasan, the plateau, Iraq, and Syria, depended on security and on the cooperation of the nomadic groups through whose territory it passed. Artisans in cities such as Nishapur, Rayy, Isfahan, and Baghdad produced textiles, ceramics, metalwork, and other goods for local and long-distance markets, and the stability of the Seljuk period helped commerce in many areas. Rural elites, landowners who held estates and collected rents and local notables, negotiated with the new rulers and often kept their positions by supporting them.

The effects on different groups were uneven. Pastoral leaders who joined the Seljuk cause gained status, assignments, and wealth. Ordinary herding families gained pasture but were also subject to the demands of military service and, in time, to the regulation and taxation of the state. Cultivators bore the burden of taxation and sometimes of the soldiers' demands. Townspeople could benefit from security and commerce but suffered from conflicts, extortion, and urban violence. Religious communities had to adapt to a new set of rulers. The experiences of these groups varied by region, by period, and by the character of the local governor.

Pastoral and settled interests were not fixed in opposition. Over time, some Turkmen settled, took up agriculture, and entered the urban economy, while some settled people took on the military and political culture of the rulers. But as the empire matured, the pressures on pastoral populations grew. The state needed revenue and order, and its officials sought to tax and regulate groups that had earlier been outside regular administration. The tension would emerge dramatically in the Oghuz revolt of 1153, discussed in Section 38.38. The problem was structural. A state built on a pastoral following needed to govern settled society, and the two projects were not always compatible.

Everyday Life and Social History

Pasture, Irrigation, Merchants, Taxation, Migration, and Religious Communities

For most people in the Seljuk world, the dynasty's history was experienced through changes in the demands of the land and the tax collector, not through palace politics. A farming family on the plateau depended on a water source, whether a river, a spring, or a qanat, and on the maintenance of the canals that distributed it. Its crops, mainly grain, with fruits, vegetables, and in some regions cotton and rice, were assessed for taxes, and the portion left after payment determined its survival. When a district was assigned as an iqtaʿ to a commander, the family dealt with his agents, and the character of the muqtaʿ could determine whether the burden was tolerable.

A pastoral family within the Seljuk following lived by moving its flocks between seasonal pastures, trading animal products in markets, and supplying fighters when called on. The family's position depended on the favor of its leader and on the access of the group to land. Some such families moved with their leaders into new regions, a migration that for many meant a long and uncertain journey, and the Turkmen movement into Azerbaijan and Anatolia was composed of countless family decisions of this sort.

Merchants, artisans, and religious scholars lived in cities, where markets, workshops, mosques, and schools provided the framework of life. Merchants needed safe roads and reliable currency, and they benefited from the political unity of the region but also paid tolls and dues. Artisans worked in guild-like groups and local associations. Religious communities, including Sunni, Shiʿi, Jewish, Christian, and Zoroastrian groups, maintained their institutions under the rulers, with rights and obligations that varied by community and by region. Because the surviving sources were written mostly by educated urban men, historians must infer the lives of cultivators, herders, and women from indirect evidence, including legal documents, chronicle asides, archaeology, and the physical remains of settlements and irrigation systems.

Section 38.29

Sunni Legitimacy, the Caliphate, and Religious Patronage

The Seljuks presented themselves as Sunni Muslims who had come to defend the caliphate and the faith. This self-presentation was central to their claim to rule. They supported jurists and judges, built and endowed mosques and schools, and cooperated with religious scholars whose approval carried weight with the urban population. Seljuk rulers were themselves adherents of the Hanafi school of law, which was dominant among Turkic and many eastern Iranian Muslims, while many of their officials and scholars followed the Shafiʿi school. The relations among these schools, and among theological currents within Sunni Islam, are subjects for Chapter 40. Here the point is how religious patronage worked as a political instrument.

Rulers needed scholars because scholars shaped public opinion in cities, administered justice, and conferred legitimacy through their endorsement. Seljuk patronage took several forms: appointing judges and preachers, funding mosques and colleges, endowing them with income from lands and shops, and providing stipends for students and teachers. Such endowments, called waqfs, were legal arrangements by which property was dedicated to a pious purpose and could not be sold or confiscated in principle, and they provided a stable income for institutions and, in practice, a way for patrons to protect assets and secure influence. Viziers, commanders, and royal women also founded endowments, and the Seljuk period saw a great increase in such foundations.

Students should not conclude that the Seljuks created Sunni Islam or invented the madrasa. Sunni religious scholarship had existed for centuries, and institutions of higher religious learning, including madrasas, had been founded in Khurasan and elsewhere before the Seljuks, with notable examples in Nishapur in the tenth and early eleventh centuries. These schools were sometimes the work of individual scholars and private patrons, and many functioned without direct royal control. The Seljuk contribution was to expand support, to create schools linked to the state and to the careers of officials, and to use patronage as a deliberate part of governance. The best-known example, the Nizamiyya colleges established under the vizier Nizam al-Mulk, belongs to Chapters 39 and 40. The institutional growth of religious education was a broader historical process in which royal patronage was one force among several.

Religious policy was not always benign or consistent. In the 1050s, officials in Khurasan, including al-Kunduri, took measures against preachers associated with the Ashʿarite theological school, causing some prominent scholars to leave the region. These measures were reversed or relaxed under Alp Arslan and Nizam al-Mulk, who became patrons of Ashʿarite and Shafiʿi scholars. The episode shows that religious patronage reflected the preferences of particular officials and the balance of influence at court, and not a fixed doctrine of the dynasty. It also shows that scholars were agents with their own networks and interests who could shape and resist state policy.

The relationship with the caliphate completed the framework of Sunni legitimacy. The caliph's recognition, the use of his name in the sermon and on coins, and the ceremony of investiture gave the Seljuk sultan a place in a religious and legal order that most Muslims accepted. The caliphs, for their part, used the Seljuk alliance to defend their position against Shiʿi and Fatimid rivals and to reassert their religious authority. This alliance, discussed in Section 38.13, was an arrangement of mutual advantage, and it helped to shape the Sunni political order of the period.

Section 38.30

Shiʿi Communities, Ismailism, and Religious Competition

The Seljuk empire contained substantial Shiʿi populations. Twelver Shiʿi communities lived in cities such as Rayy, Qum and Kashan, and in the quarters of Baghdad, and they had scholars, institutions, and local notables. Zaydi Shiʿi communities were present in the Caspian region. The Buyid period, as Chapter 36 described, had been favorable to Shiʿi scholarship and ceremonies, and the Seljuk takeover brought a Sunni dynasty to power over these communities. Seljuk policy toward Shiʿi groups varied. The sources record episodes of tension and violence, including conflicts in Baghdad between Sunni and Shiʿi neighborhoods, and the sultans' patronage of Sunni institutions affected the balance of local influence. At the same time, Shiʿi scholars and officials continued to work in many places, and some served the dynasty. The policy was one of Sunni preference combined with practical accommodation, not systematic suppression.

The Ismaili movement posed a different problem. The Fatimid caliphate in Egypt, with its Ismaili Shiʿi doctrine, maintained a network of missionaries in Iran and elsewhere who sought converts and political support. The Basasiri episode of 1058–1060 showed that Fatimid influence could reach the Abbasid capital. In the later eleventh century, a branch of the Ismaili movement, led by Hasan-i Sabbah, broke with the Fatimid court in Cairo over the succession to the Fatimid caliphate and established an independent Nizari Ismaili movement in Iran. In 1090, its followers took the mountain fortress of Alamut in the Alborz, north of Qazvin, and began to build a network of strongholds across Iran and Syria.

The political consequences were large. The Nizari community, which was a minority supported by sections of the rural and urban population in various regions, defied Seljuk authority from fortified bases in difficult terrain. Seljuk forces besieged Nizari strongholds with limited success, and the conflict became a long struggle. Nizari fighters carried out targeted assassinations of political and religious figures, and the sources of the time and later attribute the killing of Nizam al-Mulk in 1092 to a Nizari assassin, a claim treated further in Section 38.34. The Seljuk state, and Sunni society more generally, regarded the Nizaris as a grave threat, and the conflict hardened religious divisions. Rulers and scholars used the language of defending the faith to mobilize support, and suspicion of Ismailism could be used against rivals within the state.

This chapter treats the Nizari challenge only in terms of its political consequences for Seljuk government. The full history of Hasan-i Sabbah, the doctrines of the movement, the nature of the Nizari state, and the controversies surrounding its use of assassination belong to Chapter 41. It is enough here to note that the emergence of Alamut after 1090 was part of the context in which the Seljuk empire faced the succession crisis of 1092, and that the conflict between the Seljuks and the Nizaris continued for generations.

Section 38.31

Persian Language, Court Patronage, and the Turkic-Iranian Cultural Synthesis

Persian was the language of Seljuk administration in much of the east and an important language of literature at Seljuk courts, but its position was the result of a long development that began before the dynasty arrived. New Persian, written in Arabic script, had emerged as a literary language in the ninth and tenth centuries in eastern Iran and Transoxiana under the Samanids, as Chapter 35 showed, and it had been adopted by the Ghaznavids, who patronized Persian poets and historians, as Chapter 37 described. The Seljuks inherited a flourishing Persian literary culture and a corps of Persian-speaking secretaries. Their contribution was to extend its reach, to provide new patronage, and to carry it westward to Iraq, Azerbaijan, and Anatolia.

The ruling dynasty itself spoke Turkic and used it in the army and at court, and Turkic remained the language of the Seljuk military elite. Persian served as the language of administrative correspondence in many regions, of the court poetry that celebrated the ruler, and of historical writing. Arabic remained the language of Islamic law, theology, and much scholarship, as well as of the caliph's chancery and of many official documents. The multilingualism of the Seljuk world is a defining feature. A single court could use Turkic for command, Persian for administration and poetry, and Arabic for religion and law, and educated men moved among them. Mahmud al-Kashgari's Turkic dictionary, composed in Baghdad in the early 1070s and written in Arabic for an Arabic-reading audience, is evidence of curiosity about Turkic language and culture among scholars in the heart of the caliphate.

Court poetry was a major form of patronage. Poets praised rulers and viziers in elaborate verse, received payments and positions, and helped to shape the image of the dynasty. Some of the best-known poets of the period served the Seljuk courts, including Mu'izzi, a panegyrist of Malik Shah and Sanjar, and Anvari, a poet from Khurasan who wrote for Sanjar and whose verse was admired for its skill. These figures continued the court tradition established under the Ghaznavids, and the details of Persian poetry belong to Chapter 42. Historical writing in Persian also developed. Works on kingship and administration, such as the mirror-for-princes tradition, flourished, and Chapter 39 examines the best-known example. Persian prose and poetry were used for the glorification of Seljuk rulers and for the instruction of officials.

The term "synthesis" describes this combination, but it needs careful use. It did not mean the dissolution of Turkic and Iranian identities into a single culture. Persian-speaking officials, Turkic commanders, Arab scholars, and others remained distinct in language, background, and often in political interests. What changed was the creation of institutions and practices in which they worked together, and the adoption by Turkic rulers of the language of Persian and Islamic kingship. The Seljuk sultans were patrons of Persian culture even though most were not native speakers, and in this way they extended the Persianate world, the cultural sphere introduced in Chapter 1.

Comparative Evidence

Coins, Royal Titles, Administrative Texts, Chronicles, and Architectural Remains

Different kinds of evidence answer different questions about the Seljuk world, and the strongest conclusions come from comparing them.

Coins record the names and titles that rulers claimed and the order in which they placed their own name and that of the caliph. They show where a ruler's authority was recognized and, through changes of legend, how political relationships shifted. They cannot tell us about the exercise of power beyond the claim and the minting authority.

Royal titles appear on coins, inscriptions, and documents, and they express the ideology of rule. They show how the dynasty wished to be seen, but not how the claims were received.

Administrative texts, including letters, chancery manuals, and collections of documents that were copied for later use, reveal procedures and the language of government. Surviving examples are often preserved in later collections and may have been edited.

Chronicles supply the narrative framework. They were written by educated authors, often for courts, with biases and varying distance from the events.

Architectural remains provide physical evidence of patronage, resources, and taste. They survive in altered form, and inscriptions on them provide dates and names.

  1. What can coins tell historians about political authority that chronicles cannot?
  2. Why are royal titles better evidence of ideology than of actual power?
  3. How does the later copying of administrative documents affect their reliability?
  4. Which kinds of evidence give the least access to the lives of ordinary people, and why?
Section 38.32

Architecture, Urban Development, and Seljuk Artistic Patronage

Seljuk patronage left a substantial architectural record, much of it surviving in altered form. The buildings of the period show continuity with earlier Islamic and Iranian traditions and the development of new forms, and their study allows historians to see how patronage worked and what kinds of institutions rulers, viziers, and others chose to support.

Mosques were the principal monuments. The Friday or congregational mosque of a city was the center of public religious life, and its construction and enlargement were acts of royal and elite patronage. Madrasas, schools for the teaching of religious law and related subjects, were built or endowed by sultans, viziers, and commanders. Caravanserais, roadside inns for merchants and travelers, supported trade and the movement of people. Tomb structures commemorated rulers and religious figures. Palaces and fortifications served the court and defense, though fewer of them survive. Brick was the dominant material in much of Iran, and builders developed a repertoire of patterned brickwork, inscriptions, and tiles to decorate surfaces.

The Friday Mosque of Isfahan is the most important surviving example of Seljuk-era architecture in Iran, and its history illustrates how Seljuk building worked within an older tradition. The mosque had been founded centuries earlier and went through several phases of construction in the Abbasid and Buyid periods. In the later eleventh century, under Malik Shah, two large domed chambers were added to the building, one at the south end, in front of the prayer niche, associated with Nizam al-Mulk and dated by inscription to about 1086–1087, and one at the north end, associated with the rival official Taj al-Mulk and dated to about 1088–1089. These domes, which are admired for their proportions and brickwork, are among the most important examples of the development of domed chambers in Iranian architecture. The courtyard was also reorganized into the four-iwan plan, in which four vaulted halls, open on one side, face one another across a courtyard. The date and sequence of that reorganization are debated, with many scholars placing it in the late eleventh and early twelfth centuries. UNESCO inscribed the mosque as a World Heritage site in 2012, citing its combination of these Seljuk-era transformations with earlier and later work.

Beyond Isfahan, caravanserais and other buildings show the reach of Seljuk patronage. The Ribat-i Sharaf, a large caravanserai on the Khurasan road, built in the early twelfth century, shows the scale and decoration of such structures. The mausoleum of Sanjar at Merv, with a large double-shelled dome, belongs to the same architectural tradition. Ceramics from centers such as Rayy and Kashan, including lusterware and painted pottery, and metalwork and textiles, document a high level of craft production and the consumption of luxury goods in the cities of the empire.

These buildings are evidence of continuity as well as of innovation. The dome, the iwan, and brick decoration all had earlier antecedents in Iranian and Islamic architecture. What the Seljuk period did was to develop these forms and to combine them in new configurations, using the resources and organization that the empire made available. Students should therefore avoid describing a distinct "Seljuk style" invented from nothing. The better description is that architects and patrons working within a long tradition adapted it to new purposes and scales.

Architecture Study

The Seljuk-Era Building Phases of the Friday Mosque of Isfahan

Earlier foundation: The site developed over several centuries before the Seljuks, with congregational mosque construction in the Abbasid and Buyid periods. The Seljuk work was an addition to and transformation of an existing building.

Domed chambers: The south dome (c. 1086–1087) and the north dome (c. 1088–1089), identified by inscriptions and associated in the sources with Nizam al-Mulk and Taj al-Mulk. They show rivalry as well as cooperation among the elite of the empire, since two senior officials sponsored monumental structures in the same building.

Courtyard plan: The four-iwan arrangement, in which four vaulted halls open onto the courtyard on the cardinal axes. The date of its completion is debated, and scholars see it as emerging from earlier Iranian and Islamic prototypes and developing over a period.

Evidence: The inscriptions, brickwork, and structural analysis of the surviving building, together with literary references, support the reconstruction. Later alterations in the Mongol, Safavid, and modern periods complicate interpretation.

Section 38.33

The Peoples and Religions of the Seljuk World

The Seljuk empire was a society of many peoples and faiths. Treating it as a Turkic state ruling an Iranian population oversimplifies the picture. The empire included Persian-speaking Iranians in the plateau and Khurasan, along with Kurds, Lurs, and other Iranian-speaking groups in the mountains. Turkic-speaking groups were present as soldiers, commanders, and pastoralists, with numbers growing in the northwest and later in Anatolia. Arabic-speaking populations dominated Iraq, Khuzestan, and parts of Syria and the Jazira. Armenians and Georgians lived in the Caucasus and eastern Anatolia, Greek-speaking Christians in Anatolia, and many other communities elsewhere.

Religious diversity was equally great. Most subjects were Muslims, mainly Sunni, with significant Twelver Shiʿi and Zaydi populations and the Ismaili minority described above. Christian communities of several churches, including Armenian, Georgian, Greek Orthodox, and Syriac traditions, lived in the western and northern territories. Jewish communities lived in cities such as Isfahan and Baghdad, where there were scholars and institutions. Zoroastrian communities remained in Fars, Kirman, and Yazd. Their position was shaped by Islamic law, which gave non-Muslims of recognized religions a protected status in return for payment of the poll tax, and by local conditions.

Coexistence was real but unequal. Non-Muslims could practice their religion, maintain their institutions, and participate in economic life, but they were politically subordinate and subject to special taxes and, at times, restrictions. Their experience varied. Some individuals held office in the administration, while at other times and places communities suffered pressure, violence, or displacement. The conflicts on the Armenian and Georgian frontiers produced migrations, including the movement of Armenians toward Cilicia, and the conquests changed the demography of regions in Anatolia and the Caucasus. These changes were gradual and uneven, and they involved conversion, migration, coexistence, and conflict. Historians must resist both the picture of uniform tolerance and that of uniform persecution.

The empire was also a society of different political statuses. Turkmen followers, Persian officials, Arab scholars, Kurdish chiefs, and Christian princes occupied different positions in relation to the ruling elite. Some groups held power and others were governed. Treating the empire as culturally or religiously uniform would hide these differences. The Seljuk achievement was to organize such diversity within a single political framework, relying on local leaders, religious institutions, and negotiated arrangements, and its limits were clear in the repeated conflicts between groups and between the center and the regions.

Historical Debate

The Turkic-Iranian Synthesis: Integration, Domination, Negotiation, or a Combination?

The question: How should historians describe the relationship between the Turkic military elite and the Iranian and Arab populations and officials of the Seljuk empire? Was the result a genuine synthesis, a form of domination by a conquering minority, an ongoing negotiation, or some mixture?

Integration: Some historians emphasize the extent to which Turkic rulers adopted Persian and Islamic political culture, patronized Persian literature, relied on Iranian administrators, and became part of a shared Persianate world. Intermarriage, shared religion, and common interests in the stability of the state, on this view, produced a real fusion.
Domination: Others stress that the Seljuks conquered by force, held power through a military elite distinct in language and origin from most of their subjects, and drew revenue from the settled population for the benefit of the army. They argue that the language of synthesis obscures the dependence of Iranian and Arab populations on rulers who were not accountable to them.
Negotiation and combination: A third position holds that both elements were present and that the relationship is best seen as a continuing negotiation among groups with different resources and interests. Turkic commanders, Persian officials, religious scholars, and local elites bargained over power and revenue, and their relations changed over time and varied by region.

Evidence: Historians use the careers of officials and commanders, patronage of literature and buildings, chronicle accounts of conflicts and cooperation, and the structure of the army and the fiscal system. Much of the surviving evidence reflects the views of educated Persian and Arabic writers, which affects the picture.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns interpretation and terminology, and also what kinds of evidence should carry the greatest weight, the cultural patronage that survives or the economic and military relationships that are harder to document.

Why it matters: The question affects how the Seljuk period is placed in the long history of Iran and the Islamic world, whether as a break, a continuation, or a new stage of a long process of interaction.

Division VIII · §§38.34–38.39 Succession, Fragmentation, and the End of Seljuk Supremacy
Section 38.34

The Crisis of 1092: Nizam al-Mulk, Malik Shah, and the Succession Struggle

In the autumn of 1092, within roughly five weeks, the Seljuk empire lost both its vizier and its sultan. On 14 October, Nizam al-Mulk was stabbed to death while traveling in the region of Nahavand, on the road from Isfahan toward Baghdad. The sources describe the attacker as a man in the guise of a Sufi, and the tradition, repeated by many writers, attributes the act to a Nizari Ismaili assassin. Contemporary evidence for the attribution is not conclusive. The Nizaris were known to target prominent Sunni figures, and later Nizari tradition celebrated the killing, but other explanations, including court rivalry, were suggested at the time, and historians generally treat the attribution as probable but not fully proven. In mid-November, Malik Shah died in Baghdad, aged about thirty-seven, after an illness. Some sources suspect poison, but there is no secure evidence for it.

The two deaths removed the pair that had led the empire for twenty years, and they did so at a moment when relations between them had already deteriorated. The sources report tension between the sultan and the vizier in the final years, involving the vizier's extensive family network of offices, disputes over appointments, and the sultan's wish to place his own choice of heir in the caliphate. A rival faction at court, centered on the sultan's wife Terken Khatun, challenged the vizier's power. The details of these disputes come mainly from narrative sources that favored one side or another, and students should treat them with caution.

Terken Khatun, a Qarakhanid princess by origin according to the sources, was the mother of Malik Shah's son Mahmud, who was a small child in 1092. Royal women held significant resources, and she maintained her own household, officials, and military followers. She supported the vizier Taj al-Mulk, who was the rival of Nizam al-Mulk, and she sought to secure the succession for her son. When Malik Shah died, she acted quickly. She obtained recognition for Mahmud from commanders, secured oaths from some of the amirs, and sought the endorsement of the caliph, al-Muqtadi, who agreed to have Mahmud named in the sermon. Her policy has been described by hostile writers as ambitious and self-serving, but this reflects the sources' attitudes toward women in politics, and her actions resembled those of any claimant's supporters. She was a legitimate political actor with a stake in the outcome, and the way the sources portray her illustrates how evidence about powerful women is often shaped by the bias of its authors.

The other main claimant was Barkiyaruq, Malik Shah's older son by another wife, who was in his early teens. He was supported by the faction associated with the late Nizam al-Mulk, including the slave troops of the vizier and the vizier's sons, who had good reason to fear the new regime. Barkiyaruq escaped to Rayy, where his supporters gathered, and he claimed the sultanate. Other relatives, including Malik Shah's brother Tutush in Syria, also entered the struggle. Taj al-Mulk was killed in 1093 by members of the Nizamiyya faction. Terken Khatun died in 1094, and her son Mahmud died in the same year, ending that branch of the contest.

The breakdown of the imperial settlement in 1092 was not simply the result of bad luck. The deaths of the two leaders removed the central balance, but the underlying features of the system, including shared family authority, military factions, and competing offices, determined how the crisis unfolded. Nor was the crisis the end of the Seljuks. The dynasty continued for another century, but the unified authority that Malik Shah had exercised did not return.

Section 38.35

Barkiyaruq, Muhammad Tapar, and the Civil Wars, 1092–1118

The quarter-century after 1092 was a period of civil wars, shifting alliances, and attempts to rebuild authority. Barkiyaruq prevailed in the first round, defeating the forces of Tutush in 1095, when Tutush was killed in battle, and securing recognition from the caliph. But he faced challenges for the rest of his reign. His half-brothers Muhammad Tapar and Sanjar, sons of Malik Shah, contested his rule. Muhammad took a base in Azerbaijan and the Jibal and had himself proclaimed sultan, and the brothers fought a series of battles in the later 1090s and early 1100s. In 1097, Sanjar was appointed governor of Khurasan, a role that would become the basis of his later power.

The conflicts were driven by several forces. Regional commanders shifted their allegiance to the contender who offered the best assignments, and their defections decided battles. Revenue was a continuing problem. Barkiyaruq had to pay his supporters, and he could not do so from the territories he controlled, leading to the debasement of rewards and to the alienation of followers. The caliphs, who had to choose which sultan to recognize, used the opportunity to extract concessions. Syria, which had been under Tutush, fragmented among his sons and local commanders, and the First Crusade arrived in this divided landscape. Meanwhile the Nizari movement expanded in Iran and Syria, exploiting the weakness of the central government.

In 1104, a settlement divided the territory between Barkiyaruq and Muhammad. Barkiyaruq died in 1105, and after a brief interlude Muhammad Tapar emerged as the sole sultan in the west. Muhammad's reign, which lasted until 1118, was a period of reconstruction. He restored a measure of central authority, reasserted control over the caliphate, and relied on the support of his brother Sanjar, who ruled the east in his name. He campaigned against the Nizaris, besieging Alamut and other strongholds, though without conclusive success, and he reasserted Seljuk claims in Iraq and the northwest. His government has been seen as a partial recovery of Seljuk power, but it rested on personal authority and on the management of powerful commanders, and it did not restore the cohesion of the Malik Shah era.

These decades show the family system in action. Princes fought for the sultanate, commanders chose sides, and institutions such as the iqtaʿ and the atabegate shaped the outcome. They also show the effort to restore order. Sultans sought to reduce the autonomy of commanders, to reestablish a regular system of revenue, and to maintain a relationship with the caliph. The record is one of partial success, and it illustrates both the resilience and the limits of the Seljuk system.

Historical Debate

What Caused the Fragmentation Following Malik Shah's Death?

The question: Why did the Great Seljuk empire, which reached its height in 1092, break into competing regional authorities within a generation? Was the cause built into the structure of Seljuk rule, the result of events in 1092, or the product of wider pressures?

Structural explanation: The shared sovereignty of the ruling family, the appanage system, and the absence of a clear succession rule made fragmentation likely whenever a strong ruler died. On this view the empire was always a confederation held together by a powerful individual.
Contingent explanation: The near-simultaneous deaths of Malik Shah and Nizam al-Mulk, and the youth of the heirs, removed the leadership that had held the system together. A different sequence of events might have produced a smoother transition, as in 1072.
Military and fiscal explanation: The iqtaʿ system, the competition among military groups, and the declining ability of the center to control revenue strengthened commanders and atabegs at the expense of the sultan.
External pressures: The Nizari movement, the Crusaders, the Qara Khitai, the revival of the caliphate, and the rise of Khwarazm all weakened or competed with Seljuk power, and they did so in part because of the earlier fragmentation.

Evidence: Chronicles, documents on assignments and appointments, and the sequence of political events support different emphases. The sources are mostly narrative accounts that stress individuals, and the structural factors are inferred from patterns.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement is largely over weight and interaction, not over whether any factor existed. Many historians combine several explanations.

Why it matters: The answer affects how the Seljuk period is judged, whether as a flawed system doomed to break up or as a functioning order disrupted by contingent events and pressures. This chapter's treatment of delegation as a core feature of the system, not a sign of decline, bears on the question.

Student task: Explain which combination of causes you find most persuasive, and identify what evidence would show whether the break-up was avoidable.

Section 38.36

The Seljuks of Iraq, Atabegs, and the Growth of Regional Rule

After 1118, the Seljuk world divided into an eastern and a western sultanate. In the east, Sanjar ruled from Khurasan as Great Sultan. In the west, descendants of Muhammad Tapar and other Seljuk princes held the sultanate in Iraq and western Iran, a line often called the Seljuks of Iraq, though their authority extended to the Jibal, Azerbaijan, and other regions. Mahmud, Muhammad Tapar's son, became sultan in the west after his father's death, and Sanjar defeated him in 1119 and confirmed him as a subordinate. Over the following decades the western sultans, among them Mahmud, Masʿud, Muhammad, and others, held the title but had increasingly limited power.

The reason lay in the rise of atabegs and provincial military households. An atabeg was a commander who served as the guardian of a young prince and who often governed the prince's territory on his behalf. As princes were appointed as governors of provinces, their atabegs acquired real power, and some founded dynasties of their own while acknowledging the sultan in name. In Azerbaijan and the Jibal, the atabeg Eldigüz and his family built a powerful position, and by the middle of the twelfth century they controlled much of the northwest and the sultan's court. In Mosul and the Jazira, Zangi, a Turkic commander, established a regional dynasty, whose later history belongs to the Syrian and Crusader context. In Fars, other atabeg lines ruled. In Syria, Damascus and other cities were held by atabegs and their families. These rulers recognized the Seljuk sultan, minted coins in his name, and cooperated with the caliph, but they governed their own territories, kept their own armies, and fought one another.

The balance between the sultans and the caliphs also shifted. The Abbasid caliphs, especially al-Mustarshid (r. 1118–1135), al-Muqtafi (r. 1136–1160), and later al-Nasir (r. 1180–1225), built military forces and extended their direct control over Baghdad and parts of Iraq at the expense of the western Seljuk sultans. Al-Mustarshid was defeated and captured by Sultan Masʿud in 1135, then murdered while in captivity by attackers identified in the sources as Ismailis; responsibility for arranging the assassination remains disputed. His successors nevertheless continued the effort. By the middle of the twelfth century, the caliphate had become an independent political power in Iraq, a development that reversed the position of the eleventh century. It did not mean a full restoration of earlier caliphal power, but it altered the balance in the Abbasid heartland.

The consequences for Azerbaijan, Fars, and western Iran were significant. These regions came under atabeg dynasties that governed with considerable autonomy, patronized local culture, and fought for position. The Persian literary and architectural culture of the period continued, supported by these regional courts. The western Seljuk sultanate survived as a symbolic and legitimating institution whose holders were often controlled by the atabegs. The pattern shows that regional rule was not simply a sign of collapse. It was a transformation of the Seljuk system, in which the military and administrative methods developed in the empire were carried on by new dynasties.

Section 38.37

Ahmad Sanjar and the Eastern Sultanate, 1097–1157

Ahmad Sanjar, a son of Malik Shah, governed Khurasan from 1097 and became the senior Seljuk ruler in 1118 on the death of his brother Muhammad Tapar. His rule over the eastern lands lasted until 1157 and was the longest of any Seljuk sultan, and for most of the twelfth century he was the most prominent Seljuk figure. He ruled from Merv, a major city of Khurasan, with a court and administration that drew on the Persian secretarial tradition. His chancery and court were centers of Persian literature and Islamic scholarship.

Sanjar's authority rested on his hold over Khurasan and his relationships with neighboring rulers. He maintained overlordship over the Qarakhanids of Transoxiana, campaigning at times to enforce it. He supported Ghaznavid rulers in their territory and acted as an arbiter in the region. In Khwarazm, the governors, who had been appointed by the sultans and who became the Khwarazmshahs, built a growing power. Atsiz, the Khwarazmshah from 1127, acknowledged Sanjar's overlordship but at times rebelled, and Sanjar campaigned against him more than once, restoring his authority without removing him. This relationship, which Chapter 44 follows, shows the strains of tributary sovereignty.

The decisive event of Sanjar's reign was the battle of Qatwan, fought on 9 September 1141 on the steppe near Samarkand. A new power had appeared in Central Asia, the Qara Khitai, a people of Khitan origin who had migrated westward after the fall of the Liao dynasty in northern China and who established a state in the region. The Qarakhanid ruler of Samarkand, under pressure from steppe groups, appealed to Sanjar for help, and Sanjar led an army against the Qara Khitai. At Qatwan, he was defeated. The sources report heavy casualties and the capture of members of his household, but their figures are inconsistent and cannot be relied on. The defeat was a major blow to Seljuk prestige. The Qarakhanids of Transoxiana became subordinate to the Qara Khitai, and Seljuk influence beyond the Oxus ended.

Qatwan did not destroy Sanjar's power in Khurasan. He retained his territories, continued to rule, and campaigned against Khwarazm in the following years. But it exposed the limits of his military strength and weakened the aura of invincibility that had supported Seljuk authority on the eastern frontier. It also contributed to the rise of Khwarazm, whose rulers could now bargain from a position of greater independence. The defeat created conditions in which a new crisis, the Oghuz revolt, would prove far more damaging.

Section 38.38

The Oghuz Revolt, Sanjar's Captivity, and the Collapse of Eastern Seljuk Authority, 1153–1157

In 1153, groups of Oghuz in the region of Tokharistan and Balkh rose against Sanjar, defeated his army, and captured the sultan himself. They held him in captivity for about three years, during which they plundered the cities of Khurasan. The episode ended the effective power of the Great Seljuk line in the east and is one of the most dramatic events of the dynasty's history. It is also one of the most misunderstood, and the explanation requires care.

The Oghuz involved were pastoral groups who had been settled in the region under Seljuk authority. They were Oghuz by origin, like the Seljuk family, but they were not the Seljuk following itself, and the ruling dynasty and the rebel Oghuz were not a single community. Over the generations since the conquest, the Seljuk rulers had become a court elite with a settled administration and a Persian bureaucracy, while the Oghuz of the Balkh region remained herders with their own leaders. The sources indicate that a dispute arose over the tribute and taxes that Sanjar's officials demanded from the Oghuz, including a levy on their flocks, and over the treatment of the groups by local commanders. Accounts differ on the details, including the exact demand and the behavior of the officials. Sanjar launched a campaign to enforce his demands, and the Oghuz resisted.

Interpreting the revolt as an outbreak of nomadic hostility to civilization misrepresents the evidence. The Oghuz acted in response to taxation and treatment by officials, and the dispute escalated through political miscalculation and military overreach. Sanjar was an old man, and his forces were weakened by earlier defeats. The sources suggest that some Oghuz leaders initially sought negotiation, and that some Oghuz groups did not join the rebellion. The revolt was the result of a breakdown in the relationship between a state and the pastoral populations that it had come to tax and govern. The same period saw other pressures on Khurasan, including the rise of Khwarazm and the loss of Transoxiana.

The consequences for Khurasan were severe. The Oghuz sacked Merv, Nishapur, and other cities, and the sources describe killings of scholars and ordinary residents, the destruction of libraries and institutions, and the flight of people from the region. Contemporary and later accounts give no reliable death tolls. The damage to Nishapur, a major center of scholarship and commerce, is particularly emphasized in the sources, and Persian poets wrote laments for the devastation. Sanjar escaped from captivity in 1156 and returned to Merv, but he could not restore his authority, and he died in 1157. The eastern Seljuk state dissolved. The Oghuz rebels were divided among rival leaders, and Khurasan was contested among Oghuz chiefs, local commanders, and the Khwarazmshahs, who expanded into the region. Khurasan, the core region of Seljuk power, fell into a period of fragmentation that would last until the Khwarazmians gained control.

Primary Source Window IV

Sanjar and the Oghuz Conflict: Competing Accounts of the Revolt

Sources: Arabic and Persian chronicles of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, including Ibn al-Athir's al-Kāmil, and Persian poetry lamenting the destruction of Khurasan, notably verse attributed to the poet Anvari · Type: Chronicle narratives and literary laments

Context: The accounts of the revolt were written after the events. Chroniclers such as Ibn al-Athir compiled their narratives from earlier reports, many by writers connected to the cities that suffered, and they present explanations of the revolt that differ in emphasis. Persian poets of the period, some of whom lived through the disorders, wrote about the destruction of Khurasan in verse that is evidence of how the events were experienced and remembered, though the attribution of particular poems to individual authors is sometimes uncertain.

Thematic source-based summary (not a quotation or a paraphrase of one specific passage): Chronicle accounts generally trace the revolt to a dispute over the demands made on the Oghuz of the Balkh region, including levies on their flocks and the behavior of Sanjar's officers, and they describe Sanjar's defeat, capture, escape, and the sack of Khurasan's cities. Some accounts stress the arrogance or errors of Sanjar's officials, others the lawlessness of the Oghuz. The laments in Persian verse describe the ruin of cities and the suffering of the population in strong language. Students should compare these paraphrased summaries with published translations of the sources before relying on specific claims.

Analysis: Comparing these accounts shows how different authors assigned responsibility. Writers sympathetic to the dynasty and the settled population emphasized Oghuz violence, while those who record the dispute over tribute and officials' conduct point to failures of the state. The poetic laments testify to the human cost and shaped later memory of the revolt.

Limitations: No source preserves the Oghuz leaders' own account. The numbers of casualties, the size of the armies, and the exact sequence of the negotiations are uncertain. The authors wrote from the standpoint of urban, literate, mostly Sunni Muslims, and their descriptions of pastoral groups reflect the prejudices of that group.

Perspective: The sources represent the viewpoint of the settled elite and of the dynasty's supporters. The voices of Oghuz herders, of cultivators in the countryside, and of the Oghuz leaders who did not join the revolt are largely missing.

Section 38.39

The Last Western Seljuks and the End of the Dynasty in Iran, 1157–1194

The death of Sanjar in 1157 did not end the Seljuk dynasty. In the west, a line of sultans continued to hold the title in Iraq and western Iran for another generation, though their real power was limited. Muhammad II, Sulayman Shah, Arslan Shah, and others succeeded one another, and the sultanate was in practice dominated by the atabegs of the Eldigüzid family, who controlled Azerbaijan and the Jibal. Under Arslan Shah (r. 1160–1176), the atabeg Shams al-Din Eldigüz himself exercised the predominant power until his death in 1175. His son Muhammad Jahan Pahlavan then succeeded as atabeg, maintained control under Tughril III, and died in 1186; only thereafter did his brother Qizil Arslan assume the leading position. The sultans were important as figures of legitimacy but lacked an independent base.

Tughril III, who became sultan in 1176, tried to assert his independence. He faced the power of the Eldigüzids, whose leader Qizil Arslan held him prisoner for some years, before he escaped in the early 1190s and resumed his rule. He also faced the Abbasid caliph al-Nasir, who had built an effective state in Iraq and who sought to remove Seljuk influence from Baghdad and the surrounding region. The caliph encouraged an alternative champion, the Khwarazmshah Tekish, whose state in Khwarazm and Khurasan had grown into a major power during the second half of the twelfth century.

In 1194, Tekish marched west and defeated Tughril III in battle at Rayy. Tughril was killed, and with his death the line of the Seljuk sultans in western Iran ended. The caliph, who had hoped to benefit from the downfall of the Seljuks, found that he had exchanged one powerful neighbor for another. Tekish's victory opened Iran to Khwarazmian rule and set the scene for the developments described in Chapter 44. This chapter therefore ends its political narrative with the battle of Rayy.

Other Seljuk branches had also come to an end or changed. The Seljuks of Kirman, who had ruled the south of Iran since the 1040s, lost power in the 1180s, after conflicts with Oghuz groups and others. The Seljuk presence in Syria had disappeared earlier in the twelfth century, replaced by atabeg dynasties. The Seljuks of Rum, however, survived. Founded by Sulayman ibn Qutalmish in the aftermath of Manzikert, the Anatolian sultanate continued under rulers such as Kilij Arslan II and Kaykhusraw I and endured, in increasingly reduced and Mongol-dominated form, into the early fourteenth century. The survival of the Rum Seljuks long after the Great Seljuks had ended underlines the need to distinguish the two branches. They shared an ancestry, but they developed their own states, their own politics, and their own histories.

Section 38.40

The Seljuk Legacy: Turkic Dynastic Power and the Transformation of Persianate Islamic Government

The chapter's governing question was how the Seljuks built an imperial order across Iran and the wider Islamic world, and why the combination of Turkic dynastic traditions, Iranian administration, and Islamic legitimacy produced both durable institutions and recurring instability. The evidence examined in the preceding sections allows a direct answer.

The Seljuks built their order through a sequence of steps that were neither inevitable nor planned in advance. A family of the Oghuz world, with a pastoral following and a tradition of shared family authority, entered Khurasan, won the support of cities, defeated the Ghaznavids at Dandanqan, and extended its authority across the plateau by a mixture of conquest and negotiated submission. It secured legitimacy by taking over the protection of the Abbasid caliphate in 1055. Under Alp Arslan and Malik Shah, it expanded to the Caucasus, Syria, and Transoxiana, and it organized the empire through relatives, commanders, slave soldiers, iqtaʿ assignments, and a corps of Persian officials led by a vizier. At every stage the empire depended on negotiation among groups with their own interests.

What changed in Iranian political and cultural history because of Seljuk rule? Several things. A Turkic ruling house, with its mobile court and pastoral constituency, became the dominant power across Iran, Iraq, and the neighboring regions, and Turkic military and political presence became a permanent feature of the region. The Turkmen movement into Azerbaijan and Anatolia began a demographic and linguistic transformation that continued for centuries and led to the formation of new states, among them the Seljuks of Rum. The relationship between ruler and caliph was reordered, with a Sunni sultan holding military supremacy under caliphal recognition, and this arrangement shaped later Islamic political thought. The system of iqtaʿ assignments to support armies became a standard feature of government in many Muslim states. Atabegs became a characteristic institution of the twelfth century. Patronage of religious schools and endowments expanded, and Persian court culture reached new regions and audiences.

What continued from earlier Islamic-Iranian states? Most of the administrative apparatus, including the dīvāns, the secretarial families, the vizierate, the chancery, and the tax system, came from the Abbasid, Samanid, Buyid, and Ghaznavid worlds. Persian literary culture and the use of Persian in administration had developed under the Samanids and Ghaznavids. Sunni religious scholarship and the madrasa existed before the Seljuks. The military slave institution had been used by earlier dynasties. The caliph's recognition of rulers followed a long tradition. The Seljuks adapted, extended, and combined these inheritances, and they did not create them.

The instability was part of the same design. The shared sovereignty of the family, the appanage system, the lack of a fixed succession rule, the competition among military groups, and the tension between pastoral followers and settled administration all gave the empire its flexibility and its fragility. In strong hands, the system could project power across great distances at modest cost. When the leadership failed, as in 1092, or when relations with pastoral populations broke down, as in 1153, the same features produced fragmentation. The twelfth century shows these structures continuing in modified forms: atabeg dynasties, regional sultanates, and a revived caliphate used the institutions that the Seljuks had spread.

The Seljuk legacy also extended beyond Iran. The Seljuks of Rum and the Turkmen principalities carried Turkic and Persianate culture into Anatolia. The Persianate court model, with Turkic rulers, Persian bureaucracy, and Islamic legitimacy, became a pattern for later dynasties in Iran, Anatolia, Central Asia, and South Asia, a subject that later volumes of this textbook explore. The Mongol conquests, introduced in Chapters 44 and 45, would transform the political landscape again, but they encountered a region whose governing institutions had been shaped by the Seljuk experience.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the Seljuks created an imperial order across Iran and the wider Islamic world between the 1030s and 1194. It began with the Oghuz steppe society from which the dynasty emerged, a world of pastoral economies, tribal leadership, and long-standing relations with settled neighbors. The ruling family, represented by the founder Seljuk ibn Duqaq in later tradition, left the Oghuz yabghu and settled near Jand, where it adopted Islam. The narratives that describe this are later dynastic traditions, and the chapter treated them as evidence for Seljuk self-presentation rather than as secure record.

The migration into Khurasan between 1035 and 1038 grew from the Ghaznavid relocation of Seljuk followers and the instability of the region. The conflict concerned pasture, taxation, political recognition, and sovereignty at once. The Seljuk acquisition of Merv and Nishapur, followed by the victory at Dandanqan in 1040, opened Khurasan but did not complete the conquest of the plateau. Further campaigns and negotiated submissions brought Rayy, Hamadan, Isfahan, Azerbaijan, and Kirman under Seljuk control, while a settlement with the Ghaznavids and Qarakhanids stabilized the east.

Tughril's entry into Baghdad in December 1055 replaced Buyid supervision of the Abbasid caliphate with Seljuk supervision and established a new settlement between sultan and caliph. The al-Basasiri crisis of 1058–1060 showed how contested the arrangement remained. The settlement divided functions, with the sultan holding military and fiscal power and the caliph holding legitimating and religious authority. Historians debate whether this transformed Abbasid government or perpetuated earlier arrangements, and the chapter presented several positions.

Alp Arslan's succession in 1063 showed the absence of a fixed rule of succession. His reign saw the capture of Ani and the battle of Manzikert on 26 August 1071, a major Byzantine defeat. The chapter emphasized that Manzikert was not the immediate conquest of Anatolia. The transformation of Anatolia followed from Byzantine civil conflict, Turkmen migration, and the emergence of new regional powers, including the Seljuks of Rum, a separate branch whose later history lies outside this chapter.

Malik Shah's reign marked the height of Great Seljuk power. Supported by a capable vizier, he secured the sultanate, extended his authority to Transoxiana and Syria, and governed through relatives, commanders, and negotiated subordination. The empire was not a uniformly centralized state. Isfahan and other cities served as centers, but the court was mobile, and sovereignty took different forms in different regions.

The chapter examined Seljuk government, military organization, and society. The Great Sultan balanced relatives, commanders, officials, and religious authorities. Persian viziers and secretaries ran the dīvāns, continuing earlier practice within a new structure of appanages and military power. The army combined Turkmen cavalry, slave soldiers, and commanders. Iqtaʿ assignments supported the army through rights to revenue, not ownership of land. The relationship between pastoral and settled populations was a continuing source of both cooperation and tension.

Seljuk religious and cultural patronage supported Sunni scholarship, endowments, and Persian-language culture, but the chapter stressed that the dynasty inherited rather than invented these institutions. The Seljuk state also confronted Shiʿi communities and the Nizari Ismaili challenge that emerged at Alamut after 1090. Architectural evidence, notably the domed chambers of the Friday Mosque of Isfahan, shows patronage working within an older tradition. The empire included many peoples and religions, and the debate over the Turkic-Iranian synthesis showed the complexity of their relationships.

The crisis of 1092, with the deaths of Nizam al-Mulk and Malik Shah, opened a period of civil wars among Malik Shah's sons. Terken Khatun and other royal women took part in the struggle. The dynasty divided into eastern and western lines. In the west, atabegs and regional dynasties gained power, and the Abbasid caliphs recovered influence. In the east, Sanjar ruled until the defeat at Qatwan in 1141 and the Oghuz revolt of 1153, which the chapter explained as a conflict involving taxation, treatment of pastoral groups, and miscalculation, and not as nomadic hostility. Sanjar's death in 1157 ended the eastern line, and the defeat of Tughril III at Rayy in 1194 ended the western line in Iran.

The chapter's central argument is that Seljuk political structures combined Turkic dynastic traditions, Iranian administration, and Islamic legitimacy. The combination created durable institutions, including the iqtaʿ, the vizierate, the atabegate, and a model of Sunni sultanate, as well as recurring instability arising from shared sovereignty, contested succession, and the tension between pastoral and settled interests. The Seljuk period transformed the political organization of Iran while continuing the Persianate Islamic world that earlier dynasties had built.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a political order was built from the combination of different inheritances, and how the same features that made it strong made it fragile. The Seljuks did not erase the Persianate Islamic world that the Samanids, Buyids, and Ghaznavids had shaped. They entered it, adapted its institutions to a dynasty of tribal origin, and spread it across a larger territory than any earlier Muslim dynasty in the east had governed.

The chapter also reveals larger processes in the history of the region: the movement of Turkic populations westward, which would reshape Azerbaijan and Anatolia; the transformation of the relationship between rulers and the caliphate; the growth of institutions such as the iqtaʿ, the atabegate, and religious endowments; and the recurring tension between pastoral and settled societies. These processes continued long after the Great Seljuk empire had fragmented, and they shaped the Khwarazmian period, the Mongol conquests, and the states of later centuries.

The chapter connects Iran to Central Asia, Iraq, Syria, the Caucasus, and Anatolia. The Seljuk empire's frontiers touched the Qarakhanids, Ghaznavids, Fatimids, Byzantines, Armenians, and Georgians, and its history cannot be understood within the borders of any modern state, a principle established in Chapter 1. It also contributes to the larger textbook by tracing how the Persianate world acquired new rulers and new regions, preparing the way for the discussions of administration in Chapter 39, religion in Chapter 40, and the Nizari movement in Chapter 41.

Finally, the chapter invites students to think about how historians know what they know. The record of the Seljuks comes from court histories, chronicles written after the events, poetry, coins, and buildings, each with its own perspective and limits. Understanding the Seljuk story means reading these sources with care and recognizing where the evidence runs out. The effort to explain the rise and fragmentation of an empire without reducing it to a single cause or a single people is, in the end, the central skill of historical study.
