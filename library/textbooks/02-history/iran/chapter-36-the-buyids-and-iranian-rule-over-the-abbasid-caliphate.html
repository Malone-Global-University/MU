The Buyids and Iranian Rule over the Abbasid Caliphate

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

The Buyids, a family of Daylamite military commanders from the Caspian highlands, took control of Baghdad in 945 and held effective power over the Abbasid caliphate for a century, yet they never abolished the caliphate. They transformed it instead into an institution in which Iranian rulers commanded the armies, the treasury, and the administration, while the caliph remained a source of religious legitimacy that no military ruler of the age was prepared to do without.

On 19 December 945, which corresponds to the eleventh of Jumada I in the year 334 of the Islamic calendar, an army of Daylamite and other troops under Ahmad ibn Buya entered Baghdad. The Abbasid capital had been the largest city of the Islamic world for nearly two centuries, and its caliph, al-Mustakfi, claimed the office of successor to the Prophet Muhammad as leader of the Muslim community. According to the Arabic chronicles, the city did not resist. Within weeks, the new master of the capital had received the honorific title Muʿizz al-Dawla, had been recognized by the caliph as holder of the supreme military command, and had begun the process that ended in January 946 with the deposition of the very caliph who had received him. His replacement, al-Mutiʿ, reigned for nearly three decades as a ruler whose name appeared on the coinage and in the Friday sermon, and whose person was treated with ceremony, but who did not control the revenues or the soldiers on which his government depended.

What is striking about this episode is not that a military commander seized control of a caliph. Turkish officers had been doing something similar for more than a century, and the caliphs of the tenth century had repeatedly appointed commanders to the title amir al-umara, "commander of commanders," in the hope that one strong soldier would restore order. What is striking is what happened next. The Buyids did not declare the caliphate extinct, did not install a rival caliph of their own Shiʿi sympathies, and did not move the seat of government permanently away from Baghdad. For roughly a century, until the entry of the Seljuk sultan Tughril Beg into Baghdad in 1055, Buyid rulers governed the heart of the Abbasid empire through a caliph they could have removed but chose to preserve. This chapter asks why they did so, how the arrangement worked, and what it reveals about the transformation of political authority in the medieval Islamic world.

The Buyids were not a single state in the way that later textbooks sometimes imply. Three brothers, ʿAli, Hasan, and Ahmad, the sons of Buya, a man of Daylamite origin whom later sources describe as having risen from modest circumstances, established three separate centers of power between roughly 932 and 945, in Fars, in the Jibal of western Iran, and in Iraq. Their descendants ruled through several branches, in Shiraz, Rayy, Hamadan, Isfahan, and Baghdad, which cooperated when their interests aligned and fought one another when they did not. For a brief period between 978 and 983 a single ruler, ʿAḍud al-Dawla, held most of the Buyid territories under his personal supremacy, and this achievement has often been taken as the norm. It was in fact the exception, and understanding why the dynasty was fragmentary by design, and impressively consolidated only once, is one of the central tasks of the chapter.

The family's origins matter, though they must be handled with care. Daylam, the mountainous region south and west of the Caspian Sea, produced soldiers who served in the armies of numerous Iranian and Islamic rulers during the ninth and tenth centuries, and the Buyid commanders rose within precisely this military labor market before they became rulers in their own right. The Buyids came from a region that had been only partly Islamized, in which Zaydi missionaries had been active, and in which a Shiʿi religious environment coexisted with older local traditions. Yet it would be a serious mistake to treat Daylamite origin as a synonym for Persian identity, for a single Shiʿi doctrine, or for the political program of a modern Iranian nation. The Buyids likely spoke a northwestern Iranian dialect at home but ruled in an Islamic empire whose language of administration and scholarship was overwhelmingly Arabic. They revived Iranian royal titles, among them the Persian imperial title shahanshah, "king of kings," and drew on Sasanian imagery, but they also sought and received investiture from the Abbasid caliph. Their rule was a negotiated combination of Daylamite military practice, Iranian royal tradition, and Islamic legitimacy, and this chapter treats that combination as a historical creation rather than as a foregone expression of any single identity.

The chapter's argument extends beyond political narrative. The Buyid period saw major changes in how Islamic states paid their soldiers, as cash payments gave way increasingly to assignments of tax revenue known as iqtaʿ, though the practice varied and must not be mistaken for European feudalism. It saw the first officially sponsored public commemorations of Ashura and Ghadir Khumm in Baghdad, which transformed the religious landscape of the capital and provoked violent Sunni response. It saw one of the most ambitious programs of public works in the pre-Mongol Islamic world, under ʿAḍud al-Dawla, including a large hospital in Baghdad and a dam near Persepolis. It also saw the flourishing of a courtly culture of viziers, secretaries, historians, and poets whose Arabic prose and verse are among the great achievements of medieval literature, and whose existence shows that Iranian political power in this period did not displace Arabic but patronized it extensively.

Historians reconstruct the period from a relatively rich but unevenly distributed body of evidence. Ibn Miskawayh, a historian and treasurer who served several Buyid rulers, wrote a contemporary universal history, Tajārib al-umam ("Experiences of the Nations"), whose later volumes provide a participant's account of the court of ʿAḍud al-Dawla. Hilal al-Sabiʾ, al-Tanukhi, al-Muqaddasi, and Ibn Hawqal wrote on court ceremony, social life, geography, and travel. Coins, inscriptions, and surviving architecture complement these literary sources. Almost all of this material, however, was produced by educated secretaries, scholars, and merchants of the urban elite, writing mostly in Arabic and mostly about Iraq, Fars, and the great cities. The Daylamite soldiers who made Buyid rule possible left almost no writing of their own, and the experience of villagers, women, and laborers must usually be reconstructed indirectly. Throughout the chapter, students are asked to consider whose view a source represents and whose view is missing.

Within the textbook, this chapter follows the Tahirids and Saffarids of Chapter 34, who revived regional military power in eastern Iran, and the Samanids of Chapter 35, who built a distinctive eastern state and sponsored New Persian literature. It draws on Chapter 31's account of Iranian secretaries and the Abbasid imperial system, whose administrative inheritance the Buyids took over. It is followed by the Ghaznavids in Chapter 37, whose conquest of Rayy in 1029 shrank the Buyid world, and by the Seljuk conquest in Chapter 38, which ended Buyid supremacy in Baghdad. The religious history developed here is carried further in Chapter 40, the wider Ismaʿili setting of the Fatimid challenge in Chapter 41, and the intellectual history of the period in Chapter 43. The chapter's task is to provide the political, institutional, and social framework within which those later accounts become intelligible.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the military, fiscal, and political crisis of the early tenth-century Abbasid state and the conditions that allowed new regional dynasties to rise.
  2. Describe the Daylamite and Caspian background of the Buyids and distinguish Daylamite origin from a uniform Persian identity or a single Shiʿi denomination.
  3. Trace the establishment of Buyid power in Fars, the Jibal, Khuzestan, and Iraq between about 932 and 945, and explain why the three brothers created separate regional bases.
  4. Analyze the Buyid entry into Baghdad on 19 December 945 and the settlement of 946, distinguishing the evidence of the chronicles from later elaboration.
  5. Distinguish between the Buyid ruler's control of armies, revenues, and officials and the caliph's religious, legal, and ceremonial authority, and explain why the Buyids retained the caliphate.
  6. Compare the three principal Buyid branches, their capitals, resources, and relationships, and explain why the Buyids cannot be treated as a single centralized state.
  7. Assess the reign of ʿAḍud al-Dawla, including his unification of much of the Buyid realm, his government, his public works, and his use of Iranian royal symbolism within Islamic legitimacy.
  8. Evaluate the Buyid administrative, fiscal, and military systems, including the dīvāns, the role of viziers and secretaries, and the practice of iqtaʿ assignment.
  9. Interpret Buyid religious politics, including Shiʿi patronage, public commemorations, Sunni response, and the survival of Sunni caliphal institutions, without assuming a uniform Buyid religious program.
  10. Explain the fragmentation of Buyid authority after 983, the role of Ghaznavid and Seljuk expansion, and the distinction between the end of Buyid rule in Baghdad in 1055 and the end of the Fars branch in 1062.
  11. Evaluate competing interpretations of Buyid rule and kingship and assess the evidence on which they depend.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. Why did the Abbasid government lose control of Baghdad in the early tenth century, and what did earlier attempts to restore order through the office of amir al-umara reveal about the limits of caliphal power?

Historical Context

By the early tenth century the Abbasid caliphate, which had presided over a large and relatively unified Islamic empire from its foundation in 750, was a government under severe strain. The Abbasid imperial system, whose administrative and secretarial foundations are examined in Chapter 31, had depended on a tax-collecting bureaucracy centered in Baghdad, on a standing army paid from the proceeds of taxation, and on the caliph's capacity to appoint governors who remitted revenue to the capital. Each of these supports weakened across the ninth century. Governors in distant provinces, including the Tahirids and Saffarids of Chapter 34 and the Samanids of Chapter 35, kept an increasing share of provincial revenue, while still acknowledging the caliph's religious authority. Within Iraq itself, rebellions, the long Zanj uprising of southern Iraq, and the activity of the Qarmatian movement in eastern Arabia and Iraq eroded both revenue and security. The army that the caliphs relied upon had become dominated by Turkish soldiers, whose commanders could make or unmake caliphs, and the cost of paying them repeatedly outran the treasury.

The result was a series of crises in the early tenth century in which the caliphal court, its viziers, and its military commanders competed for control of a shrinking revenue base. Viziers were dismissed, imprisoned, and sometimes killed. Military commanders were appointed to supreme command, and quarreled with the civil bureaucracy. In 936, the caliph al-Radi created for Ibn Raʾiq the office of amir al-umara, a position that put military command and much of the administration of revenue under a single commander. This was not the end of caliphal authority, but it signaled a profound shift: the caliph was increasingly dependent on a military patron whose own position was unstable, and the contest for that position among Turkish, Daylamite, Hamdanid, and other commanders consumed the capital's resources throughout the following decade. Section 36.5 examines this crisis directly.

While Baghdad weakened, the Iranian plateau was experiencing a wider revival of regional dynastic power. The Samanids ruled in Khurasan and Transoxiana, the Ziyarids briefly held Tabaristan and Gurgan and extended their power into the Jibal under Mardavij, and numerous local rulers held the Caspian coast, Azerbaijan, Kirman, and Fars. Many of these states relied for their soldiers upon Daylamites, who had acquired a reputation as capable and relatively inexpensive infantry, and upon Turkic cavalrymen drawn from the steppe. The combination of fragmented political authority and an abundant military labor market created opportunities for ambitious soldiers. The Buyid brothers rose to prominence in precisely this setting, serving first as subordinate commanders in the service of other rulers, and then, in the space created by the weakness of those rulers, as independent masters of cities and provinces.

The environmental and economic setting also deserves attention. Iraq's wealth rested upon the alluvial plain known as the Sawad, irrigated by the Tigris and Euphrates and a dense network of canals that required constant maintenance and, therefore, a functioning state. Fars, by contrast, combined well-watered highland valleys, irrigated plains such as the Marvdasht, and a Persian Gulf coast connected to maritime commerce. The Jibal and Rayy lay on the great overland route between Iraq and Khurasan. The Buyids would draw revenue from all three zones, but each had different tax systems, different agricultural problems, and different relationships to the soldiers who were paid from them, and these differences explain much of the dynasty's political geography. Chapter 1 introduced the plateau's historical regions; this chapter follows them into one of their most politically complicated centuries.

Finally, readers should keep in mind the religious setting. The Abbasid caliphs were Sunni in the broad sense that their authority rested upon descent from the Prophet's uncle ʿAbbas and upon recognition by the majority community, but the tenth-century Islamic world also contained influential Shiʿi communities, including Twelver Shiʿis in Iraq and western Iran, Zaydis in the Caspian highlands and Yemen, and Ismaʿilis, whose Fatimid imperial state in North Africa and, after 969, Egypt claimed a rival caliphate. The Buyids were Shiʿi in affiliation, but the precise character of their Shiʿism, and its relationship to their government of a largely Sunni capital, is one of the principal questions the chapter addresses.

Section 36.1 · Division I — Daylam, the Abbasid Crisis, and the Rise of the Buyids

The Abbasid Caliphate and the Fragmentation of Political Power

The Buyids did not create the weakness of the Abbasid caliphate. They inherited a government whose military and fiscal foundations had been eroding for nearly a century, and their rise is intelligible only against the background of that erosion. This section summarizes the crisis as it stood in the early tenth century. It does not repeat the administrative history of the Abbasid secretarial system, which Chapter 31 examines, or the careers of the eastern dynasties that Chapters 34 and 35 narrate. Its purpose is to explain why, by the 930s, the question facing every ambitious commander in the Islamic world was no longer whether to obey the caliph but how to use him.

The ninth century had already revealed the fragility of the system. The caliphs had come to depend upon a professional army of Turkish soldiers, many of them recruited as slaves or purchased and trained as military servants, and the commanders of those soldiers acquired a decisive voice in the selection of caliphs, most dramatically during the period after 861 when several caliphs were deposed or killed by their own troops. In the 880s and 890s a vigorous recovery under caliphs such as al-Muʿtaḍid restored a measure of order, defeated the long Zanj rebellion in southern Iraq, and renewed the flow of revenue to Baghdad. The recovery proved short-lived. After the death of al-Muʿtaḍid, his successors presided over a court in which factions of viziers, secretaries, military commanders, and members of the caliph's household competed for control of appointments and revenue. The reign of al-Muqtadir, which began in 908 when he was a boy and ended with his death in battle in 932, became a byword in the later historical tradition for the instability of such arrangements, with viziers repeatedly appointed, dismissed, fined, and imprisoned, and with the caliph's mother and other members of the court exercising substantial influence over the allocation of resources.

The fiscal dimension of the crisis was fundamental. The Abbasid government depended principally on the land tax, kharaj, collected from the cultivators of the Sawad of Iraq and from the provinces, and on a range of other levies. When provincial governors began to retain the revenue of their territories, whether in open defiance of the caliph or under the guise of formal governorship, the proceeds that reached Baghdad shrank. At the same time, the army's pay claims remained high, and soldiers who were not paid mutinied, plundered, or changed sides. Rulers responded by borrowing from bankers, by confiscating the wealth of fallen officials, by farming out taxes to contractors who advanced cash against future collections, and by granting soldiers assignments of revenue in lieu of cash. Each of these expedients solved a short-term problem while worsening long-term fiscal capacity, because tax farmers had every incentive to squeeze cultivators and few to maintain the canals and embankments on which the Sawad's productivity depended. Scholars still debate how much the decline of revenue reflected genuine agrarian contraction, how much the diversion of revenue to provincial rulers, and how much simple failure of administration. The chronicles, written largely by secretaries and viziers, tend to emphasize administrative failure, which was the failure those authors knew best.

Security deteriorated alongside revenue. The Qarmatian movement, an offshoot of Ismaʿili Shiʿism that established an autonomous state in eastern Arabia, raided pilgrim caravans and Iraqi frontier districts, and in 930 its forces sacked Mecca and carried off the Black Stone from the Kaʿba, a humiliation to the caliph's claim to protect the pilgrimage. In southern Iraq, the Baridi family of tax officials established a semi-independent power in Basra and Khuzestan. In northern Iraq and the Jazira, the Hamdanid family built a power base at Mosul, and in Egypt and Syria the Ikhshidid governor secured effective independence from 935. In the east, the Samanids ruled Khurasan and Transoxiana as formal subordinates of the caliph, while the Ziyarid and Daylamite commanders of the Caspian region carved out independent territories. These developments were not coordinated, and the rulers involved held different attitudes toward the caliphate: the Samanids sought caliphal confirmation as a source of legitimacy, the Ismaʿili Qarmatians denied its legitimacy, and the Hamdanids, Baridis, and others sought to control rather than abolish it.

Out of this setting emerged the opportunity that the Buyids exploited. The collapse of caliphal revenue meant that the central government could no longer pay a large army, and a government that cannot pay its army is a government that soldiers will manage for their own purposes. The weakness of provincial governors appointed by Baghdad meant that talented commanders who could raise troops locally had room to seize cities such as Shiraz and Isfahan. And the abundance of mobile military labor, drawn from the Caspian highlands, the Kurdish mountains, the Turkic steppe frontier, and the Arab tribal zones, meant that any commander with the means to pay could assemble a fighting force. This was not a story of inevitable decline from a golden age, nor of a caliphate destined to fall to military usurpers. Contemporaries did not know the outcome. Some, including viziers and jurists, hoped that a stronger commander could restore the old system, which is why they made repeated attempts to install a supreme commander. Only in retrospect does the sequence appear to lead toward the Buyid solution.

Section 36.2

Daylam and Gilan: Mountain Societies, Military Traditions, and Shiʿi Influence

The Buyids came from Daylam, a rugged highland region on the southwestern and southern edges of the Caspian Sea, and a proper understanding of their rule must begin with the society that produced them. Daylam, as the term was used by Arabic and Persian writers of the tenth century, designated the mountainous zone of the Alborz range in the interior of what is now the Iranian province of Gilan and adjacent districts, with its steep valleys, forested slopes, and terraced settlements. To its north, between the mountains and the Caspian shore, lay the lowland plain of Gilan proper, inhabited by the Gil or Gilites, a related but distinct population. Chapter 1 introduced the Caspian littoral as an ecologically distinctive zone separated from the plateau interior by the Alborz, and this separation helps explain why the region retained autonomous traditions that Persian and Islamic empires only partly penetrated.

Daylamite society, so far as it can be reconstructed from the Arabic and Persian sources, was organized around local chiefs, kinship groups, and highland communities that practiced mixed agriculture, herding, and, in some districts, forest products. The population spoke northwestern Iranian dialects, ancestors of the modern Gilaki and Daylami-related languages of the region, which were mutually unintelligible with the Persian of Fars to varying degrees and different again from the languages of the Arab and Turkic soldiers with whom the Daylamites would serve. Because the evidence for the region is overwhelmingly external, produced by Muslim geographers and chroniclers from outside Daylam, it tells us much more about how Daylamites appeared as soldiers than about how they lived at home. The tenth-century geographers Ibn Hawqal and al-Muqaddasi comment on the region but are not extended ethnographers, and later chroniclers like Ibn al-Athir, writing in the thirteenth century, project later impressions backward.

The military reputation of Daylamites predates the Buyids by centuries. Greek authors of the sixth century describe infantry from the Caspian highlands, usually called Dolomites, serving in the armies of Sasanian kings, and the Arabic sources from the early Islamic period attest Daylamite soldiers in the service of various rulers. By the ninth and tenth centuries, Daylamites served as foot soldiers, armed with javelins or short spears, swords, and shields, valued for their endurance in rough terrain and their relatively low cost. Their strength was infantry combat in broken country, and their weakness, in the estimation of contemporary commanders, was lack of cavalry, which the Turkish soldiers of the steppe supplied. Daylamite warriors thus formed a mercenary labor pool whose members sought service with the various rulers of the Caspian region, the Jibal, and beyond, and a successful commander of Daylamites could rise to command an army large enough to rival regional governors. The Buyid brothers followed exactly this career path.

The religious environment of Daylam was unusual, and the Buyids' later religious attitudes cannot be understood without it. Islam reached the Caspian highlands later and less completely than it reached the plains of Iraq and the plateau. The Zaydi branch of Shiʿism, which holds that the imamate belongs to any properly qualified descendant of ʿAli and Fatima who rises in arms against unjust rule, offered a framework that appealed to local leaders and proved adaptable to the Caspian environment. In 864 a descendant of ʿAli, al-Hasan ibn Zayd, established a Zaydi state in Tabaristan, the region east of Daylam, and some decades later a more famous Zaydi leader, al-Hasan ibn ʿAli al-Utrush, known as al-Nasir al-Utrush, worked among the Daylamites around the turn of the tenth century, according to the Zaydi and general sources, converting many to Islam of the Zaydi variety. His successors established a Zaydi imamate that persisted in the Caspian highlands for centuries. The conversion was not total or uniform, and Muslim writers continued to describe some Daylamites as holding non-Islamic or only nominally Islamic beliefs well into the tenth century.

This religious environment bears on the Buyids in a way that must be stated carefully. The Buyid brothers were Shiʿi in affiliation, as the sources agree, and their origins in a region with Zaydi missionary activity suggest the likely environment of their upbringing. But the sources do not tell us which form of Shiʿism the family held initially, how deeply they understood doctrinal differences, or whether Zaydi sympathies preceded the Imami, or Twelver, orientation that their patronage in Iraq eventually supported. What can be said is that the family brought with them an affiliation with the family of ʿAli that was distinct from the Sunni Abbasid caliphate they would soon dominate, and that this affiliation was real but not doctrinally fixed. Section 36.24 returns to this problem.

Students should draw three cautions from this section. First, "Daylamite" was a regional and military identity, not a synonym for "Persian"; it overlapped with the broader Iranian-speaking world without being identical to it, and Daylamites and the Persians of Fars regarded one another as distinct. Second, Daylamite origin did not imply a single Shiʿi doctrine or a single political program, and the Buyids themselves changed their religious policies over time. Third, the Daylamites were a historical agent in their own right, with their own military culture, their own chiefs, and their own interests, not merely a reservoir of fighters for ambitious outsiders. When Daylamite soldiers later rioted over pay or defended their privileges against Turkish rivals, they were acting as a social group with a distinctive claim, which the Buyid rulers had to manage.

Section 36.3

The Sons of Buya and Their Rise under Mardavij

The three sons of Abu Shuja Buya, ʿAli, Hasan, and Ahmad, are the founders of the dynasty, and the sources about their early lives are a mixture of plausible detail and the heroic anecdotes that tend to accumulate around successful founders. Buya himself was a Daylamite of the district of Lahijan or the neighboring highlands, according to the traditional accounts, though the geographic details vary. Some later accounts describe him as a fisherman who earned his living in modest circumstances, an anecdote that fits a familiar narrative of a humble family rising to rule. Other accounts, probably produced after the family's success, claim a more exalted lineage, tracing Buya's ancestry to the Sasanian king Bahram Gur. Historians generally treat the Sasanian genealogy as a later construction designed to support the dynasty's royal claims, though they acknowledge that the poverty story may also be a literary trope. What is secure is that the brothers entered the world of Daylamite military service in the early tenth century and rose through it.

The earliest phase of their careers is attested chiefly by Ibn Miskawayh and later by Ibn al-Athir, and the accounts differ in detail. The brothers appear first in the service of Makan ibn Kaki, a Daylamite commander who held Gurgan and Tabaristan on behalf of the Samanids and later in his own interests. When Makan was defeated by the Daylamite adventurer Asfar ibn Shiruya, the brothers' fortunes shifted, and they passed into the service of Mardavij ibn Ziyar, a Gilite commander who had begun as a subordinate of Asfar and then killed or displaced him and constructed an independent state. This sequence of changing masters is itself informative. It shows that the brothers moved within an unstable military market in which loyalties were purchased and renegotiated, and in which a competent officer could expect to be hired by whoever was strongest at a given moment.

Mardavij ibn Ziyar is the pivotal figure in the early rise of the Buyids, and his career deserves consideration on its own terms. Between about 928 and his death in 935, Mardavij built an extensive territory including Gurgan, Tabaristan, Rayy, Hamadan, Qazvin, and, for a time, Isfahan and parts of Khuzestan. His army was composed of Daylamite and Gilite infantry and Turkish cavalry, and the arrangement of paying and managing these forces strained his government. Mardavij, according to the chroniclers, harbored ambitions that went beyond ordinary governorship, and was said to desire the restoration of a Persian empire on the Sasanian model, with a gilded throne, a crown, and a ceremonial court. Whether these reports reflect actual policy or are literary embellishment is uncertain, since the accounts were written after his death by authors for whom a Persian monarchical program was a vivid contrast to the caliphal order. But they probably contain a kernel of truth, and they show that Iranian royal symbolism was already available in the political culture of the Caspian military commanders before the Buyids employed it.

ʿAli, the eldest brother, rose first. According to the accounts, Mardavij assigned him to hold Karaj, a district near Hamadan, where he cultivated the loyalty of troops and the local population. He subsequently took part in Mardavij's campaign against the caliphal forces at Isfahan and was installed in that region. The relationship soured as Mardavij perceived his subordinate's growing independence, and the sources report that Mardavij moved to take direct control of Isfahan, leading ʿAli to withdraw southward toward Arrajan and the borders of Fars. In this movement, generally dated about 933 to 934, ʿAli converted the position of a commander subordinate to a stronger ruler into the leadership of an independent army. He used the resources he had acquired, including money from the Karaj district that he distributed to soldiers, to induce other commanders and their troops to follow him.

Hasan and Ahmad followed their own trajectories. Hasan, often called by his later honorific Rukn al-Dawla, appears to have been in service to Mardavij in these years, and the sources do not agree on whether he was a trusted commander or something closer to a hostage guaranteeing his brother's loyalty. After Mardavij's death in 935, Hasan established himself in the Jibal. Ahmad, the youngest, served alongside ʿAli and began to lead independent operations, first to the east and southeast toward Kirman and then westward toward Khuzestan. The three brothers thus came to hold three distinct military commands out of a single process of departure from Mardavij's state.

The death of Mardavij in 935 is significant not merely because it removed an obstacle but because of how it occurred. The sources report that he was killed by his own Turkish soldiers, who resented his severity and favoritism toward Daylamites, in a bath at Isfahan during preparations for a festival. The episode illustrates the central structural problem of the military states of this period: the very soldiers who made a commander powerful could kill him, and the competition between Daylamite infantry and Turkish cavalry within a single army was a recurring source of instability. It also left a vacuum in the Jibal that Buyid commanders and Ziyarid and Samanid rivals rushed to fill. The Buyids were not the only plausible heirs of Mardavij's state, and the outcome was by no means certain in 935. That they succeeded where others failed is a problem that the following sections examine, and one answer lies in their ability to manage not only soldiers but revenue.

Section 36.4

The Establishment of Buyid Power in Fars, Jibal, and Khuzestan, 934–945

The decade between 934 and 945 transformed three ambitious commanders into rulers of three regional states, and the sequence of campaigns that produced this result must be reconstructed from fragmentary and sometimes contradictory chronicles. The outline is nonetheless clear. ʿAli took Fars, Hasan consolidated the Jibal in a long and contested struggle, and Ahmad pushed toward Kirman and Khuzestan before turning toward Iraq. Each brother's establishment of a base reflected particular opportunities and constraints.

Fars was the most important prize and the first achieved. In 934, ʿAli advanced from the borders of the province against forces loyal to the caliphal governor, Yaqut, whose position was weakened by the collapse of the central government's authority. After a series of engagements, ʿAli entered Shiraz in 934, and the city became the seat of the first Buyid state. Fars was a rich province. Its agricultural base included the irrigated plains around Shiraz and Istakhr, its commercial links reached the Persian Gulf ports, and it had been a center of administration since the Sasanian and Saffarid periods. The geographer al-Istakhri, who wrote in the same century, treats it as a province of substantial extent and wealth. A commander who controlled Fars could pay a large army, and this revenue transformed ʿAli from a successful soldier into a ruler. His rule rested upon the combination of Daylamite troops whom he had brought with him and the administrative expertise of local secretaries, who knew how to collect the province's taxes.

A significant detail in the accounts is that ʿAli did not simply rely on force. The sources report that he sought recognition from the caliph, sent gifts, and obtained a formal acknowledgment of his governorship. This pattern recurs through the Buyid story and deserves emphasis. A military commander without legitimacy was vulnerable to challenge, while one who held a diploma of investiture from the caliph could claim to be a lawful governor. The Buyids from the beginning combined military force with attention to formal recognition, and they would do so even more consistently when they reached Baghdad. The honorific title ʿImad al-Dawla, "Pillar of the State," became attached to ʿAli, though the precise date and sequence of the family's honorific titles vary in the accounts, a point to which Section 36.6 returns.

In the Jibal, Hasan faced a more fluid situation. The Ziyarid and Samanid forces, the remaining Turkish troops of Mardavij, and local rulers all contested control of Isfahan, Rayy, Hamadan, and the surrounding districts. Hasan's position in the 930s and 940s oscillated. He held Isfahan and lost it, regained it, and contested Rayy over a period of years against Vushmgir, the Ziyarid ruler of Tabaristan and Gurgan, and against Samanid commanders operating from Khurasan. His eventual success was slower than ʿAli's and depended upon the support of his brother, who sent men and money, and upon the exhaustion of his rivals. By the mid-940s, Hasan had a recognizable principality in the central Jibal, and the process by which Rayy became his principal seat extended into the following decade. Rukn al-Dawla, the "Support of the State," as he came to be styled, would rule for about three decades and would become the senior member of the family after ʿAli's death.

Ahmad's operations took him first toward Kirman, in the southeast of the plateau, where in the late 930s he seized territory on ʿAli's behalf but was unable to hold it against local rulers, and then westward toward Khuzestan, the lowland province adjoining Iraq. The Khuzestan of this period was disputed between the Baridi family, who held Basra and Ahwaz and were powerful in southern Iraq, and other claimants. Ahmad's campaigns involved sieges, defeats, withdrawals, and renewed offensives, and in the early 940s he established himself in Khuzestan. This position gave him a platform adjacent to Iraq, from which the collapsing politics of Baghdad were visible and attractive. The importance of Khuzestan for the Buyid rise is difficult to overstate: it was a rich agricultural and commercial region, and it lay one stage from the Abbasid capital.

The reasons the brothers established separate bases rather than a single kingdom deserve explicit treatment, because they shaped everything that followed. First, the geography of the Iranian plateau and Iraq, with large distances, difficult mountain passes, and slow communications, made central administration hard. Second, the brothers' authority rested on personal armies of Daylamite followers, each loyal to its own commander. Third, each region's revenue was needed to pay the troops stationed there, and moving revenue across regions risked mutiny. Fourth, Daylamite family custom, so far as can be reconstructed, favored collective headship of the lineage, with the eldest brother recognized as senior but his brothers exercising substantial autonomy. The result was a confederation held together by kinship and mutual interest, with ʿAli recognized as the senior amir during his lifetime, and with the brothers' obligations to one another expressed in military assistance, shared strategic aims, and formal deference rather than in common administration. This structure proved resilient while the founding generation lived, and it determined the dynasty's pattern for the following century.

Section 36.5 · Division II — The Buyid Takeover of Baghdad

Baghdad before the Buyids: The Amir al-Umara and the Weakening of Caliphal Government

The Buyid seizure of Baghdad was the last act in a sequence of experiments in military guardianship, and the earlier acts shaped both what the Buyids found in the capital and what they chose to do with it. In 936, the caliph al-Radi, whose reign (934 to 940) is often treated as the last in which a caliph retained substantial independent authority, faced a bankrupt treasury, a mutinous army, and a wave of provincial defections. He turned to Muhammad ibn Raʾiq, a commander who had governed Wasit and Basra and who commanded the necessary troops, and created for him a new office. The title was amir al-umara, "commander of commanders," and it gave its holder control of the army, the revenue administration, and most of the machinery of government that had belonged to the vizier. In exchange, the caliph received a promise of regular maintenance, a ceremonial role, and the hope that a strong protector would restore order. The sources agree that the appointment marked a turning point, and later historians have often treated it as the formal beginning of the era in which caliphs reigned but did not govern.

Historical Vocabulary
Amir al-umara

Definition: Arabic for "commander of commanders" or "chief amir." The title designated a military commander placed at the head of the Abbasid government's armed forces and, in practice, much of its civil administration. The office was created for Ibn Raʾiq in 936 by the caliph al-Radi.

Historical context: Earlier caliphs had appointed military commanders to high posts, but the amir al-umara held an authority unprecedented in its breadth, because it subordinated the vizier and the revenue departments to a soldier. Between 936 and 945 a succession of commanders held the post, including Ibn Raʾiq, the Turkish officers Bajkam and Tuzun, and, briefly, a Hamdanid prince. The Buyid ruler Muʿizz al-Dawla received the title in 945 and the arrangement thereafter became the basis for Buyid rule in Iraq, though Buyid rulers also acquired other titles.

Why it matters: The office makes visible the fact that the Abbasid caliph had delegated the military and administrative functions of the state to a subordinate, while retaining the religious and legal functions of the caliphate. It was the institutional form through which the Buyids governed Iraq.

Potential misunderstanding: The title was not hereditary kingship and not a rival caliphate. The amir al-umara held office at the pleasure of the caliph in theory, and in practice at the pleasure of the soldiers who supported him. The Buyid rulers eventually supplemented it with titles that expressed a more stable and dynastic claim.

The experience of the amirs illustrates the instability of the arrangement. Ibn Raʾiq controlled Baghdad from 936 but quickly encountered a problem that each of his successors would also meet. The revenue of Iraq was not sufficient to pay an army large enough to keep order and defeat rivals, because provincial governors had stopped remitting taxes, the Baridis of Basra and Ahwaz controlled some of the richest agricultural zones of the south, and the Sawad itself was suffering from the neglect of canals and the exactions of tax contractors. Ibn Raʾiq was displaced in 938 by Bajkam, a Turkish commander who had formerly served Mardavij and who had a reputation for relative competence and fairness. Bajkam attempted to restore the irrigation works, to discipline his soldiers, and to cultivate the loyalty of the Baghdad population. His death in 941, according to the sources, in a hunting accident or ambush by Kurdish highlanders near Wasit, reopened the question.

The following four years saw a succession of struggles. A Turkish commander named Kurtakin held the post briefly. Ibn Raʾiq returned for a second term, and was killed in 942. A Hamdanid prince from Mosul, the future Nasir al-Dawla, held the title briefly before being displaced by the Turkish commander Tuzun, who controlled the capital from about 943 until his death in 945. Tuzun is notable for an act that was itself revealing of the changing balance of power. In 944 he deposed the caliph al-Muttaqi, who had tried to escape military control by seeking refuge with the Hamdanids and the Ikhshidid ruler of Egypt, and had him blinded, replacing him with al-Mustakfi. The chroniclers present this as a cruel and unprecedented event, and indeed the blinding of a caliph by his own commander cast a long shadow over the relationship between the military and the caliphate. Yet it also demonstrated what the Buyids would soon exploit: the caliph's person and title were the only available source of caliphal legitimacy, and every military ruler wanted to hold the caliph rather than abolish him.

Weakened central taxation was the underlying cause. By the mid-940s, the caliphal treasury could not pay a standing army, and the army could not collect taxes without an administrative apparatus. The amirs resorted to the expedients that had already become familiar: assigning revenue districts to officers, confiscating the property of officials, and farming taxes. Each deprived the central administration of income and discretion. Meanwhile, security in the capital itself deteriorated. Urban gangs, called ʿayyārūn, were a recurring force in Baghdad politics, sometimes enforcing order in neighborhoods in the absence of effective police and sometimes plundering. The Hanbali jurists and their popular followers in Baghdad had fought repeated street conflicts against practices they regarded as impermissible, and al-Radi had issued an edict against their activities in 935. The population of Baghdad, many of whom were wage laborers, shopkeepers, and artisans, experienced the political crisis as a breakdown of provisioning, a loss of security, and a rise in prices.

This was the situation that confronted Ahmad ibn Buya when he arrived at the gates in 945. By then, Baghdad's rulers had few resources and fewer friends, and the circle of those who held authority in the capital, the caliph's court, the Turkish commanders, the secretaries and viziers, and the leaders of neighborhoods, were each looking for protection against the collapse. It would be too simple to say that the capital invited the Buyids. It would be accurate to say that the capital had exhausted the alternatives.

Section 36.6

Ahmad ibn Buya Enters Baghdad, December 945

Ahmad ibn Buya's advance toward Baghdad built upon the base he had established in Khuzestan and upon the political vacuum in the capital. After the death of Tuzun in 945, the Turkish commanders in Baghdad were divided. The office of amir al-umara passed to Ibn Shirzad, Tuzun's former secretary, who held the title briefly, and the disagreements among Turkish and other soldiers over pay and leadership left the city without a unified defense. The caliph al-Mustakfi, who had been installed by Tuzun in 944, had little independent standing. According to Ibn Miskawayh, the caliph and some of his advisers took note of Ahmad's strength, and the sequence of events suggests that the court, unable to defend itself, regarded accommodation as less dangerous than resistance.

Ahmad moved from Khuzestan toward Wasit and then up the Tigris with an army of Daylamite troops and others. The Turkish soldiers who had controlled Baghdad withdrew or dispersed as he approached, and the caliph's government opened the city. On 19 December 945, Ahmad entered Baghdad. The sources report that the entry was peaceful: there was no battle for the capital, and the caliph received him. This is an important feature of the event, because it helps explain what followed. The Buyid takeover was not a conquest of a resisting city but the replacement of one unstable military protector by a stronger one, accompanied by the apparent acquiescence of the caliph and the court.

At the center of the reception was the conferral of status. Al-Mustakfi gave Ahmad the title amir al-umara and a robe of honor, and conferred upon him the honorific title Muʿizz al-Dawla, "Fortifier of the State." He also granted honorifics to Ahmad's brothers, the senior of whom, ʿAli, was styled ʿImad al-Dawla, "Pillar of the State," and Hasan Rukn al-Dawla, "Support of the State." The sources are not fully consistent about the dates at which each brother received his honorific and about which caliph conferred which title, and it is probable that some distinctions were extended earlier to the elder brothers, with the family's honorifics consolidated in the settlement of 945 and 946. What is clear is that all three brothers came to bear laqabs, honorific titles ending in al-Dawla, which became the characteristic form of address for Buyid rulers and were adopted by many contemporary and later dynasties.

The practice of honorific titles deserves some explanation. Arabic and Islamic political culture had long used honorific titles, called laqabs, to mark status and role. The al-Dawla titles, which began to appear in the early tenth century with the Hamdanids, contained the Arabic word dawla, meaning something like "the dynasty," "the regime," or "the state," and were intended to express a bond between the title holder and the caliphal government that he served. That the Buyid brothers should receive such titles from the caliph served multiple purposes. For the caliphate, it affirmed that the amirs were its agents. For the Buyids, it supplied a recognized official status and a basis for demanding the obedience of officials and soldiers. For both, it embedded the new arrangement in an established political language. Later in the dynasty's history, Buyid rulers would accumulate additional and grander titles, including ones that the caliphs awarded reluctantly, a trajectory examined in Section 36.18.

The first weeks of Buyid rule in Baghdad reveal the central practical problems that Muʿizz al-Dawla faced. He needed to pay his troops, who numbered in the thousands, and he lacked an obvious source of revenue. He needed to prevent rival soldiers from rising against him, which required controlling both his own Daylamite followers and the remnants of Turkish forces in the city. He needed a government capable of collecting taxes, which meant retaining the secretaries and bureaucrats of the old administration. And he needed to settle his relationship to the caliph, a settlement that quickly ran into difficulty. The relationship between these needs and the sequence of events in January 946 is the subject of the next section.

One further point of interpretation. The entry into Baghdad has sometimes been described as a "Persian conquest" of the Arab capital, an interpretation that reads the event through a modern lens of ethnic and national conflict. The sources do not support this. The troops who entered the capital were Daylamites, but they were serving a family that sought recognition from the caliph and that pursued fiscal and political interests, not a program of ethnic revenge. The chroniclers of the period, most of whom were Arabic-speaking and many of whom were of Iranian or Arab background, describe the event in terms of military and political power. The language of identity, which later writers would layer upon it, is not the language of the sources that report it.

Section 36.7

The Caliph al-Mustakfi and the Political Settlement of 946

Within about six weeks of his entry into Baghdad, Muʿizz al-Dawla had deposed the caliph who had received him. The events of January 946 illustrate both the power of the Buyid ruler and the limits of what the narrative sources allow historians to know, and this section approaches them with that tension in mind.

The standard account, which derives from Ibn Miskawayh and was elaborated by later chroniclers such as Ibn al-Athir, runs roughly as follows. In the second Islamic month of Jumada II in the year 334, corresponding to late January 946, Muʿizz al-Dawla attended an audience at the caliphal palace. Two of his Daylamite followers approached the caliph's throne, ostensibly to kiss his hand in the customary gesture of respect. They seized al-Mustakfi, pulled him from his seat, and dragged him from the hall. The caliph was subsequently deprived of his sight, and held in custody, where he died in 949. His palace was plundered. Muʿizz al-Dawla then installed a new caliph, al-Fadl ibn al-Muqtadir, who took the regnal title al-Mutiʿ lillah, "He who obeys God." Al-Mutiʿ would reign until 974.

Historians should read this account critically. It is a vivid and formulaic scene, the kind of episode that narrators of court politics preferred, and the same pattern of the throne-room seizure recurs in other accounts of deposition. The sources differ on the reasons. Some report that the caliph was suspected of conspiring with the Hamdanid rulers of Mosul or with other rivals against the new master of Baghdad, and that Muʿizz al-Dawla acted preemptively. Others suggest that the caliph's alleged plot was an excuse, and that the real motive was the Buyid commander's desire to install a caliph who would be more pliable, or to make clear to all parties where power now lay. The chronicles were written by men who either served the Buyids or lived in their world, and they tended to present Buyid actions in a manner that justified or rationalized them. At the same time, authors who disliked Buyid Shiʿism, including many later Sunni writers, were prepared to emphasize brutality and sacrilege. The details of the audience cannot be independently verified, and the best course is to treat the broad outline as probable and the staging as possibly shaped by narrative convention.

Primary Source Window

Ibn Miskawayh, Tajārib al-umam ("Experiences of the Nations"), on the Buyid Takeover of Baghdad

Creator: Abu ʿAli Ahmad ibn Muhammad ibn Miskawayh (c. 932–1030), a secretary, treasurer, and scholar born in Rayy · Date: composed in the later tenth century, covering events to about 980 · Type: Arabic universal chronicle, with a contemporary and participatory final section

Context: Ibn Miskawayh served the vizier Ibn al-ʿAmid at Rayy as librarian and secretary, and later entered the service of ʿAḍud al-Dawla, for whom he worked in Shiraz and Baghdad. His chronicle begins with the Flood and proceeds to the author's own time. For the years before about 940 he relies on earlier historians, but for the later period his account draws increasingly on his own experience and on the testimony of colleagues in the Buyid administration. The account of the transition from the late Abbasid amirs to the Buyids falls between these registers.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): Ibn Miskawayh narrates the years before 945 as a story of disintegration: viziers rise and fall, commanders compete for the post of amir al-umara, the army demands pay that the treasury cannot supply, and the caliph is helpless. Muʿizz al-Dawla appears in this account as a soldier who arrives in Baghdad at a moment of collapse, receives recognition, and then acts decisively, including against the caliph al-Mustakfi, whom the narrator presents as having been implicated in secret dealings with rivals. The deposition is reported in a scene at court in which the caliph is seized by soldiers, and the narrative moves directly to the installation of al-Mutiʿ and the Buyid ruler's arrangements for governing Iraq.

Analysis: The value of the source lies less in any single episode than in its angle of vision. Ibn Miskawayh wrote as a member of the secretarial class, for whom good government meant the orderly collection and expenditure of revenue and the competent management of the army. His narrative therefore treats the Abbasid collapse as a failure of administration, and the Buyid takeover as, among other things, a restoration of fiscal and military control. This is a perspective rather than a neutral description, but it is also the perspective of a participant who understood how the system worked.

Limitations: The work was written under Buyid patronage and reflects a view favorable to the court it served. Its account of the deposition scene is not corroborated by independent contemporary witnesses. It is centered on Baghdad and on the administrative elite, and it says little about the soldiers who carried out the seizure, the neighborhoods of the city, or rural cultivators. Students should also remember that this summary is a paraphrase, and that the wording should be checked against a published translation or edition before it is quoted.

Perspective: A Persian-born Arabic-writing secretary and moral philosopher, committed to administrative order. Not represented: the caliph and his household, Turkish and Daylamite soldiers, Sunni jurists and the crowds of Baghdad.

Whatever the precise circumstances, the political settlement that followed is clear in its essentials. Muʿizz al-Dawla retained the caliphate. He replaced the caliph with a member of the Abbasid family, al-Mutiʿ, a man who held no independent power base and who owed his office to the amir. He maintained the formal apparatus of the caliphal court, including the ceremonies, the names in the sermon and on the coinage, and the household of the caliph, but placed the caliph's income under the control of Buyid officials. He also took over the administration of revenue: the vizier of the caliph was replaced by a vizier of the amir, and from 946 this post was occupied by Abu Muhammad al-Hasan al-Muhallabi, a secretary who held it until 963 and who is credited by the sources with substantial administrative skill. The caliph was reduced to dependence on an allowance and expected to endorse the policies of the Buyid government. His religious function, that of leading the community and legitimating the offices that exercised the state's powers, remained in place.

The later chroniclers tell a story about the decision to preserve the Abbasid institution, and it is a story worth examining for what it reveals about how the tradition explained itself. According to Ibn al-Athir and others, Muʿizz al-Dawla considered, at least for a time, deposing the Abbasid and installing a descendant of ʿAli in the caliphate, consistent with the Shiʿi allegiance of his family. His advisers are said to have warned that this would be dangerous: the Daylamite soldiers, believing that a descendant of ʿAli was the true imam, might obey the new caliph rather than their commander and, if ordered, kill him. An Abbasid, by contrast, could be controlled because the soldiers did not regard him as divinely guided. Whether this story reproduces an actual conversation is unknowable, and it was written down centuries later. But it expresses an intelligent historical insight, that the Buyids' own religious position made a Shiʿi caliph less rather than more manageable, and it has often been repeated in modern accounts.

Cause and Effect

Why the Buyids Preserved the Abbasid Caliphate

Long-term causes

The caliphate had for two centuries served as the recognized source of legal and religious legitimacy in the Islamic world. Most of the population of Iraq and many Iranian communities were Sunni and looked to the Abbasids. Courts, judges, and administrative practice were built upon caliphal authority, and no alternative office had comparable standing.

Short-term causes

The Buyids' own religious position complicated the alternatives. A descendant of ʿAli installed as caliph would have been difficult to control, according to later tradition. The Turkish soldiers and the Sunni population of Baghdad would likely have resisted. Rival powers, including the Samanids, Hamdanids, and others, still recognized the Abbasid.

Immediate trigger

The Buyid entry into Baghdad in December 945 and the crisis of January 946 forced the question of what to do with the caliph. The decision to depose al-Mustakfi but to retain the institution and install another Abbasid answered it.

Effects

The caliphate survived as an institution of legitimacy, while real power rested with the Buyid amirs. The Buyids acquired a lawful standing and a vocabulary of authority. Subsequent military rulers, including the Seljuks, followed the same pattern, which became a feature of Islamic politics until 1258.

Historical complexity: The preservation of the caliphate was neither an act of simple cynicism nor an expression of religious loyalty. It reflected an assessment, shared by the Buyids and their advisers, that the caliphate provided something that armies and revenue could not: a public recognition of lawful authority. Explanations that rest on a single cause, whether Buyid weakness, Buyid moderation, or caliphal prestige, do not account for the pattern.

Section 36.8

Who Ruled the Caliphate? Military Sovereignty and Religious Legitimacy

The arrangement established in 946 divided the functions of the Abbasid state between two figures. This section analyzes the division, because it is the conceptual heart of the chapter and because modern historians have described it in conflicting terms. A useful way to begin is to ask, for each function of government, who in practice exercised it.

The Buyid ruler commanded the armies. He decided who was recruited, who commanded, and who was paid, and he led campaigns in person or through his relatives and commanders. He controlled the revenue: the departments that assessed and collected taxes answered to his vizier, and the surplus went into his treasury rather than the caliph's. He appointed and dismissed officials, including the vizier, the heads of the dīvāns, and the principal governors, though he often did so with the formal participation of the caliph. He maintained public order in Baghdad through his troops and, in practice, he determined the fate of those who challenged him, including viziers who were arrested, tortured, and had their property confiscated. These powers were the substance of government.

The caliph's role was different but not empty. He held the religious office of imam of the community, leading in theory the Friday prayer, and the name of the ruler for whom the sermon was delivered conferred recognition. He issued diplomas of investiture that conferred lawful governance on amirs and governors, and he conferred honorific titles and robes of honor that publicly signaled a ruler's standing. He was the formal source of legal appointments, including that of the chief judge, and judges derived their authority to adjudicate from the caliphate. He received embassies, presided over a ceremonial court, and was addressed as the commander of the faithful. For rulers beyond the Buyid domains, from the Samanids to the Ghaznavids, recognition by the caliph was valuable, and the fact that the Buyids controlled the caliph's person gave them a form of leverage over those rulers, a point to which the chapter returns in discussing Buyid relations with rivals.

Historical Vocabulary
Khutba and Sikka

Definition: The khutba is the sermon delivered at Friday congregational prayer, in which a preacher invokes blessings upon the ruler recognized by the community. The sikka is the coinage, struck with inscriptions that name the issuing authority. Together they were the two principal public signs of sovereignty in the medieval Islamic world.

Historical context: Inclusion of a ruler's name in the Friday sermon was a public declaration of allegiance, and its removal or substitution signaled rebellion or a change of regime. Likewise, the names and titles on coins indicated who was acknowledged as sovereign and, in the case of dynasties acting under caliphal authority, their hierarchy. Under the Buyids, the caliph's name continued to appear on the coinage and in the sermon, accompanied by the names and honorific titles of the Buyid amirs.

Why it matters: These formal signs let historians read political relationships even when narrative sources are silent or biased. Their continued use of the caliph's name indicates that the Buyids recognized the caliphate as the source of lawful authority, even as they governed in practice.

Potential misunderstanding: The presence of the caliph's name on coinage and in the sermon is not evidence of the caliph's practical control. Equally, the addition of the Buyid ruler's name is not evidence of rebellion against the caliphate. These practices signaled a particular division of authority, not its absence.

The distribution of functions was unequal. The Buyid ruler could depose a caliph, and did. The caliph could not remove a Buyid ruler, and the sources show that caliphs who tried to assert independence, such as al-Taʾiʿ and al-Qadir in their dealings with Buyid rulers at various points, did so cautiously and with limited success. When the Buyid ruler wished the caliph to confer a title or investiture, the caliph's compliance was expected, though the sources record occasions on which caliphs hesitated, as in negotiations over the grant of grander titles to ʿAḍud al-Dawla and his successors. The relationship also involved reciprocal needs. The Buyids wished to be recognized as lawful rulers by the population of Baghdad and by foreign powers; the caliphs wished to preserve their office, their dignity, and their physical safety. The arrangement worked because each side needed what the other possessed.

The institutional complexity of this arrangement is easy to miss if one thinks of it in terms of a simple contest for sovereignty. There were not two rival governments, a caliphal and a Buyid one. There was a single government with two components: the Buyid ruler's court, his troops, his viziers, and his treasury, on one side, and the caliphal court with its ceremonial and religious functions on the other, with the caliph's household occupying the palace complex in Baghdad while the Buyid ruler established his own residence nearby. Officials moved between them, and many of the secretaries and judges who served the Buyid regime had served the caliphate before. Their ability to move between these roles is a reminder that the Abbasid administrative apparatus continued to function. The Buyid ruler did not replace it. He placed himself at the head of it, and the caliph above it.

Historical Debate

Did the Buyids Rule the Caliphate, or Govern in the Caliph's Name?

The question: Historians have long disagreed about how to describe the relationship between the Buyid rulers and the Abbasid caliph. Was the caliph effectively a prisoner and figurehead, with the Buyids as the true sovereigns of Iraq? Or did the Buyids govern within a recognized constitutional order in which the caliph's authority played a real role?

Interpretation A — The Buyids ruled the caliphate: Older historiography, influenced by the medieval Sunni chronicle tradition and by European writing that emphasized caliphal decline, depicted the Buyid period as one in which Daylamite Shiʿi soldiers held a captive caliph and usurped his authority. On this view, the caliph's ceremonial role was a facade covering real military sovereignty.
Interpretation B — The Buyids governed in the caliph's name: Other scholars have emphasized that the Buyids operated within the legal and ideological framework of the caliphate, using caliphal investiture, titles, and appointments, and that contemporaries understood authority as flowing through that framework. In this reading, the caliph was a source of legitimacy that Buyid rulers actively cultivated, and the arrangement was an institutionalized division of labor.
Interpretation C — Personal loyalty and negotiated authority: A third approach, associated with studies of Buyid society that emphasize ties of personal loyalty, patronage, and reciprocal obligation, treats formal sovereignty as less important than the networks through which authority was actually exercised. Seen this way, neither "rule" nor "govern in the name of" captures a political order in which authority was layered and constantly negotiated among rulers, officials, soldiers, jurists, and urban leaders.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on the same sources: the chronicle narratives of depositions and ceremonies, the formal use of titles on coins and inscriptions, accounts of investiture diplomas and robes of honor, and descriptions of the caliph's income and household. Interpretation A relies most heavily on the episodes of coercion, such as the blinding of caliphs and the seizure of the caliph's resources. Interpretation B emphasizes the continued use of caliphal titles, the survival of the caliphal court, and the efforts Buyid rulers made to obtain formal recognition. Interpretation C draws on prosopographical and anecdotal evidence about how officials, soldiers, and notables actually related to one another.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns primarily interpretation and terminology, not the basic facts. Few scholars dispute that the Buyids controlled the army and the revenue, or that the caliph retained a recognized religious and ceremonial function. The question is how to weigh these facts: whether to treat practical control as the essence of sovereignty, or to treat the legitimating function as constitutive of lawful rule.

Why it matters: The answer changes how historians understand the later history of the Islamic world. If the Buyid arrangement was a mere usurpation, then the caliphate's later survival under Seljuk, and still later, protection is a story of persistent decline. If it was a durable institutional compromise, then it was a significant political innovation, a way of combining military power with religious legitimacy that shaped the sultanate and other later forms.

Section 36.9

Muʿizz al-Dawla and the Government of Iraq, 945–967

For the two decades that followed his entry into Baghdad, Muʿizz al-Dawla governed Iraq, and his reign shows how the Buyid system worked in practice and how many of its problems arose almost immediately. He lacked a ready-made administration, a secure revenue base, or a disciplined army. What he had was a core of Daylamite followers, a senior vizier in al-Muhallabi, an inherited bureaucracy, and support from his brothers.

The most pressing problem was the army. Muʿizz al-Dawla's Daylamite infantry formed the core of his power, but he also needed cavalry, and Baghdad contained Turkish units and others of uncertain loyalty. He retained a substantial Turkish contingent, and he had to manage rivalries between Daylamites and Turks that periodically burst into open violence. The sources describe disputes over pay, over the allocation of billets in the city, and over precedence. Pay was the underlying difficulty. The revenue of Iraq did not suffice for a large army paid in cash, and Muʿizz al-Dawla resorted to assigning to his officers the rights to collect taxes from designated districts, the arrangement known as iqtaʿ, which Section 36.20 examines. The expedient reduced the pressure on the treasury but also placed tax collection in the hands of soldiers, who were more interested in extracting revenue quickly than in sustaining the irrigation system on which long-term productivity depended.

Public order was a second problem. Baghdad was a city of several hundred thousand people, whose exact number is uncertain and probably declined over the century, and it was divided into neighborhoods with different religious and occupational identities. The Buyid regime had to contend with the ʿayyārūn, with Hanbali preachers who agitated against practices they considered unlawful, and with the competing neighborhoods of the city. Section 36.23 will examine this urban society more closely. Here it is enough to note that Muʿizz al-Dawla's government attempted to impose order through the police and the army but did not consistently succeed, and that the introduction of public Shiʿi observances in 963, discussed in Section 36.25, created a new source of confrontation.

The reach of Buyid authority beyond the capital was also limited. Basra and Wasit, the great southern cities, had been under the control of the Baridi family, whose power Muʿizz al-Dawla eventually broke, reducing Basra in the later 940s according to the chronicles, though the sequence is not securely dated and Baridi remnants continued to trouble the region. In the north, the Hamdanids of Mosul and Aleppo remained a powerful rival. Muʿizz al-Dawla campaigned against them in 948 and again in the later 950s, taking Mosul at least once and compelling the Hamdanid ruler Nasir al-Dawla to pay tribute and acknowledge Buyid suzerainty, but he did not hold the north permanently. In the south and east, the Qarmatians of Bahrayn threatened the Buyid frontier, and the Buyids at times negotiated with them rather than fighting. The Buyid ruler in Baghdad was thus the strongest military power in Iraq, but he was not unchallenged, and his authority diminished with distance from the capital.

Muʿizz al-Dawla's relations with the court at Baghdad followed the pattern set in 946. He kept al-Mutiʿ on the throne, provided for the caliph's household within limits, and regularly consulted formal propriety. He built a large palace on the east bank of the Tigris, a project whose expense later chroniclers criticized, and which symbolized the establishment of a separate Buyid court alongside the caliph's. In relations with his brothers, he accepted the seniority of ʿImad al-Dawla until the latter's death in 949 and the headship of Rukn al-Dawla thereafter, and he cooperated with Rukn al-Dawla against external enemies. The family's formal unity, expressed through names on coinage and in the sermon, coexisted with real independence of decision.

Evidence for Muʿizz al-Dawla's rule comes primarily from Ibn Miskawayh, who was not yet in Baghdad during these years but who drew on earlier sources and colleagues, and from the later Baghdad historians. The picture that emerges is of a vigorous but financially constrained ruler, adept at military campaign and diplomacy, attentive to religious sensibilities in some respects and bold in others. His government is often evaluated unfavorably in terms of the long-term health of Iraq's agriculture, since his policies of military assignment and revenue expedients are blamed in the sources for contributing to rural decay. But the same sources record that he reached accommodation with the Baghdad population, maintained a functioning administration, and preserved the caliphal institution at a moment when many expected its dissolution.

Muʿizz al-Dawla died in April 967, in the Islamic month of Rabiʿ II of 356, and was buried in Baghdad. He had designated his son Bakhtiyar, later styled ʿIzz al-Dawla, as successor. The succession was accepted, but the military and fiscal difficulties of Iraq passed to a young ruler who lacked his father's energy and authority, and the following decade would expose how fragile the Iraqi regime was without a strong hand. Section 36.13 returns to that story after the chapter has examined the structure of the Buyid confederation as a whole.

Section 36.10 · Division III — A Confederation of Buyid Principalities

The Three Principal Buyid Branches: Fars, Jibal, and Iraq

Having followed the Buyid takeover of Baghdad, the chapter now steps back to ask what kind of political entity the Buyids actually formed. The answer is more complicated than the phrase "Buyid dynasty" suggests. For most of the period between 945 and the 1050s the Buyids were not a single state with a single capital and a single treasury but a confederation of related principalities, each ruled by a member of the family, each with its own army and revenue, and each linked to the others by kinship, shared interests, and a framework of recognized seniority. Understanding this structure is essential, because it explains both the geographical reach of the dynasty and its recurring conflicts.

Three principal branches emerged from the founding generation. The first was centered on Fars, with its capital at Shiraz, founded by ʿAli ibn Buya (ʿImad al-Dawla) and later ruled by his nephew ʿAḍud al-Dawla. Kirman, to the southeast, was usually attached to the Fars branch, and Khuzestan, the lowland province between Fars and Iraq, was contested among branches and changed hands repeatedly. The second branch was centered on the Jibal, the western and central Iranian plateau, with principal seats at Rayy, Isfahan, and Hamadan, founded by Hasan ibn Buya (Rukn al-Dawla) and later divided among his sons. The third was centered on Iraq, with its capital at Baghdad, founded by Ahmad ibn Buya (Muʿizz al-Dawla) and ruled by his son Bakhtiyar until ʿAḍud al-Dawla took it. Each branch occupied a different environmental and economic position, a point developed in the comparative study at the end of this division.

The relations among the branches were governed by a recognized, if loosely defined, seniority. During his lifetime, ʿImad al-Dawla was acknowledged as head of the family, and after his death in 949 that position passed to Rukn al-Dawla, who held it until his death in 976. The senior amir's name preceded those of his kinsmen on coinage and in the sermon in the territories of his relatives, and the junior rulers were expected to consult him on major decisions, to send military aid when requested, and to honor his arbitration of disputes. Marriage alliances, in which Buyid princes and princesses married into one another's households and into the families of neighboring dynasties, reinforced the ties. But this seniority was not command. The senior amir could advise, press, and in exceptional cases coerce, as when Rukn al-Dawla compelled his son ʿAḍud al-Dawla to withdraw from Baghdad in 975, but he did not administer his kinsmen's territories.

The use of honorific titles of the al-Dawla type deserves renewed attention here, because the titles served as a language of kinship and rank. Each ruler held a laqab, bestowed by the caliph, which compounded the Arabic word dawla with a word expressing some quality of support or strength: ʿImad ("pillar"), Rukn ("support"), Muʿizz ("fortifier"), ʿAḍud ("arm"), Muʾayyad ("aided"), Fakhr ("glory"), ʿIzz ("might"), Sharaf ("honor"), Samsam ("sword"), Bahaʾ ("splendor"), and Majd ("glory"), among others. The titles distinguished rulers from one another in documents, coinage, and ceremony, and the sequence in which they appeared signaled rank. Over time, titles proliferated and inflated, and the caliphs, who conferred them, sometimes resisted requests for grander ones, a sign of the continuing significance of caliphal recognition. These titles are not decorative details. They show how a family of soldiers wove itself into the vocabulary of Islamic public life.

The confederation's strengths were real. Because each ruler governed his own territory with his own revenues, the dynasty was resilient against local disasters: the loss of Iraq did not mean the loss of Fars, and the weakness of one branch could be offset by the resources of another. Because kinship provided a framework for cooperation, the family could mobilize against outside threats, as when Buyid forces from several branches combined against the Hamdanids or the Samanids. And because the system gave ambitious family members a stake in the dynasty's territories, it reduced the likelihood that a Buyid prince would seek his fortune in the service of a rival state.

Its weaknesses were equally real. Because no mechanism of succession existed beyond family bargaining and force, the death of a ruler regularly produced disputes among sons, brothers, and cousins, and the losers often sought help from outside powers. Because each branch's army depended on its own revenues, branches competed for resources such as Khuzestan and Kirman, and rulers could not easily lend soldiers to one another for long. And because the dynasty had no shared administrative apparatus, the quality of government varied sharply from region to region. The dynasty's pattern of rule thus involved regular cooperation and regular conflict, with the same persons often playing both roles. The historian's task is to avoid reading the dynasty as either an empire or as chaos, and to treat the kinship confederation as a coherent but fragile political form.

Section 36.11

Rukn al-Dawla and the Consolidation of Western Iran

Hasan ibn Buya, called Rukn al-Dawla, ruled in the Jibal for roughly four decades and presided over the family as its senior member from 949 to 976. His reign was less glamorous than his brother Muʿizz al-Dawla's seizure of Baghdad or his nephew ʿAḍud al-Dawla's later triumphs, but in many respects it was the foundation of Buyid endurance. He held the central western plateau, the zone through which the main overland route between Iraq and Khurasan passed, and he did so against persistent pressure from the Samanids to the northeast, the Ziyarids to the north, and numerous local rulers.

The consolidation of Rukn al-Dawla's territory was slow. By the early 940s he held Isfahan and its surroundings, and over the following years he extended his authority to Hamadan and the districts of the central Jibal. Rayy, the great city on the edge of the Alborz, was the principal prize. It lay on the road to Khurasan, commanded rich agricultural land, and was a major commercial and intellectual center. Samanid forces, advancing from Khurasan, repeatedly took it, and the Ziyarid ruler Vushmgir of Tabaristan and Gurgan also contested it. Rukn al-Dawla recovered it several times and finally established himself there as his principal residence, though the details and dates of this process, which extended through the 940s and into the 950s, are not securely fixed in the sources. His ability to hold Rayy rested on a combination of military resilience, the aid of his brothers, and a series of negotiated settlements with the Samanids, who accepted his authority in exchange for a recognition of Samanid suzerainty, symbolized by tribute, that Rukn al-Dawla was willing to pay.

This pragmatic diplomacy was characteristic. Rukn al-Dawla did not consistently seek conquest. He appears in the sources as a ruler who preferred to settle disputes by agreement, to make marriages that connected his family to neighboring dynasties, and to accept formal subordination when it cost little. His relations with the Ziyarids illustrate this. Vushmgir's son Qabus, who succeeded to the Ziyarid state in 977, was a cultivated ruler, a patron of literature, and a notable Arabic prose writer, and his career intersected with the Buyids at several points, including a period when ʿAḍud al-Dawla and Fakhr al-Dawla drove him out of his territories in 981. His eventual return to power in 997 or 998 shows how local dynastic restorations could recur even after apparent defeats. For Rukn al-Dawla, neighbors were to be managed, not necessarily eliminated.

The administrative culture of Rukn al-Dawla's court is better documented than its military history. His vizier, Abu al-Fadl Ibn al-ʿAmid, was one of the great Arabic secretaries of the age, celebrated for his prose style, his learning, and his political skills, and called by later admirers a second al-Jahiz, after the great ninth-century Arabic stylist. Ibn al-ʿAmid's career, which ended with his death in 970, shows how Iranian secretaries could manage a regional government in Arabic, negotiating with the Samanids, administering revenue, and patronizing scholars. Ibn Miskawayh, the historian, served as his librarian and secretary, and thus the chronicle examined in Section 36.7 emerged from this circle. Ibn al-ʿAmid's son, Abu al-Fath, succeeded him as vizier for a short time. These secretaries were not merely functionaries but participants in a literary culture, a subject examined in Section 36.28.

Rukn al-Dawla's later years were occupied with the problem of succession. He had several sons, the most capable of whom was ʿAḍud al-Dawla, whom he had sent to rule Fars, while other sons held Isfahan and the northern Jibal. His arrangements, set out in his testament of 976, divided the territory among them while assigning a position of seniority to ʿAḍud al-Dawla. In practice, Fakhr al-Dawla received Hamadan and Rayy, Muʾayyad al-Dawla received Isfahan, and ʿAḍud al-Dawla kept Fars and was recognized as the senior ruler of the family. This arrangement tried to preserve family unity through a recognized head, a model that functioned for a short period under ʿAḍud al-Dawla but did not survive his death.

Rukn al-Dawla also showed some capacity for restraint toward his own family. In 975, when ʿAḍud al-Dawla seized Baghdad during the disorder in Bakhtiyar's regime, Rukn al-Dawla insisted that he withdraw and restore his cousin. The reasons given in the sources include family loyalty and a prudent distaste for open conflict among the Buyids. This episode shows Rukn al-Dawla acting as senior arbiter in a way that his authority permitted, and it also reveals a limit: when he died in 976, no one remained to exercise comparable restraint over ʿAḍud al-Dawla's ambitions. The steadier but less spectacular Rukn al-Dawla thus deserves credit as the guarantor of stability in the dynasty's middle generation.

Section 36.12

ʿImad al-Dawla, Shiraz, and the Political Importance of Fars

The oldest of the three brothers established the first Buyid state and gave the dynasty its most valuable base. ʿAli ibn Buya, known by his honorific ʿImad al-Dawla, ruled Fars from 934 until his death in 949, and in those years he fashioned from the province a state capable of supporting the ambitions of the entire family. His government of Fars is less well documented than Muʿizz al-Dawla's rule in Baghdad, but its outlines are visible.

The strategic and fiscal significance of Fars was considerable. The province combined several kinds of wealth. Its irrigated plains around Shiraz and the Marvdasht, near the ruins of Persepolis, produced grain, fruits, and other crops. Its highland valleys supported pastoral and agricultural populations, including tribal groups whose relationship to the city-based government required management. Its southern districts opened onto the Persian Gulf, where ports such as Siraf, which was among the major trading ports of the tenth-century Gulf, connected Fars to Iraq, Oman, India, and East Africa. The geographers of the age describe Fars as a rich and densely settled province, and for a ruler seeking to pay a large army, it was an obvious prize. Chapter 1 noted that Fars occupies a special place in Iranian historical geography as the homeland of the Achaemenids and the source of the name Persia. In the tenth century it was also the homeland of a remarkable concentration of Sasanian-period remains and memories, and later sections show how Buyid rulers drew upon this heritage.

ʿImad al-Dawla's government was built upon a few basic elements. He relied on his Daylamite soldiers, who formed the core of the army, and on the Persian and Arab secretaries of Fars, who knew how to assess and collect the province's taxes. The sources suggest that he took pains to pay his troops regularly, to keep the confidence of his commanders, and to cultivate local notables. He established Shiraz as his capital, and the city grew as a seat of government, with a palace, administrative offices, and a concentration of troops and officials. Shiraz had earlier been secondary to Istakhr, the old Sasanian-period center near Persepolis, but under the Buyids it became the dominant city of Fars, a position it would retain for most of the following centuries. This growth illustrates how the choice of a capital by a dynasty could reshape the urban map.

ʿImad al-Dawla recognized the caliph's authority and received the honorific title that bore his name. He acknowledged the Abbasid caliph in the sermon and on the coinage of Fars, while his own role as a ruler was expressed through his titles and through his arrangements with the soldiers. In keeping with the confederative character of the dynasty, he maintained ties with his brothers, aiding Muʿizz al-Dawla's operations in Khuzestan and Iraq and supporting Rukn al-Dawla in the Jibal. When Muʿizz al-Dawla entered Baghdad in 945, ʿImad al-Dawla was acknowledged as the senior member of the family, and the sources suggest that Muʿizz al-Dawla treated him with deference.

The problem of succession arose because ʿImad al-Dawla had no sons. Faced with the prospect that his territory would pass into disorder, he looked to his family. According to the sources, he asked Rukn al-Dawla to send one of his sons to Fars, and in 948 or thereabouts the young ʿAḍud al-Dawla, then in his early teens, arrived in Shiraz and was received and recognized as heir. When ʿImad al-Dawla died in 949, ʿAḍud al-Dawla succeeded to the rule of Fars, though not without difficulty. Some Daylamite commanders were reluctant to accept a youth, and the sources report that Rukn al-Dawla himself intervened in Fars to secure his son's position. The succession thus rested on a combination of the old ruler's designation, the support of the senior family member, and the acquiescence of the army, a combination that would prove typical of Buyid succession and that was fragile because the army's acquiescence could not be assumed.

The consequences of this succession were enormous. By placing ʿAḍud al-Dawla in Fars, ʿImad al-Dawla and Rukn al-Dawla gave the dynasty its most capable ruler a rich base from which he would build the power that carried him to Baghdad three decades later. Fars under ʿAḍud al-Dawla also became the setting for the most ambitious Buyid public works and for the clearest expression of Iranian royal ideology, themes taken up in Division IV. The importance of Fars in the Buyid system is difficult to overstate: it was the dynasty's most secure and productive territory, and it was the last to fall to the Seljuks.

Section 36.13

ʿIzz al-Dawla Bakhtiyar and the Crisis of Buyid Rule in Iraq, 967–978

While Fars flourished under a capable ruler and the Jibal were governed by a steady senior amir, Iraq slid into crisis. The decade that followed Muʿizz al-Dawla's death in 967 exposed the structural weaknesses of Buyid rule in the capital, and it explains why ʿAḍud al-Dawla was able to take Baghdad with relatively little legitimate resistance.

Bakhtiyar, styled ʿIzz al-Dawla, was a young man when he succeeded his father. The chroniclers, whose judgments reflect the viewpoint of administrators frustrated by his regime, describe him as more interested in hunting, entertainment, and pleasure than in governing, and they emphasize his dependence on his viziers and commanders. Such characterizations should not be taken as the whole story, since they were produced after his fall by writers who served his conqueror. But the structural problems that his regime faced were real, and they would have tested a more vigorous ruler.

The first problem was the army. Bakhtiyar inherited a force in which Daylamite and Turkish contingents were in competition, and the Turkish commanders, notably Sebüktigin and Alptakin, grew in power. Both had been officers under Muʿizz al-Dawla, and in the 970s they used their positions to dominate the capital. Under pressure from Sebüktigin, the caliph al-Mutiʿ, who had suffered a stroke, abdicated in 974 in favor of his son, who became al-Taʾiʿ. After Sebüktigin's death, Alptakin took the lead of the Turkish faction, and the sources report repeated mutinies, street fighting, and demands for pay. The tension between Daylamites and Turks, examined in Section 36.22, became a central problem of Bakhtiyar's reign.

The second problem was money. The revenues of Iraq, already strained under Muʿizz al-Dawla, did not increase, and Bakhtiyar's government faced mounting demands from soldiers, from the cost of campaigns against the Hamdanids and other rivals, and from the expense of the court. Bakhtiyar's viziers, among them Muhammad ibn Baqiyya, who rose to prominence in the early 970s, attempted to meet these demands through confiscations, new levies, and the manipulation of coinage and assignments, expedients that antagonized merchants and officials and failed to restore stability. The later chroniclers, who saw in these measures a symptom of the regime's decay, were probably right about the pressure the government was under, though they may have exaggerated its personal causes.

The third problem was the loss of cohesion within the Buyid family. Bakhtiyar's relations with his cousin ʿAḍud al-Dawla, who ruled the more prosperous and better-governed Fars, deteriorated. ʿAḍud al-Dawla was ambitious and aware that Iraq, with its capital, its prestige, and its caliph, was a far more valuable prize than his own province. Disputes over Khuzestan and Kirman, claims about family seniority, and the weakness of Bakhtiyar's regime combined to invite intervention. In 975, taking advantage of disorder in Baghdad, ʿAḍud al-Dawla advanced into Iraq and entered the capital, taking control for a short period. But Rukn al-Dawla, who was then alive and who held the family's seniority, demanded that Bakhtiyar be restored, and ʿAḍud al-Dawla withdrew. The episode revealed both the family's mechanisms of restraint and their dependence on the senior amir.

After Rukn al-Dawla's death in 976, the restraint disappeared. Bakhtiyar, meanwhile, sought allies against ʿAḍud al-Dawla's mounting pressure, including the Hamdanid ruler Abu Taghlib of Mosul, and tried to hold the loyalty of his soldiers. In 977, ʿAḍud al-Dawla invaded Iraq in force. The following year, Bakhtiyar's army was defeated, ʿAḍud al-Dawla entered Baghdad, and Bakhtiyar was captured and killed. The conquest, discussed in Section 36.15, was thus a result of three converging factors: Iraq's fiscal and military weakness, the break-up of family cohesion after Rukn al-Dawla, and ʿAḍud al-Dawla's own resources and ambition.

The story of Bakhtiyar's decade also provides an opportunity to reflect on how historians should evaluate failing regimes. It is easy to attribute such failure to the character of the ruler, as the sources tend to do. But a ruler who inherited an overextended army, an eroding fiscal base, a divided family, and a capital whose population was hostile or indifferent faced structural constraints that no single individual could have easily overcome. Bakhtiyar's example shows how the confederative structure, with its separate resources and separate loyalties, could work against the dynasty's unity precisely when unity was needed.

Section 36.14 · Division IV — ʿAḍud al-Dawla and the Height of Buyid Power

The Rise of ʿAḍud al-Dawla: Fars, Ambition, and Dynastic Rivalry

The ruler who gave the Buyid dynasty its moment of greatest consolidation came to prominence not in Baghdad but in Shiraz. Fanā Khusraw, who became ʿAḍud al-Dawla, "Arm of the State," was born in Isfahan in 936, the son of Rukn al-Dawla, and arrived in Fars as a boy to serve as his childless uncle's heir. When ʿImad al-Dawla died in 949, ʿAḍud al-Dawla was about thirteen years old. His rise from that age to the master of a realm stretching from Oman to northern Mesopotamia and from Kirman to the Jibal occupied the next three decades and must be understood as the outcome of patient work in Fars, shrewd use of family connections, and an exploitation of opportunities created by others' failures.

His first task was to secure Fars itself. The Daylamite commanders who had followed ʿImad al-Dawla were not uniformly willing to obey a boy, and the province contained tribal groups, notably Kurdish and other highland communities, whose cooperation could not be assumed. The sources suggest that ʿAḍud al-Dawla, with the support of his father and a corps of loyal officers, gradually asserted control over the army and the administration, put down opposition among commanders, and established working relations with the secretaries who managed the province's finances. His name for competence in this early phase rests on the observation by the sources that Fars was well governed, tax-paying, and secure under his rule, a reputation echoed by geographers who visited the province in later decades.

Over the 950s and 960s, ʿAḍud al-Dawla expanded the resources at his command. He consolidated Fars, enlarged the army, and extended his reach to Kirman, which lay to the southeast and whose conquest the sources report in the late 960s, and he campaigned along the Gulf coast and toward Oman, whose submission the chroniclers also report, though the details and dates of these operations remain uncertain. These gains brought him revenue and prestige and provided a staging area from which to look toward Khuzestan and Iraq. At the same time, he nurtured the apparatus of an ambitious court, gathering secretaries, scholars, and engineers around him in Shiraz and cultivating a reputation for learning and generosity.

His relationships with his own family were crucial. Within the confederation, ʿAḍud al-Dawla's position was somewhat anomalous: he ruled a branch founded by his uncle but was the son of the senior amir. This double standing gave him a claim on seniority that no other Buyid ruler could match, and he used it. During Bakhtiyar's troubled reign in Iraq, he intervened in the affairs of Baghdad, sometimes as an ally and sometimes as an opponent of Bakhtiyar. In 975, when disorder in Baghdad offered an opportunity, ʿAḍud al-Dawla marched into the capital and took control of the city. Rukn al-Dawla, whose consent he had not obtained, required him to withdraw. The sources record that ʿAḍud al-Dawla complied, a measure of the old amir's authority, and returned to Fars with his ambitions undiminished. They also record the resentment that the episode left, and it is a reasonable inference that he understood the rewards available to a ruler who took Baghdad and held it.

Rukn al-Dawla's death in 976 removed the one man who could restrain him. The testament by which Rukn al-Dawla divided his territories assigned Hamadan and Rayy to Fakhr al-Dawla and Isfahan to Muʾayyad al-Dawla and recognized ʿAḍud al-Dawla as the family's senior. But recognition on paper was not the same as power in the field, and the younger brothers' acceptance of ʿAḍud al-Dawla's seniority was grudging in at least one case. Fakhr al-Dawla in particular harbored a sense that his father's arrangements favored ʿAḍud al-Dawla. These tensions would surface later, in the campaign of 980 to 981 examined in the next section.

At this point, in 976 and 977, ʿAḍud al-Dawla possessed a rich and well-governed home province, a large and relatively cohesive army, the prestige of seniority, and a reputation for ability. Iraq was in disarray under a ruler many regarded as feckless. The decision to move against Bakhtiyar was nonetheless not obvious or inevitable. Contemporaries could have imagined that the Buyid family would settle into a more stable division of territories, as the Samanid or Hamdanid families had done. That ʿAḍud al-Dawla chose to pursue Baghdad reflected his judgment of the opportunity, and the consequences of that choice are the subject of the next section.

Section 36.15

The Conquest of Iraq and the Unification of the Buyid Realm, 977–980

ʿAḍud al-Dawla's campaign against Bakhtiyar began in 977 and ended in 978 with his entry into Baghdad. The campaign combined military force with diplomacy and drew upon his resources in Fars and Khuzestan. Bakhtiyar, in turn, sought allies, including the Hamdanid Abu Taghlib of Mosul, and tried to hold the loyalty of Turkish and Daylamite troops whose behavior had become unreliable. The accounts of the fighting vary in detail, but the outline is clear. ʿAḍud al-Dawla advanced from Fars through Khuzestan toward Iraq. Bakhtiyar's forces, weakened by internal conflict and the defection of some commanders, were defeated in 978, and Bakhtiyar himself was captured and put to death. ʿAḍud al-Dawla entered Baghdad and took charge of the capital.

The conquest raised the problem of how to hold what he had taken. ʿAḍud al-Dawla could not simply replace Bakhtiyar as the amir of Iraq, because he intended to maintain his position in Fars and because the territories he now controlled were too extensive to administer from a single seat. He therefore adopted a policy of personal supremacy. He appointed trusted subordinates, including family members, to govern provinces and cities on his behalf. He placed his own officials in the key financial and military posts at Baghdad. He moved between Baghdad and Shiraz, as circumstances demanded, making his court mobile in a way that allowed him to supervise different regions. And he secured formal recognition from the caliph al-Taʾiʿ, receiving honors and investiture that legitimated his position as the senior ruler of the Buyids.

The Hamdanids were the next target. Abu Taghlib, who had allied with Bakhtiyar, was driven out of Mosul, and ʿAḍud al-Dawla's forces established control over Mosul and parts of northern Mesopotamia in 979 and 980. Abu Taghlib fled toward Syria, where he was killed in the fighting that involved the Fatimids and other local powers. The conquest of Mosul brought the Buyids to the frontier of the Byzantine and Fatimid spheres, and it represented the farthest extent of Buyid power to the northwest, though it was not durable. In the following decade, other local dynasties, including the Arab Uqaylids, took over much of the area.

The final stage of consolidation involved the family itself. ʿAḍud al-Dawla's seniority, formally recognized in Rukn al-Dawla's testament, had not been accepted by Fakhr al-Dawla in the Jibal, and in 980 ʿAḍud al-Dawla moved against him, with the help of his brother Muʾayyad al-Dawla. Fakhr al-Dawla was forced to flee and took refuge at the court of the Ziyarid ruler Qabus in Gurgan. In 981 ʿAḍud al-Dawla's forces pursued Fakhr al-Dawla and Qabus, and the Ziyarid ruler, too, was driven out, with both fugitives eventually reaching Khurasan, then under Samanid authority. By 981, ʿAḍud al-Dawla held Fars, Kirman, Khuzestan, Iraq, much of northern Mesopotamia, and the Jibal, a territorial span unmatched by any Buyid ruler before or after.

Yet the nature of this unification must be defined with care. What ʿAḍud al-Dawla achieved was personal supremacy, resting on his own energy, his resources in Fars, and his capacity to place relatives and trusted officials in command. It was not an institutional unification that would survive him. The Jibal, for example, were governed on his behalf by his brother Muʾayyad al-Dawla, whose loyalty was a function of family relations, and the revenues of the different regions continued to be collected and spent largely in those regions. No common financial administration, no central army, and no uniform legal system bound the territories together. In effect, ʿAḍud al-Dawla had made himself the head of a confederation, a role his father had once held but which he exercised with far greater force. It was a considerable achievement, but it depended on his person.

  • Around 980: The territories of ʿAḍud al-Dawla's supremacy, including Fars, Kirman, and Khuzestan; Iraq, with Baghdad and Basra; Mosul and parts of northern Mesopotamia, held briefly; and, through his brothers and relatives, the Jibal. A brief and unstable extension reached Gurgan in 981.

Section 36.16

ʿAḍud al-Dawla's Government: Centralization, Officials, and Court Authority

The reputation of ʿAḍud al-Dawla in the historical tradition rests largely on his government, which the sources, including Ibn Miskawayh, who served him, describe as unusually systematic, energetic, and effective. This section examines what made his government distinctive, and why it proved difficult to sustain.

The core of his method was personal supervision. Ibn Miskawayh and other sources present him as a ruler who involved himself in the details of administration, reviewing the accounts of the dīvāns, questioning officials, inspecting the army, and receiving reports from across his territories. This attention to detail was supported by an apparatus for gathering information, including a postal and intelligence service known as the barid, which carried both official correspondence and reports on local conditions. The sources suggest that he used this network to monitor governors and commanders, to detect corruption and disloyalty, and to remain informed about developments in distant provinces. Whether the service was a novelty or a revival of earlier Abbasid practice is difficult to establish, but it was an essential tool of rule over a territory of this size.

Finances were the second pillar. ʿAḍud al-Dawla appears to have sought to regularize revenue collection, to curb abuses by tax collectors and military assignees, and to link the army's pay more closely to centrally administered accounts. Scholars have debated the extent and success of these efforts. Some sources credit him with limiting the system of military iqtaʿ in Iraq and returning to cash payment for part of the army, while others suggest that assignments continued and that his reforms were partial. What is clear is that the sources regard his fiscal management as more orderly than that of Muʿizz al-Dawla or Bakhtiyar, and that this orderliness, backed by the revenues of Fars, enabled him to pay and discipline his troops. The question of iqtaʿ is treated more fully in Section 36.20.

Personnel were the third element. ʿAḍud al-Dawla recruited secretaries, scholars, and administrators from various backgrounds and religious communities. Among his officials were Muslims of Persian and Arab origin, and the sources record that one of his viziers, Nasr ibn Harun, was a Christian, a reminder that the Buyid bureaucracy, like the Abbasid, drew on non-Muslim secretaries and physicians, though this practice sometimes provoked criticism from Muslim jurists and writers. He also maintained close relations with jurists, grammarians, and astronomers whom he patronized, and he is credited with taking part in the scholarly culture of his court, a subject treated in Section 36.28. This blending of administrative and intellectual patronage was characteristic of the age and helped to attach officials to the ruler.

Court authority was the fourth. ʿAḍud al-Dawla governed from two main centers, Shiraz and Baghdad, and spent time in each. His court at Shiraz was the center of his family's personal rule, a place where he could concentrate resources and cultivate a following among Persian and Daylamite officials. His presence in Baghdad was necessary to maintain his relationship with the caliph and to control Iraq's revenues and army. The sources describe an elaborate court ceremonial, with audiences, robes of honor, and displays of royal grandeur, designed to impress subordinates and rivals. The relationship between his court and the caliphal court was carefully managed, and he took pains to appear as a faithful servant of the caliphate, though one who exercised effective authority. He even sought to strengthen the connection through a marriage: his daughter was wed to the caliph al-Taʾiʿ, according to the sources around 980, a union that appears to have been intended to produce a caliph with Buyid blood, though it did not achieve that outcome.

Why, then, was this government so difficult to sustain? Several reasons stand out. The system depended on the ruler's personal energy, and there was no institutional mechanism for replicating it. Revenue and troops remained largely regional, so the unification was always dependent on the ruler's capacity to command. The family structure that had given rise to the Buyid confederation did not disappear, and ʿAḍud al-Dawla's sons, cousins, and brothers, each with their own claims and followers, stood ready to contest his legacy. And the army, though better managed than its predecessors, remained divided among factions whose loyalty to the ruler's heirs could not be guaranteed. The paradox of ʿAḍud al-Dawla's government is that its very effectiveness depended on a degree of personal control that could not be inherited.

A historian must also ask what this government meant for those who lived under it. For secretaries and scholars, it brought patronage and stability. For soldiers, it offered more regular pay but also stricter discipline. For cultivators and townspeople, the effect is harder to assess, because the sources are largely silent. Praise of ʿAḍud al-Dawla's justice and the security of his roads, found in several authors, probably reflects real improvements in order, but it also reflects the interests of the literate classes who wrote. The weight of taxation and the reach of confiscation under his rule, which could be severe, are mentioned in the sources less often than the monuments he built. A balanced assessment recognizes both the order he provided and the limits of what the evidence tells us about its costs.

Section 36.17

Buildings, Irrigation, Hospitals, and Public Works

No aspect of ʿAḍud al-Dawla's reign is more frequently remembered than his building. Contemporary and later writers describe an extensive program of construction and restoration in Iraq and Fars, which served practical purposes and also expressed a particular conception of royal duty. This section considers what is known, what is uncertain, and what the program meant.

The best-known project is the hospital in Baghdad, the ʿAḍudi hospital, founded in the early 980s, around 981 or 982. The sources describe it as a large foundation on the western side of the Tigris, with physicians, pharmacists, and attendants, supported by endowments, and it is generally treated as one of the leading medical institutions of the Islamic world in its time. The number of physicians reported in some accounts is about two dozen, though figures vary with the source. The hospital was a place for treating patients and, according to accounts, also for medical teaching, and it became associated with some of the leading physicians of the age, though the particulars of its organization are known mainly from later descriptions, a limitation noted below. A later visitor in the twelfth century saw it in a diminished condition, and by that time its original glory had faded. Chapter 43 will treat the development of medical science and practice in medieval Iran more fully. Here the point is that the foundation of a hospital on this scale was a significant act of public provision, one that tied the ruler's name to a charitable institution and that served the people of the capital.

In Fars, the monument most closely associated with ʿAḍud al-Dawla is the dam across the Kur River in the Marvdasht plain, near the ruins of Persepolis, known as the Band-e Amir, "the amir's dam," a name that preserves the memory of the ruler who is credited with building it. The dam diverted the river's waters into irrigation channels, increased the agricultural productivity of the plain, and powered mills. Buyid-period sources and later traditions attribute its construction to ʿAḍud al-Dawla, and the structure, repaired and altered in later centuries, still existed in modern times. A standard specialist account dates the construction to about 975, although the precise extent of the original works and the degree of later rebuilding remain less certain than the attribution. The project illustrates a connection between royal authority and hydraulic infrastructure that Chapter 1 identified as a recurring pattern in Iranian history: where water was scarce, the capacity to build and maintain irrigation works was a measure of a ruler's competence and an instrument of his power.

Other works are reported in both regions. In Baghdad, the sources describe the restoration of canals and the repair of districts damaged by fires and neglect, the rebuilding of markets, and the reconstruction of mosques and other public buildings. In Fars, he is credited with improving roads and securing them against brigands, and with enlarging and embellishing Shiraz, including a library, discussed below. He patronized the shrines of the family of ʿAli in Iraq, including the one at Najaf, where, according to tradition, he was ultimately buried, and the sources record endowments and building at these sites. These reports derive largely from court-centered authors, and they are probably exaggerated in particular instances, but their general direction is corroborated by the persistence of structures such as the dam and by independent observations of travelers.

Irrigation increased yields, and restored canals in Iraq may have helped to rebuild the productivity of the Sawad. A hospital offered medical care and employment for physicians. Safe roads promoted trade. At the symbolic level, the program expressed a vision of rule in which the sovereign was responsible for the welfare of his subjects, a vision with deep roots in both Iranian and Islamic traditions of kingship. The Sasanian kings had also been remembered as builders of cities, bridges, and dams, and Buyid rulers, who knew this tradition, appear to have deliberately placed themselves in its line. The construction projects were also paid for out of taxation, confiscation, and labor extracted from subjects, and they must be read as acts of power as well as of benevolence.

One further caution concerns the sources. Much of what we know about ʿAḍud al-Dawla's buildings comes from literary descriptions, panegyric poetry, and later chronicles, and some structures, including the hospital, have not survived in a form that allows archaeological verification. This does not mean the reports are false, but it means that numbers, dimensions, and dates should be treated as approximations, and that the enthusiasm of court writers must be weighed against the more cautious testimony of independent observers.

Section 36.18

Iranian Kingship and Islamic Legitimacy under ʿAḍud al-Dawla

The most widely discussed aspect of ʿAḍud al-Dawla's reign is his presentation of himself as a king. He used royal titles, Iranian in origin and Islamic in framing, and he sat at the center of a court whose ceremonial drew on both Sasanian and Abbasid precedent. This section examines how these elements were combined and why modern students should resist treating Iranian and Islamic political identities as opposites.

The title that most clearly recalls the Sasanian past is shahanshah, "king of kings," the old Persian imperial title that Sasanian rulers had used and that the Islamic tradition remembered as characteristic of pre-Islamic Iranian monarchy. Buyid rulers, including ʿAḍud al-Dawla and members of his family, used this title or its Arabic equivalent in some official contexts, including coinage and inscriptions, a practice that appears to have intensified under ʿAḍud al-Dawla. The Buyids also claimed descent from the Sasanian king Bahram Gur, and their court poets and secretaries sometimes celebrated their dynasty in language that evoked the Sasanian past. ʿAḍud al-Dawla's own given name, Fanā Khusraw, was Persian, and it recalls the name of the Sasanian king Khusraw. Buyid rulers also visited the ruined monuments of Persepolis and other Fars sites, which they understood as the remains of ancient Iranian kings, and Arabic and Persian inscriptions left by visitors at such places in the Islamic centuries show that these monuments remained objects of attention.

At the same time, ʿAḍud al-Dawla worked within an Islamic framework. He received his honorific titles from the Abbasid caliph. The sources report that in the late 970s the caliph al-Taʾiʿ conferred on him a robe of honor, a crown, and a new laqab, Taj al-Milla, "Crown of the Religion." Court writers such as Hilal al-Sabiʾ, whose treatise on caliphal ceremonial (Rusūm dār al-khilāfa) is a principal source for the protocol of audiences, describe investitures and audiences of this kind, though students should consult the sources directly for the details of any single occasion. The ceremony conveyed an unmistakable message: the caliph conferred on the Buyid ruler the insignia of lawful authority, and the ruler, in turn, deferred publicly to the caliph. ʿAḍud al-Dawla's name appeared in the sermon after the caliph's, and the caliph's name continued to appear on the coinage. His patronage of Shiʿi shrines and scholars, noted above, coexisted with his support for Sunni jurists and for the institution of the caliphate itself. He positioned himself, in effect, as a king who ruled within Islam.

Language needs mention. Arabic remained the principal language of administration, law, and scholarship, in Baghdad and in Buyid chanceries elsewhere, and ʿAḍud al-Dawla himself is reported to have studied Arabic grammar under the eminent scholar Abu ʿAli al-Farisi, who wrote works for him. Persian was spoken in the courts and in much of Fars and the Jibal, and Persian titles and names, and some Persian poetry, were part of the ruler's repertoire. But the written culture of the Buyid court was largely Arabic, and one should not imagine Baghdad as a Persian-speaking capital. What the Buyids contributed was a new political vocabulary of kingship that overlay the older Abbasid forms, not a replacement of Arabic by Persian. The development of New Persian as a literary language, associated with the Samanids and treated in Chapter 35, occurred largely in the east, and its relation to the Buyid world was limited until later.

The reception of these royal claims was not uniform. The caliphs and many jurists were uneasy about titles that implied an authority independent of the caliphate, and the question of the title "king of kings" provoked controversy in the following century, when Jalal al-Dawla's assumption of a version of it in 1037 aroused objections from jurists who held that the title properly belonged to God alone. This episode, which falls outside ʿAḍud al-Dawla's reign, shows that the titulature of Buyid kingship remained contested, and it reminds students that royal claims were subject to criticism within their own society.

Historical Debate

Was Buyid Kingship an Iranian Restoration, an Islamic Military Government, or a Combination?

The question: How should historians interpret the Buyids' use of Iranian royal titles, Sasanian symbolism, and a kingly style of rule? Did these practices signal a revival of Iranian monarchy against Arab-Islamic dominance, or were they one set of tools within an Islamic political order?

Interpretation A — An Iranian restoration: Some earlier scholarship, influenced by nationalist and romantic historiography, described the Buyid and other tenth-century dynasties as an "Iranian intermezzo," a period in which Iranian rulers recovered political initiative between Arab and Turkic domination. On this view, shahanshah titles and Sasanian genealogies expressed a conscious program of reviving Iranian kingship.
Interpretation B — An Islamic military government: Other historians stress that the Buyids were soldiers who governed through the institutions of the Abbasid state, depended on caliphal recognition, patronized Arabic learning, and presented themselves in Islamic terms. In this reading, Iranian royal imagery was an embellishment of a fundamentally Islamic form of military government, similar to the claims of other rulers of the era.
Interpretation C — A layered combination: A third view treats Iranian royal ideology and Islamic legitimacy as parts of a single repertoire from which rulers drew according to audience and occasion. Before a Persian-speaking, Daylamite, or Fars audience, Sasanian references conveyed one kind of authority; before the Baghdad population and the Islamic world, caliphal investiture conveyed another. These were complementary, not contradictory.

Evidence: The evidence includes coin legends and titles, accounts of ceremonies, genealogical claims, patronage of Arabic and Persian poetry, Persian names within the family, the choice of building projects, visits to ancient monuments, and the surviving criticisms of jurists. Interpretation A emphasizes the Sasanian references. Interpretation B emphasizes the persistence of Abbasid institutions and the dominance of Arabic. Interpretation C emphasizes the simultaneity of both.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement is largely about interpretation and emphasis. Few scholars deny that the Buyids used both Iranian and Islamic symbols. They differ over whether the Iranian element reflects deep identity, strategic ornament, or a flexible tool, and over how far the modern category "Iranian" applies to Daylamite rulers who ruled in an Arabic-writing empire.

Why it matters: The interpretation affects how the Buyid period fits into the long history of Iran. If it is a restoration, it connects to a continuous Iranian monarchical tradition running from the Sasanians to later dynasties. If it is an Islamic military government, it belongs primarily to the history of the caliphate. If it is a layered combination, it shows how political identities were constructed rather than inherited, a point relevant to later periods when Iranian dynasties again combined Persian royal tradition with Islamic legitimacy.

Student task: List three pieces of evidence from this section and explain which interpretation each best supports. Then consider which kind of additional evidence, such as the language of letters sent to different audiences, might help decide among the interpretations.

Section 36.19 · Division V — Government, Economy, Military Power, and Society

Viziers, Secretaries, and the Buyid Administrative System

The Buyids governed through officials they did not invent. The Abbasid state had developed, over two centuries, a sophisticated bureaucracy of departments called dīvāns, staffed by secretaries (kuttāb) trained in accounting, correspondence, and law, and headed by a vizier who supervised the whole. Chapter 31 examined the formation of this system and the prominent role of Iranian families within it. The Buyid rulers, who came from a military background and lacked administrative traditions of their own, adopted this apparatus and adapted it to a very different political structure. Understanding how they did so is essential to explaining why Buyid government worked as well as it did, and why it worked unevenly.

The principal departments retained their familiar functions. A dīvān of the army oversaw the registration, inspection, and payment of soldiers. A dīvān of taxation, often called the dīvān of the land tax, assessed and collected revenue, maintained cadastral and tax registers, and supervised the officials who dealt with cultivators. A chancery prepared correspondence, diplomas, and decrees, and its staff was expected to command elegant Arabic prose, a skill that became a mark of prestige. Other offices managed estates belonging to the ruler, the postal and intelligence network, and the administration of justice and religious endowments. The caliph's household retained its own officials, but their authority was confined to the court.

The vizier stood at the top. Under the Buyids, each ruler had his own vizier, who ran the civil administration and mediated between the ruler and the bureaucracy. The vizier's position was precarious. The sources show viziers repeatedly dismissed, arrested, tortured, and fined, as rulers sought to recover funds, satisfy soldiers, or blame someone for failures. At the same time, successful viziers could wield great influence and were among the most visible figures of the age. Al-Muhallabi, who served Muʿizz al-Dawla from 946 to 963, stabilized the early Buyid administration in Iraq. At Rayy, Abu al-Fadl Ibn al-ʿAmid, vizier to Rukn al-Dawla until his death in 970, was a celebrated stylist and a skillful diplomat. His successor in the western branch, Ismaʿil ibn ʿAbbad, known as al-Sahib ("the Companion," from his early service to Muʾayyad al-Dawla), served Muʾayyad al-Dawla and then Fakhr al-Dawla for many years and was both an effective administrator and a major patron of letters, as Section 36.28 shows. These men were not simply bureaucrats but figures with cultural and political standing, maintaining their own households, clients, and libraries.

The adaptation of the system to Buyid conditions involved several changes. First, decentralization. Because the Buyid dynasty consisted of several principalities, each had its own vizier and its own dīvāns, so that the single imperial administration of the Abbasid heyday gave way to a set of regional bureaucracies. Revenue no longer flowed to a single treasury, and officials served particular rulers. Second, military dominance. The army's needs shaped the budget, and the heads of the army dīvān, along with the commanders themselves, had a voice in decisions about revenue. Rulers often depended on the support of commanders to remove or protect a vizier. Third, personal loyalty. Appointments depended less on caliphal sanction than on personal relationships with the ruler, and officials built networks of clients and patrons that crossed the boundaries of offices. This pattern, in which loyalty and patronage operated within formal institutions, is one of the features that has attracted historians interested in how Buyid society actually functioned.

Religious and ethnic diversity characterized the secretarial class. Muslims, Christians, Jews, and members of the Sabian community of Harran and Baghdad all served as secretaries and physicians in Buyid administrations, and their expertise in accounting, correspondence, and languages made them valuable. This inclusiveness was not unique to the Buyids, but the tenth century offers prominent examples, including the Christian Nasr ibn Harun under ʿAḍud al-Dawla and the Sabian Abu Ishaq al-Sabiʾ, head of the chancery in Baghdad in the 970s and 980s and one of the most admired Arabic prose writers of his time. The inclusion of non-Muslims drew criticism from some jurists and writers, but rulers were generally willing to prefer competence to confession.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Abbasid Administration before and during Buyid Control

What Changed

  • The vizier of the caliph was replaced in practice by the vizier of the Buyid ruler, who controlled revenue and appointments
  • A single imperial administration gave way to several regional administrations serving different branches of the family
  • The army's needs and the commanders' influence weighed more heavily on fiscal decisions
  • Personal loyalty to the ruler, rather than appointment by the caliph, became the principal basis of an official's career

What Stayed the Same

  • The dīvāns, their functions, and their record-keeping practices persisted
  • Arabic remained the language of the chancery, and the secretarial profession preserved its educational traditions
  • Many families of secretaries served both the caliphate and Buyid rulers, so personnel continuity was considerable
  • The caliph's role as source of formal appointments, titles, and investiture continued

When and how did change occur? Change was gradual and uneven, beginning with the amirs al-umara before 945, accelerating after 946, and varying by region. Who experienced change most directly? Viziers, caliphal officials, and soldiers, whose relations to power shifted most; cultivators and artisans experienced greater continuity in the basic demands of taxation. Why the pattern matters: It shows that the Buyid takeover was a change in who controlled an institution rather than the replacement of the institution, and that administrative continuity helps explain the system's durability.

Section 36.20

Revenue, Iqtaʿ, Military Payments, and Fiscal Pressure

Money was the Buyid state's central problem, and the institutions through which revenue was raised and spent explain much of the dynasty's political history. This section examines the principal sources of revenue, the ways soldiers were paid, and the pressures that made fiscal management a matter of survival.

The main source of income was the land tax, kharāj, assessed on agricultural land and collected from cultivators, either in kind or in money, according to systems that varied by region. In Iraq, the tax rested on assessments of the irrigated lands of the Sawad. In Fars and the Jibal, it rested on local practices that sometimes preserved Sasanian-period arrangements. Beyond the land tax, the state drew on levies on trade and markets, on the poll tax paid by non-Muslim subjects under protection, on the property of officials who were confiscated, and on a range of fees and irregular exactions. Confiscation, muṣādara, was a recognized instrument of government, one that al-Tanukhi's anecdotes (Section 36.23) show to have been routine. The state's revenue was therefore partly a function of how agriculture and trade fared, and partly a function of how effectively and ruthlessly officials extracted wealth from those who handled it.

The collection of taxes was often delegated by contract. Under the system of ḍamān, or tax farming, a contractor undertook to deliver a fixed sum from a district, advanced money to the government, and kept the remainder of what he collected. This gave the state immediate cash, which was valuable when soldiers demanded pay, but it created incentives for contractors to extract as much as possible, regardless of the long-term health of the land. Complaints about tax farmers appear regularly in the sources, along with the contention that the Sawad's irrigation works suffered from neglect.

Historical Vocabulary
Iqtaʿ: Revenue Assignment, Land Rights, and Ownership

Definition: Iqtaʿ (from an Arabic root meaning "to cut off" or "to allot") designated a grant by the ruler of something carved from state resources. The term covered different arrangements across time. In some cases, it meant the assignment to an individual, often a soldier or official, of the right to collect the tax revenue of a specified district in lieu of salary. In others, it meant a grant of land, especially uncultivated or abandoned land, which the recipient might develop and hold with a degree of ownership.

Historical context: Under the later Abbasids, assignments of revenue to soldiers became increasingly common as cash payment became difficult. Buyid rulers, beginning with Muʿizz al-Dawla, used revenue assignments to pay their commanders and troops, and the practice expanded and contracted under different rulers and in different regions. Sources describe attempts by ʿAḍud al-Dawla and others to restrict abuses and, in places, to return to cash payments, but assignments remained significant throughout the period.

Why it matters: The practice shifted tax collection into the hands of military men and gave soldiers a direct interest in the districts assigned to them. This affected the agrarian economy, the relationship between rulers and troops, and the later development of the Seljuk system, which built on and altered earlier practices.

Potential misunderstanding: Early iqtaʿ should not be equated with European feudalism, nor assumed to be a grant of hereditary landownership. In most Buyid cases, the holder received a right to collect revenue, not title to the land or authority over the cultivators beyond tax collection, and assignments were revocable and often rotated. The difference between a revenue assignment, a right to develop land, and outright ownership is critical, and the sources do not always make it explicit.

The Buyid use of iqtaʿ grew from fiscal necessity. When Muʿizz al-Dawla took Baghdad, he faced soldiers demanding payment that the treasury could not provide in cash. Assigning districts solved the immediate problem and relieved the government of the work of collecting taxes for the army. But the practice had consequences. Soldiers who held assignments sought to maximize short-term returns, and the commanders' retinues grew into groups with local interests. Because assignments were often shifted and revoked, holders had little incentive to invest in irrigation or in the welfare of cultivators. The chroniclers of the period connect the practice to agrarian decline in Iraq, though modern scholars note that other factors, including floods, canal silting, tax farming, and conflict, also contributed. The extent to which iqtaʿ caused decline is debated.

Military pay remained a persistent source of tension. Soldiers expected regular payment, and rulers regularly failed to provide it. The sources record episodes in which troops rioted, besieged the ruler's palace, or deserted when pay was delayed. Rulers responded with confiscations from officials, with new levies, with the debasement or manipulation of coinage, and with the sale of palace property. The fiscal dependency of the state on a large army of unpredictable loyalty was one of the structural problems of the dynasty, and it contributed to the fall of Bakhtiyar and, indirectly, to the difficulties of ʿAḍud al-Dawla's successors.

Regional differences mattered. Fars, with its well-organized revenue system, relatively secure countryside, and Gulf trade, provided its rulers with greater fiscal stability than Iraq. The Jibal depended on a more fragmented set of local revenues and on tolls from the overland route. Iraq, theoretically the richest region, suffered from the accumulated damage to the Sawad and from the heavy demands of a large capital and army. These contrasts help explain why the Fars branch was fiscally robust and why control of Iraq, though prestigious, was not always profitable.

Finally, historians should remember what the sources do not say. Budget figures reported in some chronicles are difficult to verify and may reflect idealized estimates, and tax registers survive in little detail. The experience of cultivators, who bore the burden of taxation, is rarely described directly and must be inferred from complaints about officials, accounts of flight from the land, and the occasional record of remission of taxes after disasters. Any account of Buyid fiscal institutions should therefore be offered with caution about its precision.

Section 36.21

Agriculture, Irrigation, Trade, and the Economy of Iran and Iraq

The economy that supported Buyid rule combined intensive agriculture, regional crafts, and long-distance trade, and it varied sharply across the territories the dynasty governed. This section outlines the principal features of that economy and the pressures that recurrent warfare and fiscal extraction placed upon it.

Agriculture in Iraq rested on irrigation. The Sawad, the alluvial plain between the Tigris and Euphrates, supported grain, date palms, and other crops, but only through an extensive network of canals that carried river water to the fields and drained excess. These canals silted up and required constant clearing, and embankments needed repair after floods. Maintenance depended on a state able to mobilize labor and funds and on landholders willing to invest. In the troubled decades before and after 945, the system deteriorated in places, and the sources report abandoned villages, flooded fields, and flight of cultivators. Modern historians debate how severe and how widespread this deterioration was, and whether it reflected long-term ecological and demographic trends or the effects of specific political failures. What seems clear is that Buyid rulers, from Muʿizz al-Dawla to ʿAḍud al-Dawla, were aware of the problem and that the restoration of canals appears among the achievements credited to ʿAḍud al-Dawla.

In Fars and much of the Iranian plateau, irrigation took different forms. Rivers such as the Kur were tapped by weirs and channels, and the plateau's characteristic underground channels, qanāts, which Chapter 1 introduced, brought groundwater to the surface in zones with little rain. The Band-e Amir dam on the Kur is one notable example of hydraulic engineering. Agricultural products of Fars and the Jibal included grains, fruits, nuts, rose water and other perfumes, and textiles. The Caspian region contributed rice and silk, and Khuzestan produced sugar, among other products, and textiles. This variety of products supported interregional trade.

Trade operated on several scales. Locally, markets in cities exchanged agricultural produce and crafts for money. Regionally, goods moved along rivers and roads, with caravans linking Baghdad to Rayy, Isfahan, and Khurasan along the great overland route that crossed the Jibal. Internationally, Baghdad and Basra connected to the Indian Ocean through the Persian Gulf, and Siraf and other Gulf ports served as outlets for Fars. Merchants carried textiles, metalwork, ceramics, spices, and slaves, among other goods, across these networks. Buyid rulers had an interest in encouraging trade, which provided taxes and prestige, but their wars and exactions could also damage it. The sources describe merchants whose wealth was confiscated, markets disrupted by fighting, and roads rendered unsafe by brigands. They also describe periods of security, such as those under ʿAḍud al-Dawla, when trade flourished.

The economy's resilience and vulnerability are both apparent. Cities continued to function as centers of craft production and commerce, and monetary exchange was widespread. Yet the tax burden, the cost of armies, and the habit of confiscation limited the accumulation of wealth and discouraged investment in agriculture. Historians sometimes describe the tenth and eleventh centuries as a period of economic dislocation in Iraq, with the decline of Baghdad's agricultural hinterland and the relative rise of Fars and the plateau. This pattern, which fits the Buyid shift of political weight toward Fars, should be treated as a tendency rather than a certainty, because the evidence for population and output is limited and unevenly distributed.

A final point concerns the distribution of wealth. The economy produced prosperity for some, including merchants, officials, and landholders, and hardship for others, including cultivators and poorer townspeople. Trade did not automatically benefit all groups, and the revenue extracted by the state often flowed to the ruler, the army, and the court rather than back to the producers. Students should therefore resist equating the existence of trade and public works with general prosperity.

Section 36.22

Daylamite Infantry, Turkic Cavalry, and Military Factionalism

The Buyid army was the dynasty's foundation and its most persistent problem. It combined two principal contingents, Daylamite infantry and Turkic cavalry, whose different origins, armaments, and expectations produced a political rivalry that shaped many of the events described in this chapter. The sources also mention Kurds, Arab tribal troops, and others, but the Daylamite-Turkic relationship was central.

The Daylamites formed the original core of the Buyid force. Their loyalty to the ruling family was grounded in shared origin, common service, and the expectation of reward. Daylamite commanders, many of whom were relatives or longtime companions of the Buyid brothers, held high positions and expected a share of the spoils. As infantry, they were effective in mountains, sieges, and urban fighting but less so in open-field warfare against cavalry.

Turkic soldiers supplied the mounted warfare the Daylamites could not. They came from the Central Asian steppe frontier, often as slaves purchased or captured and then trained and freed, or as free mercenaries, and they had been the dominant element of the Abbasid army since the ninth century. Their commanders held great influence in Baghdad, and the Buyids, having seized the capital, had to decide how to treat them. In practice, Buyid rulers kept Turkish contingents, valued their skills, and sought to balance them against Daylamites. The balance was unstable.

Recruitment and loyalty operated through commanders. A soldier typically served a particular officer, who recruited, equipped, and sometimes paid his men, and the officer in turn served the ruler. This meant that soldiers' primary loyalty was to their immediate commander, and a ruler who lost the support of his commanders could lose his army. Pay, whether in cash or in iqtaʿ, flowed through the commanders as well. The army dīvān maintained rolls and conducted inspections, but its control was always incomplete.

The economic cost was heavy. A standing army of tens of thousands, whose numbers are not reliably known, required regular payment, and the government's revenue was often inadequate. Competition for resources fed the rivalry between contingents: Daylamites resented Turkish privileges, and Turks resented Daylamite favoritism. In Baghdad, the sources record outbreaks of fighting between the two groups, sometimes involving the city's population. Riots over pay or precedence recurred, and rulers sometimes responded by favoring one group, then the other.

It would nevertheless be a mistake to reduce Buyid political conflict to ethnicity alone. Soldiers' interests differed along other lines: commanders competed for office and revenue, factions formed around particular patrons, and individuals changed sides. Daylamites sometimes fought each other, and Turks sometimes served Buyid rulers loyally. The Turkish commander Sebüktigin, who dominated Baghdad in the early 970s, and Alptakin, his colleague, acted for personal and factional reasons, not simply as representatives of a Turkish interest. Modern historians caution against attributing to ethnic identity what may equally reflect competition over pay, rank, and access to the ruler.

ʿAḍud al-Dawla managed the army more effectively than most. He appears to have ensured regular inspections and payment, maintained a mixed force, and distributed commands to prevent any faction from dominating. After his death, the contingents again became the arbiters of succession disputes, as the next division shows. In the longer run, the Buyid reliance on a mixed army also set the stage for the transition to Seljuk rule, since the Seljuks, a Turkic dynasty, built a military order around Turkic troops and iqtaʿ assignments, and Turkish soldiers would increasingly dominate the military life of the region.

Section 36.23

Baghdad, Shiraz, Rayy, and Isfahan: Urban Society under Buyid Rule

Cities were the stage on which Buyid rule was experienced by most of those who wrote about it, and they were also the places where the effects of military occupation, taxation, and religious politics were most visible. This section looks at the four principal urban centers of the dynasty and at the groups who inhabited them.

Baghdad, founded in the 760s as the Abbasid capital, remained the largest city of the Islamic world in the tenth century, though it had declined from its height and its population is uncertain. The city lay on both banks of the Tigris, with the eastern side containing the palaces and government and the western side including the quarter of Karkh, a commercial and industrial district with a large Shiʿi population, and other neighborhoods. The city was divided into districts that often had distinctive religious, occupational, and social identities. Merchants, artisans, laborers, religious scholars, secretaries, soldiers, and the urban poor lived in close, sometimes conflictual, proximity. Provisioning the capital required river and canal transport and a functioning market system, and disruptions in either produced hardship. The sources mention fires, floods, famine, and epidemics in Buyid-period Baghdad, along with periods of recovery.

Shiraz, the capital of Fars, grew under the Buyids from a provincial center to a major city. Under ʿAḍud al-Dawla it received palaces, a library, and other buildings, and al-Muqaddasi and other geographers describe it as flourishing. Rayy, in the Jibal, was a large city on the edge of the Alborz, a center of trade, scholarship, and, under Rukn al-Dawla and his successors, a seat of government. Isfahan, with its surrounding oasis, was another major administrative center. These cities contained mixtures of Persian, Arab, Daylamite, and other residents, and their religious composition varied, with Sunni, Shiʿi, and Zoroastrian, Jewish, and Christian communities in different proportions. The culture of these cities, including their libraries, courts, and schools, was substantial, and Section 36.28 examines it.

The social groups of the cities included, at the top, the Buyid ruling family, commanders, viziers, and senior secretaries, who held power and wealth. Below them came merchants, landholders, and the learned class of jurists, judges, and teachers, whose standing rested on property, knowledge, and communal leadership. Artisans organized into craft groups and lived in distinct districts. Day laborers, porters, boatmen, and the poor lived precariously and were vulnerable to price increases and food shortages. Women appear in the sources as members of the ruling elite, such as the powerful queen mothers and princesses of Buyid courts, as participants in religious life, and as workers in textile production and in markets, but the surviving literature, written overwhelmingly by men of the educated elite, gives little direct access to women's lives. Enslaved people, including domestic servants, concubines, and soldiers, formed another significant part of urban populations.

Public order depended on both formal and informal institutions. The ruler's troops and police patrolled the cities, and officials such as the market inspector oversaw weights and measures and public morals. But local groups also provided order. Neighborhood leaders mediated disputes. The ʿayyārūn, young men organized in bands, acted at times as protectors of their districts, at other times as extortioners or rioters, and some were recruited into the service of rulers or officials. When the state's authority weakened or when taxes and food prices rose, urban unrest followed. The material effects of military competition were felt most directly in the cities: troops were quartered in houses, markets were taxed or looted, and civil conflict damaged neighborhoods.

Primary Source Window

Al-Tanukhi, Nishwār al-muḥāḍara ("Table-Talk"), on Officials, Merchants, and Everyday Life in Buyid Iraq

Creator: Abu ʿAli al-Muhassin al-Tanukhi (d. 994), a judge and littérateur of Basra and Baghdad · Date: composed in the later tenth century · Type: Arabic collection of anecdotes, many recounted by the author's contemporaries

Context: Al-Tanukhi served as a judge in several Iraqi and Khuzestani towns under Buyid rule and gathered stories told by officials, secretaries, merchants, and others in the social circles he frequented. His compilations are valued by historians for their incidental detail about administrative life, social attitudes, and economic experience.

Thematic synthesis of the collection (not a quotation or paraphrase of one specific episode): Accounts in the collection describe secretaries and viziers who rise and fall with the favor of rulers, officials whose property is confiscated and who bargain for their lives, merchants and bankers who lend to the state and suffer when it fails to repay, and ordinary people who find help or hardship at the hands of the powerful. The narrators often present these accounts as lessons in fortune's reversals and in the importance of prudence, generosity, and sound judgment.

Analysis: The anecdotes illuminate the social world of the bureaucratic and mercantile elite and show how confiscation, patronage, and credit operated in practice. They reveal attitudes toward wealth, status, and authority, and they preserve details of daily life, such as how officials spent money, how merchants advanced funds, and how disputes were resolved, that formal chronicles rarely record.

Limitations: The stories are anecdotal and often shaped to illustrate a moral or to entertain, and their literal accuracy cannot be assumed. They were told by educated men about educated men, and the poor, women, and rural cultivators appear mostly as background figures. Identifying which stories reflect actual events and which are literary conventions requires caution. This is a thematic summary of the collection rather than a paraphrase of a single identifiable anecdote. The passage should not be quoted or treated as one discrete primary-source episode; individual stories require consultation of the edited Arabic text or a published translation.

Perspective: A judge and man of letters, writing for an elite audience of secretaries and scholars. Not represented: soldiers' own views, the experience of laborers and cultivators, and the viewpoint of the rulers who confiscated the wealth he describes.

Everyday Life and Social History

Neighborhoods, Crafts, Processions, Taxes, and the Costs of Occupation

For most inhabitants of Buyid cities, politics arrived as price, tax, and noise. A weaver in Karkh or a porter on the Tigris quays experienced Buyid rule through the cost of bread, which rose when grain shipments were disrupted, through market dues, and through the presence of troops quartered nearby. Craft production, including textiles, metalwork, paper, and leather, occupied large numbers of urban families, and artisans in a given trade clustered in particular districts and markets, where they shared the risks of fire, flood, and exaction.

Religious life shaped neighborhood rhythms. Friday prayers, Sufi and ascetic gatherings, and, after 963, Shiʿi processions on Ashura and festivals at Ghadir Khumm created public occasions that drew crowds and sometimes provoked disorder. Neighborhoods tended to align with religious identities, and street fighting between Sunni and Shiʿi quarters of Baghdad was a recurring danger. Households managed these pressures through kinship networks, communal institutions, and, for the better-off, patronage ties with officials.

Military occupation imposed its own costs. Soldiers billeted in houses, requisitioned supplies, and, when unpaid, plundered. Rulers' confiscations fell hardest on officials and merchants, but the poor suffered when conflicts disrupted provisioning or when fires, which were frequent in the dense, wooden-roofed quarters of the cities, spread during riots. Historians reconstruct this world from scattered notices in chronicles, anecdotes, and legal and geographic works, supplemented where possible by archaeology, and must acknowledge that the voices of laborers, women, and the enslaved are largely recoverable only indirectly.

Section 36.24 · Division VI — Religion, Scholarship, and the Culture of Buyid Rule

The Religious Identity of the Buyids: Shiʿism, Daylamite Background, and Political Pragmatism

The Buyids were Shiʿi, and they ruled a capital whose caliph and much of whose population were Sunni. This apparent paradox has generated both exaggerated claims and careless simplification, and the evidence supports neither the picture of a militantly Shiʿi regime imposing its faith nor the opposite picture of a dynasty indifferent to religion. This section examines what is known about the religious identity of the Buyid rulers and why it must be described with care.

The sources agree that the family had Shiʿi affiliations, but they say little about their doctrinal content. As Section 36.2 noted, the Caspian highlands from which the family came were a zone of Zaydi missionary activity, and some historians infer that the Buyid brothers initially had Zaydi leanings. Others point out that the Imami, or Twelver, community was strong in Iraq and western Iran and that Buyid rulers increasingly supported it. The evidence does not permit certainty. What can be said is that the Buyids revered the family of ʿAli, patronized the shrines and descendants of the Prophet's family, and showed more sympathy toward Shiʿi communities than the Abbasid caliphs or the Samanids did, without committing themselves publicly to a doctrinal program.

Their religious policy was pragmatic. The Buyids governed a population that was predominantly Sunni in Baghdad and varied elsewhere. They depended on Sunni and non-Muslim secretaries and on soldiers, many of them Turks, who were Sunni. They needed the caliph's legitimation. A policy of enforced Shiʿism would have been dangerous and, given their need for administrators and legitimacy, counterproductive. In practice they appointed Sunni judges, including the chief judge, supported Sunni as well as Shiʿi scholars, and generally left the legal and educational institutions of the Sunni majority intact. At the same time, they permitted and sometimes sponsored Shiʿi public observances, protected Shiʿi scholars, and promoted descendants of ʿAli to positions of honor.

Differences among rulers and regions are visible. Muʿizz al-Dawla, as Section 36.25 shows, was the most conspicuous sponsor of Shiʿi public ritual in Baghdad. ʿAḍud al-Dawla combined patronage of Shiʿi shrines with support for Sunni jurists and a wide-ranging interest in learning of all kinds. The viziers varied. Al-Sahib ibn ʿAbbad, who dominated the Rayy court, was associated with the Muʿtazili school of rational theology and with Shiʿi sympathies of a Zaydi or Imami cast, and he appointed the Muʿtazili scholar ʿAbd al-Jabbar as chief judge of Rayy. The Muʿtazila, a theological movement emphasizing divine justice and human reason, had its own relationship to Sunni and Shiʿi communities and cannot be identified with either. Several of the dynasty's rulers and viziers thus participated in intellectual currents that cut across sectarian lines.

Daylamite background mattered, but not in a deterministic way. The family's origins in a region of Zaydi proselytism and local religious variety may have predisposed them to a Shiʿi orientation and to a relaxed attitude toward doctrinal boundaries. Daylamite soldiers, in turn, brought with them their own religious habits, including reverence for the family of ʿAli, which formed part of the cultural environment in which public Shiʿi ritual developed. But the Daylamites were not uniformly pious or doctrinally literate, and some were reported to hold only nominal Islamic beliefs.

Students should distinguish four things that popular accounts often blur: the personal affiliation of the rulers; state patronage of particular institutions and rituals; the development of Shiʿi scholarship, much of it carried out by scholars working independently of the court; and the religious practices of ordinary people. The Buyid period was significant for all four, but they did not move in lockstep. Buyid patronage assisted the consolidation of Twelver learning in Iraq, but the dynasty did not turn Iran into a uniformly Shiʿi society. Sunni institutions remained influential, and the major conversion of the Iranian plateau to Twelver Shiʿism occurred centuries later, under the Safavids, as Volume V recounts.

Section 36.25

Ashura, Ghadir Khumm, and Public Religious Life in Baghdad

The most visible change in the religious landscape of Baghdad under the Buyids was the emergence of officially supported Shiʿi commemorations. Two observances stand out. Ashura, the tenth day of the month of Muharram, commemorates the death of Husayn ibn ʿAli, grandson of the Prophet, at Karbala in 680. Ghadir Khumm, observed on the eighteenth of Dhu al-Hijja, commemorates the occasion on which, according to Shiʿi tradition, the Prophet publicly designated ʿAli as his successor, a claim that Sunni tradition reads differently. For Shiʿi Muslims, both days carry profound significance. Before the Buyids, these observances were practiced privately or in small gatherings, and often at risk.

According to the chronicles, in 963 (Muharram of 352 AH), Muʿizz al-Dawla ordered that the market be closed and that the people of Baghdad observe public mourning for Husayn on Ashura. Shops were closed, and groups of mourners, including women, are reported to have processed through the streets in expressions of grief. The chroniclers place Muʿizz al-Dawla's public celebration of Ghadir Khumm later in the same Hijri year, 352/963, with festivities that included lighting, music, and public rejoicing. Some accounts add that he sanctioned inscriptions on mosque walls cursing Muʿawiya, the first Umayyad caliph, a measure that provoked Sunni resistance and was reportedly removed or altered. The exact details and dates vary among sources, and students should treat the specific sequence as approximate.

The effects were significant. State sponsorship gave Shiʿi observances a public legitimacy and visibility they had lacked. The processions and festivals allowed Shiʿi Muslims, concentrated in neighborhoods such as Karkh, to express identity openly and to organize communal life around shared commemoration. The performances also became occasions for confrontation. Sunni residents, especially in neighborhoods with Hanbali influence, objected to practices they considered offensive and to what they saw as insults to the Prophet's Companions. Riots, street fighting, and arson recurred in the following decades, and the sources record major disturbances in the 970s, 990s, and 1050s. Some Sunnis responded with counter-observances, and the chroniclers mention commemorations that Sunnis organized to honor the Prophet and his Companion Abu Bakr, which functioned as rival public rituals.

Buyid rulers' responses varied. Muʿizz al-Dawla was the principal initiator. Later rulers sometimes continued, sometimes restrained, and sometimes banned the observances according to political circumstances, in some years sending troops to separate the quarters and in others prohibiting public displays. The caliphs, whose own position gave them reason to be wary, lacked the power to prevent the rituals but encouraged Sunni counter-mobilization when they could. In Baghdad, therefore, public religion became a contested political arena in which a ruler's choices about permission or prohibition signaled alliances and risked disorder.

Religious History Study

Neighborhood affiliations, legal schools, and theological currents overlapped, and many Muslims held positions that do not fit neat categories. Conflicts in Baghdad often combined religious, economic, and neighborhood rivalries. The Buyid-sponsored observances introduced a new element of public contestation, but they did not create a permanent and uniform divide.

Section 36.26

The Abbasid Caliph, Sunni Institutions, and Shiʿi Religious Scholars

Under Buyid rule, Sunni caliphal authority coexisted with Shiʿi patronage, and the coexistence was managed through institutions: the judiciary, the leadership of the descendants of ʿAli, the shrines, and the networks of scholars. This section outlines how these worked. The fuller institutional history of Sunni and Shiʿi scholarship belongs to Chapter 40, and the discussion here is limited to the political relationships.

The judiciary remained largely in Sunni hands. The chief judge in Baghdad, appointed by the caliph on the Buyid ruler's recommendation or approval, was normally a Sunni jurist, often Hanafi or Shafiʿi, and local judges applied the law of the Sunni schools to most disputes, though Imami communities had their own scholars and arbiters. Buyid rulers valued judges' capacity to confer legitimacy on legal transactions, and they did not generally seek to replace them. Judges, for their part, could serve as intermediaries between rulers and the population, and several were prominent in the diplomacy of the period.

The Prophet's descendants held a distinctive place. The office of naqīb, the representative and chief of the descendants of ʿAli (the Talibids) and sometimes of the Abbasid family, carried honor, administrative responsibility for the descendants' affairs, and often substantial influence. Under the Buyids, members of ʿAlid families, including the family of al-Sharif al-Radi, obtained the post of naqib of the Talibids, and the office became a channel for Shiʿi leadership within a Sunni caliphal order. Al-Sharif al-Radi, who lived from 970 to 1015, was both a distinguished poet and the compiler of a famous collection of sayings and sermons attributed to ʿAli, and his brother al-Sharif al-Murtada was a leading theologian.

Imami scholarship flourished in this environment. Ibn Babawayh, known as al-Shaykh al-Saduq, worked at Rayy and died in 991, producing influential collections of Imami traditions and law. In Baghdad, al-Shaykh al-Mufid, who died in 1022, became the leading Imami theologian and jurist, and his student al-Sharif al-Murtada continued his work. Al-Shaykh al-Tusi, who came to Baghdad in the early eleventh century and studied under them, left for Najaf after the political upheavals of the 1050s. These scholars wrote, taught, and debated, sometimes in public disputations that attracted Sunni and Muʿtazili participants. A library established at Karkh in 991 by the vizier Shapur ibn Ardashir, under Bahaʾ al-Dawla, became a center of learning, though it was destroyed in the turmoil that followed the end of Buyid rule. The deeper history of this scholarly tradition is the subject of Chapter 40.

Shrines were a further focus. The tombs of imams at Najaf, Karbala, and Kazimayn near Baghdad attracted pilgrims, and Buyid rulers contributed to their building and endowment. ʿAḍud al-Dawla's patronage of Najaf has been noted. These shrines gave Shiʿi communities sacred centers and helped establish Iraq as the heartland of Twelver devotion, a role it retained. They also became targets in sectarian conflict, and the sources record damage to shrines by fire and attack at different times.

The caliph's own position in this arrangement was ambiguous. The caliphs, especially al-Qadir (r. 991 to 1031), sought to assert a Sunni religious identity for the caliphate and to rally Sunni scholars around it. Al-Qadir's circle produced a creedal statement in the early eleventh century, the so-called Qadiri creed, defining the Sunni position against Shiʿi, Muʿtazili, and other views, and it was publicized in the 1020s. This Sunni consolidation, which Chapter 40 treats more fully, did not break the Buyid arrangement but prepared the ground for the later alliance between the caliphate and the Seljuk sultans. In this sense, the Buyid period both strengthened Shiʿi institutions and stimulated a Sunni revival, two developments that fed one another.

Section 36.27

The Buyids and Their Islamic Rivals: Fatimids, Hamdanids, and Regional Powers

The Buyids did not operate in isolation. They competed with rival dynasties for territory and for the symbolic capital of legitimacy, and the contest was most acute around Iraq and the claim to speak for the Islamic community as a whole. This section examines their relations with three groups of rivals: the Hamdanids, the Fatimids, and the neighboring dynasties of Iran and the Arab frontier.

The Hamdanids, an Arab family that held Mosul and, under Sayf al-Dawla, Aleppo, were the Buyids' nearest competitors. Hamdanid princes had held the office of amir al-umara in Baghdad before 945, and Hamdanid power in the Jazira bordered Buyid Iraq. Muʿizz al-Dawla campaigned against them and extracted tribute, as Section 36.9 described, and ʿAḍud al-Dawla drove Abu Taghlib from Mosul in 979. The Hamdanids of Aleppo, famous as patrons of the poet al-Mutanabbi and under Byzantine pressure, were less directly involved with the Buyids. Eventually, both branches declined, and other Arab dynasties, including the Uqaylids in the Jazira and the Mazyadids in central Iraq, took advantage of Buyid weakness to build autonomous principalities.

The Fatimids presented a different kind of challenge. This Ismaʿili Shiʿi dynasty, which had established a caliphate in Tunisia in 909, conquered Egypt in 969 and moved its capital to Cairo in 973. The Fatimid caliphs claimed descent from ʿAli and Fatima and asserted that they, not the Abbasids, were the legitimate leaders of all Muslims. Their propaganda network reached into Iraq, Iran, and beyond, and their armies contested Syria. For the Buyids, the Fatimids posed a double threat: a rival universal claim and a competitor for the Shiʿi loyalty that the Buyids themselves sought to cultivate. This is one reason the Buyids maintained the Abbasid caliphate. A Shiʿi dynasty that deposed the Abbasid in favor of a Fatimid would have subordinated itself to a rival, and one that elevated an ʿAlid of its own, as the later tradition about Muʿizz al-Dawla suggests, would have invited comparison with the Fatimids. The Abbasid caliph, whose Sunni identity distinguished him from the Fatimids, served as a counterweight. The Turkish commander Alptakin's attempt to establish himself in Syria in the 970s ended in defeat by Fatimid forces, which closed the Buyid prospects there. The theme returns in the early eleventh century, when in 1011 the caliph al-Qadir sponsored a public declaration, signed by leading Sunni and Shiʿi notables in Baghdad, denying the genealogical claims of the Fatimid rulers. The Fatimid challenge in its wider Ismaʿili setting is a subject for Chapter 41.

Among Iranian neighbors, the Samanids to the northeast recognized the Abbasid caliph and exchanged embassies and, at times, armies with the Buyids, contesting Rayy and the northern Jibal. The Ziyarids of Gurgan and Tabaristan maintained a more tenuous independence. In the later decades of the dynasty, regional powers such as the Kakuyids at Isfahan and Hamadan, the Kurdish Hasanwayhids, and the Daylamite and Kurdish dynasties of Azerbaijan arose in the space left by Buyid fragmentation, as Section 36.31 examines. The Qarmatians of eastern Arabia, whose raids on Iraq and the pilgrimage routes had troubled the Abbasids, were handled by a mixture of negotiation and force. In Oman and on the Gulf, Buyid rulers contended with local dynasties and maritime interests.

What this pattern shows is that legitimacy was a resource contested among competing claimants, and that the Buyid retention of the Abbasid caliphate was embedded in a wider competition. The claims of the Abbasids, the Fatimids, and, from the west, the Umayyad rulers of al-Andalus, who also adopted the caliphal title in 929, produced a world with several caliphs, which made the identification of the Baghdad caliph as a legitimate Sunni authority all the more valuable to those who could control him.

Section 36.28

Court Patronage, Historical Writing, Literature, and Intellectual Life

The Buyid courts were centers of intellectual and literary life, and the culture they supported was overwhelmingly Arabic in language, though many of its practitioners were Iranian in origin. This section surveys the patronage of the courts of Baghdad, Shiraz, Rayy, and Isfahan, leaving the technical history of science, medicine, and philosophy to Chapter 43.

Patronage was a function of political culture. A ruler who supported poets, secretaries, and scholars acquired prestige and, through panegyric and dedication, spread his fame. The viziers who headed the administrations were themselves patrons, and their households served as literary salons. At Rayy, Ibn al-ʿAmid and later al-Sahib ibn ʿAbbad gathered poets and men of letters, and al-Sahib was celebrated for his epistles, his poetry, and a large library, with later tradition giving fantastical figures for its size. The poet al-Mutanabbi, whose Arabic verse is among the most admired of the age, visited Buyid courts in the 960s, composing poems for Ibn al-ʿAmid and ʿAḍud al-Dawla, before he was killed on the road in 965. In Baghdad, Abu Ishaq al-Sabiʾ, the chancery head, was famed as a prose stylist. The grammarian Abu ʿAli al-Farisi, who worked at Shiraz and Baghdad and died in 987, taught ʿAḍud al-Dawla and wrote for him. The astronomer ʿAbd al-Rahman al-Sufi, who died in 986, also enjoyed ʿAḍud al-Dawla's favor, according to the tradition.

Historical writing flourished. Ibn Miskawayh's Tajārib al-umam was the most ambitious, and it also reflected a distinctive approach to history, emphasizing practical lessons for administrators and rulers drawn from the experience of earlier ages. Ibn Miskawayh was also a philosopher, and his work on ethics, a subject Chapter 43 will treat, shows the connection between historical and moral reflection in his circle. Hilal al-Sabiʾ, grandson of Abu Ishaq, wrote on court ceremonial and continued history in the early eleventh century. The anthologist al-Thaʿalibi, working in Nishapur, compiled a huge collection of contemporary Arabic poets that preserves the verse of many Buyid-era writers. The adab tradition of anecdote and entertaining instruction, represented by al-Tanukhi, flourished alongside it.

Not all was harmony. Abu Hayyan al-Tawhidi, a philosopher and essayist who frequented the circles of viziers, left bitter portraits of the patrons whom he served and from whom he sought support, in works that criticize both al-Sahib and Ibn al-ʿAmid and that record the frustrations of a dependent intellectual. His writings are a valuable counterweight to the courtly praise of panegyric, and they show the precariousness of intellectual life under patronage.

Libraries and institutions of learning supported this culture. The library at Shiraz, the library of al-Sahib at Rayy, and the library of Shapur ibn Ardashir at Baghdad are among those recorded. Some scholars, including the physician and philosopher Ibn Sina, who was born in 980, served Buyid and related courts in Hamadan and Isfahan in the early eleventh century, a subject for Chapter 43. The ʿAḍudi hospital, described earlier, linked scholarship to public service.

The relationship between Arabic scholarship and Iranian political patronage deserves emphasis. Buyid rulers were not Arabic speakers by origin, yet they supported Arabic literature and learning lavishly, because Arabic was the language of the Islamic empire's culture, administration, and law, and because patronage of its masters conferred prestige. At the same time, the Persian-language literary revival associated with the Samanid east, treated in Chapter 35, and the later flowering of Persian poetry discussed in Chapter 42, drew on different patrons and centers. The Buyid courts thus illustrate that the Iranian role in Islamic culture did not depend on the replacement of Arabic by Persian. Persian-speaking officials, writing in Arabic, created some of the finest works of the Arabic literary tradition, a point that complicates any simple equation of language with identity.

Section 36.29 · Division VII — Fragmentation, Rival Dynasties, and the End of Buyid Rule

The Succession Crisis after 983: Sharaf al-Dawla, Samsam al-Dawla, and the Buyid Family

ʿAḍud al-Dawla died in Baghdad in March 983, at about forty-seven, and the structure he had built did not long survive him. The succession crisis that followed is instructive because it demonstrates, in a short span, how dependent the unification of 978 to 983 had been on a single person, and because it shows the political forces that thereafter shaped the dynasty: army factions, regional administrations, ambitious princes, and the caliph.

The specialist historical account does not establish that ʿAḍud al-Dawla designated a successor. At his death, his son Marzuban, later Samsam al-Dawla, was in Baghdad and was proclaimed amir al-umara by the officials and commanders there; the caliph al-Taʾiʿ then conferred his titles. Another son, Shirzil, later Sharaf al-Dawla, controlled Kirman and disputed his brother's claim from the outset. The absence of an accepted succession settlement exposed the limits of ʿAḍud al-Dawla's personal supremacy. Sharaf al-Dawla, who was in the east, seized Shiraz and Fars and then turned to claim Iraq. In the Jibal, the brother who had been ʿAḍud al-Dawla's lieutenant, Muʾayyad al-Dawla, died in 983, and Fakhr al-Dawla, who had been in exile since 980 or 981, returned in 984 with the support of al-Sahib ibn ʿAbbad, the vizier of the Rayy branch, and recovered Rayy, Hamadan, and Isfahan. The territories that ʿAḍud al-Dawla had gathered thus came apart along the lines of the older confederation, now with new heirs and new grievances.

The conflict between Samsam al-Dawla and Sharaf al-Dawla occupied the following years. Sharaf al-Dawla seized Khuzestan and Basra and, in 987, took Baghdad itself, forcing Samsam al-Dawla out. The caliph, as always, accepted the new master, and Sharaf al-Dawla received the customary honors. He did not rule long. He died in 989, and his brother Bahaʾ al-Dawla took the succession in Baghdad. Samsam al-Dawla, meanwhile, recovered a position in Fars, which he held through the 990s, and the two brothers struggled for the rest of the decade. In 991 Bahaʾ al-Dawla, who needed money and was in conflict with the caliph, removed al-Taʾiʿ and installed al-Qadir, as the timeline in Section 36.9 noted. According to the historical narrative, Samsam al-Dawla was assassinated by a Buyid prince near Shiraz in late 998; Bahaʾ al-Dawla then extended his control over Fars.

Why did the unity of 978 to 983 prove impossible to preserve? Several reasons stand out. First, the absence of institutionalized succession. The Buyids had no recognized rule that determined who inherited which territory, and no uncontested successor had been designated by ʿAḍud al-Dawla; soldiers and commanders helped determine the outcome. Second, the persistence of regional bases. The revenue and army of Fars, of Iraq, and of the Jibal remained largely distinct, so that an heir who held one region commanded the resources to contest the others. Third, the factionalism of the army. Daylamite and Turkish contingents supported different claimants, and mutinies over pay played into each succession. Fourth, the role of viziers and regional bureaucracies, whose own interests tied them to particular princes. Al-Sahib's support for Fakhr al-Dawla is the clearest case. Fifth, the family's own structure: with several adult princes, each with followers, the available resources could not satisfy all claims.

The result was a period of warfare and shifting alliances rather than collapse. In the sources the 980s and 990s appear as a time of troubles, but they also saw ongoing government, patronage, and scholarship. Buyid princes continued to rule important territories and to claim the caliph's recognition. The dynasty's problem was not that it ceased to exist but that the personal supremacy that had briefly made it a single polity could not be recreated by the generation that followed. This was a structural limitation, not a failure of any one prince.

Section 36.30

Bahaʾ al-Dawla, the Eastern Branches, and Continuing Dynastic Competition

It would be wrong to read the period after 983 as an unbroken decline. Bahaʾ al-Dawla, the ruler who emerged from the struggles of the 980s and 990s, achieved a significant reconsolidation of power, and the dynasty's other branches continued to command substantial resources. The political history of the late tenth and early eleventh centuries is better understood as a series of shifting configurations than as a straight line downward.

Bahaʾ al-Dawla, who was born in 971 and ruled in Iraq from 989 until his death in 1012, was the most successful ruler of the post-ʿAḍudi generation. He faced a Baghdad that was financially weak, politically volatile, and filled with troops of uncertain loyalty, and he dealt with its problems through a combination of concession, force, and expedient. He changed the caliph in 991, accumulated honorific titles from the new caliph, al-Qadir, and, after the death of Samsam al-Dawla in 998, brought Fars under his authority, giving him control of Iraq, Fars, Khuzestan, and Kirman. His government employed capable viziers and patronized scholarship, and the library of Shapur ibn Ardashir dates from his reign. But he also faced the erosion of Buyid control at the margins: the Uqaylids took Mosul, the Mazyadids established themselves in central Iraq, and Kurdish and other local dynasties carved out autonomous spaces.

A notable feature of Bahaʾ al-Dawla's rule was the growing emphasis on Shiraz and Fars. He spent a considerable time in Fars, and he died at Arrajan, on the border of Fars and Khuzestan, in 1012. This shift reflected the relative strength of Fars's economy and its military reliability, and it left Baghdad under deputies. After his death, his sons divided and contested power. Sultan al-Dawla held Fars and, for a time, Iraq. Musharrif al-Dawla and then Jalal al-Dawla held Iraq in the following decades, often in conflict with their brothers and nephews. The pattern of kin rivalry persisted, and it interacted with the weakness of the Iraqi treasury and the demands of the army.

In the Jibal, the eastern branch had its own history. Fakhr al-Dawla, restored in 984 with al-Sahib's help, ruled the Rayy region until his death in 997. His son Majd al-Dawla was a child, and the government of Rayy was taken by his mother, known to the sources as al-Sayyida, "the Lady," whose given name appears as Shirin in some accounts. She governed for her son, first as regent and then, because Majd al-Dawla proved unable or unwilling to rule vigorously, for most of the following three decades. Her rule is one of the most remarkable episodes of the era. The chroniclers describe her as a capable administrator who managed the revenue, supervised the army, received petitioners, and negotiated with neighboring rulers, and they treat her competence as noteworthy precisely because the conventions of their world did not expect it. Her task was formidable. Another son, Shams al-Dawla, held Hamadan and contested her authority, Daylamite troops repeatedly demanded concessions, and powerful neighbors, among them the Kurdish prince Badr ibn Hasanwayh and, increasingly, Mahmud of Ghazna, pressed on Rayy. She made use of diplomacy, payment of tribute, and the support of allied rulers. The sources say less than students might like about the precise institutions of her administration, but they leave no doubt of her political skill. She died around 1028, and with her passing, the protection that she had provided to Rayy ended.

The importance of these developments lies in what they show about Buyid resilience and weakness. The dynasty retained, throughout the early eleventh century, large territories, cities, and revenues, and it had capable rulers and administrators. Yet the divisions among its branches, the pressure on its frontiers, and the dependence of each ruler on unpredictable armies meant that every succession and every fiscal crisis threatened its position. The Buyid world in 1010 was not collapsing, but it lacked the capacity to meet a major external challenge, and such a challenge was gathering in the east.

Section 36.31

The Weakening of Buyid Power: Military Rivalries, Regional Separatism, and Ghaznavid Expansion

The eleventh century saw a combination of internal and external pressures that eroded Buyid authority. Understanding the decline requires attention to several factors operating together, not to a single cause such as military weakness. This section examines these factors and then the Ghaznavid seizure of Rayy in 1029, the event that most visibly changed the political geography of western Iran before the Seljuk conquest.

Fiscal difficulties persisted. Baghdad's treasury could not reliably pay its soldiers, and the reign of Jalal al-Dawla, who held Iraq from 1025 to 1044, was marked by recurrent military mutinies, the flight of rulers, and appeals to outside parties for support. The sources describe periods in which the ruler was effectively a hostage of his own troops and in which the caliph exercised more independence than at any time since 945. In 1037, Jalal al-Dawla accepted a grander title, "king of kings," which, as Section 36.18 noted, provoked jurists' objections and the caliph's reluctance, a sign of the contested nature of Buyid prestige in this period.

Military rivalries within the Buyid armies continued. Daylamite and Turkish factions competed for control, and commanders increasingly acted independently of the ruler. Urban disturbances were a recurring problem in Baghdad, where Sunni and Shiʿi neighborhoods fought and gangs exploited disorder, and the sources mention fires and riots with some frequency. The rulers lacked the resources to impose lasting order, and the caliph and the leaders of the Sunni community began to look for stronger protectors.

Regional separatism was the third factor. Throughout the Buyid domains, local dynasties and military commanders asserted autonomy. In the Jibal, the Kakuyids, a family related by marriage to the Buyids, established themselves at Isfahan and Yazd, at first as subordinates and then as largely independent rulers. The Kurdish Hasanwayhids held districts around Hamadan and Dinavar. In northern Mesopotamia, the Uqaylids held Mosul, and in central Iraq the Mazyadids ruled around Hilla. In Azerbaijan and Arran, Daylamite and Kurdish families established their own principalities. In Khuzestan and Fars, local commanders and tribal leaders played increasingly independent roles. These powers sometimes acknowledged Buyid overlordship and sometimes did not, and the sources show them making and breaking alliances as it suited them. Their rise did not require the Buyids to be militarily weak so much as it required the Buyid branches to be divided and their resources committed elsewhere.

The Ghaznavids supplied the external shock. The Ghaznavid dynasty, which Chapter 37 treats in full, arose in the Afghan highlands under Turkish commanders formerly in Samanid service, and under Mahmud, who ruled from 998 to 1030, it became a major power that conquered large parts of eastern Iran, Afghanistan, and northwestern India. As Samanid authority collapsed around the turn of the century, Mahmud turned his attention westward. In 1029 he moved against Rayy, where Majd al-Dawla, weakened by the death of his mother and by troop disorder, was unable to resist. The Ghaznavid army took the city, and Majd al-Dawla was captured and sent into custody in the east. The sources, mostly written from a Sunni perspective, report that Mahmud also acted against Shiʿi, Muʿtazili, and Ismaʿili figures in Rayy, and that books were burned, though the details are reported with the partisan coloring typical of the sources. The conquest ended the Buyid branch at Rayy and brought Ghaznavid power to the borders of the Jibal. Mahmud died in 1030, and his successors could not consolidate the gain, but the damage to the Buyid position was done.

The result was a transformed political map of western Iran. Rayy was no longer Buyid. The Kakuyids at Isfahan and other local rulers dominated much of the Jibal, and the Buyids' remaining strength lay in Fars, Khuzestan, Kirman, and Iraq. Meanwhile, a new force was emerging: Turkmen groups led by the Seljuk family, who defeated the Ghaznavids at Dandanqan in 1040 and moved quickly into western Iran. The Buyids, divided and distracted, faced them from a position of fragmentation. Students should note that this sequence, not an abrupt military collapse, produced the situation in which Seljuk power could enter Baghdad.

Section 36.32

The Seljuk Entry into Baghdad, 1055, and the Last Buyids in Fars, 1062

The final years of Buyid supremacy in Baghdad unfolded against a background of deepening disorder and approaching Seljuk power. This section narrates the end briefly, because Chapter 38 examines the Seljuk conquest and the political order that followed, and it distinguishes carefully between the end of Buyid rule in Baghdad in 1055 and the final disappearance of the dynasty in Fars in 1062.

After Jalal al-Dawla's death in 1044, the Buyid prince Abu Kalijar, who held Fars and Khuzestan, took Baghdad and for a few years reunited Iraq and Fars under a single ruler. He died in 1048, and the succession in Iraq passed to his son al-Malik al-Rahim Khusraw Firuz, the last Buyid ruler of Baghdad, while another branch of the family, led by his brother Fulad Sutun, held Fars. The family quarreled, as it had for generations, and the Iraqi regime was fiscally and militarily frail. In Baghdad, the Turkish commander Arslan al-Basasiri accumulated power and provoked the hostility of the caliph's vizier. The caliph al-Qaʾim, who reigned from 1031 to 1075, observed the approach of the Seljuks, who had by then absorbed much of the Jibal, with concern and with interest. Many Sunni leaders welcomed the prospect of a Sunni protector.

In December 1055, the Seljuk leader Tughril Beg entered Baghdad. According to the sources, the caliph welcomed him, the Friday sermon was read in the name of the Seljuk ruler, and al-Malik al-Rahim was arrested and later held in captivity, where he died. Buyid military supremacy over the caliphate ended. Tughril Beg and his successors assumed the role of protector that the Buyids had played, but in the name of the Sunni order and with a different composition of military support. The dynamics of the Seljuk settlement, including the titles Tughril received, his relationship with the caliph, and the later turmoil associated with al-Basasiri's rebellion, are the subject of Chapter 38 and are not narrated here.

The end of Buyid rule in Baghdad was not the immediate extinction of the family in Fars. Abu Mansur Fulad Sutun, a son of Abu Kalijar, continued to represent Buyid dynastic authority there, although his position was increasingly precarious and he had accepted Seljuk overlordship. The Shabankara leader Fadlūya seized Shiraz around 1056–57 and, according to the specialist account, overthrew and killed Fulad Sutun in 1062. Control of Shiraz was contested: the Seljuk prince Qavurt took the city from Fadlūya in 1062, while Alp Arslan's more substantial intervention in Fars occurred later in the 1060s. Thus 1062 marks the end of the surviving Buyid line in Fars, not a conquest of Shiraz by Alp Arslan, who did not succeed Tughril as sultan until 1063. The distinction between 1055 and 1062 remains important, but it should not be mistaken for seven years of stable, independent Buyid control over Shiraz.

Interpreting the end requires resisting simple explanations. The Buyids did not fall solely because their army had weakened, and the Seljuks did not triumph solely because of superior force. Succession rivalries, divided resources, fiscal troubles, urban disorder, and the rise of regional dynasties all weakened the Buyids, and the Seljuks benefited from the Sunni caliphate's willingness to accept a new protector, from the Seljuk army's strength, and from the opportunity that the Ghaznavid defeat and Buyid fragmentation created. The contingency of the outcome deserves emphasis. A different sequence of successions, or a more durable fiscal base, might have prolonged Buyid power. The outcome depended on the interaction of all these factors.

Section 36.33 · Division VIII — Historical Synthesis

The Buyid Legacy: Iranian Military Rule and the Transformation of the Caliphate

The chapter's central question was how a dynasty of Daylamite military rulers could govern the heart of the Abbasid caliphate without abolishing it, and what this arrangement revealed about the transformation of political authority in the medieval Islamic world. The preceding sections have supplied the evidence for an answer. This final section draws the findings together and considers why Buyid practices mattered after Buyid rule ended.

The first finding is that the Buyids separated military and administrative control from religious legitimacy more systematically than any previous regime. Military control of Baghdad had been in the hands of commanders since the ninth century, and the office of amir al-umara had formalized it. What the Buyids added was dynastic continuity and a developed political vocabulary. Their rulers held honorific titles from the caliph, were invested through diplomas and ceremonies, and had their names proclaimed in the sermon and struck on coins alongside the caliph's. This arrangement made the Abbasid caliph a source of legitimation that rulers acquired from him, though they could not command him, and it set a pattern that the Seljuk sultans, and later rulers, would follow.

The second finding is that the Buyid state was a confederation of kin-ruled principalities, not an empire. The unification under ʿAḍud al-Dawla was an exception that depended on his person. Understanding this structure explains why the dynasty reached from Fars to Mosul yet could not hold its territories together, and it connects to a long pattern in Islamic and Iranian political history in which ruling families treated territory as shared patrimony, a pattern that recurs in later dynasties.

The third finding concerns administration. The Buyids inherited the Abbasid dīvāns and the class of secretaries who staffed them, adapted them to a decentralized dynastic system, and relied on the loyalty and patronage networks of viziers and officials. Their fiscal practices, especially the extensive use of revenue assignments to soldiers, reshaped the relationship between armies and agrarian taxation. Though the precise forms varied and should not be confused with feudalism, the practices contributed to the institutions that the Seljuks and their vizier Nizam al-Mulk would systematize, a subject for Chapter 39.

The fourth finding is that Buyid rule transformed the religious landscape without making Iran uniformly Shiʿi. Buyid patronage permitted public Shiʿi commemorations, protected Imami scholars, and supported shrines, contributing to the consolidation of Twelver learning and devotion in Iraq. It also provoked Sunni mobilization and, through it, helped stimulate the Sunni revival that Chapter 40 examines. The relationship between rulers' religious affiliation, state patronage, scholarship, and popular practice was complex and changed over time.

The fifth finding concerns identity and symbol. Buyid rulers combined Daylamite military practice, Iranian royal imagery, Arabic learning, and Islamic legitimacy. They did not make Baghdad a Persian-speaking capital, and they did not pursue a program that can be equated with modern Iranian nationalism. They did show that Iranian royal symbols could be incorporated into an Islamic political order, which later rulers, including the Ghaznavids and the Seljuks, would also do, and they prepared the ground for the historical memory work of Chapter 42. The Buyid courts also confirm that Persian-origin officials wrote some of the finest Arabic prose, complicating any equation of language with ethnicity.

The sixth finding is that urban life and intellectual culture flourished within fiscal and military instability. The courts of Baghdad, Shiraz, Rayy, and Isfahan supported historians, poets, grammarians, physicians, and libraries, and the hospital and dam of ʿAḍud al-Dawla testify to ambitious public works. At the same time, cultivators, laborers, and the poor bore the costs of taxation, confiscation, and conflict, and the sources record them chiefly through indirect traces. A balanced assessment must hold both facts together.

Finally, the Buyid experience illustrates the contingency of history. The caliphate might have been abolished in 946, or replaced by a rival, or protected by a different kind of ruler. The Buyids' choice to preserve it reflected a calculation about religion, soldiers, and rivals, and the survival of the caliphate until the Mongol conquest of Baghdad in 1258, a subject for Chapter 45, owes much to that choice. The dynasty's fall likewise was not inevitable. In a century of rule, the Buyids reshaped the relationship between power and legitimacy in the Islamic world, and the institutions they stabilized outlasted them. That is the reason the Buyid period holds a lasting place in the history of Iran and of Islamic political life.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Buyids transformed the Abbasid caliphate from a government in which the caliph claimed supreme political and religious authority into a political order in which Iranian military rulers exercised much of the effective governing power while the caliph remained an important source of Islamic legitimacy. The transformation was neither a straightforward overthrow of the caliphate nor an Iranian restoration of Sasanian kingship. It was the creation of a durable arrangement in which command of armies, revenue, and administration was separated from religious, legal, and ceremonial authority.

The Buyids rose from the Daylamite military networks of the Caspian highlands, a region of mountain societies, infantry traditions, and Zaydi missionary influence. Three sons of Abu Shuja Buya, ʿAli, Hasan, and Ahmad, advanced in the service of Daylamite and Ziyarid commanders, above all Mardavij ibn Ziyar, and between about 932 and 945 established separate bases in Fars, the Jibal, and Khuzestan. The separation of these bases reflected geography, the personal loyalties of their armies, the need to pay troops from local revenue, and Daylamite family custom, and it gave the dynasty the character of a confederation of kin-ruled principalities rather than a unitary state.

The Abbasid government meanwhile had lost control of its own capital. Fiscal decline, military dependence on Turkish commanders, provincial separatism, and security crises had led the caliphs to create the office of amir al-umara in 936, and a succession of commanders had held it with little stability. When Ahmad ibn Buya entered Baghdad on 19 December 945, he replaced one unstable protector with a stronger one. Within weeks, the deposition of al-Mustakfi and the installation of al-Mutiʿ showed where power lay, yet the Buyids retained the caliphate, for reasons that included the caliph's legitimating function, the difficulty of controlling an ʿAlid claimant, the Sunni composition of much of the population and army, and the competition of rival claimants to universal authority, notably the Fatimids.

The resulting division of functions was unequal but institutionally complex. The Buyid ruler commanded troops, controlled revenue, and appointed officials; the caliph conferred investiture and titles, appointed judges in principle, led the community symbolically, and appeared in the sermon and on the coinage. Historians disagree about whether to describe this as Buyid rule over the caliphate, government in the caliph's name, or a negotiated order of personal loyalties, and the debate turns largely on how to weigh practical control against legitimating authority.

Within the confederation, the three principal branches faced different conditions. Fars, under ʿImad al-Dawla and then ʿAḍud al-Dawla, was fiscally secure and well governed. The Jibal under Rukn al-Dawla was a frontier state managing the Samanids, Ziyarids, and local rulers. Iraq under Muʿizz al-Dawla and Bakhtiyar held the greatest prestige but suffered from fiscal deficit, army rivalry, and urban unrest. The weakness of Bakhtiyar's regime and the end of Rukn al-Dawla's restraining seniority allowed ʿAḍud al-Dawla to take Baghdad in 978 and to establish, by 981, personal supremacy over most of the Buyid world.

ʿAḍud al-Dawla's reign was the dynasty's height. His government combined personal supervision, an intelligence and postal network, attention to finance, and the employment of secretaries of varied backgrounds. His public works, including the ʿAḍudi hospital in Baghdad, the Band-e Amir dam near Persepolis, canal restoration, and a library at Shiraz, benefited subjects and expressed a conception of kingship. His titles, among them shahanshah and Taj al-Milla, combined Iranian royal imagery with caliphal investiture. Historians interpret this combination as an Iranian restoration, an Islamic military government, or a layered repertoire, and the evidence best supports the last, with Arabic remaining the language of administration and learning.

The Buyid system of government rested on inherited Abbasid dīvāns adapted to decentralized dynastic rule, on viziers and secretaries such as al-Muhallabi, Ibn al-ʿAmid, and al-Sahib ibn ʿAbbad, and on fiscal practices including tax farming, confiscation, and iqtaʿ assignments to soldiers. The army, composed of Daylamite infantry and Turkic cavalry, was costly and factional, and pay disputes frequently produced mutiny. The economy combined irrigated agriculture in the Sawad, Fars, and elsewhere with regional crafts and long-distance trade, and cities such as Baghdad, Shiraz, Rayy, and Isfahan were centers of social complexity whose inhabitants experienced Buyid rule as taxation, military presence, and religious politics.

Buyid religious policy was pragmatic and varied. The rulers' Shiʿi affiliation, rooted in a region of Zaydi activity and developing toward patronage of Imami communities, led to officially supported commemorations of Ashura and Ghadir Khumm from 963, to patronage of shrines and scholars such as al-Mufid and the Sharifs al-Radi and al-Murtada, and to Sunni counter-mobilization. Yet Sunni judges, jurists, and the caliphate itself remained influential, and the dynasty did not make Iran uniformly Shiʿi. Courts at Baghdad, Shiraz, Rayy, and Isfahan patronized Arabic historians, poets, grammarians, and scholars, including Ibn Miskawayh and al-Sahib ibn ʿAbbad.

After 983, the unity achieved by ʿAḍud al-Dawla dissolved among his heirs, but the dynasty did not simply decline. Bahaʾ al-Dawla reconsolidated Iraq and Fars, and Sayyida governed the Rayy principality with notable political skill. Over time, fiscal strain, army factionalism, regional separatism by dynasties such as the Kakuyids, Uqaylids, and Mazyadids, and external pressure combined to weaken the Buyids. Mahmud of Ghazna took Rayy in 1029, the Seljuks defeated the Ghaznavids in 1040, and Tughril Beg entered Baghdad in December 1055, ending Buyid military supremacy over the caliphate. The last surviving Buyid ruler in Fars, Fulad Sutun, was overthrown and killed by the Shabankara leader Fadlūya in 1062, after Shiraz had already changed hands amid local and Seljuk competition.

The legacy of the Buyids lay in the stabilization of a pattern in which military rulers governed under caliphal legitimation, in confederative dynastic politics, in administrative and fiscal practices that later regimes refined, in the public life and scholarship of Twelver Shiʿism and the Sunni revival it provoked, and in the demonstration that Iranian royal symbolism could be integrated into an Islamic political order. These developments shaped the Seljuk, Ghaznavid, and later states, even as the Buyid dynasty itself disappeared.

Key Terms

Buyids (Buwayhids)
A dynasty of Daylamite origin descended from Abu Shuja Buya, ruling through several related branches in Fars, the Jibal, and Iraq between the 930s and 1062.
Daylam / Daylamites
The mountainous Caspian highlands and their inhabitants, who supplied infantry to many regional rulers; a regional and military identity, not a synonym for Persian.
Gilan and the Gilites
The Caspian lowland and its population, distinct from but related to the Daylamites; Mardavij ibn Ziyar was a Gilite.
Zaydism
A branch of Shiʿism holding that the imamate may belong to any qualified descendant of ʿAli and Fatima who rises against unjust rule; influential in the Caspian highlands.
Twelver (Imami) Shiʿism
The Shiʿi tradition recognizing twelve imams, the last in occultation; its scholarship consolidated in Iraq and Rayy under Buyid patronage.
Abbasid caliphate
The dynasty descended from the Prophet's uncle ʿAbbas, holding the caliphal office in Baghdad; under the Buyids its religious and legal authority persisted while military and fiscal control passed to others.
Amir al-umara
"Commander of commanders," an office created in 936 giving a military commander control of the army and much of the administration under the caliph.
Laqab
An honorific title, in the Buyid case compounded with al-Dawla (for example Muʿizz al-Dawla), conferred by the caliph and signaling rank and relationship to the caliphate.
Shahanshah
Persian "king of kings," the old imperial title of Iranian kings, used by Buyid rulers in some official contexts and contested by some jurists.
Khutba
The Friday sermon in which the recognized ruler is named; its content was a public sign of allegiance.
Sikka
The coinage, whose inscribed names and titles indicated whom the issuing authority recognized as sovereign.
ʿAhd
An investiture diploma by which the caliph delegated lawful authority to an amir or governor.
Dīvān
A government department, such as those for the army, land tax, or chancery, inherited by the Buyids from the Abbasid administration.
Vizier (wazir)
The chief minister supervising civil administration; under the Buyids each ruler had his own vizier, often precariously placed.
Kātib (pl. kuttāb)
A secretary trained in accounting, correspondence, and administration, drawn from various religious and ethnic backgrounds.
Kharaj
The land tax on agricultural land, the state's principal revenue.
Ḍamān
Tax farming, whereby a contractor advanced money to the state and kept what he collected from a district.
Muṣādara
The confiscation of the property of officials, a routine fiscal and political instrument.
Iqtaʿ
A grant of revenue rights, or in some cases land, from state resources, often to soldiers in lieu of pay; not equivalent to feudal landownership.
Sawad
The irrigated alluvial plain of Iraq, the principal agricultural base of the Abbasid and Buyid state in Iraq.
Jibal
The central and western Iranian plateau, including Rayy, Isfahan, and Hamadan, on the road between Iraq and Khurasan.
ʿAyyārūn
Organized bands of urban young men who acted as neighborhood protectors, rioters, or extortioners in Baghdad and other cities.
Ashura
The tenth of Muharram, commemorating the death of Husayn at Karbala in 680; publicly observed in Baghdad with state support from 963.
Ghadir Khumm
The festival of 18 Dhu al-Hijja commemorating, in Shiʿi tradition, the Prophet's designation of ʿAli; publicly celebrated under Buyid patronage.
Naqīb
The head and representative of the descendants of ʿAli (Talibids) or of another noble lineage, an office of honor and influence.
Muʿtazila
A theological movement emphasizing divine justice and human reason, influential among Buyid-era viziers and scholars without being identical to Sunni or Shiʿi communities.
Barīd
The postal and intelligence service that carried correspondence and reports to the ruler.
ʿAḍudi hospital
The large hospital founded by ʿAḍud al-Dawla in Baghdad in the early 980s, known mainly from literary descriptions.
Band-e Amir
The dam on the Kur River near Persepolis attributed to ʿAḍud al-Dawla, whose name preserves his memory; its original dating and extent remain discussed.
Persianate
A modern analytical term for cultural and administrative traditions rooted in New Persian; distinguished from the largely Arabic written culture of the Buyid courts.

Chapter Reflection

The Buyid century matters because it shows how political authority in the Islamic world was reorganized without being replaced. The dynasty did not destroy the caliphate; it redefined what the caliphate was for. By holding military power while preserving religious legitimacy in another person, the Buyids created a division of functions that made later forms of rule possible, including the Seljuk sultanate and its successors. Students who understand this arrangement can read much of later medieval political history as variations on a Buyid theme.

The chapter also reveals a larger process in the history of Iran and the Islamic world: the movement of political initiative to regional and frontier military elites, and the integration of those elites into existing frameworks of law, administration, and learning. Daylamite soldiers became rulers of the capital, Iranian secretaries governed in Arabic, and Iranian royal symbols were absorbed into an Islamic idiom. Language, descent, and sect did not line up in the neat ways that later nationalist or sectarian narratives suggest, and the Buyid world is a reminder to resist reading the tenth century through the categories of the twenty-first.

Several developments begun here continued long after 1062. The public life of Twelver Shiʿism in Iraq, with its scholars, shrines, and commemorations, grew from foundations that Buyid patronage helped secure, even as Sunni institutions reorganized in response. The fiscal and military practices of revenue assignment, the confederative handling of dynastic succession, and the use of honorific titles reappear in later dynasties. The Buyid shift of political weight toward Fars, and the persistence of Baghdad as a symbolic center despite its declining wealth, anticipate later patterns of regional and imperial politics.

For the textbook as a whole, this chapter connects the Persianate revival of the east, traced in Chapters 34 and 35, with the Turkic-Iranian synthesis that Chapters 37 through 39 will examine, while holding the Abbasid center in view. It also prepares the religious and intellectual history of Chapters 40 through 43. The Buyids were neither an Iranian restoration nor a mere interlude. They were a creative and unstable solution to a real political problem, and understanding them helps explain why the institutions of the medieval Islamic world took the shapes they did.
