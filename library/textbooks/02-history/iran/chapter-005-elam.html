Elam: The First Great Civilization of Southwestern Iran

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

Long before any Persian king ruled from Fars, a civilization already old carried the memory of kingship, writing, and sacred architecture across the lowlands of Susiana and the highlands of Anshan. That civilization called itself, in its own language, Haltamti. Its neighbors in Mesopotamia called it Elam. It endured, in one political form or another, for roughly two thousand years.

In 1901–1902, French archaeologists excavating at Susa uncovered a basalt stela about 2.25 meters high, covered in cuneiform writing and topped by a relief showing Hammurabi standing before the seated sun god Shamash, who extends the ring-and-rod insignia of royal authority toward the king. The laws inscribed below the relief were not Elamite. They were the laws of Hammurabi, king of Babylon, composed in Babylonia centuries before the stela was found at Susa. The stela had not always stood in Elam. It had once stood in Babylonia, where Hammurabi placed it to proclaim his justice to his own subjects. An Elamite king, Shutruk-Nahhunte, seized it as plunder during a military campaign into Mesopotamia in the twelfth century BCE and carried it home to Susa. Several columns of Hammurabi's original inscription were erased, apparently to make room for a commemorative Elamite inscription, but the intended replacement text was never carved, leaving the erased columns blank. The stela was eventually buried in the ruins of Susa, where French archaeologists recovered it in 1902; it was subsequently taken to the Louvre in Paris, where it remains one of the most famous objects in the history of law.

This single stone captures much of what this chapter needs to teach. It shows a civilization, Elam, capable of projecting military power deep into Mesopotamia and of appropriating the most celebrated symbol of Mesopotamian royal justice as a trophy of its own authority. It shows a capital city, Susa, that functioned across many centuries as a treasury of captured monuments, sacred images, and royal inscriptions gathered from a wide surrounding world. And it shows, in the deliberate erasure of part of Hammurabi's own text to make space for an Elamite inscription that was, in the end, never carved, a civilization asserting a claim to reinterpret and absorb the cultural inheritance of its neighbors, even where that claim was left visibly unfinished. Elam was not a passive borderland between Mesopotamia and the Iranian plateau. It was an independent center of political and religious power that repeatedly shaped, and was shaped by, the wider world around it.

Chapter 4 asked how historians and archaeologists can detect increasing social and political complexity at Susa and in the Zagros piedmont before any securely documented state existed there. This chapter asks a different question. It asks how Elam emerged as a historically documented civilization, one whose rulers, treaties, wars, and religious institutions can be traced, however imperfectly, across roughly two millennia of written history. It asks how a civilization that repeatedly joined the irrigated lowlands around Susa with the highland plateau around Anshan, far to the southeast in what is now the Iranian province of Fars, managed to survive dynastic collapse, foreign invasion, and the loss of its principal capital, only to reemerge again and again under new rulers and new political forms.

Students should resist two temptations from the outset. The first temptation is to treat Elam as simply "ancient Khuzestan," a local predecessor confined to the southwestern Iranian lowlands and overshadowed by Mesopotamia. Elam's political geography was never so narrow. Its rulers, at their most powerful, styled themselves kings of Anshan and Susa together, uniting a highland Iranian territory with a lowland Mesopotamian-facing one under a single royal title. The second temptation is to assume that because Elam eventually gave way to Persian power on the Iranian plateau, Elamite history is best understood as a mere preface to Persian history, a warm-up act before the "real" story begins with Cyrus the Great. This chapter rejects that framing. Elam possessed its own gods, its own language, its own systems of kingship, and its own historical trajectory that must be understood on its own terms before later chapters examine how Iranian-speaking peoples, and eventually Persian kings, inherited a plateau that Elam had already shaped for two thousand years.

A further caution governs everything that follows. Elamite was not an Iranian language. The people who built Susa's temples, fought Sargon of Akkad's armies, and dedicated the ziggurat at Dur-Untash to their gods were not, in any linguistic sense, Iranians, Persians, or Medes. Elamite belongs to no securely established language family; most specialists treat it as a language isolate, unrelated to Sumerian, Akkadian, or the Indo-Iranian languages that later Median and Persian speakers would bring onto the plateau. Elam belongs to Iranian history because it unfolded substantially within the geographic space this textbook studies, the same lowlands and highlands introduced in Chapter 1, and because its institutions and territory were eventually absorbed into an Iranian imperial world. It does not belong to Iranian history because its people were themselves Iranian speakers. Holding this distinction clearly in mind from the first page of this chapter prevents a serious and common error: retroactively describing Elam as an early "Iranian" civilization in the ethnic or linguistic sense that later chapters will apply to Medes, Persians, and other Iranian-speaking peoples.

Much of what follows must be pieced together from fragmentary and uneven evidence, and a considerable share of that evidence was written not by Elamites describing themselves but by their Mesopotamian neighbors and, at times, their conquerors. Akkadian, Sumerian, Babylonian, and Assyrian scribes recorded Elamite kings chiefly when those kings fought, negotiated with, married into, or were defeated by Mesopotamian rulers. Reconstructing Elamite history therefore means reading against the grain of hostile or self-interested sources as often as it means reading Elam's own inscriptions. This chapter treats that evidentiary problem as a central subject in its own right, not merely as an obstacle to be apologized for and set aside.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Define Elam as a changing historical and political category rather than a fixed state, a modern nation, or a synonym for Susa.
  2. Explain why Elamite is classified as a language isolate and why this matters for how Elam relates to later Iranian-speaking peoples.
  3. Distinguish the Proto-Elamite administrative world from historically documented, dynastic Elam, and explain why the two should not be automatically equated.
  4. Describe the dual geography of Elam, linking lowland Susa and Susiana with highland Anshan in Fars, and explain how the balance between these regions shifted over time.
  5. Trace the major political periods of Elamite history, including the Awan, Shimashki, Sukkalmah, Middle Elamite, and Neo-Elamite periods, without treating them as a simple linear progression.
  6. Analyze the significance of major rulers, including Puzur-Inshushinak, Kindattu, Untash-Napirisha, and Shutruk-Nahhunte, within their specific historical contexts.
  7. Evaluate the problem of writing Elamite history primarily from Mesopotamian sources, and explain how this shapes what historians can and cannot confidently claim.
  8. Describe Elamite writing systems, including Proto-Elamite, Linear Elamite, and Elamite cuneiform, and the continuing uncertainties surrounding their decipherment.
  9. Explain the religious significance of Inshushinak and Napirisha and their association with Susa and the highlands respectively.
  10. Assess the significance of Dur-Untash (Chogha Zanbil) as an expression of Elamite religious and political integration.
  11. Explain why the Assyrian sack of Susa in 646 BCE should not be understood as the end of Elamite history.
  12. Trace the continuity of Elamite language, administrative practice, and population into the Achaemenid Persian imperial system.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. What does it mean to call "Elam" a civilization when its territory, capital, ruling dynasty, and even dominant language of administration changed repeatedly across two thousand years?
  2. How should historians handle a civilization whose political history survives mainly through the records of its rivals and conquerors?
  3. Why does the relationship between lowland Susa and highland Anshan matter for understanding Elamite political structure?
  4. In what ways does the sukkalmah system of shared or layered kingship challenge simple assumptions about how ancient states organized succession?
  5. How can a single object, such as the captured stela of Hammurabi's laws, illuminate an entire pattern of Elamite royal ideology?
  6. Why should the Assyrian destruction of Susa in 646 BCE be understood as a catastrophe rather than as the end of Elamite history?
  7. What does the continued use of Elamite as an administrative language under the Achaemenid Persian kings suggest about the relationship between conquest and cultural survival?
  8. Why is it historically inaccurate to describe Elam as an early Iranian civilization in the same linguistic sense used for the Medes and Persians?
  9. What kinds of evidence allow historians to reconstruct Elamite religion, and what are the limits of that evidence?

Historical Context

Chapter 4 traced the development of settlement hierarchy, sealing practices, numerical notation, and increasingly complex administration at Susa and across Susiana through the later fourth millennium BCE. Chapter 5 now extends that archaeological background into the Proto-Elamite phenomenon of the late fourth and early third millennia BCE, a widespread but still incompletely understood administrative and artistic tradition centered on Susa and extending, through shared sealing practices and a still largely undeciphered script, across much of the Iranian plateau, before moving into historically documented dynastic Elam. Neither Chapter 4 nor the Proto-Elamite material introduced here supports describing a historically documented Elamite state, because the textual and archaeological evidence for continuous, identifiable political institutions does not yet support such a claim for this earlier phase.

This chapter picks up where Chapter 4 stopped and moves, by way of the Proto-Elamite phenomenon, toward the point where Mesopotamian and, eventually, Elamite textual sources allow historians to speak of specific rulers, dynasties, treaties, and wars associated with a place its neighbors called Elam. That transition is neither sudden nor complete. Sources remain fragmentary throughout, and entire generations of rulers are sometimes known only from a single inscription, a single Mesopotamian chronicle entry, or a single votive object. Even so, from roughly the middle of the third millennium BCE onward, Elam possesses a political history that can be narrated, however unevenly, in a way the Proto-Elamite period does not yet allow.

Students should also recall the geographic framework established in Chapter 1 and developed further in Chapter 4. Elam's core territory spanned two very different environments: the irrigated alluvial lowlands of Susiana, watered by rivers descending from the Zagros and closely tied, both economically and politically, to nearby Mesopotamia; and the highland plateau of Anshan, centered on the site now known as Tal-e Malyan in the modern Iranian province of Fars, hundreds of kilometers to the southeast. No single, permanently fixed capital or political center governed this combined territory throughout Elamite history. Instead, the relative importance of Susa and Anshan shifted repeatedly, sometimes uniting under rulers who claimed both, sometimes separating under distinct highland and lowland authorities, and sometimes contracting so that one region eclipsed the other for a time. This shifting balance is not a sign of political weakness to be explained away. It is a defining structural feature of Elamite civilization that recurs throughout this chapter.

Finally, students should understand why so much of the evidence for early Elamite political history comes from Mesopotamia rather than from Elam itself. Sargon of Akkad, his successors, the kings of the Third Dynasty of Ur, and later Babylonian and Assyrian rulers all fought, negotiated with, and at times ruled portions of Elamite territory, and their scribes recorded these encounters in considerable detail. Elam's own textual record, by contrast, is comparatively sparse in its earliest centuries, uneven across different periods, and written in scripts, including Proto-Elamite and Linear Elamite, that remain only partially understood. This imbalance shapes, and sometimes distorts, the historical narrative that follows, a problem this chapter addresses directly rather than passing over in silence.

Section 5.1

What Was Elam?

"Elam" is the name Mesopotamian sources gave to a civilization, or more precisely to a shifting constellation of polities, occupying southwestern Iran and adjoining highland territory across roughly two thousand years. The Elamites themselves, in their own language, appear to have called their land Haltamti, a term that survives in various forms across Elamite inscriptions. Neither name corresponds neatly to a fixed set of borders, a single continuous dynasty, or a single unchanging capital. Students should therefore resist imagining Elam as a modern nation-state with settled boundaries and a continuous government. It is better understood as a durable civilizational and political tradition, centered on specific cities, cults, and royal titles, that persisted through repeated changes of dynasty, capital, and even dominant language of administration.

This chapter's title describes Elam as "the first great civilization of southwestern Iran," and that description is accurate, but it requires immediate qualification. Elam was not confined to southwestern Iran alone. Its political history, as this chapter will show, repeatedly extended into the highland region of Fars, hundreds of kilometers to the southeast, and its rulers at various points controlled, contested, or campaigned across large portions of Mesopotamia to the west. Nor should "civilization" imply a single unified state maintaining continuous central control. Elamite history includes long periods of apparent fragmentation, competing highland and lowland authorities, and gaps in the textual record so severe that entire generations of rulers remain almost entirely unknown.

A further point demands emphasis before this chapter proceeds any further: Elamite was not an Iranian language. Most specialists in ancient Near Eastern linguistics classify Elamite as a language isolate, meaning it cannot be confidently placed within any larger, well-established language family, certainly not within the Indo-Iranian family that includes Old Persian, Avestan, and the languages of the Medes. This matters because popular and even some older scholarly accounts have sometimes blurred the distinction between "ancient Iran" as a geographic space and "Iranian" as a linguistic and ethnic category, implying that Elam was simply an early phase of Iranian civilization in the same sense that later Median and Persian societies were. This textbook's editorial framework, established in Chapter 1, explicitly rejects that conflation. Elam belongs to the history of the Iranian plateau because it unfolded substantially within the geographic space this textbook studies and because its territory and institutions were eventually absorbed into the Achaemenid Persian world. It does not belong to "Iranian" history in the narrower linguistic or ethnic sense reserved for Indo-Iranian-speaking peoples, a category examined beginning in Chapter 6.

Much of what historians can say about Elam's political history, particularly in its earlier centuries, depends heavily on Mesopotamian sources: Akkadian royal inscriptions, Sumerian literary and administrative texts, and later Babylonian and Assyrian chronicles and annals. This creates an evidentiary imbalance that recurs throughout the chapter and receives direct attention in this chapter's Historical Method feature below. Elam's own textual tradition, written in a succession of only partially understood scripts, becomes progressively richer over time, but even at its fullest it never rivals the sheer volume of contemporary Mesopotamian documentation. Students should therefore expect a narrative that is at points detailed and at other points frustratingly thin, not because Elamite history was itself uneven in importance, but because the surviving evidence is uneven in coverage.

Section 5.2

From the Proto-Elamite World to Historical Elam

Building on the later-fourth-millennium administrative developments examined in Chapter 4, this chapter now introduces the Proto-Elamite phenomenon: a widespread administrative and artistic tradition, centered on Susa, that flourished across roughly the last centuries of the fourth millennium BCE and the earliest centuries of the third, marked by a distinctive glyptic art style, an accounting system using numerical and pictographic notation, and a still largely undeciphered script found at sites scattered across a surprisingly wide area of the Iranian plateau, from Susa itself to sites far to the east. This world should not be described as a unified Elamite state, because the evidence does not support such a claim.

This chapter must state that caution clearly rather than pass over it. The relationship between the Proto-Elamite administrative tradition and the historically documented, dynastic Elam that appears in Mesopotamian sources from roughly the middle of the third millennium BCE onward is not simple or automatic. The Proto-Elamite script itself remains substantially undeciphered, and its relationship to the later Linear Elamite script and to Elamite cuneiform is a matter of ongoing scholarly investigation rather than settled fact. Historians cannot currently demonstrate a direct, unbroken linguistic or political continuity between the anonymous administrators who used Proto-Elamite notation and the named kings of Awan who appear in Akkadian royal inscriptions a few centuries later. It is entirely possible, and indeed likely, that significant continuity of population, religious practice, and regional identity existed across this transition. It is not possible to prove, on current evidence, that the Proto-Elamite world constituted the same political entity later Mesopotamian sources called Elam.

What can be said with more confidence is that the underlying geographic and economic structures examined in Chapter 4, the concentration of population and political authority around Susa in the fertile Susiana lowlands, the connections between that lowland zone and highland centers to the southeast, and the region's deep economic and cultural entanglement with Mesopotamia, persisted across this transition and shaped the historical Elam that emerges into clearer textual view beginning in the Old Elamite period. Students should therefore treat the Proto-Elamite period as essential background, not as an early chapter in Elam's own dynastic history, and should be prepared to encounter genuine uncertainty rather than a tidy, unbroken narrative connecting the two eras.

Section 5.3

Susa, Anshan, and the Geography of Elam

No feature of Elamite political geography matters more than the relationship between Susa and Anshan. Susa, situated in the Susiana lowlands near the Zagros foothills and closely connected by rivers and overland routes to nearby Mesopotamia, functioned across most of Elamite history as the civilization's principal city, its most important religious center, and, for long stretches, its political capital. Anshan, by contrast, lay far to the southeast in the highland region later known as Fars, at the site archaeologists have identified as Tal-e Malyan. Anshan's material remains show it to have been an extensive and important urban center in its own right during several periods of Elamite history, even though it is far less thoroughly documented in surviving texts than Susa.

Elamite kings at their most powerful did not rule from one of these centers alone. They claimed the combined title "king of Anshan and Susa," a formula that recurs across Elamite history from at least the Old Elamite period through the Middle Elamite revival examined later in this chapter. This title should not be read as a mere ceremonial flourish. It reflects a genuine and recurring political structure in which authority over the highland and lowland components of Elamite territory had to be actively achieved and maintained, rather than being an automatic or permanent feature of any single dynasty's rule. At various periods, the two regions functioned under separate or loosely coordinated authorities; at others, a single ruler or ruling house genuinely integrated both under unified control.

This dual geography also explains much of Elam's distinctive historical rhythm. Susa's position near Mesopotamia exposed it repeatedly to Mesopotamian military campaigns, diplomatic pressure, and cultural influence, including the widespread use of Akkadian language and cuneiform script in administration during several periods. Anshan's position on the Iranian plateau, considerably more distant from Mesopotamia, offered a measure of insulation from these same pressures and, at various points in Elamite history, served as a refuge or alternative center of authority when Susa came under external threat or fell under foreign control. Students should keep this lowland-highland duality in mind throughout the chapter, since it recurs as an organizing theme across nearly every period examined below.

Section 5.4

Awan and Elam's Entry into Written History

Elam enters recorded history chiefly through the eyes of its rivals. Sargon of Akkad, who built the first great Mesopotamian territorial empire in the twenty-fourth century BCE, and his successors, campaigned repeatedly against Elamite territory, and their scribes recorded these campaigns in royal inscriptions that survive far better than any comparable Elamite source from the same period. These Akkadian texts associate early Elamite political authority with a place or dynasty called Awan, whose precise location has not been securely identified archaeologically, though its rulers appear repeatedly as opponents, tribute payers, or occasional allies of Akkadian kings.

The single most important surviving document from the earlier phase of this era, for the purposes of this textbook, is an agreement between an Elamite ruler of Awan, perhaps named Hita or Khita, and the Akkadian king Naram-Sin, grandson of Sargon, who reigned in the twenty-third century BCE. This text, examined in detail in this chapter's Primary Source Window below, is written in the Elamite language using cuneiform script, and it stands among the earliest surviving substantial Elamite-language texts. Its existence demonstrates that by this period an Elamite ruler was capable of producing a formal political document in his own language, even as much of the surrounding evidence for Elamite political history continues to come primarily from Akkadian sources. The precise political character of the agreement, and the relative status of the two parties, remain debated, and this chapter does not treat the text as proof that Awan and Akkad stood on equal diplomatic footing.

Considerably later, toward the end of the Awan sequence, a ruler of Susa known as Puzur-Inshushinak, generally placed around 2100 BCE, near the time of Ur-Nammu's rise in Mesopotamia, provides the clearest and most historically valuable evidence from this period. Puzur-Inshushinak's inscriptions describe extensive military campaigning and claim control over a considerable territory, and his building activity at Susa is well attested archaeologically. His reign is particularly significant for this chapter's purposes because it provides the strongest evidence of experimentation with a distinctly Elamite script, known as Linear Elamite, used alongside Akkadian cuneiform in some of his inscriptions. He is a separate and later figure from the Elamite ruler associated with the Naram-Sin agreement roughly a century and a half earlier, and the two episodes should not be treated as belonging to the same moment in Elamite history. This dual use of scripts under Puzur-Inshushinak illustrates a pattern that recurs throughout Elamite history: engagement with Mesopotamian administrative and linguistic conventions alongside the maintenance, or active assertion, of a distinct Elamite tradition.

Students should resist treating this period as a simple story of Mesopotamian domination over a passive Elamite periphery. Awan-period Elamite rulers fought Akkadian kings, negotiated formal treaties with them, and at times pursued their own independent territorial ambitions. The imbalance in the surviving evidence, weighted heavily toward Akkadian sources, should not be mistaken for an imbalance in actual historical agency during the period itself.

Section 5.5

Shimashki and the Fall of Ur

Following the decline of the Awan dynasty and of Akkadian imperial power more broadly, Elamite political authority became associated with a new dynastic center known as Shimashki, generally understood to have been oriented more strongly toward the highland zone than Awan had been, though its precise territorial extent remains uncertain. The Shimashki period corresponds roughly to the era of the Third Dynasty of Ur, the Mesopotamian state that briefly reasserted centralized control over southern Mesopotamia in the twenty-first century BCE.

The most dramatic and best-documented event of this period is the fall of Ur itself. Mesopotamian sources, particularly later literary laments composed after the event, describe how forces associated with Shimashki, under a ruler remembered as Kindattu, participated in the defeat of Ur and the capture of its last king, Ibbi-Sin, an event conventionally dated to 2004 BCE under the Middle Chronology widely used in Near Eastern history. The event's relative position, at the very end of Ibbi-Sin's reign and the collapse of the Third Dynasty of Ur, is well established from Mesopotamian king lists and year-name formulas, but its precise absolute BCE date depends on which of several competing chronological schemes historians adopt, and students should treat 2004 BCE as a conventional reference point rather than an unquestionable fixed date.

This chapter must immediately qualify the popular version of this story. It is tempting, and historically inaccurate, to describe this event as "Elam destroying Ur" as though a single unified Elamite empire had launched a deliberate war of conquest against a rival civilization. The actual political configuration was considerably more complicated. Mesopotamian sources indicate that forces from Shimashki acted in some form of coordination with other groups, and the collapse of Ur's authority resulted from a longer process of internal weakening, including famine, administrative strain, and the erosion of Ur's control over outlying territories, rather than from a single external invasion alone. Kindattu and Shimashki should be understood as taking advantage of, and contributing to, an already unfolding crisis rather than single-handedly toppling a stable empire at the height of its power.

The aftermath of 2004 BCE nonetheless mattered enormously for Elamite history. Ibbi-Sin's capture and the sack of Ur removed, at least temporarily, the most significant centralized Mesopotamian power capable of contesting Elamite authority in the region, and the episode entered later Mesopotamian literary tradition as a byword for catastrophic reversal. For Elam, the period marks a moment when highland-oriented political authority, associated with Shimashki, achieved genuine prominence on the wider historical stage, foreshadowing the more elaborate highland-lowland political structures that would characterize the following centuries.

Section 5.6

The Sukkalmah Period and a Different Form of Kingship

Beginning in approximately the early second millennium BCE, Elamite political authority took on a distinctive institutional form associated with the title sukkalmah, often rendered "grand regent" or "supreme sukkal," used alongside related titles such as sukkal of Elam and Shimashki and sukkal of Susa. A sukkalmah could delegate authority to holders of these related titles, but the simultaneous association of a sukkal of Elam and Shimashki and a sukkal of Susa with a single supreme ruler was not a fixed rule applied consistently across the period. The pattern of delegated or shared authority attested in the inscriptions was considerably more flexible and situational than a permanent three-office system, and considerable scholarly uncertainty remains about the precise relationship among the various titles and their holders at any given moment.

Historical Vocabulary
Sukkalmah

Definition: A royal title used by the senior ruling authority in Elam during roughly the early second millennium BCE, generally translated as "grand regent" or "supreme sukkal," existing alongside related titles such as sukkal of Elam and Shimashki and sukkal of Susa.

Historical context: The precise relationship among the sukkalmah and the holders of subordinate sukkal titles, whether reflecting a fixed system of succession, a genuine division of highland and lowland authority, or a more flexible and situational arrangement, remains actively debated among specialists, and inscriptions from the period do not provide a single, consistent account of how the offices related to one another across time.

Why it matters: The sukkalmah system illustrates that Elamite kingship did not necessarily follow patterns familiar from single-ruler Mesopotamian monarchy. Authority could be distributed, shared, or layered in ways that modern students, accustomed to thinking of ancient kingship as a single individual ruling alone, may find unfamiliar.

Potential misunderstanding: Older scholarship sometimes described the sukkalmah system as operating according to a fixed rule of fraternal or avuncular succession, in which power passed systematically among brothers before descending to nephews. This textbook treats that reconstruction with caution rather than presenting it as settled fact, since more recent analysis of the surviving inscriptions suggests the actual pattern of succession and office-holding was considerably more variable and complicated than any single formula can capture.

Whatever its precise internal mechanics, the sukkalmah period is significant for demonstrating sustained diplomatic and commercial engagement between Elam and various Mesopotamian kingdoms, including states based at Babylon, Larsa, and elsewhere during the complex political landscape of the early second millennium BCE Near East. Elamite rulers of this period appear in Mesopotamian archives as trading partners, diplomatic correspondents, and, at various points, military adversaries, reflecting a relationship considerably more textured than simple hostility or simple subordination in either direction.

Students should approach this period, and indeed much of pre-Middle Elamite history, with appropriate caution regarding the limits of current knowledge. Long stretches of the sukkalmah period are known only through scattered inscriptions, occasional references in Mesopotamian archives, and archaeological evidence that does not always align neatly with the textual record. Historians continue to revise proposed king lists and chronological reconstructions for this period as new evidence emerges, and students encountering older textbooks or popular accounts should expect to find confident-sounding king lists that more recent scholarship treats with considerably greater caution.

Section 5.7

Language, Writing, and Religion in Elam

This section pauses the political narrative to examine three subjects essential to understanding Elamite civilization on its own terms: language, writing, and religion. These subjects do not divide neatly by period, and understanding them here will help students interpret the political narrative that resumes in the following sections.

Language. As already established, Elamite is classified by most specialists as a language isolate, without secure demonstrated relationship to Sumerian, Akkadian, or the Indo-Iranian languages that would later be spoken on the Iranian plateau by Medes and Persians. Elamite was written using several different systems across its long history. Akkadian cuneiform, borrowed from Mesopotamia, was used extensively for Elamite in various periods, particularly for administrative and diplomatic purposes, and Akkadian itself, as a separate language, also served as an important language of diplomacy and scholarship in Elam at various times, much as it did throughout the wider ancient Near East.

Writing systems. Two other, more distinctively Elamite writing systems deserve attention, and both remain the subject of active scholarly investigation rather than settled understanding. Proto-Elamite, introduced earlier in this chapter as an extension of the archaeological background examined in Chapter 4, is a script used across a wide area of the Iranian plateau in the later fourth and earlier third millennium BCE; despite considerable research, it remains substantially undeciphered, and its precise relationship to the spoken Elamite language of later periods is not established with confidence. Linear Elamite, a separate and later script attested in a small but significantly expanded corpus from southern Iran dating to approximately 2300–1880 BCE, includes inscriptions associated with Puzur-Inshushinak as well as a group of inscribed silver vessels, including eight silver beakers published in 2018. A peer-reviewed 2022 study proposed a substantially expanded decipherment of Linear Elamite based partly on this silver-vessel corpus, and this work materially advanced scholarly discussion of the script. Specialists nevertheless continue to debate aspects of the proposed decipherment, including some sign values, the possible role of logograms, and how much of the language can yet be translated with confidence. Students should understand that Linear Elamite is therefore neither simply "undeciphered" nor a fully settled matter, and this textbook treats the 2022 proposal as a significant but still actively debated contribution rather than a closed question. Cuneiform Elamite, using signs adapted from Akkadian cuneiform to write the Elamite language, is by far the best understood and most extensively attested of the three systems, particularly from the Middle Elamite period onward.

Religion. Elamite religion centered on a substantial pantheon of deities, several of which recur prominently across the chapter's political narrative. Inshushinak, whose name is closely tied to Susa itself, functioned as the principal god of that city, associated with kingship, justice, and the underworld; kings across many centuries of Elamite history invoked Inshushinak's authority to legitimate their rule and their building projects at Susa. Napirisha, by contrast, was strongly associated with the highland regions around Anshan, and the joint dedication of major religious monuments to both Inshushinak and Napirisha, examined further in the discussion of Dur-Untash below, reflects the same lowland-highland duality that structured Elamite political geography more broadly. Other deities, goddesses, and local cults are attested at various sites and periods, and historians should be cautious about assuming that Elamite religious practice was uniform across the civilization's long history and broad territory; regional variation, and change over time, likely characterized Elamite religious life just as it did Elamite political organization.

Historical Method

Writing History from Unequal Sources

Much of what historians know about early and middle Elamite political history derives from Akkadian, Sumerian, Babylonian, and Assyrian texts, produced by scribes working for Elam's neighbors, rivals, and, at times, conquerors. This creates a source problem distinct from the primarily archaeological evidentiary problem addressed in Chapter 4. There, historians worked chiefly from settlement patterns, material remains, sealing practices, and administrative technologies without a legible textual record. Here, the challenge is the survival of abundant texts written from a hostile or self-interested outside perspective, alongside a comparatively thinner internal Elamite record.

This problem is distinct from, though related to, the evidentiary problems encountered in earlier and adjoining phases of this civilization's record. Chapter 4's account of Susa relied primarily on archaeological and non-textual evidence, settlement patterns, sealing practices, and material culture, because no legible textual record yet existed. The Proto-Elamite phenomenon introduced in this chapter contributes a written, notational record that survives physically but remains substantially undeciphered, a problem of illegibility rather than of absence or bias. Historically documented Elam, the subject of most of this chapter, presents yet a third kind of problem: texts that are perfectly legible but unevenly distributed and, for long stretches, produced primarily by outside and often hostile parties. Keeping these three distinct evidentiary situations separate, absence, illegibility, and imbalance, helps students understand why historians make different kinds of claims, with different degrees of confidence, about different periods of this same overall history.

This imbalance shapes the surviving narrative in specific and identifiable ways. Mesopotamian sources tend to record Elamite rulers chiefly at moments of conflict, negotiation, or subordination involving Mesopotamian kingdoms, meaning that periods or aspects of Elamite history disconnected from Mesopotamian affairs are systematically underrepresented in the textual record, whatever their actual historical importance may have been. Mesopotamian royal inscriptions also had clear propagandistic purposes, designed to celebrate the issuing king's victories and minimize his setbacks, meaning that claims of total Elamite defeat or destruction, examined further in this chapter's treatment of the 646 BCE sack of Susa, must be read critically rather than accepted at face value.

Historians address this imbalance by cross-checking Mesopotamian claims against Elamite inscriptions where they survive, against archaeological evidence of continued occupation, building activity, and material culture at Elamite sites, and against the internal logic and plausibility of the Mesopotamian accounts themselves. Where Elamite evidence is silent or absent, careful historians distinguish between "Elam did not do X" and "Elam's own sources happen not to mention X," a distinction that matters enormously for how confidently any particular historical claim should be stated.

Method questions for students:

  1. Why might a Mesopotamian king's inscription overstate the completeness of a victory over Elam?
  2. How can archaeological evidence help historians evaluate a Mesopotamian claim of "total destruction"?
  3. What kinds of Elamite history are least likely to be recorded at all, given the pattern by which Mesopotamian scribes recorded Elamite affairs?
  4. Why is source imbalance a different historical problem from source absence, and what different methods does each problem require?
Section 5.8

The Middle Elamite Transformation

Beginning in roughly the sixteenth century BCE, Elamite political and cultural life entered a period historians conventionally label Middle Elamite, lasting through approximately the twelfth century BCE. This period marks a significant transformation in the visibility and character of Elamite civilization within the historical record. Royal inscriptions increasingly employ the Elamite language itself rather than relying primarily on Akkadian, and the combined title "king of Anshan and Susa," attested already in earlier periods, reappears with renewed emphasis, suggesting a deliberate assertion of unified highland-lowland authority and a distinctly Elamite royal ideology.

Archaeological evidence from this period is considerably richer than for earlier centuries, including substantial building activity at Susa and the foundation of entirely new religious and administrative centers, most notably the city discussed in the following section. Middle Elamite rulers also developed more elaborate royal titulature emphasizing their relationship to Elamite deities, particularly Inshushinak, and inscriptions from this period provide historians with considerably more direct access to Elamite self-presentation than the largely Mesopotamian-filtered record of earlier centuries.

Students should nonetheless avoid treating "centralization" during the Middle Elamite period as an assumption to be taken for granted rather than a claim requiring evidence. The increased visibility of Elamite kingship in this period reflects, in part, an increase in the survival and legibility of Elamite sources themselves, not necessarily a straightforward, linear increase in actual political centralization compared to earlier periods whose internal Elamite record happens to be far thinner. Historians must distinguish between "we can now see more of Elamite political life" and "Elamite political life became more centralized," even though the two possibilities are not mutually exclusive and likely operated together to some degree.

Section 5.9

Untash-Napirisha and Dur-Untash

The clearest and most dramatic material expression of Middle Elamite religious and political ambition is the city of Dur-Untash, known today by its modern name, Chogha Zanbil, founded by the king Untash-Napirisha in approximately the thirteenth century BCE. Rather than expanding an existing city, Untash-Napirisha constructed an entirely new urban and religious center, centered on a massive stepped ziggurat, one of the best-preserved structures of its kind surviving anywhere in the ancient Near East.

The dedication of the ziggurat is itself historically significant. Untash-Napirisha dedicated the structure jointly to Inshushinak, the great god of Susa associated with the lowlands, and to Napirisha, the god most strongly associated with the highland world around Anshan. This joint dedication should be read as a deliberate statement of religious and political integration, an attempt to bring together, within a single monumental sacred space, the two principal religious traditions associated with Elam's dual lowland and highland geography. International heritage assessments of the site describe Chogha Zanbil as one of the most important surviving examples of Elamite religious architecture and place its foundation in approximately the thirteenth century BCE, consistent with the reign of Untash-Napirisha.

The later history of Dur-Untash requires careful handling. Older popular accounts sometimes state simply that the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal destroyed the city during his seventh-century BCE campaigns against Elam, treating its abandonment as a single dramatic event coinciding with the broader Neo-Elamite catastrophes examined later in this chapter. More recent archaeological analysis suggests a more complicated picture: substantial portions of the city's central religious complex appear to have already fallen out of active use well before the end of the second millennium BCE, considerably earlier than Ashurbanipal's seventh-century campaigns. This does not mean the site was entirely abandoned or historically insignificant after that point, but it does mean students should be cautious about attributing a single dramatic destruction event to a site whose decline more likely unfolded gradually and for reasons that remain incompletely understood.

Cause and Effect

Why Untash-Napirisha Built Dur-Untash

Long-term causes

The enduring structural duality of Elamite geography and religion, joining lowland Susa and its god Inshushinak with highland Anshan and its god Napirisha, created a recurring political incentive to demonstrate unified authority over both traditions.

Short-term causes

The specific political and religious priorities of Untash-Napirisha's reign, including a probable desire to establish a new royal and religious center associated directly with his own dynasty rather than relying solely on existing sites at Susa.

Effect

The construction of an entirely new city built around a monumental ziggurat jointly dedicated to the principal lowland and highland deities, materially embodying the integration of Elam's two core regions within a single sacred landscape.

This textbook's response

Treating Dur-Untash as a case study in how Elamite kings used monumental religious architecture to assert political ideology, while remaining cautious about oversimplified narratives of the site's later abandonment or destruction.

Historical complexity: A single monument rarely has a single cause. Religious devotion, political ambition, dynastic legitimation, and practical considerations of urban planning likely all played some role in Untash-Napirisha's decision, in proportions that surviving evidence cannot precisely establish.

Section 5.10

The Shutrukids at the Height of Middle Elamite Power

The final and most militarily assertive phase of the Middle Elamite period is associated with a group of rulers historians commonly call the Shutrukid dynasty, including Shutruk-Nahhunte, his sons Kutir-Nahhunte and Shilhak-Inshushinak, and other members of the same royal house, active chiefly during the twelfth century BCE. This period represents the height of Middle Elamite military and political power, marked by extensive campaigning into Babylonia, territorial expansion, and an ambitious program of monumental building and inscription at Susa.

Shutruk-Nahhunte's campaigns into Babylonia are particularly well documented archaeologically because of the practice, apparently deliberate and ideologically significant, of transporting captured Mesopotamian monuments back to Susa as trophies of conquest. The most famous example, already introduced in this chapter's opening, is the stela inscribed with the laws of Hammurabi, king of Babylon roughly six centuries earlier, which Shutruk-Nahhunte's forces removed from its original Babylonian location and installed at Susa. Several columns of Hammurabi's original text were erased, apparently to make room for a commemorative Elamite inscription, though that intended inscription was, in the end, never carved. Other captured Mesopotamian monuments, including additional royal stelae and inscribed objects, were brought to Susa during the same general period, transforming the city into something like a treasury of conquest as well as a religious and administrative capital.

This practice illuminates important aspects of Elamite royal ideology, even without a completed replacement inscription. Bringing celebrated Mesopotamian monuments to Susa was not merely an act of plunder for its own sake; it asserted a claim that Elamite kingship now possessed and controlled the most potent symbols of Mesopotamian royal authority, and the erasure of part of Hammurabi's text shows an evident intention to reinterpret the monument in Elamite terms, whatever the reason the replacement inscription was ultimately left unfinished. The stela's removal and adaptation at Susa transformed an object of Mesopotamian royal ideology into an instrument, however incompletely realized, of Elamite royal display instead.

Kutir-Nahhunte and Shilhak-Inshushinak, Shutruk-Nahhunte's successors, continued extensive campaigning and building activity, and inscriptions from their reigns provide some of the richest surviving documentation for Middle Elamite religious practice, royal genealogy, and administrative organization. This period of Elamite strength did not, however, prove permanent. Mesopotamian sources indicate that Babylonian forces eventually reversed several Elamite territorial gains in the decades following the height of Shutrukid power, and the following centuries saw a marked decline in the density and clarity of the surviving Elamite historical record, a gap historians sometimes describe, with appropriate caution about overstating its completeness, as a period of reduced Elamite political visibility preceding the Neo-Elamite period examined next.

Section 5.11

Neo-Elam: Assyria, Babylonia, Fragmentation, and Survival

The final major phase of independent Elamite political history, conventionally labeled the Neo-Elamite period and dated approximately from 1050 or 1100 BCE to 539 BCE, depending on the periodization scheme used, is characterized by shifting political centers, intense involvement in the escalating rivalry between Assyria and Babylonia, and, in its later stages, severe political fragmentation. The Assyrian sack of Susa in 646 BCE, examined below, marks a major rupture within this long period rather than its endpoint, and Elamite political life continued, in reorganized and diminished form, through the remaining decades of the Neo-Elamite period, not, as this chapter emphasizes throughout, ending in historical extinction.

Throughout the ninth, eighth, and seventh centuries BCE, Elamite rulers repeatedly intervened in Babylonian affairs, at times supporting Babylonian kings against Assyrian pressure and at times pursuing independent territorial ambitions of their own. This period coincides with the expansion of Assyrian imperial power under a succession of increasingly assertive kings, and Elam found itself drawn into direct and increasingly costly confrontation with Assyrian military power as Assyrian ambitions extended toward controlling or neutralizing Babylonia and its regional allies.

The most significant military confrontation of this final period occurred in 653 BCE at the Ulai River, where Assyrian forces under Ashurbanipal decisively defeated an Elamite army. Assyrian royal inscriptions describe this battle in vivid and triumphant detail, including claims regarding the death of the Elamite king Teumman, and the episode was commemorated in Assyrian palace relief sculpture, providing historians with unusually rich, though obviously one-sided, documentation of the engagement.

The culminating catastrophe of this period came in 646 BCE, when Ashurbanipal's forces sacked Susa itself. Assyrian inscriptions describe the destruction in extensive and deliberately dramatic terms, claiming the leveling of temples, the desecration of royal tombs, the removal or destruction of sacred images, and the effective annihilation of Elam as a political entity. These claims, precisely because they served clear Assyrian propagandistic purposes celebrating the completeness of Ashurbanipal's triumph, must be evaluated with the critical caution this chapter's Historical Method feature has already emphasized.

Archaeological and textual evidence indicates that Assyrian claims of total annihilation were considerably overstated. Elamite political activity, though certainly diminished and reorganized, resumed in the years following 646 BCE, with continuing evidence of local rulers, administrative activity, and regional political life in Susiana and adjoining territories through the final decades of the Neo-Elamite period, down to approximately 539 BCE. The year 646 BCE therefore deserves to be remembered as a genuine catastrophe for Elamite political power, a moment of severe destruction and disruption at Susa itself, but not as the terminal date of Elamite history or even of the Neo-Elamite period itself, a distinction this chapter regards as one of its central historiographical lessons.

Section 5.12

Elamites and the Arrival of Iranian-Speaking Peoples

During the same general centuries in which Elam confronted Assyrian and Babylonian power, a different and, for the purposes of this textbook, equally significant development was unfolding across the Iranian plateau: the increasing historical visibility of Iranian-speaking peoples, including the Medes and the Persians, whose migrations and settlement onto the plateau are examined in full in Chapter 6, and whose emergence as identifiable political actors in western Iran is examined in Chapters 7 and 8. This section previews, without narrating in detail, how these two historical processes, Elamite decline and Iranian-speaking ascendancy, came to intersect on the same geographic terrain.

The region of Anshan, so central to Elamite royal titulature across many centuries, provides the clearest point of intersection. By the seventh and sixth centuries BCE, evidence increasingly associates rulers connected to Anshan with a Persian royal lineage, the same lineage that would later produce Cyrus the Great, examined beginning in Chapter 9. This does not mean that Persian rulers simply replaced Elamite ones in a single dramatic transition. The relationship between emerging Persian authority in and around Anshan and continuing, though increasingly localized, Elamite political activity centered on Susiana appears to have involved a complex and gradual process of overlap, interaction, and eventual absorption rather than a single conquest or a clean historical handoff.

This chapter deliberately stops short of narrating that process in detail. The migrations of Iranian-speaking peoples onto the plateau, their linguistic and cultural background, and the specific historical circumstances under which Median and Persian power became securely documented are the proper subjects of Chapters 6 through 8, and this chapter's task is only to establish the essential geographic and chronological point of contact: Elam was already an ancient civilization, with roughly two thousand years of documented political history behind it, at the moment Iranian-speaking peoples began to become historically visible in the same territory. Persian history, when it begins in the chapters that follow, does not begin on an empty or newly settled plateau. It begins in a landscape already deeply shaped by Elamite cities, cults, administrative practices, and political traditions.

Section 5.13

Elam Did Not Simply Disappear: The Achaemenid Inheritance

This chapter's concluding section addresses a question with significance extending well beyond ancient history alone: what became of Elamite civilization once Persian kings, beginning with Cyrus the Great and his Achaemenid successors, established imperial control over the territories Elam had once ruled? The short answer, developed throughout this chapter, is that Elam did not simply vanish. Its language, its administrative personnel, its scribal traditions, its religious sites, and its population were substantially incorporated into the new Persian imperial system rather than being erased by it.

The clearest evidence for this continuity comes from the Achaemenid administrative capital of Persepolis, examined in detail in Chapter 12, where archaeologists have recovered a substantial archive of administrative tablets recording the movement of goods, rations, and labor across the Achaemenid heartland. The overwhelming majority of these tablets, belonging mainly to the Persepolis Fortification archive of roughly 509 to 494 BCE and the smaller Treasury archive of roughly 492 to 458 BCE, are written not in Old Persian but in the Elamite language, using Elamite cuneiform, demonstrating that Elamite functioned as a primary working language of Achaemenid imperial administration well into the Achaemenid period, decades after Elam had been incorporated into the empire and after independent Elamite rule had ended. Elamite also appears, alongside Old Persian and Akkadian, in several major royal Achaemenid inscriptions, including celebrated trilingual texts that present the same royal proclamation in all three languages, a practice examined further in Chapter 12.

This administrative continuity should reshape how students understand the relationship between Elamite and Persian history. Persian imperial civilization, as it emerges in the chapters that follow, did not develop in isolation or spring fully formed from an exclusively Iranian-speaking cultural tradition. It developed, in substantial part, by inheriting and incorporating the institutions, personnel, administrative practices, and even the language of the much older Elamite world it had absorbed. The scribes who recorded the daily operations of the Achaemenid court and administration at Persepolis were working within long-established Elamite-language scribal and administrative traditions that the Achaemenid state incorporated and adapted, rather than a single unchanged bureaucratic institution that had somehow operated continuously and without interruption across the full two thousand years of Elamite history examined in this chapter.

This chapter closes, then, not with an ending but with a controlled transition. Elam was already ancient, its political and religious traditions already deeply rooted across Susiana and the highlands of Fars, when Iranian-speaking peoples began to become historically visible on the plateau. The chapters that follow will rewind the historical clock to examine where those Iranian-speaking peoples came from, how their languages and cultures developed, and how Median and Persian power eventually emerged from the same landscape Elam had shaped for two millennia. Understanding Elam thoroughly, on its own terms and across its full historical arc, is therefore not merely background information to be set aside. It is essential preparation for understanding what Persian civilization actually was: not a civilization built on empty ground, but one built substantially atop, and partly out of, the inheritance of Elam.

Historical Debate

What Was Elam: State, Region, or Civilizational Network?

The question: Should historians describe Elam as a single continuous state comparable, in kind if not in scale, to Mesopotamian kingdoms such as Akkad or Babylon, or is it more accurate to describe Elam as a region, or as a network of related highland and lowland polities sharing language, religion, and royal titulature without necessarily constituting a single continuous government?

Interpretation A — Elam as a continuous political state: Some historians emphasize the recurring royal title "king of Anshan and Susa," the continuity of major cults such as Inshushinak's across many centuries, and the persistence of a recognizable Elamite political and cultural identity as evidence that Elam constituted a genuine, continuous political entity, comparable to major Mesopotamian states, whatever internal changes of dynasty or capital occurred along the way.
Interpretation B — Elam as a shifting regional and civilizational network: Other historians emphasize the repeated fragmentation between highland and lowland authorities, the long gaps in the documented succession of rulers, the shifting relationship between Susa and Anshan, and the absence of clear evidence for continuous centralized administration across the whole of Elamite territory throughout its history, arguing that "Elam" is better understood as a durable regional and religious identity uniting a changing constellation of polities rather than as a single continuous state.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same underlying evidence, royal inscriptions, Mesopotamian synchronisms, and archaeological remains at Susa, Anshan, and Dur-Untash, but differ in how much political unity and institutional continuity they are willing to infer from a fragmentary record punctuated by long gaps.

Why it matters: How historians answer this question shapes how confidently they can speak of "Elamite history" as a single continuous narrative, and it bears directly on how students should read this chapter's own organization, which presents Elam as a durable civilizational tradition without claiming that a single continuous government ruled it throughout its two-thousand-year span.

Student task: Using the evidence presented across this chapter, particularly the sukkalmah system, the Neo-Elamite fragmentation, and the recurring highland-lowland duality, construct an argument for each interpretation and explain which you find more persuasive, and why, while acknowledging what the alternative view captures that your preferred interpretation may understate.

Historical Vocabulary
Elam and Elamite

Definition: "Elam" is the Mesopotamian-derived name conventionally applied by historians to a civilization and shifting set of polities centered on Susa and Anshan in southwestern and highland Iran; the Elamites' own term for their land appears to have been Haltamti. "Elamite" refers both to the people and civilization associated with Elam and to their language, a language isolate not related to Sumerian, Akkadian, or the Indo-Iranian languages later spoken by Medes and Persians.

Historical context: Much of the terminology historians use to describe Elamite history, including "Elam" itself, derives from outside sources rather than from Elamite self-designation, a pattern this textbook has already flagged in its discussion of "Iran" and "Persia" in Chapter 1 and will continue to flag whenever externally applied historical labels shape how a civilization is remembered.

Why it matters: Recognizing that "Elam" is substantially a Mesopotamian and modern scholarly construction, rather than a term the Elamites necessarily used to describe their entire civilization across all periods, helps students avoid treating Elamite political and cultural identity as more fixed or self-consciously unified than the evidence actually supports.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not conclude that Elam therefore lacked genuine political or cultural coherence. Recurring royal titulature, shared religious cults, and persistent geographic and administrative patterns across many centuries demonstrate real continuity, even though the specific label "Elam" and its precise political boundaries were neither self-designated in a simple way nor fixed over time.

Multiple Perspectives

Living in Elamite Space: Perspectives Across the Lowland-Highland World

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians and archaeologists have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor.

From the perspective of a scribe at Susa: Professional training required fluency in cuneiform writing and, depending on the period, competence in both Akkadian and Elamite, since royal inscriptions, administrative records, and diplomatic correspondence might be composed in either language depending on audience and purpose.
From the perspective of a highland official connected to Anshan: Political loyalty and administrative practice were oriented toward a distant highland center whose relationship to Susa and the lowland court might shift, sometimes toward unified royal authority and sometimes toward greater regional independence, depending on the strength of the reigning dynasty.
From the perspective of a temple official serving the cult of Inshushinak: Successive kings across many centuries sought the god's favor and legitimation for their rule and their building projects at Susa, making the temple establishment a continuous institutional presence that outlasted individual dynasties and political crises.
From the perspective of a Babylonian scribe recording an Elamite invasion: Elamite kings appeared chiefly as threatening or opportunistic outside powers, intervening in Mesopotamian succession disputes and occasionally seizing treasured monuments, a portrayal shaped by Babylonian political interests as much as by the underlying events themselves.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Dynasty after Dynasty: What Persisted and What Changed in Elamite History

What Changed

  • Ruling dynasties and political centers shifted repeatedly, from Awan to Shimashki to the sukkalmah system to the Middle Elamite and Shutrukid houses to Neo-Elamite fragmentation
  • The relative importance of Susa and Anshan shifted across different periods, sometimes uniting under a single royal title and sometimes separating under distinct authorities
  • The dominant written language of administration shifted between Akkadian and Elamite at different periods
  • Elam's political relationship to Mesopotamian powers ranged across alliance, rivalry, subordination, and conquest at different moments

What Stayed the Same

  • Susa remained the most consistently important city and religious center across nearly the entire span of Elamite history
  • The cult of Inshushinak persisted as a central source of royal legitimation across many centuries and multiple dynasties
  • The combined title "king of Anshan and Susa" recurred as an ideal of unified authority even during periods when it was not fully achieved
  • Deep economic and political entanglement with Mesopotamia remained a constant feature of Elamite history from its earliest documented centuries through its final independent centuries

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both change and continuity allows students to understand Elam as a genuine historical civilization with real institutional persistence, rather than either an unchanging monolith or a meaningless label applied to unrelated, disconnected political episodes.

Everyday Life and Social History

Beyond the Kings: Ordinary Life in Elamite Society

The political narrative of rulers, treaties, and wars that occupies much of this chapter reflects the bias of the surviving evidence as much as it reflects the actual texture of Elamite life. Most people who lived within Elamite territory across its two-thousand-year history were not kings, scribes, or temple officials, but farmers working the irrigated fields of Susiana, pastoral populations moving herds through the highland zones around Anshan, artisans producing pottery, textiles, and metalwork, and laborers involved in the substantial construction projects associated with royal building programs such as Dur-Untash.

Archaeological evidence, including household remains, agricultural tools, and craft production debris recovered at Susa and other Elamite sites, provides historians with indirect evidence about these populations, even where surviving texts say little about them directly. Temple institutions, well attested through administrative texts across several periods, evidently controlled substantial agricultural land and labor, employing personnel ranging from priests and scribes to agricultural workers and craftspeople, making temples significant economic as well as religious institutions throughout much of Elamite history. Highland pastoral populations connected to the broader Zagros world, introduced already in Chapter 1's discussion of the region's geography, likely maintained economic and social relationships with lowland Elamite society that shifted according to the same political rhythms that shaped relations between Susa and Anshan more generally, though the details of these relationships are considerably less well documented than royal political history.

Historians must read the available evidence carefully for indirect testimony about these populations, since they left few or no records of their own, supplementing the political narrative with archaeological, material, and comparative evidence wherever possible, an approach this textbook will continue to apply throughout its treatment of subsequent ancient societies.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has traced the full historical arc of Elam, the first great civilization of southwestern Iran, from the later-fourth-millennium archaeological background examined in Chapter 4 through the Proto-Elamite phenomenon introduced in this chapter, and on through its final centuries as an independent political power and its subsequent incorporation into the Achaemenid Persian imperial system. It began by carefully defining Elam as a changing historical and political category rather than a fixed state or a synonym for Susa, and by establishing that Elamite, classified by most specialists as a language isolate, was not an Iranian language, a distinction essential to understanding how Elam relates to the Iranian-speaking peoples examined beginning in Chapter 6.

The chapter then examined the transition from the still incompletely understood Proto-Elamite administrative world to historically documented, dynastic Elam, cautioning against assuming automatic continuity between the two. It established the lowland-highland duality between Susa in Susiana and Anshan in highland Fars as a defining structural feature of Elamite political geography, recurring across the combined royal title "king of Anshan and Susa" that appears repeatedly throughout Elamite history.

Students traced Elam's political history through the Awan dynasty and its confrontations with Sargon of Akkad and his successors, including an Elamite-language agreement associated with Naram-Sin in the twenty-third century BCE and, roughly a century and a half later, the reign of Puzur-Inshushinak, the last Awan ruler and the strongest early evidence of Elamite political centralization. The chapter then examined the Shimashki period and the fall of Ur, conventionally dated to 2004 BCE under the Middle Chronology, cautioning against a simplified narrative of Elamite conquest, before turning to the sukkalmah period's flexible and still only partially understood patterns of delegated or shared highland and lowland authority during the early second millennium BCE.

A dedicated section examined Elamite language, writing systems, and religion, distinguishing Proto-Elamite, Linear Elamite, and Elamite cuneiform, and introducing the central cults of Inshushinak, associated with Susa, and Napirisha, associated with the highlands. The chapter then traced the Middle Elamite transformation, including the revival of unified highland-lowland royal titulature, the construction of Dur-Untash by Untash-Napirisha as a monumental expression of religious integration, and the military and political height of Shutrukid power, illustrated vividly through the capture and appropriation of Hammurabi's law stela as war booty.

The chapter then examined the Neo-Elamite period's escalating confrontation with Assyrian power, culminating in the battle of the Ulai River in 653 BCE and the sack of Susa in 646 BCE, while insisting that Assyrian claims of total destruction were considerably overstated and that Elamite political activity continued after this catastrophe. It previewed, without narrating, the gradual and complex process by which Iranian-speaking peoples, particularly those associated with Persian rulers at Anshan, came to occupy the same geographic and political space Elam had long inhabited.

Finally, the chapter demonstrated that Elam did not simply disappear with the rise of Persian imperial power. The extensive use of the Elamite language in the Achaemenid administrative archives at Persepolis, and the appearance of Elamite alongside Old Persian and Akkadian in major royal inscriptions, demonstrate that Persian imperial civilization incorporated substantial elements of the much older Elamite administrative and cultural tradition rather than replacing it wholesale.

Throughout the chapter, several recurring themes structured the narrative: the shifting balance between lowland Susa and highland Anshan; the complicated, often adversarial relationship between Elam and Mesopotamia; the recurring pattern of political fragmentation followed by reconsolidation; and the essential distinction between Elamite and Iranian-speaking populations, even as their histories became increasingly intertwined across the plateau's political geography.

These themes, and the specific historical narrative developed across this chapter, provide essential context for the chapters that follow. Chapter 6 will turn to the origins, languages, and migrations of Indo-Iranian peoples, examining a historical process that unfolded, in its later stages, within a landscape Elam had already shaped for two thousand years.

Key Terms

Elam
The Mesopotamian-derived name for a civilization and shifting constellation of polities centered on Susa and Anshan in southwestern and highland Iran, known to its own people, at least in part, as Haltamti.
Elamite
The language of Elam, classified as a language isolate not related to Sumerian, Akkadian, or the Indo-Iranian languages later spoken by Medes and Persians; also used to describe the people and civilization of Elam.
Language isolate
A language that cannot be confidently classified within any larger, well-established language family based on current evidence.
Susa
The principal lowland city of Elam, in Susiana, serving repeatedly as royal capital, religious center, and treasury of captured monuments.
Susiana
The fertile lowland plain surrounding Susa, closely connected economically and politically to nearby Mesopotamia.
Anshan
The principal highland center of Elam, located at the site now known as Tal-e Malyan in the modern Iranian province of Fars.
Awan
An early Elamite dynastic center, not securely located archaeologically, associated with Elam's earliest recorded conflicts with Akkad.
Puzur-Inshushinak
The last ruler of the Awan sequence, active around 2100 BCE, associated with strong political centralization and experimentation with Linear Elamite script alongside Akkadian cuneiform.
Shimashki
A highland-oriented Elamite dynasty succeeding Awan, associated with Kindattu and the fall of Ur at the end of Ibbi-Sin's reign, conventionally dated to 2004 BCE under the Middle Chronology.
Sukkalmah
The senior royal title of the early second millennium BCE Elamite political system, existing alongside subordinate sukkal titles in a still only partially understood layered structure.
Middle Elamite period
The Elamite historical period from roughly the sixteenth through twelfth centuries BCE, marked by revived unified royal titulature and increased use of the Elamite language in royal inscriptions.
Untash-Napirisha
A Middle Elamite king who founded Dur-Untash and its ziggurat, dedicating it to both Inshushinak and Napirisha.
Dur-Untash (Chogha Zanbil)
A purpose-built religious city centered on a massive ziggurat, founded by Untash-Napirisha in approximately the thirteenth century BCE.
Ziggurat
A stepped, terraced monumental religious structure characteristic of ancient Mesopotamian and Elamite temple architecture.
Inshushinak
The principal god of Susa, associated with kingship, justice, and the underworld, invoked by Elamite kings across many centuries.
Napirisha
A major Elamite deity strongly associated with the highland regions around Anshan.
Shutrukid dynasty
A powerful twelfth-century BCE Elamite royal house, including Shutruk-Nahhunte, Kutir-Nahhunte, and Shilhak-Inshushinak, known for extensive campaigns into Babylonia.
War trophy
A captured monument or object removed from a defeated power and displayed to assert the victor's authority, exemplified by the Elamite capture and removal of Hammurabi's law stela to Susa.
Neo-Elamite period
The final phase of independent Elamite political history, conventionally dated c. 1050/1100–539 BCE; the 646 BCE sack of Susa marks a major rupture within this period rather than its endpoint.
Battle of the Ulai River
A decisive 653 BCE defeat of Elamite forces by the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal.
Sack of Susa (646 BCE)
Ashurbanipal's destructive campaign against Susa, described in triumphant Assyrian inscriptions but not, as this chapter demonstrates, the end of Elamite history.
Persepolis Elamite tablets
A large Achaemenid administrative archive written predominantly in Elamite, demonstrating the language's continued importance under Persian imperial rule.
King of Anshan and Susa
A recurring Elamite royal title asserting unified authority over both the highland and lowland components of Elamite territory.
Proto-Elamite script
An undeciphered script used across a wide area of the Iranian plateau in the late fourth and early third millennium BCE, introduced in Chapter 5 as an extension of the later-fourth-millennium archaeological and administrative developments examined in Chapter 4.
Linear Elamite
A distinct Elamite script attested c. 2300–1880 BCE, associated with Puzur-Inshushinak and a corpus of inscribed silver vessels; a 2022 study proposed a substantially expanded decipherment, though specialists continue to debate significant aspects of it.
Cuneiform
The wedge-shaped writing system originating in Mesopotamia, adapted for writing both Akkadian and, in a distinct sign system, Elamite.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has told the story of a civilization easily overshadowed, in popular imagination, by its more famous Mesopotamian neighbors and by the Persian empire that eventually absorbed its territory. Elam deserves better than that treatment. For roughly two thousand years, Elamite kings ruled, or contested rule over, a territory spanning the fertile lowlands of Susiana and the highland plateau of Anshan, building temples, waging wars, negotiating treaties, and developing distinct religious and administrative traditions that left a permanent mark on the history of the Iranian plateau.

The habits of mind this chapter has modeled, reading hostile sources critically, distinguishing catastrophe from extinction, resisting the temptation to treat a civilization's ending as a tidy or inevitable conclusion, and recognizing that institutions and languages can survive the fall of the states that created them, will recur throughout this textbook's treatment of later empires and dynasties. Elam's story previews a pattern this textbook will encounter repeatedly: the Achaemenid, Parthian, and Sasanian empires examined in the volumes that follow will each, in turn, absorb, transform, and eventually give way to new political systems, and each transition will require the same careful attention to continuity and change that this chapter has applied to the end of independent Elamite rule.

Elam also matters because it complicates any simple narrative in which Iranian history begins with Iranian-speaking peoples. This textbook's editorial framework, established in Chapter 1, insists that Iranian history includes the many peoples who have shaped the plateau, whether or not they spoke an Iranian language. Elam is the clearest possible illustration of that principle applied to the ancient world: a civilization that was never ethnically or linguistically Iranian, yet whose cities, cults, and administrative practices became part of the inheritance that Persian kings would carry forward for centuries.

The chapters that follow will turn to a different historical process, the migrations and gradual settlement of Iranian-speaking peoples across the plateau, examined in Chapter 6, and the emergence of the Medes and Persians as historically documented peoples, examined in Chapters 7 and 8. Students should carry Elam with them into those chapters, not as a closed and finished story, but as the deep, still-active background against which Median and Persian history would unfold.
