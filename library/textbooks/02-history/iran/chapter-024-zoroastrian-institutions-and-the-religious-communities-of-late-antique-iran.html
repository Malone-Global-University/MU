Zoroastrian Institutions and the Religious Communities of Late Antique Iran

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

On a cliff face at Naqsh-i Rustam, not far from the tombs of the Achaemenid kings, a Sasanian priest named Kartir had his career recorded in several monumental inscriptions. These self-inscriptions were probably carved in several versions during the later third century, when Kartir had reached the height of his power under Bahram II, and they retrospectively recount a priestly career that had begun under Shapur I and continued through Hormizd I and Bahram I; they were not a running diary composed reign by reign, and their precise relative dating remains debated. In them he claimed to have strengthened the Zoroastrian priesthood, expanded fire temples, and confronted rival religious communities. No other Sasanian source speaks with such personal confidence about religious authority; no other source makes so plain how much a single ambitious priest wanted future readers, and perhaps the gods themselves, to know of his own importance.

Kartir's inscriptions are among the most valuable documents to survive from the entire Sasanian period, and they are also among the most dangerous to use carelessly. They tell historians, in unusually direct language, what one powerful priest believed he had accomplished across a career that spanned the reigns of Shapur I, Hormizd I, Bahram I, and Bahram II in the mid-to-late third century. But the surviving inscriptions themselves are later retrospective monuments looking back over that career rather than a series of documents produced contemporaneously with each reign, a distinction that matters for how confidently students can date any particular claim to a particular moment. The inscriptions do not, by themselves, tell historians what actually happened everywhere in the empire, how ordinary Zoroastrians practiced their religion in Fars or Khuzestan or northeastern Iran, or how the communities Kartir describes as targets of his zeal actually experienced whatever measures he claims credit for. Learning to read Kartir critically, taking his claims seriously as evidence of priestly ambition and religious politics while refusing to accept them as a transparent, contemporaneously produced government record, is one of the central historical skills this chapter asks students to develop.

The temptation to tell a simple story about religion in the Sasanian Empire is strong, and this chapter resists it deliberately. One simple story holds that the Sasanian dynasty, from its very founding in 224 CE, imposed a centralized, uniform Zoroastrian state church across its entire territory, persecuting Christians, Jews, Manichaeans, and dissenters as a matter of settled royal policy. Another simple story, reacting against the first, treats Sasanian Iran as a broadly tolerant, religiously indifferent empire in which persecution appears only as isolated and exceptional violence. Neither story survives close contact with the evidence. The four centuries examined in this chapter, from Ardashir I's founding of the dynasty in the 220s CE through the eve of the final Roman-Persian wars addressed in Chapter 27, contain long periods of royal patronage for Zoroastrian institutions alongside long periods in which Christian, Jewish, and Manichaean communities built substantial institutions of their own; moments of severe persecution alongside moments of pragmatic accommodation; and a priesthood whose authority grew more visible and more organized over time without ever becoming a single, centrally controlled hierarchy comparable to a modern state church.

This chapter's real subject, then, is not simply "what Zoroastrians believed." It is how religious institutions, religious communities, royal power, and law interacted across a large and diverse empire whose territory stretched, at various points, from Mesopotamia to the edges of Central Asia and from the Caucasus to the Persian Gulf. Chapter 23 examined the Sasanian imperial state, its kingship, administration, and social order, and explicitly set aside a fuller treatment of religious institutions and communities for this chapter. This chapter now takes up that task directly, asking how Zoroastrian priests organized themselves and their fire temples, how Zoroastrian sacred learning was preserved and eventually written down, how a new universal religion associated with the prophet Mani arose from within the empire and spread far beyond it, how Christian communities in Sasanian Mesopotamia built an ecclesiastical structure distinct from the Roman Christian world, how Jewish communities in Babylonia produced one of the most significant bodies of religious scholarship in Jewish history, and how the Sasanian monarchy responded to religious diversity and dissent across four centuries of shifting circumstances.

Evidence for this history survives in strikingly uneven forms. Zoroastrian priests left royal and priestly inscriptions in stone, along with a body of Middle Persian religious and legal literature whose final written form mostly postdates the Sasanian collapse by centuries. Christian communities left Syriac chronicles, martyrdom accounts, and ecclesiastical records produced by and for the church itself. Jewish communities in Babylonia produced an enormous body of rabbinic legal and narrative material later compiled as the Babylonian Talmud. Manichaean communities left missionary texts, some preserved as far away as Central Asia and Egypt, describing a religion its own followers understood as a universal revelation superseding earlier traditions. Each of these bodies of evidence speaks from within a particular community, for particular purposes, and often long after the events it describes. None of them offers a neutral outside view of Sasanian religious life as a whole. Reading them together, critically and comparatively, is the only way to approach an honest picture of how religion actually functioned within this empire.

This chapter also introduces a methodological problem that will recur throughout the remainder of this textbook whenever religious history intersects with political history: labels can mislead. Terms such as "state religion," "orthodoxy," "heresy," and even the modern scholarly convenience "Zurvanism" each carry assumptions drawn from later or different religious contexts that do not map neatly onto the Sasanian evidence. Students who learn to ask what a given label actually claims, and what evidence supports or complicates that claim, will be better prepared not only for the remainder of this chapter but for religious history throughout this textbook, from the Islamic conquest examined in Chapter 28 through the Sunni-Shi'i questions of Chapter 40 and the Safavid religious transformation examined in Chapter 58.

Finally, this chapter connects directly to the chapters that surround it. It builds on the administrative and social order Chapter 23 has already described, and it prepares the ground for Chapter 25's examination of Rome-Byzantium and Chapter 26's discussion of the eastern frontier and transregional exchange, both of which touch religious communities, particularly Christians and Manichaeans, whose histories extended across Sasanian borders. It also anticipates Chapter 27's account of the Mazdakite crisis and Khosrow I's reforms, a political story this chapter deliberately does not narrate in full, reserving the religious and social dimensions of Mazdak's teaching for treatment here while leaving the political consequences to the chapter that follows.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Sasanian Empire's religious landscape included substantial regional and communal diversity rather than a single uniform religious experience.
  2. Describe the relationship between Zoroastrian religious language and Sasanian royal ideology, and explain why this relationship developed gradually rather than appearing fully formed in 224 CE.
  3. Analyze the organization of the Zoroastrian priesthood, including priestly titles, fire temples, and religious endowments, as evolving institutions rather than static structures.
  4. Evaluate the inscriptions of Kartir as historical evidence, distinguishing his personal claims from a complete picture of Sasanian religious policy.
  5. Explain why the written compilation of the Avesta and related Zoroastrian literature complicates efforts to reconstruct third-century religious belief and practice.
  6. Trace the career of Mani and the early history of Manichaeism, including its relationship to royal patronage and subsequent persecution.
  7. Describe the institutional development of Christian communities in Sasanian Mesopotamia, distinguishing their experience from that of Christians within the Roman Empire.
  8. Explain the significance of Sasanian Babylonia as a center of Jewish communal and scholarly life, including its connection to the Babylonian Talmud.
  9. Assess the religious dimensions of the Mazdakite movement while recognizing the limitations of the surviving, largely hostile, source record.
  10. Distinguish between periods of royal patronage, toleration, and persecution across Sasanian history, and explain why religious policy varied by ruler, region, and political circumstance.
  11. Critically evaluate labels such as "state religion," "orthodoxy," and "Zurvanism," explaining the risks of applying them without qualification.
  12. Connect Sasanian religious history to the state and society examined in Chapter 23 and to the political, military, and social developments examined in Chapters 25 through 27.

Historical Context

Chapter 22 examined Ardashir I's overthrow of Artabanus IV, the principal Arsacid king of kings, and the establishment of Sasanian rule in the 220s CE, and Chapter 23 examined the imperial state that the early Sasanian kings and their successors built: its administrative structure, its social hierarchy, and its evolving relationship between monarchy and aristocracy. That chapter noted, without developing in full, that Sasanian kingship drew heavily on Zoroastrian religious language and imagery from an early period, and that the empire's religious communities, Zoroastrian priests foremost among them but also Christians, Jews, Manichaeans, and others, formed an important part of the social order Chapter 23 described. This chapter now supplies the fuller treatment that Chapter 23 deliberately postponed.

Several developments predating the Sasanian period shape the context for this chapter. Chapter 21 examined religion, art, language, and society under the Arsacids, noting that Parthian era Zoroastrianism, insofar as it can be reconstructed, appears considerably less institutionally centralized and less visibly tied to explicit royal religious ideology than what emerges under the Sasanians. Christian communities had already taken root in Mesopotamia under Arsacid rule, benefiting from that region's connections to the wider Roman and eastern Mediterranean world along Silk Road and Persian Gulf trade routes examined in Chapters 20 and 26. Jewish communities in Babylonia possessed a continuous history reaching back centuries before the Sasanian period, shaped by earlier Babylonian, Achaemenid, and Parthian rule.

The Sasanian dynasty's rise therefore did not create religious diversity in Iran and Mesopotamia; it inherited an already diverse religious landscape and, over time, developed new and more visible institutional relationships with it. The early Sasanian kings, beginning with Ardashir I and his son Shapur I, associated their rule closely with Zoroastrian religious language, royal fire cults, and the patronage of priests, a pattern this chapter examines in Section 24.2. At the same time, the third century witnessed the career of Mani, a religious teacher who arose from within the Sasanian world and who, for a period, enjoyed access to the highest levels of the Sasanian court before his movement collided with priestly and royal authority, a story this chapter examines in Section 24.5.

Across the fourth, fifth, and sixth centuries, covered by the remainder of this chapter and continuing into the political narrative of Chapter 27, the empire's relationship with its religious communities continued to change. Christian communities faced episodes of severe persecution, particularly from the fourth century onward once Christianity became the dominant religion of the rival Roman Empire, but they also built lasting ecclesiastical institutions and, at times, received royal toleration and even patronage. Jewish communities in Babylonia developed sophisticated legal and scholarly institutions largely without the same intensity of persecution, producing, over centuries, the Babylonian Talmud. New or revived movements, including the controversial teaching associated with Mazdak in the late fifth and early sixth centuries, raised further questions about religious authority, social order, and the limits of royal toleration, questions this chapter examines in Section 24.8 while leaving their full political consequences to Chapter 27.

Section 24.1

The Religious Landscape of Sasanian Iran

Before examining any single religious community in detail, students must understand a basic fact about the Sasanian Empire: it was religiously diverse, and that diversity was not incidental but structural. The empire's territory, stretching at various points from Mesopotamia in the west to the edges of Central Asia in the east, and from the Caucasus in the north to the Persian Gulf in the south, contained Zoroastrians of varying degrees of institutional attachment, Christians belonging to several distinct traditions, Jewish communities with a long continuous history in Babylonia, followers of Mani's new universal religion, and a range of other local, regional, and syncretic religious practices that left less complete documentary traces.

This diversity was also profoundly uneven across the empire's geography. Fars, the historical Sasanian homeland examined in earlier chapters, provided the base from which royal Zoroastrian patronage most directly radiated. Mesopotamia, by contrast, was home to substantial Christian and Jewish populations, including locally concentrated communities in many parts of Mesopotamia, whose presence long predated Sasanian rule and who remained significant throughout the Sasanian period, though neither community formed a demographic majority across the region as a whole. Armenia and the wider Caucasus, examined further in Chapter 25's treatment of the Roman-Sasanian rivalry, developed their own distinct Christian institutional history, entangled with but never simply identical to the Church of the East further south. Khuzestan, astride important trade and administrative routes, hosted a religiously mixed population reflecting its position as a frontier and connective zone. Northeastern Iran and the frontier regions examined more fully in Chapter 26 present their own patterns of religious life, shaped by contact with Central Asian populations and by the region's role as a corridor for missionary movement in multiple directions.

Historians reconstructing this diverse landscape must rely on radically different kinds of evidence for different communities, each shaped by its own purposes and preserved through its own institutional channels. Royal and priestly inscriptions, most famously those associated with Kartir, present a Zoroastrian priestly perspective concerned above all with asserting religious authority and royal favor. Syriac Christian chronicles, synodal records, and martyrdom accounts preserve an ecclesiastical perspective focused on persecution, resistance, and institutional consolidation. Armenian sources offer a related but distinct perspective shaped by that region's own political and religious trajectory. Jewish literature, above all the vast corpus eventually compiled as the Babylonian Talmud, preserves an extraordinarily rich but primarily legal and academic record whose relationship to political events is often oblique. Manichaean texts, some preserved only because Mani's religion spread far beyond Iran into the Roman Empire, North Africa, Central Asia, and eventually China, present the self-understanding of a movement that saw itself as superseding all earlier revelations. Later Middle Persian Zoroastrian literature, compiled well after the Sasanian Empire's collapse, offers a retrospective and often idealized picture of a "properly" ordered Zoroastrian past. Archaeological evidence, including fire temple remains, religious seals, and inscribed vessels, supplements but rarely resolves the gaps and contradictions among these textual traditions.

No single description, then, can adequately capture "Sasanian religion." This chapter proceeds region by region and community by community only in a loose sense; its deeper organizing principle is institutional and comparative, asking how each community organized religious authority, how each related to Sasanian kingship and law, and how each experienced the empire's shifting religious policy across four centuries of change.

Section 24.2

Zoroastrianism, Kingship, and the Early Sasanian Dynasty

From its founding, Sasanian kingship drew on Zoroastrian religious language in ways that distinguished it, at least in degree, from the more religiously understated public image of many Arsacid kings examined in Chapter 18. Royal investiture reliefs, most famously those at Naqsh-i Rustam, depict Sasanian kings receiving the ring of kingship directly from the god Ohrmazd, known in an earlier form as Ahura Mazda, the principal deity of Zoroastrian tradition. Royal inscriptions invoke Ohrmazd's favor and describe the king's rule in terms connected to the concept of xwarrah, a divinely granted royal glory or fortune whose loss or possession was understood to mark legitimate or illegitimate rule.

Fire, central to Zoroastrian ritual practice, also appears prominently in early Sasanian royal imagery and in the establishment of royal fire foundations associated with individual kings. These fires, and the temples built to house them, connected dynastic legitimacy directly to Zoroastrian religious practice in a manner that later chapters, particularly Chapter 57's discussion of Safavid religious legitimation, will show recurring in different form under a very different religious tradition centuries later.

Despite this visible religious language, students must resist an easy but misleading conclusion: that the Sasanians created, in 224 CE or shortly thereafter, a fully centralized, uniformly administered Zoroastrian state church comparable to a modern religious bureaucracy. The evidence does not support such a claim. Early Sasanian religious policy appears to have combined royal patronage of Zoroastrian priests and fire temples with considerable local and regional variation in how thoroughly, and by whom, Zoroastrian institutions were actually organized. The very existence of Kartir's inscriptions, in which a single priest boasts at length of his own efforts to expand and strengthen priestly authority across several reigns, suggests that this expansion was an ongoing project pursued by ambitious individuals and institutions rather than a single completed act of state formation.

The relationship between monarchy and priesthood, moreover, was one of mutual dependence and occasional tension rather than simple identity. Kings relied on priestly ritual and ideological support to legitimate their rule, and priests relied on royal patronage to expand fire temples, religious endowments, and institutional authority. But royal and priestly interests did not always align perfectly. Some kings appear to have granted other religious communities, including at times Mani's new movement, greater access and toleration than an unqualified "Zoroastrian state church" model would predict, at least until priestly and other political pressures shifted royal policy. Understanding Sasanian kingship's relationship to Zoroastrianism therefore requires holding two facts together: religious language and priestly patronage were genuinely central to Sasanian royal ideology from an early period, and that relationship remained a developing, negotiated one rather than a static institutional fact fixed at the dynasty's founding.

Historical Vocabulary
"State Religion"

Definition: "State religion" is a modern analytical term describing a religion granted official, privileged status by a government. It is not a term Sasanian sources used of themselves, and applying it to Zoroastrianism under the Sasanians requires care about what the term does and does not claim.

Historical context: Sasanian kings consistently associated their rule with Zoroastrian imagery and patronized Zoroastrian priests and fire temples, evidence that supports some version of the "state religion" label. At the same time, the priesthood's own organization developed gradually rather than appearing as a complete state apparatus in 224 CE, and royal policy toward other communities varied considerably by ruler and period, complicating any simple application of the term.

Why it matters: Whether or how a student qualifies this label shapes how confidently other evidence in this chapter, including Kartir's claims and the treatment of Christian, Jewish, and Manichaean communities, gets interpreted. This chapter's Historical Debate feature, below, examines the question directly.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that calling Zoroastrianism a "state religion" implies a centralized bureaucratic institution comparable to a modern established church, uniformly administered across the empire from the dynasty's founding.

Section 24.3

The Zoroastrian Priesthood, Fire Temples, and Religious Institutions

Zoroastrian priestly organization became considerably more visible and more institutionally elaborate over the course of the Sasanian period, though reconstructing its precise structure at any given moment remains difficult. Several priestly titles appear in the surviving evidence, most importantly mowbed, a senior priestly title whose specific functions and jurisdiction varied by period and context; hērbed, a title associated with religious teaching and learning; and, from a later point in Sasanian history, mowbedān mowbed, literally "mowbed of mowbeds," suggesting the emergence of a senior priestly office with some claim to authority over other priests. Students should understand these titles as reflecting a developing hierarchy rather than assume that a fully formed pyramidal priestly bureaucracy existed from the dynasty's earliest years.

Fire temples served as the central institutional anchor of Zoroastrian religious life. These sites maintained sacred fires, conducted rituals, and, importantly for their broader social role, frequently held significant landed endowments that supported priestly personnel and temple maintenance. Religious endowment, a pattern with parallels across many late antique and medieval religious traditions, tied fire temples into the empire's broader economic and legal structures, since endowed land, its management, and the obligations attached to it fell partly under religious authority and partly under the administrative and legal structures examined in Chapter 23. Priests connected to fire temples also performed important social and legal functions beyond ritual, including roles in education, in the certification of certain contracts and family matters, and in the transmission of religious and legal learning to the next generation of priests.

Kartir stands as the single most important individual source for this institutional history, and also as its single greatest interpretive challenge. His surviving self-inscriptions were probably carved in several versions during the later third century, when he had reached the height of his power under Bahram II, and they retrospectively recount a priestly career that had begun under Shapur I and continued through Hormizd I and Bahram I, describing his own rising priestly rank, his role in establishing and strengthening fire temples across the empire, and his claims to have curtailed the activities of rival religious communities. The specific groups he names are themselves a matter of ongoing scholarly discussion, since the Middle Persian terms in his inscriptions are not always transparent. Modern translations render the list, with varying degrees of confidence, as Jews, Shamans or Buddhists, Brahmans, Christians, a group called Nāṣrā, an uncertain group sometimes rendered as Makdags, whose identification as "baptists" is explicitly uncertain, and Zandīks, generally understood as Manichaeans or other groups accused of heretical "interpretation." The precise identification and boundaries of each named community remain debated rather than settled. These claims are extraordinarily valuable as evidence of one ambitious priest's self-presentation and of the kinds of religious authority a senior priest in this period might aspire to exercise. They are considerably less reliable as evidence that Kartir's program was uniformly implemented, empire-wide, exactly as he describes it, and their retrospective composition means individual claims cannot always be securely pinned to a specific year or even a specific reign. No corroborating outside source confirms the full scope of Kartir's claimed achievements, and inscriptions of this kind, composed for royal and religious display, characteristically emphasize the achievements of their subject rather than offering a balanced administrative record.

This source problem is not a reason to dismiss Kartir; it is a reason to read him as one perspective among several, asking what kind of document an inscription is, who commissioned it, and what purposes it served, questions this chapter's Primary Source Window develops in detail below.

Historical Vocabulary
Mowbed and Priestly Titles

Definition: Mowbed designates a senior Zoroastrian priestly title whose specific functions, rank, and jurisdiction appear to have varied considerably by period, region, and individual holder, rather than corresponding to one fixed office with uniform responsibilities. Hērbed designates a priestly role connected especially to religious teaching and the transmission of sacred learning. Mowbedān mowbed, attested from a later point in Sasanian history, denotes a senior priestly title implying authority over other mowbeds, though the practical extent of that authority is not fully clear from the surviving evidence.

Historical context: These titles appear unevenly across the surviving evidence, and their precise responsibilities, relative rank, and geographic scope of authority likely varied across the Sasanian period rather than remaining fixed. Kartir's inscriptions record his own progression through several priestly ranks and titles across multiple reigns, offering the clearest, though still partial, window onto how such a career could develop.

Why it matters: Understanding these titles prevents students from imagining a single, unchanging priestly bureaucracy comparable to a modern religious hierarchy. Priestly organization developed gradually and unevenly, and its visibility in the sources often reflects the ambitions and resources of individual priests and temples as much as any single empire-wide administrative structure.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that a fully centralized priestly chain of command, with a single figure exercising uniform authority over all Zoroastrian priests throughout the empire, existed from an early date. The mowbedān mowbed title's emergence over time suggests increasing, but never complete, institutional centralization.

Section 24.4

The Avesta, Religious Learning, Law, and the Problem of Reconstruction

Any account of Sasanian Zoroastrianism must confront a fundamental evidentiary problem directly: much of what survives as "Zoroastrian literature" reached its final written form well after the Sasanian Empire itself had collapsed, following the Arab-Muslim conquest examined in Chapter 28. This creates a persistent risk of anachronism, in which later, sometimes considerably later, textual traditions are mistakenly read as transparent windows onto third-, fourth-, or fifth-century religious practice.

The Avesta, the core corpus of Zoroastrian sacred literature, was transmitted for a very long period primarily through oral recitation by trained priests, a method of transmission that Zoroastrian tradition itself regarded as appropriate to sacred material. Only later, using a specially developed script well suited to representing the sounds of the Avestan language, was a substantial portion of this material committed to writing, a process most plausibly associated with the Sasanian period though its precise chronology, scope, and the degree of royal versus priestly initiative involved remain debated among specialists. Popular tradition sometimes credits a specific early Sasanian king, such as Ardashir I or Shapur I, with definitively "collecting the Avesta" in a single act. This textbook treats such claims with caution: later tradition often compresses a gradual, uneven process of textual transmission and compilation into a single tidy founding narrative, and the evidence does not support treating any single king's reign as marking the complete and final establishment of a written Avestan canon.

Alongside the Avesta itself, a substantial body of Middle Persian religious and legal literature, often referred to by the adjective "Pahlavi" in scholarly usage, developed to explain, interpret, and apply Avestan material. This literature includes commentary, theological reflection, legal compilations addressing family law, property, and religious obligation, and narrative and cosmological texts. Much of this material reached its surviving written form during the early Islamic centuries, compiled by Zoroastrian communities working to preserve and systematize their tradition under new and often difficult political circumstances. This later compilation context means that Middle Persian religious literature, while an invaluable resource for understanding Zoroastrian belief and practice, cannot simply be read as an unmediated record of Sasanian era religion; it reflects, at least in part, later priestly efforts at systematization, and possibly at the resolution or suppression of internal disagreements that existed in earlier periods.

Zoroastrian legal thought deserves particular attention, since religious and legal authority overlapped substantially in this tradition. Priests played significant roles in matters such as marriage, inheritance, and the management of religious endowments, and later Middle Persian legal compilations preserve detailed, if not always straightforwardly datable, evidence of how such matters were understood to be governed. Students encountering this material in more advanced study should apply the same caution urged throughout this section: later legal compilations describe an idealized and systematized legal-religious order whose relationship to actual third- or fourth-century practice remains a matter for careful historical judgment rather than simple assumption.

The practical lesson of this section is methodological rather than merely factual. When later chapters, or more advanced study of Sasanian religion, draw on Middle Persian literature to describe "Zoroastrian belief," students should ask when the cited text reached its surviving form, how confidently that content can be projected backward into the Sasanian period under discussion, and what alternative, more nearly contemporary evidence, such as inscriptions, seals, or outside observers, might corroborate or complicate the picture.

Section 24.5

Mani and Manichaeism

Mani, born in Mesopotamia in the early third century CE, grew up within a religious community showing influences from several traditions of the region before, according to Manichaean tradition, receiving revelations that led him to proclaim a new and, in his own understanding, universal and final religious message. Mani's teaching drew selectively on Zoroastrian, Christian, Jewish, and other religious concepts current in the third-century Mesopotamian religious environment, synthesizing them into a distinctive dualistic cosmology contrasting realms of light and darkness, and presenting Mani himself as the final in a line of prophets that included, in his own account, figures such as Zoroaster, the Buddha, and Jesus.

Mani's early career benefited from remarkable royal access. He appears to have gained the favor of Shapur I, the powerful second Sasanian king examined in earlier chapters for his military campaigns against Rome, and Manichaean tradition credits this royal relationship with allowing Mani considerable freedom to teach and to send missionaries across and beyond Sasanian territory. This period illustrates an important point about early Sasanian religious policy: royal favor was not narrowly restricted to Zoroastrian priestly interests alone, and a king could, at least for a time, extend patronage or toleration to a new religious movement whose relationship to established Zoroastrian institutions was, from a priestly perspective, deeply threatening.

That relationship changed sharply within a few decades. Kartir's own inscriptions list followers of Mani among the religious communities he claims to have confronted, and Manichaean and other sources describe Mani's eventual imprisonment and death under King Bahram I in the 270s CE, a dramatic reversal from his earlier standing at Shapur I's court. The most plausible explanation for this reversal combines several factors rather than a single cause: growing priestly hostility toward a movement that directly challenged Zoroastrian religious claims and priestly authority, shifting political calculations under a new king less invested in Mani's earlier patronage relationship, and perhaps concern regarding the social and political implications of a rapidly spreading new religious movement with its own missionary institutions and international reach.

Manichaeism's subsequent history extended far beyond Iran, spreading westward into the Roman Empire and North Africa, where it influenced, among others, the young Augustine of Hippo before his conversion to Christianity, and eastward along trade routes into Central Asia and eventually China, where Manichaean communities persisted for centuries and where important Manichaean manuscript discoveries in the twentieth century have significantly expanded modern understanding of the religion's teachings and institutional life. This transregional spread connects directly to the broader networks examined in Chapter 26's treatment of the eastern frontier and Silk Road exchange, though this chapter's focus remains on Manichaeism's origins within, and its complicated relationship to, the Sasanian court and priesthood rather than its full later transregional history.

Mani's career offers this chapter's clearest single illustration of a broader argument: Sasanian religious policy was not a fixed, predetermined program but a matter of shifting relationships among kings, priests, and religious communities, capable of moving within a single dynasty's early history from patronage to persecution as political and institutional circumstances changed.

Section 24.6

Christianity in the Sasanian Empire

Christian communities within the Sasanian Empire, concentrated especially in Mesopotamia but present in varying strength across much of the empire's territory, developed a history and institutional structure distinct from Christianity within the Roman Empire, a distinction students must hold clearly in mind. These were, from an early period, indigenous communities embedded in Mesopotamian society, worshipping and organizing largely within a Syriac Christian tradition with its own liturgical language, theological emphases, and ecclesiastical institutions, rather than simply an extension of Roman Christianity operating under Sasanian rule.

Ecclesiastical organization developed steadily across the fourth and fifth centuries. Seleucia-Ctesiphon, already significant as a Sasanian royal center examined in Chapter 23, also emerged as a major seat of Christian ecclesiastical authority, and church synods held within the empire addressed matters of doctrine, discipline, and institutional structure for what would become known as the Church of the East. A particularly concrete milestone came in 410 CE, when a synod convened at Seleucia-Ctesiphon under King Yazdegerd I formally organized the empire's Christian communities under a recognized ecclesiastical hierarchy, an arrangement that royal authority itself helped to sanction. According to the surviving synodical tradition, a further synod in 424 CE asserted the eastern church's autonomy from western ecclesiastical authorities, within the Roman Empire, a step some historians read as reinforcing the community's standing as an indigenous Sasanian institution rather than an extension of Roman Christianity, although historians have questioned aspects of the transmitted account, which survives chiefly through the later Synodicon orientale. This institutional consolidation proceeded, notably, during a period that also included some of the most severe persecution Sasanian Christians experienced, a juxtaposition that itself illustrates the complexity this chapter emphasizes: persecution and institutional development were not mutually exclusive, and a community under periodic royal pressure could nonetheless organize itself with increasing sophistication.

The relationship between persecution and the Roman-Sasanian rivalry deserves careful, non-simplistic treatment. Students often encounter a shorthand explanation holding that Sasanian kings persecuted Christians because they suspected them of loyalty to a Christian Roman Empire following Constantine's conversion and the Christianization of Roman state institutions in the fourth century. There is real substance to this explanation: the coincidence of intensified persecution with periods of heightened Roman-Sasanian conflict, examined further in Chapter 25, suggests that political suspicion regarding Christian loyalty did influence royal policy at various points. However, this explanation cannot be treated as a complete or uniform account. Sasanian Christian communities were not simply a Roman fifth column; they were long-established, indigenous Mesopotamian populations with their own regional identities, and their relationship to Roman Christianity was, especially following later doctrinal disputes within the wider Christian world, often one of theological distinction and even estrangement rather than straightforward political solidarity. Persecution, moreover, was not continuous across the full four centuries of Sasanian rule; it occurred in identifiable episodes, under particular kings and particular political circumstances, interspersed with periods of relative toleration and, at times, meaningful royal accommodation of Christian communities and their leadership.

Students should therefore avoid two equally misleading claims: that Sasanian Christians were persecuted continuously for four centuries as a matter of settled state policy, and that persecution can be explained solely by simple political suspicion of Roman loyalty. The more accurate picture recognizes indigenous Christian communities building durable ecclesiastical institutions across the Sasanian period, experiencing serious and sometimes violent episodes of persecution shaped by a combination of religious, political, and international factors, while also negotiating, and at times achieving, considerable institutional stability and even royal accommodation. Armenian Christianity, closely connected to but institutionally distinct from the Church of the East further south, developed its own trajectory shaped heavily by the Roman-Sasanian contest for influence over Armenia and the wider Caucasus, a political and military story this chapter leaves principally to Chapter 25.

Section 24.7

Jewish Communities and Sasanian Babylonia

Sasanian Mesopotamia, and Babylonia in particular, was home to one of the most significant Jewish communities anywhere in Late Antiquity, a population with roots reaching back centuries before Sasanian rule to earlier Babylonian, Achaemenid, and Parthian periods. This community deserves sustained attention in its own right rather than passing mention within a general category of "religious minorities," both because of its scale and continuity and because of the extraordinary body of religious and legal scholarship it produced.

Jewish communal life in Sasanian Babylonia sustained important networks and centers of rabbinic learning in which scholars developed and debated legal interpretation, ritual practice, and religious narrative across successive generations. Later Geonic tradition described major centers such as Sura and Pumbedita as formal academies continuing in unbroken institutional form from the Sasanian period, but historians debate how closely that later institutional model can be projected backward onto the third through sixth centuries; the underlying scholarly and communal activity is well attested, even where its precise institutional shape at any given moment remains uncertain. This scholarly activity, conducted over several centuries spanning much of the Sasanian period and continuing after its collapse, eventually produced the Babylonian Talmud, a vast compilation of legal discussion, narrative material, and religious interpretation that became, and remains, one of the foundational texts of rabbinic Judaism.

The relationship between these Jewish communities and Sasanian political authority appears, on the whole, to have differed significantly from the pattern of periodic severe persecution documented for Christian communities, though Jewish communities were not wholly exempt from tension or occasional difficulty with royal or local authorities. Jewish tradition preserves the position of an exilarch, a communal leader whose office mediated between the Jewish community and Sasanian authority, suggesting an institutionalized relationship that allowed for a considerable degree of internal Jewish legal and communal autonomy under the wider framework of Sasanian rule.

Students must apply careful source criticism to this material just as to Christian, Zoroastrian, and Manichaean sources. The Talmud and related rabbinic literature are overwhelmingly legal, ritual, and narrative in character rather than straightforward political chronicles; they preserve an extraordinarily rich picture of social, legal, and religious life within the rabbinic community, but they cannot simply be mined as a direct record of Sasanian court politics or general Sasanian policy toward Jewish communities as a whole. References to Sasanian rulers, institutions, or events within rabbinic literature must be read within their literary and legal context rather than treated as equivalent to an administrative document. At the same time, the depth and continuity of this scholarly tradition itself constitutes important historical evidence: it demonstrates that Sasanian Babylonia provided conditions, whatever their precise legal and political character, under which sustained, multi-generational Jewish legal and religious scholarship, carried on through rabbinic scholarly circles rather than necessarily through formally chartered academies in the later sense, could flourish over a very long period.

Including Babylonian Jewish life as a major section of this chapter, rather than as a brief addition to a "minorities" discussion, also serves a broader interpretive purpose: it prevents the mistaken impression that late antique Iranian religious history can be adequately understood as a two-sided contest between Zoroastrianism and Christianity. The Sasanian religious landscape was considerably more plural than that framing suggests, and Jewish Babylonia stands as clear evidence of a third major, long-lived, and institutionally productive religious tradition flourishing within Sasanian territory.

Section 24.8

Religious Diversity, Dissent, and the Mazdakite Problem

The late fifth and early sixth centuries produced one of the most historically contested episodes in this chapter's subject: the movement associated with a religious teacher named Mazdak. According to later sources, most of them hostile and composed well after the events they describe, Mazdak's teaching combined religious ideas, drawing in some fashion on Zoroastrian tradition, with radical social prescriptions concerning the redistribution of property and, in some accounts, of women, and gained sufficient influence to attract the reported support of King Kavad I for a period before an eventual and violent reversal associated with Kavad's son and eventual successor, Khosrow I.

This chapter addresses Mazdak's religious and social teaching directly while resisting the temptation to narrate the full political crisis, since that political narrative, including Kavad's temporary deposition, his restoration, and Khosrow I's subsequent reforms, belongs properly to Chapter 27. What belongs here is a critical assessment of what can and cannot be known about Mazdak's actual teaching. The overwhelming majority of surviving accounts were composed by later, often explicitly hostile, sources writing after Mazdak's movement had been violently suppressed and after subsequent generations, including the Zoroastrian priesthood and later Islamic era historians drawing on Sasanian era tradition, had strong reasons to portray Mazdak in the most negative possible terms, as a dangerous heretic whose teachings threatened social and religious order. Students should treat vivid details, such as claims regarding the communal sharing of property or women, with considerable caution, recognizing that hostile heresiographical traditions across many religious contexts, not only this one, frequently attribute extreme and scandalous practices to condemned movements, sometimes with limited basis in the movement's actual teaching.

What can be said with somewhat greater confidence is that Mazdak's movement represented a genuine internal challenge to established religious and social authority, one taken seriously enough by contemporaries to provoke, eventually, a severe royal and priestly response, and one that briefly found support at the highest level of Sasanian political authority. This alone demonstrates an important point for this chapter's broader argument: internal Zoroastrian religious dissent, not only competition from separate religions such as Christianity, Judaism, or Manichaeism, posed real challenges to religious and political authority within the Sasanian Empire, and royal responses to such internal dissent could be as severe as, or more severe than, responses to established outside religious communities.

This section is also the appropriate place to address theological diversity within Zoroastrian tradition more broadly, particularly the modern scholarly label Zurvanism. Some later evidence suggests currents of Zoroastrian thought that emphasized Zurvan, a personification of Time, as a principle temporally and ontologically prior to both Ohrmazd and Ahriman, the opposed principles of good and evil in more standard Zoroastrian cosmology. Some earlier modern scholarship treated Zurvanism as though it constituted a fully distinct, organized rival religion or sect competing directly with a more orthodox Zoroastrianism. More recent and cautious scholarship suggests this may overstate the case: the evidence more plausibly indicates a spectrum of theological emphasis and speculation within a broader Zoroastrian tradition rather than two neatly separated, institutionally distinct churches. Students should therefore use "Zurvanism" carefully, as a scholarly label pointing to a real theological tendency rather than as shorthand for a fully separate organized religious institution comparable to, say, the distinction between the Church of the East and the Sasanian Zoroastrian priesthood.

Taken together, the Mazdakite movement and the more diffuse theological currents associated with Zurvan demonstrate that "Zoroastrianism" itself was not a single, doctrinally uniform tradition even among those who considered themselves within it. Religious boundaries in Sasanian Iran were real and consequential, but the religious landscape inside as well as outside of Zoroastrian tradition was considerably more complicated than a simple list of separate, internally uniform, mutually isolated religious communities would suggest.

Section 24.9

Religion, Monarchy, Law, and Community in the Sasanian Empire

This concluding section draws together the evidence examined across this chapter into a synthesis that returns to the central question posed at the outset: how did Zoroastrian institutions, other religious communities, and the Sasanian monarchy interact across four centuries of changing political and social conditions? The answer this chapter offers rejects two tempting simplifications in favor of a more historically accurate, if less tidy, picture.

Religion functioned, across this period, simultaneously as several distinct kinds of historical force. It served as a source of royal legitimacy, visible in the Zoroastrian imagery of royal investiture and the concept of divinely granted xwarrah examined in Section 24.2. It served as an institutional basis of priestly authority, expressed through the developing hierarchy of mowbeds, hērbeds, and eventually a mowbedān mowbed, and through the landed wealth and social functions of fire temples examined in Section 24.3. It served as a framework for law and social classification, particularly evident in Zoroastrian legal literature and in the distinct communal legal institutions of Babylonian Jewish life examined in Section 24.7. It served as a basis for communal organization among Christians, Jews, and Manichaeans alike, each building institutions, whether ecclesiastical, academic, or missionary, that gave their communities structure and continuity independent of, though never wholly separate from, Sasanian political authority. It served, at various points, as a source of political suspicion, most visibly in the treatment of Christian communities amid Roman-Sasanian conflict examined in Section 24.6, but also in the priesthood's suspicion of Manichaean and Mazdakite movements examined in Sections 24.5 and 24.8. And it served as a means through which communities negotiated with the state, evident in the institutionalized relationship suggested by the Jewish exilarchate, in periods of Christian ecclesiastical accommodation, and in the shifting royal treatment of Mani's movement across a single generation.

The chapter's central conclusion, then, is this: the Sasanian Empire was neither a secular multi-religious state indifferent to religion nor a uniformly administered Zoroastrian theocracy. Religious authority within this empire was institutional, in that it depended on developing organizations, priesthoods, and legal frameworks rather than diffuse belief alone; politically consequential, in that religious identity and religious institutions repeatedly shaped and were shaped by royal policy and international rivalry; regionally uneven, in that the experience of a Zoroastrian priest in Fars, a Christian bishop in Mesopotamia, a Jewish scholar in Babylonia, and a Manichaean missionary operating across imperial borders differed substantially from one another; and historically changing, in that the relationship between any given community and the Sasanian monarchy shifted considerably across four centuries in response to individual rulers, priestly influence, political circumstance, and international conflict.

This conclusion connects directly back to Chapter 23's account of a Sasanian imperial state built on negotiated relationships among monarchy, aristocracy, and administrative institutions rather than on simple centralized command. Chapter 23 explained who governed the Sasanian Empire and how that governance was organized; this chapter has explained how organized religious authority and diverse religious communities existed within, and helped to shape, that same negotiated political order. The chapters that follow will show this religious history intersecting repeatedly with political and military narrative: Chapter 25 will examine how Christian communities and Armenian religious identity became entangled in the long Roman-Sasanian rivalry, Chapter 26 will trace religious and cultural exchange along the empire's eastern frontier, and Chapter 27 will return to the Mazdakite crisis introduced here to explain its full political consequences and its connection to Khosrow I's reforms and the empire's eventual collapse.

Paraphrase: Kartir described his own rise through a series of priestly titles under successive kings, his role in establishing and strengthening fire temples across the empire, and his claims to have confronted and constrained the activities of several named religious communities, presenting these achievements as service to the Zoroastrian religion and to royal authority.

Analysis: Kartir's inscriptions represent an unusually direct and personal statement of priestly ambition and religious politics for this period, offering historians rare insight into how a senior priest wished his career and his relationship to royal authority to be remembered. The recurrence of these claims across several inscriptional versions, probably produced late in Kartir's career, shows his sustained effort to memorialize and publicize an institutional standing that had developed across several reigns.

Historical Debate

Was Zoroastrianism a "State Religion" under the Sasanians?

The question: Historians frequently describe Zoroastrianism as the "state religion" of the Sasanian Empire. Does this label accurately capture the historical relationship between Zoroastrian institutions and Sasanian political authority, or does it import assumptions about centralized, uniform religious establishment that the evidence does not fully support?

Interpretation A — Zoroastrianism functioned as a genuine state religion: Some historians point to the consistent Zoroastrian imagery of royal investiture, the sustained pattern of royal patronage for fire temples and priests, Kartir's claims of priestly expansion under royal favor, and episodes of persecution directed against rival religious communities as evidence that Zoroastrianism enjoyed a privileged, officially established position closely bound to the Sasanian state throughout most of its history.
Interpretation B — "State religion" overstates the degree of centralization and uniformity: Other historians caution that the term "state religion" implies a level of institutional centralization, doctrinal uniformity, and consistent policy that the Sasanian evidence does not clearly support. They point to the gradual and uneven development of priestly hierarchy, the significant royal toleration extended to Mani's movement for a period, the substantial and sometimes officially accommodated Christian and Jewish institutional life, and considerable regional variation, arguing that "close and evolving royal patronage of Zoroastrian institutions, combined with periodic intolerance of rivals," describes the evidence more accurately than the modern term "state religion."

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of evidence: royal investiture reliefs and inscriptions, Kartir's priestly inscriptions, Christian and Jewish institutional records, and the later Middle Persian literature discussed in Section 24.4, but differ in how much weight to give evidence of consistent central policy as against evidence of variation, negotiation, and change over time.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns primarily interpretation and terminology rather than a dispute over basic facts: both sides generally agree that Zoroastrian priests enjoyed substantial royal patronage and that other communities experienced both toleration and persecution at different times; they disagree about whether "state religion" is the most historically precise term for describing this relationship.

Why it matters: The label chosen shapes how students and scholars interpret every other piece of evidence in this chapter. A reader who assumes a fully centralized state church may be tempted to treat every persecution as centrally ordered policy and every priestly claim as accomplished fact; a reader alert to the more negotiated, evolving relationship this chapter describes will look more carefully for regional variation, royal individual variation, and change over time.

Student task: Using the evidence presented across this chapter, construct an argument for how you would qualify or refine the term "state religion" when applied to Sasanian Zoroastrianism, identifying what the term captures accurately and what it risks obscuring.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Religious Organization from the Arsacids to the Sasanians

What Changed

  • Zoroastrian priestly organization became considerably more visible and hierarchical, with titles such as mowbed, hērbed, and eventually mowbedān mowbed appearing in the sources
  • Royal investiture imagery and inscriptions display an explicit Zoroastrian religious idiom more consistently than in most surviving Arsacid era royal representation, examined in Chapter 21
  • Christian and, somewhat later, Manichaean communities developed increasingly formal ecclesiastical or missionary institutions over the course of the Sasanian period
  • Episodes of severe persecution, particularly of Christians, became a documented and recurring feature of the fourth and later centuries in a way not equally attested for the Arsacid period

What Stayed the Same

  • Substantial regional and religious diversity, particularly the strong Christian and Jewish presence in Mesopotamia, continued from the Arsacid into the Sasanian period
  • Fire and Zoroastrian ritual practice remained central to religious life across both periods, even as its institutional and royal expression grew more elaborate
  • No single religious community's experience can be taken as representative of the whole empire in either period
  • Religious identity continued to intersect with legal, economic, and communal organization rather than remaining a separate sphere of belief alone

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing genuine institutional development alongside real continuity prevents overstating the novelty of Sasanian religious policy while still crediting the Sasanian period with a meaningfully more organized and visible relationship between religious institutions and the state than what can be documented for the Arsacid centuries.

Cause and Effect

Organization, Identity, and Political Relationship

Organization

Priesthoods, ecclesiastical synods, rabbinic scholarly circles, and missionary networks gave religious communities institutional structure, leadership, and continuity across generations.

Communal identity

Institutional organization reinforced a sense of distinct communal identity, whether Zoroastrian, Christian, Jewish, or Manichaean, that could persist and adapt across changing political circumstances.

Political relationship

Organized communities became visible and, at times, politically significant actors that Sasanian kings had to accommodate, patronize, tax, regulate, or, in some cases, suppress.

Accommodation or repression

Depending on the ruler, the international political climate, priestly influence, and the specific community involved, this political relationship could produce sustained patronage, pragmatic toleration, or severe persecution, sometimes shifting within a single reign.

Historical complexity: This causal chain was not one-directional. Political pressure and persecution could themselves strengthen communal identity and organizational cohesion, just as institutional strength could invite renewed political suspicion, meaning cause and effect ran in both directions across the period this chapter examines.

Object Study

The Fire Altar on Sasanian Coinage

Sasanian silver coinage typically displays, on its reverse, an image of a fire altar, often flanked by attendant figures, alongside a royal portrait on the obverse. This consistent numismatic pairing of king and sacred fire was widely circulated through taxation, state and military payment, and broader commerce, offering a different kind of evidence from inscriptions or literary texts: a visual and economic artifact whose imagery reached far beyond the court, even if silver drachms themselves were more central to taxation, large payments, and long-distance trade than to the smallest everyday transactions, which more often relied on copper coinage.

Students should consider what this pairing suggests and what it cannot, by itself, prove. It strongly suggests that Sasanian rulers wished to advertise their religious legitimacy and their association with Zoroastrian ritual practice widely and consistently, reaching populations who might never see a royal inscription or attend a court ceremony. It cannot, by itself, establish how uniformly Zoroastrian institutions were actually organized across the empire, how ordinary religious minorities experienced this constant visual reminder of royal religious identity, or how deeply this coinage imagery shaped popular religious sentiment as opposed to serving primarily as royal propaganda. Read alongside the textual evidence examined throughout this chapter, the fire altar coinage reinforces the picture of a monarchy that consistently projected Zoroastrian religious legitimacy even as the empire's actual religious life remained considerably more diverse and complicated than that projected image alone would suggest.

Everyday Life and Social History

Religion in Ordinary Sasanian Life

The institutional and political history examined throughout this chapter should not obscure the fact that religion shaped the daily lives of ordinary Sasanian subjects in ways that had little directly to do with royal policy or high theology. Marriage practices, inheritance arrangements, dietary observance, calendars and festivals, burial customs, and the resolution of everyday disputes were all mediated, in varying degrees depending on community and region, by religious authorities and religious law. A Zoroastrian family in Fars, a Christian family in Mesopotamia, and a Jewish family in Babylonia might each organize household life, mark important life events, and resolve legal disputes according to distinct religious frameworks, even while living within the same empire and, in many regions, the same cities and neighborhoods.

Religiously mixed regions, particularly Mesopotamia and Khuzestan, required practical accommodation among neighbors of different religious communities regardless of the empire's shifting official policies at any given moment. Markets, agricultural labor, craft production, and local administration frequently required cooperation across religious lines even during periods of officially sanctioned persecution directed at a particular community. Historians must read the elite, institutional sources examined throughout this chapter, priestly inscriptions, ecclesiastical records, and rabbinic literature, as evidence produced largely by and for educated religious leadership, supplementing them where possible with archaeological and material evidence to gain some sense of how religious diversity was actually negotiated in daily practice by populations who left few records of their own.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how Zoroastrian institutions, other religious communities, and the Sasanian monarchy interacted across four centuries of changing political and social conditions, building directly on Chapter 23's account of the Sasanian imperial state and preparing the ground for the political, military, and social developments examined in Chapters 25 through 27.

The chapter began by establishing that the Sasanian Empire's religious landscape was diverse and regionally uneven from the outset, containing Zoroastrians, Christians of several traditions, Jewish communities, Manichaeans, and other religious populations whose experiences differed considerably by region and period. It then examined the early relationship between Zoroastrian religious language and Sasanian kingship, cautioning against the assumption that a fully centralized Zoroastrian state church emerged immediately upon the dynasty's founding in 224 CE.

A substantial section examined the Zoroastrian priesthood's developing institutional structure, including priestly titles such as mowbed, hērbed, and mowbedān mowbed, and the central role of fire temples and religious endowments in priestly authority and local social life. The priest Kartir received particular attention as both an invaluable and a challenging source, whose later retrospective inscriptions record claims about a career spanning several reigns that historians must read critically rather than accept as a complete administrative record.

The chapter then addressed the difficult problem of reconstructing Sasanian religion from later evidence, explaining why the Avesta's long oral transmission and eventual, gradual written compilation, along with the later compilation of Middle Persian religious and legal literature, complicate any effort to read later texts as a transparent record of earlier practice.

Mani and Manichaeism received sustained treatment as a case study in shifting royal religious policy, tracing Mani's early access to Shapur I's court through his eventual imprisonment and death under Bahram I, a reversal illustrating how quickly religious policy could change within a single dynasty's early history.

Christianity within the Sasanian Empire was examined as an indigenous Mesopotamian tradition with its own Syriac ecclesiastical institutions, distinct from Christianity within the Roman Empire, whose experience of periodic severe persecution intersected with, but cannot be reduced entirely to, Roman-Sasanian political rivalry. Jewish communities in Sasanian Babylonia received similarly sustained treatment as a major, long-lived religious tradition whose rabbinic scholarly circles produced the Babylonian Talmud, generally experiencing a different pattern of relative institutional stability compared to the Christian experience.

The chapter then turned to religious dissent and diversity within and around Zoroastrian tradition itself, examining the controversial Mazdakite movement of the late fifth and early sixth centuries with appropriate caution regarding its overwhelmingly hostile surviving source record, and addressing the modern scholarly label "Zurvanism" as a theological tendency requiring careful, qualified use rather than treatment as a fully separate organized religion.

The concluding section synthesized this material around a central argument: the Sasanian Empire was neither a secular multi-religious state indifferent to religion nor a uniformly administered Zoroastrian theocracy. Religious authority functioned as a source of royal legitimacy, an institutional basis of priestly power, a framework for law and communal organization, a basis for communal identity among Christians, Jews, and Manichaeans, a source of political suspicion at various points, and a means through which communities negotiated with the state, with the balance among these functions shifting considerably across the four centuries this chapter examines.

The Historical Debate feature on whether Zoroastrianism should be described as a Sasanian "state religion" illustrated this complexity concretely, showing that even historians who agree on the basic evidence can reach different conclusions about how best to characterize the relationship between religious institutions and political authority in this period.

These conclusions carry directly into the chapters that follow. Chapter 25 will show Christian and Armenian religious identity becoming entangled in the long Roman-Sasanian rivalry along the empire's western frontier. Chapter 26 will trace religious and cultural exchange, including the spread of Manichaeism and Christianity, along the empire's eastern frontier and Central Asian connections. Chapter 27 will return to the Mazdakite crisis introduced in this chapter to explain its full political consequences under Kavad I and Khosrow I, connecting the religious history this chapter has developed to the empire's final Roman-Persian wars and eventual collapse.

Key Terms

Zoroastrianism
The religious tradition centered on the worship of Ohrmazd (Ahura Mazda), historically associated with Sasanian kingship and priestly institutions, whose organization and literature developed considerably over the Sasanian period.
Ohrmazd (Ahura Mazda)
The principal deity of Zoroastrian tradition, depicted in Sasanian royal art as the source of the king's investiture and legitimacy.
Ahriman
The principle or spirit of evil and destruction in Zoroastrian cosmology, opposed to Ohrmazd.
Xwarrah
A concept of divinely granted royal glory or fortune in Iranian and Zoroastrian tradition, whose possession or loss was understood to mark legitimate or illegitimate kingship.
Mowbed
A senior Zoroastrian priestly title whose functions and jurisdiction varied by period, region, and holder rather than corresponding to one uniform office.
Hērbed
A Zoroastrian priestly title associated especially with religious teaching and the transmission of sacred learning.
Mowbedān mowbed
A senior Zoroastrian priestly title, attested from a later point in Sasanian history, implying authority over other mowbeds.
Fire temple
An institution central to Zoroastrian ritual practice, maintaining sacred fire and often holding significant landed endowment and social functions.
Kartir
An influential Zoroastrian priest whose later retrospective inscriptions recount a career spanning several third-century reigns and provide central but self-interested evidence for priestly ambition and religious politics.
The Avesta
The core corpus of Zoroastrian sacred literature, transmitted orally for a long period before eventual, gradual written compilation, likely substantially during the Sasanian period.
Middle Persian (Pahlavi) literature
A body of religious, legal, and narrative literature composed in Middle Persian, much of which reached its surviving written form after the Sasanian period's end.
State religion
A contested modern label describing a religion's official, privileged relationship to a state; applied to Sasanian Zoroastrianism with important qualifications, examined in this chapter's Historical Debate feature.
Mani
A third-century religious teacher who proclaimed a new universal religion, gained temporary favor at Shapur I's court, and was later imprisoned and died under Bahram I.
Manichaeism
The dualistic religious tradition founded by Mani, which spread widely beyond Iran into the Roman Empire, North Africa, Central Asia, and eventually China after facing persecution within the Sasanian Empire.
Church of the East
The Christian ecclesiastical institution that developed within the Sasanian Empire, organizationally distinct from churches within the Roman Empire.
Syriac Christianity
The Christian tradition using the Syriac language for liturgy and scholarship, dominant among Christian communities in Sasanian Mesopotamia.
Seleucia-Ctesiphon
The twin-city royal center on the Tigris that also served as a major seat of Christian ecclesiastical authority within the Sasanian Empire.
Babylonian Talmud
The vast compilation of rabbinic legal discussion, narrative, and interpretation produced by Jewish scholars in Sasanian Babylonia and after, foundational to rabbinic Judaism.
Exilarch
A Jewish communal leadership office in Sasanian Babylonia, mediating between the Jewish community and Sasanian authority.
Mazdakite movement
A late fifth- and early sixth-century religious and social movement associated with the teacher Mazdak, known primarily through later, largely hostile sources.
Zurvanism
A modern scholarly label for theological currents within Zoroastrian tradition emphasizing Zurvan, or Time, requiring cautious rather than uncritical use.
Religious endowment
Landed or other property dedicated to the support of a religious institution such as a fire temple, tying religious authority into broader economic and legal structures.
Heresiography
Literature composed by an established religious tradition describing and condemning movements it considers heretical, often shaping how later historians must critically interpret groups such as the Mazdakites.
Kavad I
The Sasanian king associated with reported early support for Mazdak's movement and with a temporary deposition, whose reign and its consequences are examined fully in Chapter 27.
Persecution (religious)
Organized repression of a religious community by political or religious authority, documented episodically rather than continuously across Sasanian history for groups such as Christians and followers of Mani.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to hold together a genuinely complicated picture of religious life in the Sasanian Empire, one in which royal patronage of Zoroastrian institutions coexisted with substantial Christian, Jewish, and Manichaean communities that built their own lasting institutions, and in which periods of persecution alternated with periods of accommodation according to ruler, region, and political circumstance. This complexity is not a failure of historical clarity; it is the actual shape of the evidence, and learning to hold complexity honestly, rather than resolving it into a simpler and more satisfying but less accurate story, is one of the central skills this textbook asks students to practice.

The chapter's treatment of sources, particularly Kartir's inscriptions, rabbinic literature, Christian ecclesiastical records, and later Middle Persian religious texts, has emphasized a lesson that will recur throughout this textbook: evidence produced by a religious or political institution for its own purposes must be read critically, with attention to who created it, why, and what it can and cannot establish. This lesson applies as much to the Constitutional Revolution examined in Chapter 78 or the contested politics of the modern Islamic Republic examined in the textbook's final volume as it does to a third-century priestly inscription.

The religious history examined in this chapter also shaped developments far beyond the Sasanian period itself. The Church of the East's institutions, Babylonian Jewish scholarship, and the broader pattern of religious diversity and negotiated authority this chapter describes did not simply vanish with the Sasanian collapse examined in Chapter 27; they persisted, in transformed circumstances, into the early Islamic centuries examined in Volume III, where Chapter 32's discussion of Islamization without Arabization and Chapter 40's treatment of Sunni-Shi'i relations will return to questions of religious institution, identity, and political authority in a new political framework built, in important ways, on patterns this chapter has already introduced.

Understanding Sasanian religious history with this degree of care equips students to approach the remainder of this textbook's frequent engagement with religion, from the Islamic conquest through the Safavid establishment of Twelver Shi'ism examined in Chapter 58 to the religious dimensions of the contemporary Islamic Republic, with the same careful attention to institutions, evidence, diversity, and historical change that this chapter has modeled.
