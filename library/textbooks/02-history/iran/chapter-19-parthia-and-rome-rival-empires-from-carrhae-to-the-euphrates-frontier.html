Parthia and Rome: Rival Empires from Carrhae to the Euphrates Frontier

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

For nearly three centuries the Arsacid kings and the rulers of Rome contested Armenia, northern Mesopotamia, and the Euphrates approaches to Syria. Neither could destroy the other. Their relationship was not one continuous war but a long sequence of invasions, treaties, dynastic bargains, and frontier accommodations, and it left behind a rivalry that the Sasanian dynasty would inherit.

In May 53 BCE, near the Mesopotamian town of Carrhae (modern Harran, in southeastern Turkey), a Roman army led by Marcus Licinius Crassus was defeated by a Parthian force commanded by a nobleman whom Greek and Latin sources call Surena. Crassus, one of the wealthiest and most politically powerful men in the Roman Republic, was killed, and the Roman standards taken in the disaster became for the Romans a lasting symbol of unfinished business. The battle is among the best known episodes of ancient military history, and it has often been retold as a simple contest between Roman infantry and Parthian horsemen.

That familiar account is a useful doorway into the chapter but a poor summary of it. Carrhae did not lead to a Parthian conquest of Syria, and it did not end Roman ambitions in the east. Within two decades Roman armies were again marching toward Parthian territory, and in later centuries they captured Ctesiphon and the adjoining Arsacid capital complex around Seleucia on three separate occasions, in 116, 165, and 198 CE, and in 216 CE a further Roman raid reached Arbela and disturbed royal graves there. Yet Rome never held that region permanently. Parthian kings, for their part, repeatedly intervened in Syria, Anatolia, and Armenia, and they too failed to keep what they briefly won. The interest of the story lies in this pattern of repeated success without permanent conquest.

This chapter therefore studies the relationship between two imperial systems rather than a single battle. It follows the crisis of the first century BCE, when the Roman Republic's eastern commanders and the Arsacid court first came into direct contact; the struggle over Armenia, a mountain kingdom whose rulers were courted, deposed, and recognized by both sides; the development of a frontier zone along and beyond the Euphrates that was made of cities, client kingdoms, roads, and garrisons rather than of a surveyed line; and the great invasions of the second and early third centuries CE that stretched Roman power to the Persian Gulf and then withdrew from most of it. Throughout, the chapter asks why neither empire could impose a durable settlement by force.

The chapter also attends to the fact that our evidence is uneven. Much of what survives about Arsacid-Roman relations was written in Greek and Latin by authors who wrote for Roman or Greek-speaking readers, some of them centuries after the events. Parthian royal voices are heard chiefly through coins, a handful of inscriptions, and the actions Roman writers report. Section 19.2 explains what this means for reading the period, and the Primary Source Window on Plutarch's account of Carrhae shows how one influential narrative was constructed.

Within the textbook, the chapter takes up where Chapter 18 leaves off. Chapter 18 examined Arsacid kingship, the great noble houses, and the structure of the Parthian realm; this chapter uses that political system as context, especially when explaining why Arsacid kings could not always mobilize the full strength of their empire, but it does not retell it. Chapter 17 treated the rise of the Parthians and the dynasty's expansion at Seleucid expense. Chapter 20 will turn to trade, cities, and the Silk Roads, and Chapter 21 to religion, art, language, and society; mentions of commerce and culture here are therefore limited to what the political and military story requires. The far longer rivalry between the Sasanian Empire and Rome and Byzantium, belongs to Chapter 25.

A note on terminology. The dynasty is called both "Arsacid" (after its founder, Arsaces) and "Parthian" (after the northeastern region of Parthia from which it arose). This chapter uses "Arsacid" for the ruling house and its kings and "Parthian" for the wider empire and its military and cultural traditions, as the ancient Roman authors themselves generally used "Parthi." The Romans, for their part, often called the Arsacid realm "Persia" or its people "Persians" in poetic or archaizing writing, an echo of the Achaemenid past that the chapter on continuity and change below examines.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the decline of Seleucid power and Roman expansion in Syria brought the Roman Republic and the Arsacid realm into direct contact.
  2. Trace the major wars and settlements between Arsacid Parthia and Rome from Carrhae in 53 BCE to the fall of the Arsacid dynasty in the 220s CE.
  3. Analyze the causes, course, and limited consequences of the Battle of Carrhae, distinguishing what ancient sources report from what historians can reasonably infer.
  4. Compare Parthian and Roman military systems, including mounted archery, heavy cavalry, legionary infantry, siege capability, and logistics.
  5. Explain why Armenia was central to Arsacid-Roman relations and how competition over it operated through dynastic appointment and recognition rather than annexation alone.
  6. Describe the Euphrates frontier as a zone of cities, client states, and shifting influence rather than a modern-style border.
  7. Assess Trajan's invasion and Hadrian's withdrawal as evidence of the limits of Roman territorial ambition in Mesopotamia.
  8. Evaluate the relationship between Arsacid political instability and Roman intervention in the second and early third centuries CE.
  9. Distinguish contemporary evidence, later literary tradition, and modern reconstruction in the sources for Arsacid-Roman relations, and identify whose perspective each represents.
  10. Interpret competing scholarly views of the relationship as territorial rivalry or as the management of a contested frontier.
  11. Connect the Arsacid-Roman frontier system to the Sasanian-Roman rivalry that followed it.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did the Roman Republic and the Arsacid realm, which for a long time had little direct contact, become rivals in the first century BCE?
  2. Why did the disaster at Carrhae shape Roman memory so strongly even though it did not produce a Parthian conquest of the Roman east?
  3. How did Armenia come to be a kingdom that neither empire could safely ignore, and why did both usually prefer influence over direct rule?
  4. In what sense was there a "border" between Rome and Parthia, and how does an ancient frontier zone differ from a modern boundary?
  5. Why did Roman armies repeatedly reach Ctesiphon but never keep Mesopotamia east of the Khabur and Euphrates lands?
  6. How did internal Arsacid political divisions, discussed in Chapter 18, affect the Arsacid response to Roman invasion?
  7. What can Roman and Greek literary sources tell us about Arsacid policy, and what can they not tell us?
  8. Was the Arsacid-Roman relationship best characterized by war, by diplomacy, or by the interplay between them?
  9. What did the Sasanian dynasty inherit from the Arsacid-Roman frontier, and what did it change?

Historical Context

By the middle of the second century BCE the Arsacid dynasty, examined in Chapter 17, had taken Media and Mesopotamia from a weakening Seleucid kingdom. The Arsacid realm that resulted was a large, decentralized structure, described in Chapter 18, in which the king ruled alongside powerful noble houses and subordinate kings, and in which the Mesopotamian cities, especially Seleucia on the Tigris and the adjoining royal encampment and later city of Ctesiphon, were centers of wealth and administration. The Seleucid rump kingdom continued to shrink until Rome, whose Republic had grown rapidly through the wars of the second century BCE, put an end to it.

The Roman general Gnaeus Pompeius (Pompey) did this in the 60s BCE. After campaigns against Mithridates VI of Pontus and Tigranes II of Armenia, Pompey deposed the last Seleucid ruler and organized Syria as a Roman province in 64 or 63 BCE. Rome thus acquired a substantial Mediterranean-facing province whose eastern edge lay on the Euphrates, a river that separated Syria from the desert steppe and the Mesopotamian plain. To the north, Rome dealt with a chain of kingdoms, Cappadocia, Commagene, Armenia, and others, which had been part of the Hellenistic and Anatolian world. At the same moment, the Arsacid kings held Mesopotamia and the Iranian plateau. The two powers had not chosen each other as rivals; their expansion had run into one another.

The geography that framed their competition is described more fully in the Geography Study in Section 19.9. In brief, the Euphrates rises in the Armenian highlands and runs south and east across Syria and Mesopotamia; the Tigris runs roughly parallel to the east. Between them lies northern Mesopotamia (the Greek name Mesopotamia means "between the rivers"), a region of steppe and rain-fed agriculture with river-fed oases and fortified cities. The desert to the south and the mountains to the north shaped where armies could move and be supplied. Neither empire had been organized to campaign easily in the other's heartland.

The two states were also organized differently. Rome under the Republic was governed by a senate and elected magistrates, with provincial commanders (proconsuls, propraetors) who held military command, raised provincial revenues, and had personal political ambitions; after Augustus, an emperor controlled the major armies and the eastern provinces. The Arsacid realm depended on the king's ability to coordinate with noble families who maintained their own retinues and on tributary or subordinate kings. This difference matters for what follows: Roman commanders could bring large, regularly organized infantry armies with siege equipment to the frontier, while Arsacid kings could call on formidable mounted forces whose availability depended on political relations with the nobility. Neither system was inherently stronger; each had its own strengths and its own vulnerabilities.

Section 19.1

Two Empires Approach One Another

For much of the second century BCE the Arsacids and Rome had no common frontier and little reason to think of one another as rivals. Rome's attention lay in the western and central Mediterranean and, increasingly, in Greece and western Anatolia; the Arsacids were occupied with the Seleucid kingdom, with steppe peoples on their eastern borders, and with consolidating Mesopotamia. The Seleucid kingdom stood between them. As that kingdom fragmented, its remnants became a prize for local dynasts, for the Armenian king Tigranes II, and finally for Rome.

The earliest contact that Roman tradition remembered took place around 96 BCE, when Lucius Cornelius Sulla, then a Roman magistrate serving in Cappadocia, met an envoy of the Arsacid king on the Euphrates. Plutarch, writing generations later, recounts that the Parthian ambassador, whom he names Orobazus, sat between Sulla and the Cappadocian king Ariobarzanes, and that the Arsacid king later punished the envoy. The date, the identity of the Arsacid king (most often taken to be Mithridates II), and the significance of the seating arrangement are all uncertain, and the story reached Plutarch through Roman memoirs that had reason to stress Roman superiority. Historians treat it as reliable evidence that an early exchange took place, but not as proof of its terms or of Arsacid attitudes.

Pompey's eastern settlement made the contact permanent. In organizing Syria and reorganizing Anatolian kingdoms, he dealt with Arsacid claims as well. Roman sources report that he and the Arsacid king Phraates III negotiated over Armenia and over the boundary between their spheres, and some ancient authors say the Euphrates was mentioned as a dividing line. The evidence is thin and later, and modern historians disagree about whether any formal agreement existed. What seems clear is that both sides found the Euphrates useful as a reference point, that Rome had acquired Syria without acquiring any settled arrangement with the Arsacids, and that the potential for miscalculation was considerable.

Political conditions on the Arsacid side added to that potential. Phraates III was killed, according to Roman tradition, by his sons Mithridates III and Orodes II; a dynastic struggle followed in which Orodes prevailed. This was the ruler who faced Crassus. The Arsacid succession, examined in Chapter 18, could produce contested reigns, and contested reigns could lower the court's readiness for prolonged foreign war, or, as with Orodes, raise the value of a decisive success.

Historical Vocabulary
Frontier (and limes)

Definition: In this chapter, a "frontier" is a zone of interaction, control, and contest between two political systems, made up of cities, kingdoms, roads, garrisons, and communities whose allegiance could change. It is distinct from a "border," a precisely defined line of sovereignty. The Latin word limes, sometimes translated "frontier," originally referred to a road or path and later to fortified military zones, and its use for a systematic imperial boundary is largely a modern scholarly convention for the Roman period examined here.

Historical context: The Euphrates was repeatedly cited by Romans and Greeks as a boundary between Roman and Parthian spheres. Yet Rome, at various times, held territory east of it, and Arsacid influence extended west of it, in the kingdoms and cities of Commagene, Osrhoene, and elsewhere.

Why it matters: Students who imagine a surveyed line between "Rome" and "Persia" will misread nearly every event in this chapter. Political authority in the zone was layered and negotiated.

Potential misunderstanding: The presence of forts and roads does not prove a fixed, permanent boundary or a fully articulated Roman "grand strategy." How far Roman policy was planned and how far it was improvised remains debated (see the Historical Debate below).

Section 19.2

Evidence and Whose Story It Is

Before following the wars, students should understand how we know about them. The principal narrative sources are Greek and Latin: Plutarch's Life of Crassus and Life of Antony (early second century CE), Cassius Dio's Roman History (early third century CE), Tacitus's Annals for the Neronian Armenian crisis (early second century CE), Appian, Josephus for Judea and the Euphrates region, and shorter texts such as the Res Gestae of Augustus. These authors were Roman senators or Greek-speaking provincials who wrote from a Roman or Hellenized elite viewpoint, often long after the events, and they tended to interpret Parthian actions in terms of Roman categories.

Arsacid perspectives are much harder to recover. The dynasty produced coins, some in Greek, showing kings and titles; a small number of inscriptions, including some from Susa, Hatra, and Dura-Europos; and parchments and ostraca in Parthian and Aramaic dialects, including administrative documents from Nisa in Turkmenistan and legal documents from the Zagros. There is no surviving Parthian narrative history of the wars with Rome. Roman descriptions of Parthian government, motives, and tactics are therefore filtered accounts, and they sometimes reflect the stereotype of the treacherous, archer-fighting easterner rather than observation.

Archaeology helps considerably. Excavations at Dura-Europos, a city on the middle Euphrates, have yielded documents, military records, and buildings from the Parthian and Roman periods. Work at Hatra, Nisibis's region, and Seleucia has illuminated urban life. Roman coins and inscriptions record how Roman authorities presented victories and settlements. The evidence is richest for Roman actions and weakest for Arsacid decision-making, and this imbalance shapes what can confidently be said.

Section 19.3

Crassus and the Road to Carrhae

Crassus received Syria after his consulship in 55 BCE and arrived in the province in the spring of 54 BCE. He was already consul, extremely rich, and politically eclipsed by the military reputations of Pompey and Julius Caesar, whose Gallic campaigns were then bringing Caesar glory and loyal troops. Ancient authors, notably Plutarch and Cassius Dio, attribute to Crassus a desire for wealth and military prestige, and modern historians usually accept that personal ambition mattered, while noting that Roman provincial commanders routinely sought glory and that a war against the Arsacids could also be presented as a defence of Syria. There was no Parthian attack that made war necessary; the decision to invade appears to have been Crassus's own, opposed by some senators and, at the departure, by at least one tribune whose formal curse became part of the story.

Crassus crossed the Euphrates in 54 BCE, occupied or took the allegiance of some Mesopotamian towns, and withdrew to Syria for the winter, leaving garrisons behind. This first season gave the Arsacid court a year's warning. When he returned in 53, the Armenian king Artavasdes II offered him substantial cavalry and a route through the Armenian highlands, where Parthian horsemen would have been at a disadvantage. Crassus, according to Plutarch, refused and preferred the direct march through northern Mesopotamia. Whether the choice was foolish or reasonable has been debated: the Armenian route was longer and depended on an ally whose own loyalties were uncertain, while the Mesopotamian route offered cities to take and a direct line toward Seleucia.

On the Arsacid side, Orodes II divided his forces. He is said to have led a part of his army against Armenia to prevent Artavasdes from assisting the Romans, and he entrusted the main mobile force to Surena. The delegation to a subordinate nobleman is instructive. It shows the aristocratic structure of the realm, in which the great houses supplied the cavalry, and it shows that the king's own forces and those of the noble houses combined only through negotiated command. According to Plutarch, Surena's force was small in heavy cavalry but large in horse archers, supported by a baggage train of camels carrying arrows. Plutarch's figures imply roughly 10,000 horsemen in all, including about 1,000 heavily armored cavalry and roughly 9,000 horse archers, against Roman forces of roughly seven legions with cavalry and light troops; these are traditional numbers of uncertain reliability, and modern scholarship treats them cautiously.

Historical Vocabulary
Cataphract and Horse Archer

Definition: A cataphract (from Greek kataphraktos, "covered") was a heavily armored mounted warrior, typically with armor protecting rider and sometimes horse, and armed with a long lance. A horse archer was a lightly equipped mounted soldier using a composite bow. The Arsacid armies combined the two, with the aristocracy and their retainers providing much of the heavy cavalry and a large body of lighter horse archers alongside.

Historical context: Mounted archery had deep roots among Iranian-speaking steppe and plateau peoples, and the Arsacid dynasty had risen from the northeastern region where these skills were well established. Roman infantry, by contrast, relied on close-order legionary formation and, for missile power, on slingers and archers, often auxiliaries.

Why it matters: The combination offered mobility, range, and shock, but it was expensive and relied on the loyalty and readiness of aristocratic households.

Potential misunderstanding: It is inaccurate to say that "Parthian tactics always beat Roman infantry." At Carrhae, terrain, supply, leadership errors, and Roman inability to close with the enemy mattered. Elsewhere, Roman armies with adequate cavalry, archers, and logistics won.

Section 19.4

Carrhae and Its Meanings

The battle took place in the open plains south of Carrhae in May 53 BCE. Ancient accounts, principally Plutarch's, describe the Romans forming a hollow square to protect themselves from encirclement, then suffering steady losses from arrow fire. When Roman skirmishers and cavalry sortied, Parthian horsemen apparently drew them away and then destroyed them, killing Crassus's son Publius, who had commanded a force of some thousands of cavalry including Gallic contingents. The legionaries could neither advance to force contact nor retreat safely; the Parthian camel train replenished arrows. Crassus withdrew toward Carrhae during the night, leaving wounded men behind, and in the following days Roman forces divided further. In negotiations that Plutarch presents as a trap, Crassus was killed. Cassius Longinus, later one of Caesar's assassins, led a group of survivors back to Syria.

Casualty figures in the sources, of about 20,000 Romans killed and 10,000 taken prisoner, are traditional and cannot be verified, though there is no doubt that the defeat was severe. The Parthian victory owed something to Roman errors of route, dispositions, and command, and something to the effective use of the horse archers, whose effectiveness depended on supply arrangements and on terrain that permitted maneuver. It owed little to any single "wonder weapon." The battle is often used to illustrate a supposed superiority of mounted archery over infantry, but the historical record does not support so general a conclusion, as the campaigns of the following centuries show.

Primary Source Window

Plutarch, Life of Crassus, on the Parthian Campaign and the Battle of Carrhae

Creator: Plutarch of Chaeronea, a Greek biographer and philosopher who also held Roman citizenship · Date: written around the late first or early second century CE, roughly 150 years after the battle · Type: Literary biography, drawing on earlier historians and memoirs

Context: Plutarch wrote paired lives of Greek and Roman figures for a Greek-speaking and Roman-elite readership, and used the Crassus narrative to illustrate the consequences of greed and poor judgment. He relied on earlier authors, some possibly including surviving participants or their reports, and he gives details about the battle, the camel supply train, and the death of Crassus that other surviving sources do not corroborate in full. He also reports an anecdote, widely discussed, that Parthian courtiers performed a scene from a Greek tragedy using Crassus's head at a royal celebration in Armenia.

Paraphrase (not a quotation): Plutarch presents Crassus as driven by desire for wealth and glory, contrasts his impatience with the sound advice of his officers and the Armenian king, and describes the Parthian force as swift, using shower after shower of arrows, and unafraid of Roman infantry. He then describes how the Roman survivors became divided and how Crassus was killed at a conference, and he reports that the Arsacid king received the news while entertaining the Armenian king.

Analysis: The passage shows how a Greek author in the Roman world integrated the disaster into a moral story about ambition. It also preserves details, such as the supply train, that modern historians have found plausible for explaining Parthian endurance in the field. The anecdote about the Greek play is often cited to illustrate Arsacid engagement with Hellenistic culture, which fits other evidence, but the specific episode cannot be independently verified.

Limitations: The account centers Roman actors and reflects a Greek elite perspective within the Roman imperial world; it is also retrospective and shaped by moral purpose. Numbers, motives (especially Crassus's), and the alleged treachery at the parley are difficult to test. No surviving Arsacid narrative tells us how the court itself represented the victory.

Perspective: A Greek-speaking Roman citizen writing for educated readers across the Roman world. Missing are the perspectives of Parthian nobles, Mesopotamian townspeople, ordinary Roman soldiers, and the Roman prisoners who, Roman tradition held, were resettled far to the east.

Questions for students:

  1. What does Plutarch say about the causes of the Roman defeat?
  2. Who was Plutarch, and how much time separated him from the events?
  3. How might Plutarch's moral purpose have shaped his portrayal of Crassus?
  4. What can historians learn from this account about the operations of the Parthian army?
  5. What can historians not establish from Plutarch alone?

What did Carrhae mean? For Rome, it became a scandal. The captured legionary standards, the "eagles," symbolized both the defeat and a debt of honor, and Roman literature recurs to the theme for generations. Roman tradition also held that thousands of prisoners were settled by the Arsacid authorities in Margiana (in the region of Merv, in Central Asia), a story that is plausible in light of the widespread use of resettlement by ancient states but of uncertain detail. For the Arsacid court, the victory enhanced the prestige of Orodes II and, in a different way, of the Suren house; according to Plutarch, Orodes later had Surena put to death, apparently out of suspicion of his success, though the report is difficult to verify.

Yet Carrhae also revealed what the Arsacids could not do. They did not immediately conquer Syria, because a mounted army suited to defeating a Roman column in the open was not equipped for prolonged sieges of Roman-held cities and for holding territory far from its bases. The Arsacid system also depended on the willingness of great nobles to campaign, and on the king's control of them. The victory was significant, and it was not a strategy for conquest.

Section 19.5

Pacorus and the Roman Civil Wars

In 51 BCE the young Pacorus, son of Orodes II, led a raid into Syria that the Roman quaestor Gaius Cassius Longinus repelled. Rome's own attention was soon consumed by civil war between Caesar and Pompey, and then by the wars that followed Caesar's assassination in 44 BCE. Roman factions sought Parthian support: Pompeian and Republican leaders had approached the Arsacid court, and Quintus Labienus, who had served with the Republican cause and had been sent to Orodes as an envoy, remained with the Parthians after the Republican defeat at Philippi in 42 BCE.

In 40 BCE Labienus and Pacorus led a combined invasion. The Parthians took most of Syria, and Labienus advanced into Anatolia while Parthian forces entered Judea, where they deposed the Roman-backed Hyrcanus II and installed Antigonus (Mattathias), a Hasmonean rival. Herod, later king of Judea, fled and eventually went to Rome. Some of the Roman garrisons in Syria and several local rulers accommodated the invaders. The episode is significant for three reasons: it shows that Arsacid armies could hold Roman provinces for a time, it shows Arsacid willingness to take advantage of Roman civil conflict with Roman collaborators, and it shows how local politics, as in Judea, became entangled with the great-power contest.

The reversal came quickly. In 39 BCE Publius Ventidius Bassus, Mark Antony's commander in the east, defeated Labienus and then a Parthian force in Cilicia and Syria, and in 38 BCE he defeated Pacorus at Mount Gindarus in Syria, where Pacorus was killed. Roman writers presented the victory as revenge for Carrhae, and Ventidius celebrated a triumph, the first Rome awarded over the Parthians. Orodes, according to tradition, was grief-stricken and yielded the throne, or was killed, in the succession that placed Phraates IV in power. Once again, an Arsacid offensive had gained territory that it could not maintain against a reorganized Roman response.

Section 19.6

Antony, Augustus, and the Settlement of 20 BCE

Mark Antony, who governed the eastern provinces after the Triumvirate's division of authority, mounted a large invasion in 36 BCE. He advanced through Armenia into Media Atropatene (roughly the region of modern Iranian Azerbaijan), intending to besiege the city of Phraaspa. Antony left his siege train behind to move faster, and Parthian forces destroyed it with its escort, leaving him without the equipment needed for the siege. The Armenian king Artavasdes II withdrew his support. After a failed siege, Antony began a long retreat under Parthian harassment, and losses from combat, exposure, and hunger were heavy; Plutarch's account gives large numbers that historians treat as uncertain. Antony later turned on Artavasdes, whom he blamed, and in 34 BCE led him captive to Alexandria.

The campaign's lesson was one that later Roman commanders would relearn: the northern route toward the Iranian plateau was demanding, siege equipment was essential, and dependence on client allies could fail at critical moments. It also revealed that the Arsacid king, Phraates IV, could mobilize resources despite internal opposition. In the next years, Phraates faced rebellions and a rival, Tiridates II, who took refuge in Roman-controlled Syria and later with Augustus, along with one of Phraates's young sons. This gave Augustus, victor in the civil wars by 30 BCE, a lever in negotiation.

The settlement of 20 BCE combined pressure with diplomacy. Augustus did not launch a full invasion. Instead, with a Roman army available in the east and Tiberius sent as his representative, he negotiated with Phraates IV for the return of the Roman standards and surviving captives from Carrhae and later defeats. Roman coinage and monuments then proclaimed the recovery of the standards. Augustus's own account in the Res Gestae presents the Parthians as compelled to return the spoils and to seek Roman friendship, and the standards were housed in a temple of Mars the Avenger. The cuirassed statue of Augustus known from Prima Porta includes a scene often interpreted as the return of the standards.

From an Arsacid standpoint the episode looks different. Phraates IV secured his position, retained Armenia's ambiguous status, and obtained a stable relationship with Rome at the cost of returning objects with symbolic rather than military value. The two powers, in effect, reached a settlement that each could present as a success. The relationship remained intensely dynastic. Phraates sent four of his sons to Rome around 10 to 9 BCE, whether as hostages, as protection for the succession, or both. Roman tradition, notably Josephus, reports that Phraates later was killed by his son Phraataces (Phraates V) and the Italian woman Musa, who had been given to him by Augustus, and that the Parthian nobility subsequently expelled the pair. In 1 CE Gaius Caesar, Augustus's grandson, met Phraataces on an island in the Euphrates, an encounter Roman sources present as a diplomatic settlement over Armenia. The sources emphasize how far the two courts had become entangled in each other's succession politics.

Section 19.7

Armenia: A Kingdom between Empires

Armenia lies in the highlands east of Anatolia, north of Mesopotamia, and south of the Caucasus. Its rulers had been part of the Achaemenid and Seleucid worlds, and under Tigranes II (reigned c. 95–55 BCE) the Artaxiad dynasty briefly built a large kingdom before Pompey's campaigns reduced it. Its strategic importance was threefold. Armenia commanded routes into eastern Anatolia and toward the Caucasus; it overlooked northern Mesopotamia; and its elites were tied by marriage, culture, and politics to both the Iranian and the Roman worlds. Its cavalry-based nobility could also provide substantial military support to whichever empire held its allegiance.

For both empires, direct annexation of Armenia was difficult and rarely attempted. The country was mountainous, its nobility powerful, and its aristocrats capable of shifting allegiance. Instead, Rome and the Arsacids competed by supporting rival candidates, arranging marriages, and demanding recognition. The Roman practice of granting or confirming the diadem of a friendly king, and the Arsacid practice of placing members of the dynasty on the Armenian throne, made succession the central diplomatic question. Vonones, an Arsacid prince who had been raised in Rome, was installed as king by Parthians in the early first century CE, rejected by the Parthian nobility as too Romanized, and later moved to Armenia, where he was again pushed aside; the case shows how cultural identification could become a political liability in either camp. Under Tiberius, Germanicus crowned an Armenian king in 18 CE in an arrangement Rome presented as a settlement.

Historical Vocabulary
Client Kingdom

Definition: A "client kingdom" (a modern scholarly term) refers to a formally independent monarchy whose ruler was recognized, and often confirmed or installed, by a stronger power, and who was expected to provide loyalty, military support, or diplomatic alignment.

Historical context: Rome used client kings across Anatolia, Syria, and the Caucasus region, and the Arsacids maintained subordinate kings within their own realm, as discussed in Chapter 18. Armenia and Osrhoene were "in between" states that were courted by both.

Why it matters: Client status was negotiated and reversible. It allowed empires to extend influence without administering territory directly.

Potential misunderstanding: "Client" does not mean "puppet." Client rulers had their own interests and often maneuvered between larger powers.

Section 19.8

Nero, Corbulo, and the Compromise of Rhandeia

The Armenian question returned in the reign of Nero. Around 52 CE, Vologases I of Parthia, who had recently secured the Arsacid throne after a period of dynastic conflict, intervened in Armenia to install his brother Tiridates, after a rival, Rhadamistus, who was backed by the king of Iberia, had been removed. Roman authorities in the east regarded this as a challenge. Nero appointed Gnaeus Domitius Corbulo, a respected commander, to the eastern command. Corbulo spent time retraining troops from Syria, whom Tacitus describes as demoralized by long inactivity, and in 58 and 59 CE his forces took the Armenian capitals Artaxata and Tigranocerta and installed a friendly king, Tigranes VI.

The settlement did not hold. Vologases was for a time occupied by revolts elsewhere, but a new crisis in 61 and 62 CE returned the conflict to Armenia. A second Roman commander, Lucius Caesennius Paetus, was defeated and forced to accept humiliating terms at Rhandeia in 62 CE after a Parthian siege of his position. Corbulo negotiated a withdrawal and then a settlement. Under its terms, as Tacitus and Cassius Dio report, Tiridates would hold Armenia but receive his diadem from Nero, and he would travel to Rome for the purpose. In 66 CE he did so, and Nero crowned him in a grand ceremony, a rare occasion when an Arsacid prince knelt in the Roman capital.

The outcome is the classic Arsacid-Roman compromise. Armenia was governed by an Arsacid dynasty, which provided Armenia's ruling house for much of the following centuries, though with significant interruptions, including Trajan's brief annexation and later Roman and Sasanian interventions, until the monarchy was abolished in 428 CE. In this initial settlement, the king accepted Roman recognition, and the Roman emperor could present the arrangement as his own gift. Both empires accepted the compromise because it preserved essential interests: Armenia would not be a Roman province, and it would not be an unqualified base for Arsacid power. The settlement also mattered because it showed that after a military stalemate both courts preferred a negotiated outcome that allowed each to claim honor. Corbulo's later fate, ordered to commit suicide under Nero in 67 CE, reminds us that Roman commanders could be more endangered by their own court than by the Arsacid one.

Multiple Perspectives

Section 19.9

The Euphrates Frontier Zone

By the later first century CE the two empires had produced a recognizable frontier system. On the Roman side, the emperors Vespasian and later rulers strengthened the Euphrates and Anatolian sectors: Commagene, with its capital Samosata, was annexed in 72 CE; Cappadocia, already a Roman province, was reorganized and more heavily militarized, with legionary bases at Melitene and Satala; and Zeugma, a river crossing, held a Roman garrison. Roads and forts connected the Syrian province to the river. On the Arsacid side, kings founded or reinforced cities, and subordinate rulers held territory in the Mesopotamian plain. Vologases I, for example, founded Vologesias, a city intended, according to later reports, as a commercial and political counterweight in Mesopotamia.

Between the two lay small states and cities whose position depended on adroit balancing. Osrhoene, centered on Edessa (modern Şanlıurfa), was ruled by a local dynasty that leaned at various times toward Rome or Parthia. Hatra, a fortified city in the northern Mesopotamian desert, developed as an Arsacid-aligned regional kingdom and withstood Trajan and Septimius Severus. Adiabene, in the Tigris plain, had a royal family that, according to Josephus, converted to Judaism in the first century CE. Dura-Europos, on the Euphrates, was an Arsacid-controlled city until Roman forces took it in the 160s CE. Nisibis, in the north, changed hands and was later a key Roman base. In these places, allegiance could change with the prospects of war, and the populations, who spoke Greek, Aramaic dialects, Parthian, Arabic, and other languages, had their own interests.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living in a Contested Zone

Diplomacy and war looked different to those who lived on the routes of armies. Towns in Mesopotamia and Armenia had to supply grain, fodder, and quarters to passing forces, whichever side they belonged to, and could be punished for changing allegiance or for failing to change it. Ancient authors report the sale or deportation of captives in nearly every major campaign, and Roman tradition on Crassus's prisoners settled in the east reflects a wider practice of resettlement. Soldiers in frontier garrisons, including at Dura-Europos in its later Roman phase, lived in communities that included families, local recruits, priests, and merchants, and documents from the city show a multilingual, multireligious population.

Royal families also lived in politically exposed households. The sons of Phraates IV in Rome, or Vonones's experience in two courts, show that dynastic hostages and exiles were part of the diplomatic order. Jewish communities in Mesopotamia, whose relations with Roman Judea and with Arsacid authorities are recorded by Josephus, were affected by both empires' policies, including during Trajan's war, when revolts broke out in several regions. Because most of the surviving written evidence was produced by elites, the experience of peasants, pastoralists, and townspeople must be inferred from archaeology, incidental remarks, and documents like those from Dura.

Section 19.10

Trajan's Invasion

After the Armenian compromise and the frontier reorganization of the Flavian era, relations between Rome and the Arsacids were largely stable for decades. This ended under Trajan. The immediate occasion was again Armenia: the Arsacid king Osroes I (Chosroes) installed a nominee, Parthamasiris, on the Armenian throne without Roman consent, and Trajan, who left Rome in 113 CE and came to Antioch, used the violation as grounds for action. Ancient authors, notably Cassius Dio, also attribute to Trajan a desire for glory, and modern historians commonly weigh this together with strategic considerations, including Roman interest in the routes toward the Persian Gulf and the southern trade approaches, and the fact that Osroes was then involved in a dynastic conflict with a rival, Vologases III. Which motive predominated remains uncertain.

In 114 CE Trajan moved into Armenia, received Parthamasiris at Elegeia and, after a confrontation in which the prince was killed, organized Armenia as a Roman province. In 115 he took northern Mesopotamia, including Nisibis and Singara, and the territory became a province. In 116 he advanced down the Euphrates, took Seleucia and Ctesiphon, the Arsacid capital complex, and captured members of the royal household and, according to later tradition, the golden throne. He then reached the Persian Gulf, where Dio's account reports that he watched ships sail toward India and regretted that he was too old to follow. Roman coins proclaimed that Armenia and Mesopotamia had been brought under Roman power. Trajan installed a client, Parthamaspates, as king at Ctesiphon.

Within months the structure of conquest failed. Revolts broke out in Mesopotamian cities, and Jewish uprisings, part of a larger diaspora revolt that also affected Egypt, Cyrene, and Cyprus, complicated Roman control. Roman commanders reconquered some places, but Trajan's siege of Hatra failed. Arsacid resistance continued, especially in the east. Trajan fell ill and died in August 117 CE at Selinus in Cilicia. His successor Hadrian faced revolts, financial strain, and other frontier problems.

Trajan's war is central to understanding the frontier because it shows both what Rome could do and what it could not. Roman armies could defeat Arsacid forces, reach the capital, and sail the Tigris and Euphrates. But holding a large territory of uncertain loyalty, at the end of extended supply lines and among populations with local ties to the Arsacids, demanded resources and garrisons that Rome was not prepared to commit indefinitely. The Arsacid state, though damaged and divided, retained its aristocratic base on the plateau and its ability to recover.

Section 19.11

Hadrian and the Second-Century Wars

Hadrian gave up most of Trajan's conquests in Mesopotamia and returned to the Euphrates and to a client arrangement in Armenia. Roman sources present this partly as a matter of prudence; later writers regarded it either as sensible retrenchment or as abandonment. Later Roman tradition reports that Hadrian met an Arsacid king (probably Osroes I) around 122 to defuse a crisis and that captured members of the Arsacid royal family were eventually returned, though the details are given by late and sometimes unreliable sources. Peace of a kind endured for roughly four decades.

In 161 CE the Arsacid king Vologases IV invaded Armenia, installing a candidate favored by the Arsacid court, and destroyed a Roman force at Elegeia; the governor of Cappadocia, who had marched to meet him, died. Parthian forces then invaded Syria. The new Roman emperors Marcus Aurelius and Lucius Verus responded, and Verus traveled east, though the campaign was directed principally by generals, among them Statius Priscus in Armenia and Avidius Cassius in Mesopotamia. In 163 Priscus took Artaxata and installed a new king, Sohaemus, and by 165 Roman forces moved down the Euphrates, took Dura-Europos, and sacked Seleucia on the Tigris and the adjoining royal complex at Ctesiphon. The war ended in 166 with Roman control over the middle Euphrates and the region of the Khabur and with Osrhoene under Roman influence. Dura-Europos remained under Roman control and garrison until its destruction in the 250s CE, examined with the Sasanian period.

The return of troops from Mesopotamia coincided with the spread of a devastating epidemic, known as the Antonine Plague, through the Roman empire. The ancient sources connect the two, though the plague's epidemiology and the extent of its effects remain subjects of scholarly debate. The episode shows that warfare across the frontier had consequences beyond the immediate battlefield, including for the movement of disease. The sack of the twin capitals likewise damaged the region's prosperity, though the cities continued to function.

Section 19.12

Septimius Severus, Caracalla, and the Last Arsacid War

Septimius Severus took power in a civil war in 193 to 197 CE. During the conflict with Pescennius Niger, whose base was Syria, some cities and client rulers in Mesopotamia, and the Arsacid king Vologases V according to Roman accounts, supported or were suspected of supporting Niger. After defeating his rivals, Severus led two campaigns, in 195 and again in 197 to 198. Osrhoene and other regions were brought under closer Roman control. In 198 he took Ctesiphon, allegedly enslaving very many of its inhabitants (an unverifiable figure in the sources) and carrying off royal treasure. Attempts to take Hatra failed.

The outcome was a Roman province of Mesopotamia, with its center at Nisibis, governed by an official of equestrian rank, together with Roman provincial territory carved out of Osrhoene. Yet sovereignty here remained layered rather than absolute: a reduced Abgarid kingdom centered on Edessa continued to exist alongside the new Roman territory, illustrating again that authority in this zone was rarely all-or-nothing. Roman territory nonetheless reached farther east than before, and it stayed under Roman control. The Arsacid dynasty, meanwhile, was weakened by rivalry. Vologases V was followed by a period in which two kings, Vologases VI and Artabanus IV, ruled or claimed rule in different parts of the empire.

The path to this last war was more tangled than a single grievance. In 215 Caracalla demanded the surrender of Roman fugitives from Vologases VI, one of the rival Arsacid claimants, and Vologases eventually complied. In 216 Caracalla then approached Artabanus IV, the rival Arsacid king who controlled the Iranian plateau, with a proposal of marriage to Artabanus's daughter; when Artabanus rejected the proposal, Caracalla used the refusal as a pretext for invasion. The Roman army, meeting little organized opposition, reached Arbela in Adiabene, where, according to Cassius Dio, Caracalla's forces disturbed royal graves; these appear to have been local Adiabenian royal tombs rather than the tombs of the Arsacid dynasty itself, whatever claim Caracalla's own propaganda made. In April 217, on his way to Carrhae, Caracalla was murdered by officers, and Macrinus, who succeeded him, faced an Arsacid army under Artabanus IV near Nisibis. After a battle of several days generally regarded as a Roman defeat, Macrinus agreed to peace, and Dio reports that Rome paid a very large sum to Artabanus. Details of the settlement, and the precise extent of the damage to Roman prestige, remain debated, but the outcome was not favorable to Rome.

Within a few years Artabanus IV was himself defeated. Ardashir I, a ruler from Fars, defeated and killed Artabanus IV, the last dominant Arsacid Great King, in 224 CE. Vologases VI, a rival Arsacid claimant, had remained in control of Seleucia and its mint into at least 221 or 222; whether any resistance in his name continued after Artabanus's death is uncertain. By about 226 Ardashir held Ctesiphon. That transition, its causes and its political ideology, is the subject of Chapter 22. It meant that the Roman frontier was now faced by a new dynasty with a new ideology of kingship and a clear ambition to define itself against Rome.

Section 19.13

Why Neither Empire Prevailed

Explaining the stalemate requires attention to several structural conditions. The first was distance and supply. A Roman army marching from Syria or Anatolia to Ctesiphon crossed several hundred kilometers of steppe or river valley, dependent on river transport, local supplies, and forts. Seasonality mattered: summer heat and winter cold restricted operations. The Arsacid court, in turn, could not easily project power to Syria without the cooperation of nobles whose retinues expected to return home. The second was the nature of the military systems, discussed above: Roman infantry and siege capacity were strong in sieges and pitched battles when supplied and supported by cavalry, while Arsacid mobile forces were formidable in open ground but ill-suited to garrisoning conquered cities.

A third condition was political. Roman campaigns were often shaped by the ambitions of individual commanders and emperors and by domestic crises; the death of Trajan, the murder of Caracalla, and the civil wars of the 190s all interrupted policy. On the Arsacid side, dynastic instability, with rival kings and powerful noble houses, could either weaken the response to Rome or, as in the 190s and 210s, give Rome opportunities, though Roman intervention could also unify Arsacid nobles against an external enemy. The fourth was the resilience of local societies. Cities such as Hatra and Nisibis could defend themselves; Mesopotamian towns could rebel against conquerors; and much of the plateau lay beyond the reach of Roman armies.

Finally, both empires derived benefits from a limited relationship. Client kingdoms and buffer zones reduced the cost of confrontation. Trade, discussed in Chapter 20, crossed the frontier and provided revenue and customs to each side. Diplomatic contact allowed rulers to exchange envoys, hostages, and recognition. None of this means the empires were friends; it means that each often found stalemate preferable to the risks of escalation.

Historical Debate

Territorial Rivals or Managers of a Contested Frontier?

The question: Did Rome and Parthia compete principally for territory, each seeking to expand at the other's expense, or did they operate a shared frontier system in which war was a periodic instrument within a broader framework of diplomacy and mutual recognition?

Interpretation A — Territorial rivalry: Some scholars emphasize Roman conquest attempts (Crassus, Antony, Trajan, Verus, Severus, Caracalla) and Arsacid invasions of Syria, arguing that both states pursued expansion and that the frontier reflects the balance of resources.
Interpretation B — Managed frontier: Others emphasize the settlements of 20 BCE and 63–66 CE, the persistence of client kingdoms, and long periods of peace, arguing that both powers routinely accepted limited aims. Some also question whether Rome had a single coherent grand strategy, seeing improvisation and the ambitions of individual emperors as important.

Evidence: Both readings draw on Roman narrative sources, coins, inscriptions, and archaeology of frontier installations. The limited Arsacid evidence makes Arsacid intentions harder to determine.

Points of disagreement: The debate concerns interpretation and terminology, including how far Roman policy was planned, and whether ancient states can be said to have had frontier "policy" in the modern sense. It also concerns how far Roman sources exaggerate Roman victories and diplomatic submissions.

Why it matters: Depending on the view, Trajan's campaign appears either as an aberrant conquest or as one episode in a continuous contest, and the relationship as either persistent hostility or a durable system of coexistence. Many historians combine both views.

Student task: Choose two events from this chapter, one that seems to support Interpretation A and one that seems to support Interpretation B, and explain what additional evidence, especially from the Arsacid side, would help decide between them.

Cause and Effect

Why Repeated Invasions Failed to Produce Permanent Conquest

Long-term causes

Large distances between core regions and the frontier; two decentralized or overstretched imperial systems; the strategic value of buffer kingdoms; fortified cities and river corridors that favored defense.

Short-term causes

Ambitions of individual commanders or emperors; dynastic disputes in Armenia and the Arsacid court; opportunistic responses to the rival's civil wars.

Immediate trigger or context

A particular crisis, such as an Armenian succession, an Arsacid raid, or a Roman succession struggle, prompted each campaign.

Event or transformation

A major offensive achieves victories, often including the taking of a capital or the defeat of a field army.

Immediate effects

Plunder, captives, deportations, damage to cities, and diplomatic advantage; revolts and counterattacks soon follow.

Long-term consequences

A recurring frontier system, a settled expectation of client recognition, Roman provinces in northern Mesopotamia after 198, and an Arsacid dynasty weakened by external and internal pressure.

Historical complexity: No single cause, such as cavalry or geography, explains the pattern. Success depended on supply, political cohesion, local allegiance, and the ability of each empire to keep its own internal politics stable.

Section 19.14

The Legacy of the Rivalry

The end of the Arsacid dynasty did not end the frontier. The Sasanians inherited the Mesopotamian core, the twin capitals, and a long-standing contest with Rome over Armenia and northern Mesopotamia. They also inherited a set of practices: diplomacy by dynastic recognition, use of client states, the exchange of envoys and hostages, and mutual awareness of one another as legitimate imperial powers. On the Roman side, the emperors inherited the provinces of Mesopotamia and Osrhoene and the fortified installations along the Euphrates and Khabur. Chapter 25 examines how these inheritances unfolded over four centuries.

The Arsacid-Roman period also left an image. Roman writers portrayed Parthia as the great rival power of the east, and the Roman memory of Carrhae, the standards, and the later campaigns shaped how Romans later understood Sasanian Persia. Arsacid coins and the surviving archaeology suggest that the Arsacid court, in contrast, understood itself within Iranian, Hellenistic, and regional traditions rather than mainly as Rome's antagonist. Students should therefore be careful not to read Arsacid history only through the Roman frame.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Iran's Western Frontier from the Achaemenids to the Sasanians

What Changed

  • The principal western rival changed from Greek city-states and later Macedonian kingdoms (Chapter 13 and Chapter 15) to Rome, a state with legions, provinces, and much greater administrative reach.
  • The Achaemenid monarchy had already been polycentric, moving among several royal centers including Susa and Babylon as well as Persepolis in Fars; the Arsacid realm carried this further, with its principal western capital complex now sited in Mesopotamia itself.
  • Armenia became a principal object of competition, and the concept of an Arsacid or Roman "recognized" king became standard.

What Stayed the Same

  • Mesopotamia remained a major imperial core and communications corridor throughout, while the principal zone of interstate conflict to the west shifted from Anatolia and the Aegean under the Achaemenids to the Euphrates-Mesopotamian frontier under the Arsacids and Sasanians.
  • Iranian rulers continued to manage a large, diverse realm through aristocratic partnership and subordinate kings.
  • Diplomacy and war alternated, and buffer states mattered.

Timing and experience: Change was gradual and uneven. The Roman military presence east of the Euphrates hardened suddenly in 165 and 198, while many local populations experienced continuity in language, religion, and urban life despite changing overlords. Elites, garrisons, and communities along the campaign routes experienced change most directly. Why it matters: The pattern prevents the idea of a permanent "East against West" struggle and shows a frontier that evolved through negotiation, contingency, and institutional adaptation.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Rome and Arsacid Parthia were durable rival empires whose competition created a long-lived western Asian frontier system. Neither dominated the other. Military victories repeatedly encountered political, geographic, logistical, and institutional limits, and the relationship they produced combined warfare, diplomacy, buffer kingdoms, and negotiated coexistence.

The rivalry began when Pompey's organization of Syria placed Rome on the Euphrates, in territory the Arsacids had already been moving toward through the collapse of the Seleucid kingdom. Early diplomatic contact, such as the meeting associated with Sulla, did not create a settled boundary, and the Euphrates functioned as a reference point rather than as a border.

Carrhae in 53 BCE was the first great test. Crassus invaded without a Parthian provocation, chose a route through Mesopotamia, and was defeated by a force commanded by the noble Surena that combined horse archers with heavy cavalry and a supply of arrows. The victory reflected Roman errors and effective Arsacid logistics, but it did not lead to conquest, and it should not be generalized into a claim that mounted archers inevitably defeated infantry. Our main narrative, Plutarch's, is a Greek moral biography written under Roman imperial rule, long after the events it describes.

The following decades showed the reversibility of gains. Parthian forces, with the Roman defector Labienus, occupied Syria and Anatolia in 40 BCE and were expelled by 38. Antony's invasion failed in 36 BCE. Augustus recovered the standards in 20 BCE through negotiation and presented the settlement as a victory; the Arsacid court accepted a stable arrangement that cost little of substance.

Armenia emerged as the central diplomatic problem. Because neither empire could easily annex it, both competed through dynastic appointments and recognition. The compromise of 62 to 66 CE, in which an Arsacid prince ruled Armenia as a king crowned by the Roman emperor, became the model for later settlements. The compromise established a durable pattern of contested and negotiated influence, although that arrangement was repeatedly disrupted, and Armenia was eventually partitioned between Rome and the Sasanians around 387 CE, with the eastern Arsacid monarchy there lasting until 428 CE.

The Euphrates frontier was a zone, not a line. Roman forts and legionary bases faced Mesopotamian cities and client kingdoms such as Osrhoene, Hatra, and Adiabene, whose allegiance could shift. Ordinary people in this zone experienced requisition, deportation, garrison life, and shifting authority, though the sources often obscure them.

The second and early third centuries CE saw the largest Roman offensives. Trajan reached the Persian Gulf, but revolts and his death ended his conquests, and Hadrian withdrew. Verus's generals took Dura-Europos and sacked the twin capitals in 165, and Septimius Severus sacked Ctesiphon in 198 and created a province of Mesopotamia. Caracalla's invasion and Macrinus's costly peace followed. The Antonine Plague illustrates how war moved beyond the battlefield.

The reasons neither empire eliminated the other included distance, supply, seasonality, the strengths and weaknesses of their military systems, the political instability of both courts, the resilience of fortified cities, and the advantages both derived from limited coexistence. Historians differ over whether to describe this primarily as territorial rivalry or as a managed frontier, and many combine the two.

Rival Arsacid kings, repeated Roman sacks of the capital region, and the independent rise of Ardashir's own power base in Fars formed part of the context within which Ardashir I overthrew the dynasty around 224 to 226 CE; none of these conditions made a Sasanian victory inevitable. The Sasanians inherited the frontier system, its diplomatic practices, and its geography. The longer story of that inheritance belongs to Chapter 25.

Finally, students should carry forward a method. The Roman and Greek sources are indispensable and biased; Arsacid voices are faint; archaeology and coins correct and complicate the literary picture; and any account of the rivalry must attend to what the sources cannot show.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Arsacid
Of the dynasty founded by Arsaces that ruled Parthian Iran and Mesopotamia until the 220s CE.
Parthian
Term used for the empire and its military and cultural traditions, derived from Parthia in northeastern Iran; also the name of a Middle Iranian language.
Frontier
A zone of contact and contest between political systems, made up of cities, roads, garrisons, and client states, rather than a surveyed border.
Limes
Latin term for a road or boundary line, later applied by modern scholars to Roman frontier systems; its meaning for this period is debated.
Client kingdom
A formally independent monarchy recognized or installed by a stronger power and expected to support its interests.
Cataphract
A heavily armored lancer, mounted on an armored or partly armored horse.
Horse archer
A lightly equipped mounted soldier using a composite bow, capable of harassing enemies from a distance.
Legion
The Roman army's principal heavy infantry formation, supplemented by auxiliary troops.
Auxiliaries
Non-citizen (later mostly provincial) troops serving alongside the legions, including cavalry and archers.
Standards
Legionary emblems, including eagles, whose loss carried military and symbolic significance for Rome.
Siege train
The siege engines and equipment accompanying an army; its loss crippled Antony's campaign in 36 BCE.
Suren
One of the leading noble houses of the Arsacid realm, whose head commanded the Parthian army at Carrhae.
Proconsul
A Roman magistrate holding military and administrative command in a province, such as Crassus in Syria.
Province
A territory administered by Rome under a governor, in contrast to a client kingdom.
Armenia
A highland kingdom between the empires, ruled at various times by Artaxiad and Arsacid dynasties.
Osrhoene
A small kingdom centered on Edessa in northern Mesopotamia, alternately aligned with Parthia and Rome.
Adiabene
A kingdom in the upper Tigris region, whose royal house is reported to have converted to Judaism in the first century CE.
Hatra
A fortified city in the northern Mesopotamian steppe that resisted Trajan and Severus.
Dura-Europos
A city on the middle Euphrates, under Arsacid control until taken by Rome in the 160s CE, whose remains provide important evidence.
Diadem
The royal headband symbolizing kingship, whose conferral by Rome on Tiridates in 66 CE marked recognition.
Dynastic hostage
A member of a royal family sent to a foreign court as a guarantee or to strengthen ties, as with the sons of Phraates IV.
Indemnity
A payment imposed or accepted after a war; Macrinus's payment to Artabanus IV is an example.
Buffer state
A smaller state positioned between rivals, whose existence reduces direct confrontation.
Retrenchment
Deliberate reduction of an empire's commitments, as in Hadrian's abandonment of Trajan's conquests.
Antonine Plague
An epidemic of the later second century CE, connected by ancient sources with the return of Roman troops from Mesopotamia; its scale and cause are debated.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows that one of the best known confrontations of the ancient world was neither continuous nor simple. The image of Carrhae as a clash of civilizations gives way, on inspection, to a system of frontier politics in which negotiation, recognition, and dynastic bargaining recurred as often as invasion. Understanding this helps students read later conflicts with more care.

The chapter also demonstrates the value of asking whose voice a source represents. Our detailed narratives come from Roman and Greek authors; Arsacid actors appear largely through their actions and their coins. The Arsacid state is easy to caricature as a passive antagonist, yet its aristocratic structure, examined in Chapter 18, helps explain both its striking successes and its difficulty in sustaining campaigns.

Finally, the chapter links Volume II's Parthian and Sasanian halves. The Sasanian dynasty that emerged in the 220s inherited a frontier that was already old, a set of diplomatic habits, and a capital region that Rome had shown it could reach but not hold. Chapter 20 turns from war to the commerce and cities that crossed that frontier, and Chapter 25 follows the long rivalry that continued after the Arsacids fell.
