Peoples, Religions, Languages, and Society in the Achaemenid World

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

The Achaemenid Empire was not a country with a single people, language, or faith. It was a political framework, held together by kingship, service, and administration, inside which Persians, Babylonians, Egyptians, Lydians, Judeans, Bactrians, and many others continued to live as themselves, on terms that were never equal.

At Susa, in the reign of Darius I, a royal inscription recorded how the king's palace was built. According to this foundation text, cedar timber was brought from Lebanon, gold from Sardis and Bactria, lapis lazuli and carnelian from Sogdiana, turquoise from Chorasmia, silver and ebony from Egypt, and ivory from Ethiopia, Sind, and Arachosia. The workers who shaped these materials are named by origin as well: Babylonians made the bricks, Sardians and Ionians cut stone, and Medes and Egyptians worked gold and decorated walls. The text is written in Old Persian, and it survives in Elamite and Akkadian versions as well. Even as a piece of royal self-presentation, it shows that a single building project could draw on the labor, skill, and resources of regions separated by thousands of kilometers.

The inscription does not tell us what these workers spoke at home, whom they worshipped, how they were housed, or what they thought of the king whose palace they built. It shows the empire as the king wished it to be seen, a well-ordered assembly of lands cooperating under the favor of the god Ahuramazda. This chapter asks what lay beneath that image. What kind of human world did Achaemenid government encompass? How did people with different languages, religious traditions, local loyalties, and ways of making a living experience life inside one imperial order? And why did that order not turn them into a single, uniform people?

Chapter 11 examined how the empire was governed: its satrapies, roads, royal power, and administrative practices. The present chapter turns from institutions to the people who lived within them. Its central argument is that Achaemenid rule created an imperial framework shared by many peoples without producing cultural uniformity. Subjects held overlapping identities, of city, temple, family, region, language, and military unit as well as of empire, and the monarchy governed partly by accommodating existing communities and partly by reshaping their relationship to royal power. The chapter therefore avoids making "tolerance" its thesis. The stronger and better-supported description is managed diversity under unequal imperial rule: local institutions were often preserved, but preserved on terms that served the king, and they could be supported, taxed, supervised, or punished according to imperial interest.

A terminological point must be settled at the outset. "Achaemenid" is a modern label for the dynasty that took its name from an ancestor, Haxāmaniš, invoked in royal inscriptions; it is not a name that most of the empire's inhabitants used for themselves. "Persian" identified a specific people from Persis (Fars), whose kings and court dominated the empire. "Iranian," in the broad sense established in Chapter 1, includes Persians and Medes but also Bactrians, Sogdians, Parthians, and other Iranian-speaking peoples, most of them outside Fars. And the empire's population included very large numbers of people who were not Iranian speakers at all. A chapter on "the Achaemenid world" is therefore not a chapter on the Persians alone, and it cannot treat the Persian court as though it were representative of the empire.

The evidence is unusually varied but distinctly uneven. Royal inscriptions in several languages give the monarchy's voice. The administrative tablets from Persepolis, written mostly in Elamite, record rations, workers, and ceremonies in Fars in the late sixth and early fifth centuries BCE. Cuneiform archives from Babylonian temples and family businesses, Aramaic papyri from Egypt, Egyptian inscriptions and statues, and biblical and Greek narrative sources each preserve a different regional vantage point. Each has its own genre, purpose, and blind spots, and the chapter treats them accordingly. Much of what is known about Persians comes from what others wrote about them or from what the court chose to record; much of what is known about ordinary people is indirect.

The chapter moves from peoples to languages, from languages to religion, and from religion to social order. It begins with the problem of describing a multiethnic empire without importing the nation-state (Sections 12.1 through 12.4), examines multilingualism and what it can and cannot tell us about identity (Sections 12.5 and 12.6), turns to royal religion, the vexed relationship of the Achaemenids to Zoroastrianism, and the religions of subject communities (Sections 12.7 through 12.10), and closes with social hierarchy, labor, gender, and household life (Sections 12.11 through 12.13). The concluding section (12.14) asks what, if anything, made an "Achaemenid world." Diplomatic and military contact with the Greek world, which belongs to Chapter 13, and the empire's late history and end, which belong to Chapter 14, are deliberately left to those chapters.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Achaemenid Empire should be understood as an empire of many peoples rather than as a Persian nation-state.
  2. Distinguish Persians, Medes, other Iranian-speaking peoples, and non-Iranian subject peoples, and identify the limits of the evidence for each.
  3. Interpret the Achaemenid "peoples lists" and imperial imagery as representations of royal order rather than as ethnic censuses.
  4. Describe the languages used in Achaemenid contexts and distinguish language use from ethnic identity.
  5. Analyze the religious language of Achaemenid royal inscriptions and separate royal ideology from the beliefs and practices of the wider population.
  6. Evaluate competing scholarly answers to the question of whether the Achaemenid kings were Zoroastrian.
  7. Compare how Babylonian, Egyptian, Anatolian, Phoenician, and Judean communities related to Persian rule, and assess the claim that the empire was simply "tolerant."
  8. Assess the evidence from Elephantine for a diaspora community's religious and social life within imperial structures.
  9. Trace the varied forms of social hierarchy, labor, dependency, and slavery in different regions of the empire without assuming a single imperial system.
  10. Analyze what the evidence shows, and does not show, about women, families, and households.
  11. Connect imperial mobility, patronage, and administration to the formation of shared but not uniform cultural forms.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

Historical Context

The Achaemenid Empire arose within about three decades. Cyrus II (Chapter 9) defeated the Median king, took Lydia, and captured Babylon in 539 BCE; Cambyses II conquered Egypt in 525; Darius I (Chapter 10) secured the throne after a crisis of revolts in 522 and 521 and consolidated an imperial system. The resulting state reached from the Aegean coasts of Anatolia and the Nile valley in the west to Central Asia and the borderlands of the Indus in the east, incorporating older states with long administrative, religious, and literary traditions. Chapter 11 described how this territory was organized into provinces and connected by roads, officials, and record-keeping.

This inheritance shaped the social world. Babylonia brought thousands of years of cuneiform scholarship, temple economies, and urban families with archives. Egypt brought a priesthood, a scribal tradition, and temple estates with deep local roots. Elam, whose tradition of writing and administration was long established (Chapter 5), remained central to the administration of Fars and Susiana. In Anatolia and the Levant, Lydians, Phrygians, Carians, Greeks, Phoenicians, Judeans, and others belonged to city-states, small kingdoms, and religious communities of their own. The Persians and Medes at the top of the imperial order (Chapters 7 and 8) were themselves a small population in comparison with the territories they ruled, and Persian dominance rested on court service, land grants, military organization, and the cooperation of local elites more than on wholesale settlement.

The empire also lacked an imperial church, a standardized legal code for all subjects, or an official program of cultural assimilation. Where the king's authority mattered most was in taxation, military service, the security of routes, and the recognition of royal sovereignty. That does not mean local life was left untouched. Persian officials, estates, garrisons, and demands for labor and revenue altered local economies and relationships, and imperial power could be exercised harshly, especially against rebellion. But the range of everyday life that lay outside direct royal concern was very wide. It is this range, and the ways in which people used, negotiated, and sometimes resisted the imperial framework, that the present chapter examines.

Finally, the historian's sources tilt the picture. Clay tablets survive well, so Elamite and Babylonian cuneiform archives are richly represented, while Aramaic writing on leather and papyrus, widely used for interregional administration and correspondence, survives mostly in Egypt's dry climate and a few other places. Royal inscriptions preserve the king's voice and little else. Greek authors such as Herodotus wrote about Persians from outside and often in a context of conflict. Attention to what survives, and why, is therefore built into every section that follows.

Section 12.1

An Empire of Peoples, Not a Nation-State

The modern habit of thinking in terms of nation-states makes the Achaemenid Empire hard to describe. In a nation-state, a single citizenry is expected to share a legal status, and often a language, a history, and an official culture. The Achaemenid Empire had none of these. It comprised societies that had been organized in very different ways before Persian conquest: territorial kingdoms, city-states, temple communities, tribal groupings, and long-established bureaucratic monarchies. The king's authority reached these societies through satraps, local rulers, garrisons, and taxation (Chapter 11), and the subjects' primary loyalties were often to town, temple, kin group, or military unit rather than to any idea of an imperial people.

Ancient labels for peoples also worked differently from modern ethnic categories. In Old Persian royal inscriptions, the key term is dahyu (plural dahyāva), usually translated "land," "country," or "people," a word that combines a territory and its inhabitants without separating them (see the vocabulary feature below). Greek authors used the word ethnos for a "people," and their catalogs of Persian subjects blend geography, dress, customs, and military equipment. Babylonian texts identify individuals by city, family, or occupation. Egyptian texts distinguish Egyptians from foreigners by other criteria. None of these labels maps onto a modern ethnicity, and treating them as though it did would misrepresent how people located themselves.

The empire's size and diversity make simple demographic statements impossible. No Achaemenid census survives, and ancient authors' figures for populations and armies are not reliable enough to support numerical claims. Modern estimates of imperial population differ widely, and this textbook does not cite one. What can be said is that the peoples included Iranian speakers in the plateau and Central Asia; Elamite, Akkadian, and Aramaic speakers in the southwest, Mesopotamia, and the Levant; Egyptians; Anatolian peoples speaking Lydian, Carian, Lycian, Phrygian, and other languages; Greeks in the Aegean coastal cities; and numerous smaller communities of pastoralists, mountain peoples, and settlers.

The Achaemenids themselves were conscious of the empire's plurality and used it in their self-presentation. Darius's inscriptions repeatedly list lands that "came to" him or "bear" his throne, and the reliefs at Persepolis give the same idea visual form (Section 12.4). But this plurality was integrated hierarchically. The king, as Darius says of himself, was a Persian, son of a Persian, an Aryan of Aryan descent. Persians and their close associates held the highest positions, and access to imperial power was not equally open to all. The empire therefore combined real diversity, in which many subjects lived under their own institutions, with structural inequality, in which Persians and Medes held privileged access to office, land, and royal favor. The remainder of this chapter shows how both were true at once.

Section 12.2

Persians, Medes, and the Iranian Imperial Core

The Persians were the ruling people, but they were not culturally sealed off from the peoples they governed. In Fars the ancestors of the Persians had settled in a landscape that already belonged to Elam (Chapters 5 and 7). Archaeology and texts show a region in which Iranian personal names, Elamite administrative practice, and Elamite religious customs coexisted. The Persepolis Fortification tablets, written mostly in Elamite, record Iranian names in large numbers and offerings to both Iranian and Elamite deities, which is a strong indication that Persian identity in Fars was formed in contact with, not in isolation from, the older culture of the region.

The Medes held a comparable position of prominence. Median nobles served as commanders, satraps, and courtiers, and reliefs at Persepolis depict Medes and Persians in distinct but often paired forms of dress and status. Greek authors sometimes used "Mede" as a general term for the Persians, a usage reflected in the Greek word for collaborating with Persia, "medizing," and this has contributed to modern confusion between the two peoples. The Median language is known chiefly through names and loanwords, and it seems to have been closely related to Old Persian. The precise nature of Median dominance before Cyrus and of Median participation afterward remains a matter of interpretation (Chapter 8).

The imperial core also included Iranian speakers who were neither Persians nor Medes. Bactrians, Sogdians, Chorasmians, Arians, Parthians, and Saka appear in royal lists as separate lands, with their own languages, customs, and leadership structures. Their inclusion within the wider category of "Iranian" reflects language and shared cultural heritage, not membership in a single political community, and this is one place where the distinction between "Persian" and "Iranian" established in Chapter 1 matters directly. Darius's self-description as an Aryan, while also identifying himself specifically as Persian, shows awareness of a broader ethnolinguistic affiliation; the inscriptions do not turn that affiliation into a political identity uniting all Iranian-speaking peoples.

Persian court society is best known through the elite. It included the king and his kin, high nobles who held office and estates, and a court that moved among royal residences such as Susa, Persepolis, Ecbatana, and Babylon. Nobles held land, sometimes distant from Fars. Letters from the Persian governor Arshama (Arsames) in Egypt in the late fifth century, written in Aramaic on leather, show a Persian nobleman managing estates in several regions through stewards and dependents. Elite Persian life involved hunting, gardens (the Old Persian root of the word "paradise" belongs to this culture), banquets, and royal service. Greek writers, especially Herodotus, supply much of the traditional detail on Persian upbringing and customs, but they wrote as outsiders and often as adversaries, and their claims must be used with caution.

Ordinary Persians are much harder to see. The Fortification tablets provide glimpses of farmers, herdsmen, craftspeople, and dependent workers in Fars, but the archive concerns administration and rarely explains identity or belief. Historians should therefore avoid assuming that the values and habits of the court represent those of most Persians, and they should avoid treating "Persian" as a single social class. The Persians included a small elite, a larger body of soldiers and landholders, and a population of farmers and herders whose lives are recorded only indirectly.

Section 12.3

Subject Peoples and Regional Identities

Incorporation into the empire changed what each region owed and to whom, but it did not erase older identities. Comparing several regions shows the range of experience.

Babylonia. Babylonia was among the wealthiest and most literate parts of the empire. Its cities were organized around temples, such as Esagil at Babylon and Eanna at Uruk, and around families with long-standing archives. The Egibi family of Babylon and the Murašû family of Nippur, the latter active in the second half of the fifth century, left business records showing landholding, loans, and taxation. The Murašû archive is especially informative about social mixture: it records Babylonians, West Semitic speakers including Judeans, and holders of Iranian names, among them people who held lands tied to military service. These records show that the empire's presence was not only a matter of governors and soldiers but also of long-term land and credit relationships between communities.

Egypt. Egypt was an ancient kingdom with a strong priesthood and scribal tradition, and it was the site of repeated resistance to Persian rule. Its priests and officials could both cooperate with and criticize the Persian kings. The inscription on the statue of Udjahorresnet, an Egyptian official who served under both the last Saite pharaohs and the Persian kings, reveals a member of the local elite who accepted service under Cambyses and Darius while presenting himself as protector of Egyptian temple interests. Darius appears in Egyptian-style monuments, including the temple of Hibis in Kharga Oasis and stelae along the canal linking the Nile to the Red Sea, with inscriptions in Egyptian hieroglyphs together with Old Persian, Elamite, and Akkadian.

Anatolia. Western Anatolia combined Lydians, Phrygians, Carians, Lycians, and Greek cities on the coast, all under satrapal administration centered at places such as Sardis and Daskyleion. Persian officials and Iranian landholders lived among these communities. Archaeological finds at Daskyleion, including sculptured funerary stelae, show mixed Greek, Anatolian, and Persian visual elements, and the trilingual inscription at the Letoon near Xanthos (c. 358/7 BCE) is an example of local religious administration conducted in three languages, Lycian, Greek, and Aramaic.

Elam and Susiana. In Susiana and Fars the Elamite tradition remained central. Elamite continued as a language of administration in Fars, and Susa was a principal royal residence. The relationship between the Persians and the older Elamite population is one of the chapter's clearest examples of cultural mixing within the Iranian plateau, even though the empire's monuments typically emphasize Persian kingship.

The Levant and Phoenicia. The Phoenician city-states, notably Sidon, Tyre, Arwad, and Byblos, retained their own kings and cults and contributed ships to imperial operations. An inscription on the sarcophagus of the Sidonian king Eshmunazar II reports that Sidon was granted coastal territory, including Dor and Joppa, by a Persian ruler, and it also describes the king's building of temples. The precise date of the inscription within the Persian period is debated. It shows a local dynasty presenting its authority partly through imperial favor and partly through its own gods.

Central Asia and the east. Bactria, Sogdiana, and Chorasmia supplied valued materials and are named in royal lists, and Aramaic-language administrative documents from fourth-century Bactria show how imperial paperwork reached the eastern Iranian world. The written record here is much thinner than in Babylonia or Egypt, and archaeologists face difficulty distinguishing "Achaemenid" material culture from local traditions. Beyond the settled oases, mobile peoples such as the Saka (steppe pastoralists) occupied a more ambiguous position, sometimes as subjects, sometimes as neighbors and opponents.

What these cases share is not similarity but variety. Persian presence took different forms, from noble estates in Egypt and Babylonia to garrisons, satrapal courts, and small communities of settlers, and it was densest in places where the king had important interests. Local languages, temples, family structures, and elites continued in each region, though not unchanged, and their relationship to the king could improve or worsen depending on political circumstances.

Section 12.4

Reading the Achaemenid "Peoples Lists"

Among the most famous Achaemenid texts are the lists of lands. Darius's Bisitun inscription lists the lands he ruled, beginning with Persia and continuing through a series that includes Elam, Babylonia, Assyria, Arabia, Egypt, the coastal peoples, Lydia, the Ionian Greeks, Media, Armenia, Cappadocia, Parthia, Drangiana, Aria, Chorasmia, Bactria, Sogdiana, Gandara, the Scythians, Sattagydia, Arachosia, and Maka. Later royal inscriptions, including those on Darius's tomb façade at Naqsh-e Rustam and on royal buildings at Persepolis and Susa, provide longer lists that vary in number and order, from about two dozen to around thirty. The lists change because empires change, because scribes and kings made different choices, and because the purpose of a list was not statistical.

The same ideology is expressed in art. At Persepolis, the reliefs of the Apadana, the great audience hall, depict delegations approaching the king with gifts; the identification of the delegations, from dress, gifts, and ethnographic convention, is subject to continuing scholarly discussion. On Darius's tomb and in other monuments, representatives of the lands are shown bearing the royal throne or platform, an image of order in which the peoples of the world support the king under the protection of Ahuramazda. An Egyptian-style statue of Darius, found at Susa in 1972, carries hieroglyphic and cuneiform inscriptions and a base with figures representing the lands, so that Egyptian and Persian artistic conventions were combined in a single object.

Such lists and images should be read as representations of imperial order. They tell historians how the monarchy claimed the world was arranged and what it wanted subjects and visitors to see, but they do not provide an ethnic census, a map of settlement, or an accurate account of how people identified themselves. The delegations at Persepolis do not record a real event in which representatives of every land assembled. The peoples depicted are shown with recognizable dress and gifts because artists had to make each land distinguishable, not because they observed each people's actual clothing on a particular day. That does not make the lists worthless. They show which regions were considered part of the empire, how the king wanted his authority understood, and what categories the court used, but the categories belong to court ideology.

Comparing the lists with other sources shows the difference. The Susa foundation text names workers by origin, but its purpose is to praise the king. Administrative texts from Persepolis show workers of many origins receiving rations, and Babylonian archives show families identified by names and city. Together these sources reveal a real population that was far more mixed and fluid than any list could show.

Historical Vocabulary
Dahyu (plural dahyāva)

Definition: An Old Persian word used in royal inscriptions for a land or country together with its inhabitants; conventionally translated "land," "country," or "people," and often, in administrative discussion, associated with a province or satrapy, though the exact relationship between dahyu and the administrative unit of the satrapy remains discussed by specialists.

Historical context: Darius's inscriptions describe the dahyāva as coming to him, bearing his throne, or obeying his law, and the word is used in the Bisitun and later lists. It could refer to Persia itself as well as to conquered lands.

Why it matters: The term combines geography and population and does not separate an ethnic identity from a territory. It is a reminder that Achaemenid categories of belonging were not the same as modern ethnic or national ones.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not translate dahyu as "nation" or assume that each dahyu was ethnically uniform. A single land could contain several languages and communities, and some communities, such as Judeans, lived in more than one land.

Section 12.5

A Multilingual Empire

The Achaemenid Empire operated in many languages at once. Chapter 11 discussed the administrative functions of several of them. The present section considers what the range of languages shows about the empire's society.

Old Persian was the language of the royal house and is known mainly from royal inscriptions, written in a cuneiform script that was probably devised in the reign of Darius, although this is debated, since some scholars have proposed an earlier date for the invention of the script, and inscriptions attributed to Cyrus at Pasargadae may be later attributions. The script was used for monuments and a few objects, including seals and royal vessels, and there is little evidence that it served as a widespread everyday chancery language. Old Persian was probably spoken in Fars, but it is a mistake to call it the "official language of the empire" or the language in which most administrative business was done.

Elamite and Akkadian were older languages of administration and scholarship. Elamite, written in cuneiform, was the language of the Persepolis Fortification tablets and of many royal inscriptions, and its use in Fars shows the continuing importance of Elamite scribal traditions. Akkadian, the language of Babylonian cuneiform archives, remained essential in Mesopotamia, though spoken Akkadian was probably giving way to Aramaic in daily use in this period, a shift that historians infer from names, loanwords, and the increasing use of Aramaic in business records.

Aramaic, a West Semitic language, became the most widely used interregional language of written administration and correspondence across the empire. It appears in letters from Egypt, in a few hundred texts from Persepolis, in documents from Bactria, in inscriptions and coin legends in Anatolia, and in the private papyri of Elephantine. A standardized form, often called Imperial or Official Aramaic, was used across long distances, which helped connect places whose spoken languages were unrelated. Because Aramaic was usually written on leather and papyrus, which survive only in exceptional conditions, the surviving record almost certainly underrepresents its use.

Other languages appear in regional contexts: Egyptian in hieroglyphic, hieratic, and Demotic scripts; Greek in the coastal cities of western Anatolia and among mercenaries and traders; Lydian, Carian, Lycian, and Phrygian in Anatolia; Phoenician and Hebrew in the Levant; and eastern Iranian languages spoken in Central Asia and the plateau, many of which are not recorded in Achaemenid-period writing at all. The Avestan texts, whose oldest parts are in a dialect distinct from Old Persian, were transmitted orally for many centuries, and the surviving written Avesta is generally traced to the Sasanian period (Chapter 24).

This variety produced a class of multilingual specialists, scribes, translators, and interpreters, who moved between languages and administrative systems. The Persepolis archive itself shows scribes writing in Elamite and Aramaic in the same offices and transcribing Iranian names into Elamite spellings. A single royal inscription such as Bisitun was set up in three languages at the rock and circulated in other versions, for example an Aramaic copy found at Elephantine, so that the king's message could reach readers of different traditions. The empire's power to communicate depended on these intermediaries, whose own social positions and loyalties are largely hidden from view.

Section 12.6

Language, Identity, and Cultural Exchange

A central lesson of Achaemenid social history is that language use does not reveal identity in a simple way. Someone who wrote or dictated a letter in Aramaic was not thereby "Aramaean." Aramaic was a language of administration in which Egyptians, Judeans, Persians, Babylonians, and others could participate. Likewise, a person with an Iranian name might be a Persian, a Median, a local individual whose family had adopted an Iranian name for reasons of status or patronage, or someone born to a mixed household. Historians infer identity from names, titles, occupations, and community context, and even then with caution.

The Elephantine papyri illustrate the difficulty. The garrison at Elephantine included people called Judeans and people called "Arameans of Syene" (Aswan) in the documents. The latter is a label used in military and legal contexts for a group that spoke Aramaic but does not indicate an Aramaean ethnic origin in any simple way. Units of soldiers, called degel in Aramaic, were named after commanders whose names were Babylonian, Iranian, or Semitic, showing that the organization of the garrison cut across linguistic categories. Egyptians, Persians, and others also appear in the documents as neighbors, colleagues, and adversaries.

Cultural exchange shows in vocabulary. Old Persian and other Iranian words entered Aramaic, Akkadian, Hebrew, and Greek, including terms of office and law. The Iranian word for law or decree, dāta, appears as a loanword in Aramaic and Hebrew texts of this period. Persian names appear in Babylonian archives and in documents from Egypt and Anatolia, and some families changed their naming practice over generations, a sign of accommodation to a changing social hierarchy. Artistic exchange is visible in seals from Persepolis, which combine Elamite, Mesopotamian, and Persian imagery, in Egyptian-style monuments made for Persian kings, and in the sculptured stelae of Anatolia.

Exchange was not uniform or one-directional. Persian court culture borrowed extensively from Elamite, Mesopotamian, and Egyptian models in architecture, art, and administration, so that the "Persian" style at Persepolis is itself the product of borrowing and selection. Subjects, for their part, adopted elements of imperial culture selectively, as in the Iranian names and Persian-style seals found in local contexts. Cultural change was therefore negotiated and often took forms that suited local purposes. The empire did not create a common culture. It created channels along which language, goods, people, and styles moved, and which different communities used in different ways.

Section 12.7

Royal Religion and Ahuramazda

The religious language of the Achaemenid kings is best known from their own inscriptions. Darius's Bisitun inscription attributes his victories to the favor of Ahuramazda, and later inscriptions, including the text on his tomb at Naqsh-e Rustam, describe Ahuramazda as the creator of the earth, the sky, humanity, and human well-being, and as the god who made Darius king. The kings thus present their rule as delegated by a supreme god and as part of a cosmic order. Rebels, in the same texts, are followers of "the Lie" (Old Persian drauga), while the king upholds arta, a term for truth or right order. This contrast between order and falsehood runs through the royal ideology and gives it a moral vocabulary that reinforced political loyalty.

The inscriptions also show the kings acting as religious agents. In the Bisitun text Darius says that he restored sanctuaries, pastures, herds, and dwellings destroyed by the pretender Gaumata, who is called a Magian. In a text from his reign Xerxes describes a place where daivas (a class of divine beings rejected in this ideology) had been worshipped, states that he destroyed the sanctuary, and adds that he worshipped Ahuramazda in the proper manner. The text does not name the location, and scholars debate whether it records an actual act against a specific cult, a rhetorical statement of royal piety, or a reference to a broader category of practices. It should not be read as evidence of a general campaign against non-Persian religions.

Change is also visible. Inscriptions of Artaxerxes II, who reigned from about 404 to 359 BCE, invoke Mithra and Anahita alongside Ahuramazda, the first royal texts to do so. This suggests that royal religious language was not static and that other deities of Iranian tradition acquired a place in it over time. The kings' inscriptions also mention "the other gods who exist," a phrase in Darius's Bisitun text, which indicates that Ahuramazda's prominence did not deny the existence of other divine beings.

Royal religion had visible forms. Fire-related ritual installations are depicted on royal tombs, and stone fire-holders, altar fragments, and ritual plinths at Pasargadae have been connected with royal fire worship, although the exact function of some structures remains debated. The winged figure above the king in reliefs is often interpreted as representing Ahuramazda, though this identification is itself debated. Greek writers reported that Persians sacrificed in the open air without temples or images, and offered to the sky, sun, moon, earth, fire, water, and winds. Herodotus's account contains elements that fit other evidence and elements that seem influenced by Greek assumptions, and it cannot be treated as a neutral description.

The essential caution is that royal inscriptions represent royal ideology. They tell historians what the kings said about their relation to the divine and how they wished subjects to see them. They do not provide a complete description of the religious practice of Persian farmers, soldiers, or women, and they say nothing about how subjects outside the court understood Ahuramazda. The Persepolis tablets show state-supplied offerings for a range of deities, including Elamite gods, and the picture of religion in Fars was more varied than the inscriptions alone would suggest.

Historical Vocabulary
Magus (plural Magi)

Definition: A term (Old Persian maguš) attested in Achaemenid texts for a man associated with ritual and priestly functions, and, in Greek sources, for members of a group that performed sacrifices and other rites. Herodotus also describes the Magi as one of the tribes of the Medes.

Historical context: The Bisitun inscription calls the pretender Gaumata a Magian. Elamite texts from Persepolis mention individuals identified as Magi in connection with offerings. Greek sources say the Magi conducted sacrifices, interpreted dreams, and practiced distinctive customs, including exposure of the dead, a claim that does not match the royal tombs, which are rock-cut or built for entombment.

Why it matters: The Magi are often assumed to be the Achaemenid equivalent of the later Zoroastrian priesthood. The evidence for their functions, organization, and religious teachings in this period is limited, and the identification of them with the developed priestly institutions of later centuries (Chapter 24) is an inference, not an established fact.

Potential misunderstanding: The word "magic" derives from the Greek use of the term, but Achaemenid Magi should not be described as sorcerers, and they should not be assumed to be a single unified clergy.

Section 12.8

Were the Achaemenids Zoroastrian?

The relationship between Achaemenid religion and Zoroastrianism is one of the hardest questions in ancient Iranian history. Zoroastrianism, in the sense of the organized religion of later Iranian tradition, is known from the Avesta, from Middle Persian books, and from the institutions of the Sasanian period and after. The oldest layer of the Avesta, the Gathas, is composed in an old dialect, Old Avestan, and is attributed to Zarathustra. The date and homeland of Zarathustra are uncertain. Linguistic arguments about Old Avestan have led many scholars to place the composition of the Gathas in the second or early first millennium BCE, probably in the eastern Iranian world, while later Zoroastrian and Greek traditions gave dates in the sixth century BCE or earlier. No date can be treated as established.

The Achaemenid inscriptions contain elements that resemble later Zoroastrianism. They center on Ahuramazda, the same divine name that appears in the Avestan tradition in the form Ahura Mazdā; they contrast arta with drauga, concepts with counterparts in the Avesta; and they reject daivas. But they also differ. They never mention Zarathustra. They invoke, at least from Artaxerxes II onward, deities such as Mithra and Anahita. Their language is Old Persian, not Avestan. Greek testimony on Persian ritual, including the reports about the treatment of corpses, does not simply confirm the later practices of Zoroastrian communities, and the Achaemenid royal tombs do not match later Zoroastrian funerary norms.

For these reasons, statements that "Zoroastrianism was the official religion of the Persian Empire" are misleading. There was no official state church in the Achaemenid period, and the texts do not show a codified orthodoxy enforced on Persians or on subjects. It is more careful to say that the kings worshipped Ahuramazda and shared with later Zoroastrianism a group of concepts and names, and that the exact relationship between the royal religion, the Gathic tradition, and the religion that emerged in later centuries is debated. Some scholars use terms such as "Mazdaism" or "Achaemenid Mazdaism" to describe the religion of the kings without committing to a Zoroastrian identity.

The problem of evidence is central. Later Zoroastrian sources were written down centuries after the Achaemenid Empire and reflect their own institutional concerns. Greek authors wrote from outside. The Achaemenid inscriptions are short royal statements. The Avesta was transmitted orally, so its early history must be reconstructed from linguistic and internal evidence. Historians must therefore avoid projecting the Sasanian institutions of Chapter 24 back onto the Achaemenid period, while also avoiding the opposite error of dismissing the evident continuities in divine names, moral vocabulary, and ritual practice. The section that follows presents the main lines of scholarly disagreement.

Historical Debate

Were the Achaemenid Kings Zoroastrian?

The question: Did the Achaemenid kings follow a form of the religion associated with Zarathustra, or did they practice a related but distinct Iranian religion, and how should historians describe royal religion when the evidence is limited?

Interpretation A — The kings were Zoroastrians, in a meaningful sense: Some scholars emphasize Ahuramazda's central position, the opposition between arta and drauga, and the rejection of daivas as signs that the kings belonged to the tradition of Zarathustra, even if they did not mention him and even if their practice differed in detail from later norms.
Interpretation B — The kings worshipped Ahuramazda but were not clearly Zoroastrian: Others stress that the inscriptions never name Zarathustra, that the language and the divine company (including Mithra and Anahita) differ from the Gathas, and that the tradition of the Avesta developed in ways not visible in royal texts. On this view, Achaemenid religion belonged to a broader Iranian religious world of which Zoroastrianism was one strand.
Interpretation C — The categories are anachronistic: A further approach holds that the sharp distinction between "Zoroastrian" and "non-Zoroastrian" belongs to later religious institutions, and that in the Achaemenid period Iranian religion was fluid, regionally varied, and not yet organized under a single authority. Scholars in this camp describe royal religion in terms of its own evidence and avoid the label.

Evidence: Royal inscriptions in Old Persian, Elamite, and Akkadian; the Elamite texts from Persepolis; Greek descriptions of Persian religion; the Old and Young Avestan texts and their linguistic dating; the archaeology of fire altars and royal tombs; and the later Zoroastrian tradition.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns chronology (when the Gathas were composed), terminology (what "Zoroastrian" means), and interpretation (how much weight the shared concepts carry against the differences). It is not primarily a disagreement about the basic facts of the inscriptions.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians treat the origins of Zoroastrianism, the history of Iranian religion before the Sasanians, and the claim that Iran had a continuous religious tradition from the Achaemenid period to late antiquity. It also affects the way modern national and religious narratives use ancient Iran, which is why careful terminology is important.

Then identify one piece of evidence that would be difficult for that interpretation to explain, and describe what new evidence, if it were discovered, would most change your view.

Section 12.9

Local Religions under Persian Rule

Subject communities kept their own gods, temples, and priesthoods, and Persian rulers regularly interacted with them. The pattern was not uniform, and the common description of Persian rule as "tolerant" is too simple to explain it. Persian kings supported local cults when doing so helped secure the loyalty of important elites, and they intervened in or punished temple communities when those communities resisted or when royal interests required it.

Babylonia. The Cyrus Cylinder, a Babylonian text on a clay barrel, presents Cyrus as the choice of the god Marduk and describes the return of divine images and of displaced peoples to their sanctuaries and settlements. It was written from the perspective of Babylonian priestly tradition, and it uses the conventions of Mesopotamian royal writing. It should not be treated as a universal declaration of rights or as proof of a general policy of restoring all religions, but it is good evidence of how the new regime spoke in the idiom of the society it conquered. Temples such as Eanna at Uruk continued to function under Persian rule, with their own personnel and economies, but they were subject to royal demands and supervision. Babylonian revolts in the early reign of Xerxes were followed by changes in the leadership of some institutions, though scholars disagree about how severe and lasting the repression was.

Egypt. The Egyptian evidence is mixed. The statue inscription of Udjahorresnet describes a priestly official who claims that, under Cambyses, he purified the temple of Neith at Sais and restored its revenues, and Darius supported temple building, as at Hibis. Later Egyptian tradition, preserved in the Demotic Chronicle, accuses Cambyses of reducing temple income, and Greek writers, especially Herodotus, report that Cambyses killed the Apis bull. An Apis bull received an official burial under Cambyses's authority, evidence that complicates the broader hostile portrait of his religious policy; that burial alone, however, cannot determine whether the separate story that he wounded another Apis bull is historical. This is a good example of hostile later memory interacting with contemporary evidence. The Egyptian priesthood engaged with the Persians in varied ways, and their relationship deteriorated at times of revolt.

Anatolia, Phoenicia, and Fars. In Anatolia, Persian officials and nobles lived alongside cults of local and Greek deities, and inscriptions such as the Letoon trilingual show local religious foundations recognized within the imperial administrative context. In Phoenicia, kings such as Eshmunazar II built temples for their own gods. In Fars, the Fortification tablets show that the administration supplied grain, wine, and animals for ceremonies for a number of deities, including Elamite gods such as Humban, as well as Ahuramazda and other Iranian deities, and they refer to Magi and to ritual ceremonies with Elamite names. The state was thus involved in supplying religious activity, and the record shows more religious variety within Fars than royal inscriptions suggest.

Persian rule therefore looks neither like principled toleration nor like systematic persecution. It looks like pragmatic governance in which religious institutions were useful sources of local authority, revenue, and legitimacy, and were treated as allies when they cooperated and as threats when they did not. The experience of these communities differed, and so did their memory of Persian rule.

Continuity and Change Over Time

What Changed When a Region Entered Persian Rule?

What Changed

  • Sovereignty passed to a distant king, and revenues, in the form of taxes, dues, and labor, flowed toward the imperial system
  • Persian and Median officials, nobles, and garrisons entered local societies, sometimes holding estates
  • Aramaic and imperial administrative practice provided new channels of communication
  • Some local elites gained access to imperial patronage, and others lost status after revolts
  • People, goods, and ideas moved more widely, including deported and resettled groups

What Stayed the Same

  • Local languages, temples, and priestly families continued in Babylonia, Egypt, Anatolia, and the Levant
  • Family and property law, marriage practice, and business customs largely continued along local lines
  • Village and city organization and agricultural cycles were mostly unchanged
  • Local elites remained the main intermediaries between the population and the crown

Pattern of change: Change was gradual and uneven, and it was contested at moments of revolt. The people who experienced it most directly were those in contact with the state: temple officials, landholders with service obligations, garrison communities, and workers moved for royal projects. Farmers and craftspeople in many villages may have experienced greater continuity in local life, but the evidence for their experience is much thinner. Why it matters: The pattern explains why regional identities survived for centuries under Persian rule, and why cultural continuity is not evidence that Persian control was weak.

Section 12.10

Jewish and Other Diaspora Communities

Judean communities provide one of the best-documented examples of religious and social life under Persian rule, because they appear in several kinds of sources. In Babylonia, cuneiform tablets from a settlement called Āl-Yahūdu ("Judah-town"), dating from about 572 to 477 BCE and so spanning the late Neo-Babylonian and early Achaemenid periods, show families with Judean names living in the countryside and taking part in the local economy, holding land, paying taxes, and appearing in contracts. Judeans also appear in the Murašû archive. These documents show communities that kept their names and identity while operating within Babylonian legal and economic structures.

In Judah itself, which became the Persian province of Yehud, biblical texts describe the rebuilding of the Jerusalem temple, traditionally dated to 516/515 BCE, and the missions of Ezra and Nehemiah. The books of Ezra and Nehemiah preserve documents in Aramaic presented as Persian royal letters and decrees. Historians treat these texts as sources with their own religious and literary purposes. Their authenticity and the date of the events they describe are debated, including the question of which Artaxerxes was reigning at the time of Ezra's mission. Other biblical texts, such as passages in Isaiah that describe Cyrus as the Lord's anointed, reflect the perspective of communities that viewed Persian rule in religious terms. These sources are valuable, but they are not neutral administrative records. Coins and jar-handle stamps bearing the name Yehud provide non-biblical evidence for provincial administration.

Elephantine offers direct documentation. The island fortress at the southern frontier of Egypt housed a military community that included Judeans, who had a temple of the god Yahu. Aramaic papyri from the fifth century BCE record the family life, business dealings, and religious concerns of this community. A letter of 419 BCE, traditionally called the “Passover Letter,” gives calendrical and festival instructions and appears to reflect an imperial mandate transmitted to the community; recent scholarship disputes exactly how the text should be interpreted and whether it specifically concerns Passover. In 410 BCE the temple was destroyed, and the community petitioned the governor of Judah for permission to rebuild it. The petition is the subject of the Primary Source Window below.

Elephantine demonstrates several features of imperial life. A community kept its religious identity while serving in a Persian-administered military structure, wrote in Aramaic, used Persian-era legal formulas, and lived alongside Egyptians whose own temples and priesthoods could be in conflict with the newcomers. It also shows how local rivalries could be brought into the imperial system through petitions to governors. It does not show that all Judeans followed the same religious practices as the temple in Jerusalem, and it warns against assuming a uniform "Jewish" religion in the Persian period.

Section 12.11

Social Hierarchy across the Empire

No single social structure governed the whole empire. At the top stood the king and the royal family, followed by a Persian and Median nobility that held office, estates, and access to the court. Darius's account of the conspirators who helped him seize power names a small group of noble supporters, and Persian noble families remain prominent in later sources. Below and beside them were regional rulers and local notables, such as Phoenician kings and Egyptian priestly families, who kept power at the local level because the crown found them useful.

Religious personnel formed another group. Babylonian and Egyptian temples employed priests, administrators, and craftspeople, controlled land, and participated in local economies. Scribes and literate specialists formed a professional class, whether in the Elamite and Aramaic offices at Persepolis, in Babylonian temple schools, or in Egyptian scribal households. Soldiers were also a social category: garrison communities such as Elephantine and holders of land tied to military service in Babylonia had obligations to the crown and a status based on service. Below these groups were merchants, artisans, farmers, herders, and a large population of dependent and unfree laborers.

Regional systems differed. In Babylonia, land held in exchange for military service was organized in units named for the equipment or service required, such as bow, horse, or chariot land, and holders often relied on intermediaries such as the Murašû firm to manage it. In Egypt, temple estates and the older administrative hierarchy continued, alongside Persian estates and garrisons. In Fars, the archives show a system of officials, supervisors, and workers organized around royal and elite estates, with commodity rations dominating the surviving compensation records of the state- and estate-administered sector documented by the Persepolis archives (coinage was not yet widely used in Fars). In Anatolia, local dynasts and city elites continued to exist under satrapal authority.

Social mobility is difficult to trace, but service to the crown could preserve, reshape, or in some cases enhance the position of non-Persian elites, while access to the highest offices remained largely restricted. Udjahorresnet is best understood as an already prominent local official adapting his position to a new imperial regime. Historians should also avoid drawing one hierarchy from the Persian court and applying it everywhere. The empire's social order was a composite of local orders linked to a royal hierarchy at the top.

Section 12.12

Labor, Dependence, and Slavery

Terms such as "slave," "free worker," and "serf" do not map neatly onto the evidence, and historians must be careful about using modern categories. The Persepolis Fortification tablets record large numbers of workers receiving rations of grain, and sometimes wine, beer, or meat, in exchange for labor. The workers included skilled specialists as well as unskilled laborers, men, women, and children. Rations varied by skill, sex, and status, and workers appear in groups under supervisors, moving between locations. A term found in the tablets, kurtaš, refers to groups of workers whose legal status is debated (see below). The documents record allocations, not personal experience, so they do not tell us how workers understood their position or whether they could leave.

Slavery is much better documented in other regions. Babylonian contracts record the sale, lease, and inheritance of slaves, and temples held dependents dedicated to the temple. Egyptian and Elephantine papyri record slave sales and the ownership of slaves by members of the community, including Judeans. Slaves in these documents could be identified by marks, could be part of dowries, and could be transferred as property. Debt bondage and dependency existed as well, and in each region there were categories between free and enslaved. The empire did not create a single labor system. It drew on existing ones, and in some cases moved people through them.

Royal projects added forced or organized mobility. Greek sources report that populations were resettled, for example, Milesians near the head of the Persian Gulf, Eretrians near Susa, and Libyans from Barca in Bactria, and while such reports are from an outside source and need care, they are consistent with the documentary evidence for the movement of specialists and workers. The Susa foundation text lists workers from many regions, and the Persepolis archive shows people of diverse origins receiving rations. Whether the individuals involved were compelled, hired, or dependent is often unclear from the surviving texts.

A careful account therefore avoids two errors. It should not describe all Persepolis workers as slaves, since the evidence does not allow that conclusion, and it should not describe them as free wage earners in a modern sense, since rations, supervision, and mobility suggest degrees of dependency. It should also avoid assuming the Persepolis pattern for the whole empire. Comparing the regions shows that the crown used, adapted, and sometimes intensified existing forms of labor and dependency.

Historical Vocabulary
Kurtaš

Definition: An Elamite term in the Persepolis Fortification tablets for groups of workers who received rations and were organized in work teams under supervisors.

Historical context: The tablets record kurtaš of both sexes and different skill levels, and their rations varied accordingly. The origins of workers are sometimes indicated, and some appear to have come from distant regions.

Why it matters: The term is central to the discussion of labor at Persepolis, but its exact legal meaning is disputed, and translations range from "workers" to "dependent workers" and others.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not translate kurtaš automatically as "slave" or as "free laborer." The evidence supports the idea of organized, rationed, supervised labor, but not a precise legal classification.

Cause and Effect

Imperial Mobility and Cultural Contact

Long-term causes

The creation of a single empire across older states with established trade, scribal, and religious networks; the demands of building and provisioning royal centers; the need for garrisons and officials across large distances.

Short-term causes and trigger

Darius's building programs at Susa and Persepolis created demand for mobile workers, craftspeople, and scribes, while conquest and the suppression of revolts produced some coerced resettlements; these were distinct mechanisms.

Immediate effects

Persons from different regions worked, served, and lived side by side: Egyptians and Ionians at Susa, Judeans and Iranians in Babylonian land records, Judean soldiers in Egypt, Persian nobles on estates in Anatolia and Egypt.

Long-term consequences

Wider circulation of languages, names, artistic styles, and religious ideas, and the formation of diaspora communities that persisted after the empire, without erasing local identities.

Historical complexity: Mobility included voluntary migration, hired service, and coerced resettlement, and each had different effects. It also depended on local receptivity, and it did not lead automatically to cultural mixing. The effects on the mobile persons, on their host communities, and on the state differed.

Section 12.13

Women, Families, and Households

The Persepolis Fortification tablets are the best single source for women's work in the empire. Women appear among the workers who received rations, sometimes in groups of their own, and their allocations varied with their tasks and skills. Some texts record additional allocations to women who had recently given birth, and the amounts differed for the birth of a boy and a girl. These entries show that the administration took account of maternity and childbirth in provisioning workers, and that women's labor was part of the estate economy. They do not by themselves reveal how women were treated within their families, and they should not be read as a sign of modern-style equality.

Elite women appear in different roles. A woman named Irdabama receives and directs substantial quantities of commodities in the tablets, and her exact relationship to the royal family is debated. Other texts refer to royal women, including Darius's wife Irtaštuna (Greek Artystone), who held estates and resources. These records show that elite women could control property and administrative resources in Fars. Greek authors, in contrast, describe Persian royal women mainly in the context of court intrigue and from an outside perspective that tends to emphasize the reported seclusion of royal women, and these accounts should be read with attention to their purposes.

Regional evidence shows variation. Babylonian marriage contracts specified dowry and the protection of a woman's property, and Egyptian legal tradition gave women the ability to own and dispose of property. Aramaic documents from Elephantine include marriage and property contracts in which women, such as Mibtahiah, appear as owners of houses and other property, parties to contracts, and, in some documents, initiators of divorce. This evidence comes from a specific community in a specific legal setting, and cannot be generalized to the whole empire. In Persian noble families, polygyny and dynastic marriage are documented especially in royal and elite circles, and what is known about ordinary Persian households is very limited.

Households were the units of production, inheritance, and religious practice. They included family members, dependents, and slaves, and they held land, animals, and workshops. Family contracts, wills, and adoption documents in Babylonian, Egyptian, and Aramaic sources show how property passed between generations. The historian must therefore be careful not to draw conclusions about gender status from any single archive, and must recognize that men and women of different classes, regions, and legal traditions had very different opportunities.

Multiple Perspectives

One Empire, Five Vantage Points

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns visible in the sources. They are not quotations from any single named individual, and the groups did not have equal power, knowledge, or freedom of action.

From the perspective of a Persian court noble: The empire is an order sustained by the king's favor and the god Ahuramazda, in which service brings estates and status. Subject peoples are lands that owe obedience, and local institutions matter chiefly for the revenue and stability they provide.
From the perspective of a Babylonian temple official: The temple, its property, and its rites are the center of communal life. Persian rule can bring patronage and protection, but also demands on temple resources and the danger of punishment if the city rebels.
From the perspective of an Egyptian priest or official: Cooperation with the Persian king may secure temple revenues and personal standing, as in the case of Udjahorresnet, but the presence of foreign rulers and garrisons is also a source of tension, and memory of Persian conquest could turn hostile.
From the perspective of a Judean family at Elephantine: Membership in a garrison brings a place within the imperial system, with a temple, contracts, and a claim on the governor's attention, but it also means dependence on officials and exposure to conflict with the Egyptian neighbors.
From the perspective of a woman worker recorded in the Persepolis archive: Her labor is counted and rationed by an administration she does not control. The tablets show the allocation she received, and in some cases the added ration after childbirth, but they do not record her own view of her position.
Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Food, Work, and Belief

The daily life of most people in the empire was organized around agriculture, herding, and craft work, and it varied with the environment. In Babylonia, irrigated fields produced barley and dates, and households lived in towns and villages around temples and estates. In Egypt, the Nile flood and temple estates structured the agricultural year. In Fars and other parts of the plateau, farming depended on rivers, springs, and, increasingly, engineered water channels (Chapter 1), and herders moved flocks seasonally. Food was mostly grain, with beer or wine, meat from sheep and goats for those who could obtain it, and locally available vegetables, fruit, and fish. The Persepolis rations, expressed in grain, wine, and sheep, give a picture of provisioning in one region and cannot be applied everywhere.

Clothing and appearance signaled identity and status. The reliefs at Persepolis show distinct dress for Persians, Medes, and other peoples, though artists used dress to make groups recognizable and may have simplified real variation. Religious life was likewise local. Households had domestic cults and local festivals, while priests, temples, and shrines organized communal worship. Ordinary people probably had little contact with the royal religion described in inscriptions, and their beliefs are known mostly through indirect evidence such as names, offerings, and temple records. The historian must therefore read sources produced by administrators, priests, and kings for indirect traces of laboring, rural, and domestic life, and supplement them with archaeology wherever possible.

Section 12.14

Unity without Uniformity: What Made an "Achaemenid World"?

If the empire's peoples spoke different languages, worshipped different gods, and lived under different social systems, what justifies speaking of an Achaemenid world at all? The answer lies in the shared structures that linked them. Roads and official travel connected regions, as Chapter 11 showed. Aramaic provided a common medium of written communication. The court and the administration moved people, goods, and information over long distances. Royal ideology, expressed in inscriptions and monuments, gave subjects a common vocabulary of kingship and order. Military service, taxation, and labor obligations tied local communities to the crown. Trade and migration created contacts among people who would otherwise have had little reason to meet.

These connections did not produce uniformity. A Babylonian temple family, an Egyptian priest, a Judean soldier at Elephantine, a Lydian landholder, and a Persian noble each experienced the empire differently and interpreted it through their own traditions. Shared forms, such as Aramaic script or Persian-style seals, were adapted to local purposes. The crown supported local institutions when that was useful and pressed its demands when it was not. This is the meaning of the chapter's central term, managed diversity: a world in which difference was not eliminated but was organized, exploited, and sometimes suppressed by an empire whose power was real and unequal.

The evidence also cautions against two opposite errors. One is to describe the empire as a harmonious multicultural society, a picture that ignores taxation, forced labor, deportation, and the violence used against rebels. The other is to describe it as a machine of domination that left subject peoples culturally passive, which ignores the agency of local elites, scribes, priests, soldiers, and families who used the imperial framework for their own ends. The reality lay between these extremes and varied by region and period.

This chapter has described a society more than a state. The next chapter turns outward, to the Greek world, where Achaemenid power met communities with their own political traditions and where war, diplomacy, and cultural exchange were closely intertwined. Those interactions took place within the wider imperial world described here, and they should be read in that light.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined the human world governed by the Achaemenid monarchy. Its central argument is that Achaemenid rule created an imperial framework shared by many peoples without producing cultural uniformity, and that the empire is best described as managed diversity under unequal imperial rule rather than as either a tolerant multicultural society or a force of cultural assimilation.

The chapter first established that the empire cannot be understood through the model of the nation-state. Its subjects included Persians, Medes, and other Iranian speakers, but also Elamites, Babylonians, Egyptians, Anatolian peoples, Greeks, Phoenicians, Judeans, and many others. Ancient labels such as dahyu combined territory and population and did not correspond to modern ethnicity. Persians and Medes held privileged positions, but Persian identity in Fars was itself formed in contact with Elamite culture, and the Iranian world included many peoples who were not Persian.

Regional comparisons showed that incorporation into the empire changed sovereignty, revenue, and access to power without erasing older identities. Babylonian temple and family archives, the Egyptian statue of Udjahorresnet, Anatolian inscriptions, Phoenician royal texts, and Central Asian documents each revealed a different relationship between local society and the crown. The royal lists of lands and the imagery of Persepolis were interpreted as representations of imperial order, not censuses.

The empire was multilingual. Old Persian served the royal house and monuments, Elamite and Akkadian carried older administrative traditions, and Aramaic became the most widely used interregional language of written administration and correspondence. The chapter emphasized that language use does not identify ethnicity, that scribes and translators were crucial intermediaries, and that the surviving record is shaped by the durability of clay tablets compared with leather and papyrus.

Royal religion centered on Ahuramazda, with a moral vocabulary of order and falsehood, and it changed over time, as the invocation of Mithra and Anahita by Artaxerxes II shows. The relationship between this religion and Zoroastrianism is a matter of scholarly debate. The chapter cautioned against calling Zoroastrianism the official religion of the empire and against projecting Sasanian institutions back onto the Achaemenid period, while recognizing genuine continuities in names and concepts.

The religions of subject communities continued under Persian rule, with support, supervision, taxation, or punishment depending on circumstances. Neither the description "tolerant" nor "persecuting" fit the evidence. Judean communities in Babylonia, Yehud, and Elephantine illustrated how a diaspora kept its religious identity within imperial structures, and the Elephantine petition provided a non-royal voice in a source that must be read with awareness of its purposes.

Social hierarchy, labor, and household life varied by region. The Persepolis archive documents rationed and supervised labor, including women's work, but its terminology resists modern categories. Slavery and dependency were better documented in Babylonia and Egypt. Evidence from Persepolis, Babylonia, and Elephantine showed women as workers, property holders, and parties to contracts, without supporting a claim of equality. Imperial mobility brought people from many regions into contact through building projects, service, and resettlement.

The chapter concluded that an "Achaemenid world" existed because roads, administration, Aramaic, royal ideology, and mobility connected many communities, not because those communities became culturally alike. This understanding prepares the way for Chapter 13, which considers how that imperial world interacted with the Greek world, and for the later history of the empire in Chapter 14.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Achaemenid
A modern label for the dynasty of Cyrus, Cambyses, Darius, and their successors, derived from the ancestor Haxāmaniš named in royal inscriptions.
Dahyu (pl. dahyāva)
An Old Persian word for a land together with its inhabitants, used in royal inscriptions for the empire's constituent regions.
Managed diversity
This chapter's term for imperial governance that preserved many local institutions while subordinating them to royal interests.
Old Persian
The Iranian language of the royal house, known mainly from cuneiform royal inscriptions.
Elamite
The language of the older civilization of southwestern Iran, used in Achaemenid administration, notably the Persepolis tablets.
Akkadian
The Mesopotamian cuneiform language of Babylonian temple and business archives.
Imperial Aramaic
A standardized form of Aramaic used for administration and correspondence across the empire.
Demotic
A late Egyptian script and stage of the language used in documents, including some Persian-period texts.
Ahuramazda
The god named in royal inscriptions as the source of the kings' authority.
Arta and drauga
Old Persian terms for truth or right order and for falsehood, used in royal inscriptions to contrast the king with rebels.
Daiva
A class of divine beings rejected in royal ideology; the word appears in Xerxes' inscription concerning a sanctuary he says he destroyed.
Avesta
The collection of Zoroastrian sacred texts, transmitted orally for centuries and written down in the Sasanian period.
Gathas
The oldest part of the Avesta, in Old Avestan, attributed to Zarathustra.
Magus (pl. Magi)
A person associated with priestly and ritual functions in Iranian tradition; their Achaemenid-period organization is uncertain.
Zoroastrianism
The religion of later Iranian tradition associated with Zarathustra; its relationship to Achaemenid royal religion is debated.
Cyrus Cylinder
A Babylonian-language clay text presenting Cyrus as chosen by Marduk; a source for royal legitimation, not a modern rights charter.
Fortification archive
The tablets, mostly in Elamite, found in the fortification wall at Persepolis, dating to about 509–493 BCE.
Kurtaš
An Elamite term for rationed work groups in the Persepolis tablets; legal status debated.
Ration
A fixed allocation of grain and other goods given to workers or travelers in exchange for labor or service.
Temple economy
The system in which temples held land and personnel and took part in agriculture, credit, and craft production, as in Babylonia and Egypt.
Diaspora
A community living outside its place of origin while maintaining a distinct identity, such as the Judeans in Babylonia and Egypt.
Yehud
The Persian-era province centered on Jerusalem, corresponding to the earlier kingdom of Judah.
Elephantine
A Nile island fortress whose Judean garrison community left Aramaic papyri.
Satrapy
A large administrative province under a satrap, discussed in Chapter 11.
Ethnonym
A name for a people; ancient ethnonyms often combined geography, kinship, and political status, and do not equal modern ethnic categories.
Cuneiform
A wedge-shaped writing system used for Akkadian, Elamite, and Old Persian, though Old Persian used a distinct adapted script.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it changes the question asked of an empire. Instead of asking only how a large state was governed, it asks who lived within it and how they experienced its power. The Achaemenid Empire was the largest state of its time in the region, and understanding it requires attention to Babylonian temple families, Egyptian priests, Judean soldiers, Persian nobles, and the workers whose labor is recorded in ration lists.

The chapter also shows how much depends on the kind of evidence available. Royal inscriptions, administrative tablets, private archives, and later narratives offer different windows onto the same world, and none of them gives a complete picture. Careful reading of what each source can and cannot establish is one of the main skills of the historian, and it is especially important for questions about religion, identity, and social status.

Later developments were shaped by this world in several ways. Aramaic administration, the movement of peoples, and the interaction of local traditions with imperial structures continued to influence the regions of the former empire. Questions about the origins and early history of Zoroastrianism, which this chapter treated with caution, become more concrete in the Sasanian period (Chapter 24). The problem of governing a plural society, introduced in Chapter 1 as a recurring feature of the Iranian plateau, appears here in one of its earliest well-documented forms.

Chapter 13 will take up the empire's relations with the Greek world. Readers should carry forward the recognition that the Achaemenid Empire was not a single culture confronting an outside world, but a system of many communities under a common monarchy, with its own internal exchanges and tensions. That recognition makes the history of the empire, and of Iranian history more broadly, more accurate and more interesting.
