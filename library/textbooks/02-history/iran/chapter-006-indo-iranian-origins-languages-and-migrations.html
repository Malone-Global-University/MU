Indo-Iranian Origins, Languages, and Migrations

Persian and Iranian History: From Ancient Persia to the Islamic Republic

Chapter Opening

Long before any text calls a people "Median" or "Persian," a family of related languages was already taking shape somewhere across the Eurasian steppe and its southern oasis borderlands. No one who spoke an early form of that language left a written record of doing so. Historians reconstruct this world not from its own testimony but from the fainter and more difficult evidence of comparative linguistics, archaeology, and, most recently, ancient DNA, each of which answers a different question and none of which, alone, can identify a people.

Chapter 5 followed Elam from its earliest highland and lowland beginnings through its long Old, Middle, and Neo-Elamite history, ending at the point where an Elamite political world gave way, in the first millennium BCE, to new arrangements dominated by peoples speaking Iranian languages. That chapter necessarily left a question unanswered: where did those Iranian-speaking peoples come from, and how do historians know anything about them before they appear, already organized into recognizable groups, in the written sources examined in Chapters 7 and 8? This chapter answers that question by moving backward in time, away from Elam's lowland cities and highland strongholds, into the Bronze Age steppe and the oasis societies of Central Asia, where the deeper linguistic and cultural background of Medes, Persians, and their many Iranian-speaking relatives began to take shape.

This is a different kind of chapter than the ones surrounding it. It is not a political narrative, because no state, king, or army from this period left records in a language later historians can read. It is a chapter about method as much as about content: about how historians and linguists build confident, evidence-based accounts of peoples and processes that occurred centuries or millennia before any surviving text mentions them by name. Readers will meet Proto-Indo-European and Proto-Indo-Iranian, reconstructed languages that no one ever spoke exactly as scholars write them down, recovered through the patient comparison of later, attested languages. They will meet archaeological cultures on the southern Ural steppe and in the oasis towns of Bactria and Margiana, whose pottery, burials, and settlements provide evidence of a very different kind, evidence that can suggest correlations with linguistic change but cannot, by itself, prove that its makers spoke any particular language. And they will meet a small but growing body of ancient DNA evidence, which reveals population movement and mixture with a precision earlier generations of scholars could not have imagined, while raising its own sharp methodological warning: genes do not speak languages, worship gods, or belong to named historical peoples.

The stakes of getting this chapter right are not merely academic. The word later rendered into English as "Aryan," a term with real and important ancient meaning within Indo-Iranian religious and social vocabulary, was seized upon in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries by racial theorists and put to catastrophic political use, culminating in genocide. This textbook must therefore be especially careful to distinguish the ancient linguistic and cultural term from its later racial distortion, and to resist any temptation to treat archaeological cultures, genetic clusters, or reconstructed vocabularies as evidence for biological races, national character, or civilizational destiny. Language, material culture, and ancestry are three different things, and one of this chapter's central purposes is to teach students to keep them apart.

The chapter's narrative arc runs roughly as follows. It begins with the classification of Indo-Iranian within the larger Indo-European language family and explains how comparative linguistics allows scholars to reconstruct languages that left no texts. It then turns to the Bronze Age steppe, examining the Sintashta archaeological horizon and the debated question of its relationship to the earliest Indo-Iranian speech community, before considering how horses, wheeled vehicles, and pastoral mobility have featured in scholarly attempts to link language and archaeology. From there the chapter moves south, to the sophisticated oasis societies of Bactria and Margiana, where steppe populations and settled agricultural communities interacted in ways that are still being worked out by specialists. It then follows the linguistic split between Indo-Aryan and Iranian branches, draws briefly and carefully on Indo-Aryan evidence to illuminate the shared Indo-Iranian past, and reconstructs the emergence of Proto-Iranian and its early dialect divisions. Two further sections examine the movement of Iranian speakers toward Central Asia and the Iranian plateau and the geographic horizon implied by the Avesta, the oldest surviving Iranian religious text. A dedicated methodological section addresses the limits of archaeogenetics for questions of language and identity. The chapter closes at the threshold of written history, at the point where Iranian-speaking communities become visible enough, in outside written sources, for Chapter 7 to take up their story as named historical peoples.

Students should approach this chapter expecting uncertainty, debate, and qualified conclusions rather than a single confident narrative. That uncertainty is not a weakness of the evidence or of this textbook; it is an honest reflection of what prehistoric linguistic and cultural reconstruction can and cannot establish. Learning to think carefully within that uncertainty, distinguishing strong evidence from weak evidence, correlation from identity, and reconstruction from invention, is itself one of the chapter's principal educational goals.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the place of Iranian and Indo-Aryan languages within the Indo-Iranian branch of the Indo-European language family, including the qualified position of Nuristani.
  2. Describe how comparative linguistics reconstructs unattested languages through cognates, sound correspondences, and reconstructed vocabulary.
  3. Distinguish "Iranian" as a linguistic classification from its use as a modern national, ethnic, or racial category.
  4. Evaluate the evidence connecting the Sintashta archaeological horizon to the formation of Indo-Iranian-speaking communities, including its limitations.
  5. Analyze the role of horses, chariots, and pastoral mobility in scholarly reconstructions of Indo-Iranian dispersal without treating any single technology as a sufficient cause.
  6. Describe the Bactria-Margiana Archaeological Complex and the debated nature of its interaction with steppe populations.
  7. Explain the linguistic differentiation of Indo-Iranian into Iranian and Indo-Aryan branches as a gradual process rather than a single dated event.
  8. Assess what Avestan geographic evidence can and cannot establish about the early history of Iranian-speaking peoples.
  9. Critically evaluate the relationship between archaeological culture, genetic ancestry, and linguistic or ethnic identity, avoiding the equation of any one with the others.
  10. Identify the historical misuse of the term "Aryan" and distinguish it clearly from the term's legitimate ancient linguistic and religious meaning.
  11. Trace the general, multiple, and debated routes by which Iranian-speaking peoples moved toward Central Asia and the Iranian plateau.
  12. Contextualize the point at which reconstructed Indo-Iranian and early Iranian prehistory gives way, in Chapter 7, to Iranian peoples visible in written political history.

Historical Context

Chapter 5 traced Elam's long history from its early highland and lowland components through the Neo-Elamite period, closing at the point where Elam entered the orbit of an expanding Achaemenid Persian state in the sixth century BCE. Achaemenid Persia, and the Median political world that historians examine in Chapter 8, were built by peoples speaking Iranian languages, a branch of the wider Indo-Iranian family. Understanding those peoples requires stepping back, chronologically, well before Elam's final centuries, into a Bronze Age world in which no Mede, Persian, Elamite, or any other named historical people yet existed as later sources would recognize them.

The linguistic family that eventually produced Old Persian, Median, Avestan, and, farther afield, Sanskrit and its many descendant languages, belongs to the Indo-European family, one of the most extensively studied language families in the world. Historical linguists have established, through more than two centuries of comparative work, that Indo-European languages share a common ancestor, conventionally called Proto-Indo-European, spoken at some point in later prehistory by a community whose location and precise date remain subjects of ongoing research and debate. Indo-Iranian, the branch from which Iranian and Indo-Aryan languages both descend, represents one major subdivision of this larger family, and its formation is conventionally associated with communities occupying parts of the Eurasian steppe and adjoining regions during the Bronze Age, roughly the second millennium BCE and the centuries immediately preceding it.

This chapter draws on three distinct bodies of evidence, each with its own strengths and limitations. Comparative linguistics reconstructs vocabulary, grammar, and sound systems by systematically comparing later attested languages, producing reconstructed forms, conventionally marked with an asterisk, that represent scholarly hypotheses about earlier, unattested stages of a language. Archaeology recovers material remains, settlements, burials, tools, and evidence of technology and subsistence, that can be dated and mapped but that cannot, by themselves, reveal what language their makers spoke. Archaeogenetics, a comparatively young field that analyzes ancient DNA extracted from human remains, can trace patterns of biological ancestry, population movement, and admixture with increasing precision, but, like archaeology, cannot directly establish what language an ancient individual spoke or how that individual understood their own identity.

No single one of these evidentiary streams can, by itself, answer the question this chapter poses: where did Indo-Iranian speech communities come from, and how did their descendants eventually arrive on the Iranian plateau? Responsible historical reconstruction requires triangulating among linguistic, archaeological, and genetic evidence while remaining honest about the points at which they converge, the points at which they diverge, and the points at which the evidence simply runs out. This chapter models that triangulation throughout, and it treats each unresolved question not as a defect in the textbook's account but as an accurate reflection of the current state of scholarly knowledge.

Section 6.1

From Elam to Iranian-Speaking Peoples

Chapter 5 closed its account of Elam at a point of transition. By the time the Achaemenid dynasty absorbed Elamite territory and administrative traditions into a new imperial order in the sixth century BCE, Iranian-speaking peoples, Medes and Persians most prominently, had already been established in western Iran for centuries, gradually reshaping the political landscape that Elam had long inhabited. Chapter 5 did not explain where these Iranian-speaking peoples came from, because that question required moving backward in time, away from the political narrative of Elam's final centuries, into a much older and more difficult evidentiary terrain. This chapter undertakes that backward movement.

It is essential to state at the outset what should already be clear from Chapter 5: Iranian-speaking peoples arrived, over time, in landscapes that were not empty. Elam itself, with roots reaching back into the third millennium BCE, represents only one of several non-Iranian-language societies already established on and around the Iranian plateau before, during, and even for some time after Iranian-speaking populations became significant there. Other communities, some known to history only through their material remains, others named in surrounding written traditions, occupied various parts of the plateau and its neighboring lowlands. This chapter's account of Indo-Iranian origins and migrations should therefore never be read as the story of an empty land being settled for the first time. It is the story of new linguistic communities arriving, over a long and uneven process, among populations who were often already there.

This chapter also marks a deliberate shift in the kind of history this textbook can write. Chapters 1 through 5 combined geography, archaeology, and, for Elam's later centuries, increasingly substantial textual evidence, to construct a narrative with named places, named rulers, and datable events. This chapter, by contrast, deals for its formative Bronze Age period with processes for which no contemporary written testimony survives in any language its subjects themselves spoke; only later, indirect evidence, such as the Mitanni material examined in Section 6.8 and the Avestan and Vedic traditions examined in Sections 6.8 and 6.11, sheds any textual light backward onto that earlier world. Historians reconstruct the world examined here using methods, comparative linguistics, archaeology, and archaeogenetics, that are individually powerful but that answer different questions and cannot simply be added together to produce a single, seamless narrative. Readers should expect this chapter's account to feel more provisional, more heavily qualified, and more explicitly methodological than the political narratives that will follow it in Chapters 7 and 8.

Section 6.2

What Does "Indo-Iranian" Mean?

The word Indo-Iranian names a branch of the Indo-European language family. Indo-European itself is among the most thoroughly documented language families in the world, encompassing languages spoken today across a vast territory extending from Iceland and Ireland in the west to Bangladesh and Sri Lanka in the east, along with many extinct languages known only from ancient texts. Historical linguists classify these languages into a family tree based on shared, systematic features of vocabulary, grammar, and sound patterning that cannot plausibly be explained by chance resemblance or by borrowing alone, and that instead point to descent from a common ancestral language.

Within this larger family, Indo-Iranian designates the branch that includes, most prominently, the Iranian languages, examined throughout this textbook, and the Indo-Aryan languages, the ancestors of Sanskrit and the great majority of languages spoken today across northern and central South Asia. A third branch, Nuristani, spoken by small communities in the mountainous Nuristan region of northeastern Afghanistan, is conventionally treated as related to Indo-Iranian but distinct from both the Iranian and Indo-Aryan branches proper; specialists continue to debate the precise details of Nuristani's position within the larger Indo-Iranian family, and this textbook accordingly treats that classification as broadly established but not fully settled in every particular.

This chapter's most important terminological task is to fix clearly, before proceeding further, what the word "Iranian" means in a linguistic sense, since the same word carries other meanings elsewhere in this textbook and in ordinary usage. In this chapter, and in the linguistic sense introduced in Chapter 1, "Iranian" designates a language family classification: a group of related languages descended from a common ancestor, Proto-Iranian, itself a branch of the larger Indo-Iranian family. It does not, in this sense, designate citizenship of a modern or ancient state, membership in a particular political community, a biological or racial category, or simply "people who lived in the territory of modern Iran." Speakers of Iranian languages have historically lived, and continue to live, across a territory reaching from eastern Turkey and the Caucasus through the Iranian plateau, Afghanistan, and Central Asia into parts of western China, a geographic range considerably larger than any state that has ever called itself Iran.

Historians and linguists distinguish an Indo-Iranian speech community, a hypothetical population whose language was ancestral to both the Iranian and Indo-Aryan branches, from the much later, historically attested peoples, Medes, Persians, various Scythian and Saka groups, Bactrians, Sogdians, and others, who spoke individual Iranian languages descended from that common ancestor. No historian can name a single Indo-Iranian tribe, ruler, or settlement with confidence, because the Indo-Iranian speech community, by definition, existed before any of its descendant groups had yet diverged into the separately identifiable peoples examined in later chapters. What can be reconstructed is the language itself, and, with considerably more caution, some elements of the culture, economy, and religion associated with its speakers, through the comparative methods introduced in the next section.

Section 6.3

Reconstructing Languages That Left No Texts

No text survives in Proto-Indo-European, Proto-Indo-Iranian, or Proto-Iranian. These languages are entirely reconstructed, meaning that everything scholars say about their vocabulary, grammar, and sound system rests on inference from later, attested descendant languages rather than on direct observation. Understanding how this reconstruction works is essential before students can evaluate any claim this chapter makes about Indo-Iranian origins.

The foundational method is the identification of cognates, words in different languages that share a common origin rather than resulting from chance resemblance or borrowing. When comparative linguists find words with similar sound and meaning recurring across many related languages in patterned, predictable ways, rather than as isolated coincidences, they can propose that those words descend from a shared ancestral form. Crucially, the comparison must reveal systematic sound correspondences, regular and repeated patterns in how sounds in one language correspond to sounds in another, rather than superficial similarity in a handful of words. A single resemblance between two languages proves very little; dozens or hundreds of words displaying the same systematic pattern of sound correspondence provide strong evidence of common descent.

From these systematic correspondences, linguists construct reconstructed forms, hypothetical ancestral words and grammatical structures, conventionally marked with an asterisk to signal that they are not directly attested but inferred. A reconstructed Proto-Indo-Iranian word, for example, might be proposed on the basis of related words attested in Old Persian, Avestan, and Sanskrit, three languages descended, through different paths, from a common Indo-Iranian ancestor. The asterisk convention is important pedagogically: it reminds students, and reminds scholars themselves, that reconstructed forms are carefully argued hypotheses rather than directly observed facts, subject to revision as new evidence or improved methods become available.

Comparative linguistics can reconstruct a surprising amount of grammar and vocabulary this way, sometimes including words for social institutions, technologies, plants, animals, and religious concepts, which has occasionally tempted scholars to use reconstructed vocabulary as direct evidence for the material culture, environment, or homeland of the speech community that used it. This approach, sometimes called linguistic paleontology, can be genuinely useful, but it carries real risks: a reconstructed word for a technology, for instance, establishes only that a term existed at some point during a language's history, not necessarily that every speaker of every dialect at every period possessed the technology, and reconstructed vocabulary can be borrowed between neighboring language families in ways that complicate simple inferences about origin and homeland. This chapter uses linguistic reconstruction as important evidence throughout, while consistently flagging its limitations alongside its genuine explanatory power.

Section 6.4

The Bronze Age Steppe and the Sintashta Problem

If comparative linguistics can reconstruct a language, archaeology must supply the material world in which that language's speakers might plausibly have lived. One of the most closely studied candidates for this material context is the Sintashta culture, an archaeological horizon centered on the southern Ural steppe, in the border zone between present-day Russia and Kazakhstan, and dated by most specialists to roughly the twenty-first through eighteenth centuries BCE.

Sintashta sites are known for fortified, carefully planned settlements, evidence of intensive metalworking, particularly bronze production, and a distinctive burial tradition that includes, in a number of graves, the remains of horses and of wheeled vehicles. These features, taken together, have led many archaeologists and linguists to propose Sintashta as an especially strong candidate for the material context associated with the formation of the Indo-Iranian speech community, or at least with a population closely ancestral to later Indo-Iranian speakers. This proposal rests on a convergence of several kinds of evidence: the chronological plausibility of Sintashta's dates relative to linguistic estimates for early Indo-Iranian; the presence of technologies, horse-related equipment and wheeled vehicles most prominently, that also appear as reconstructed vocabulary in Proto-Indo-Iranian; and Sintashta's geographic position within a broader zone of steppe archaeological cultures often linked, with varying degrees of confidence, to the wider Indo-European dispersal.

Students must nonetheless understand precisely what this convergence of evidence does, and does not, establish. Archaeology alone cannot recover the language spoken by the people buried in Sintashta cemeteries; no inscription, no written record, and no directly observable feature of a grave or a settlement can specify what language its occupants used. What the Sintashta case represents is a strong correlation, argued from multiple independent lines of evidence, chronology, technology, and geography, rather than a proven identification. Responsible historians and linguists describe Sintashta as an especially plausible candidate for the Indo-Iranian material world, not as a confirmed, one-to-one match between an archaeological culture and a linguistic community. This distinction between correlation and identification recurs throughout this chapter and receives sustained, explicit treatment in the Historical Method feature later in this section grouping.

Sintashta did not exist in isolation. It formed part of a broader network of related Bronze Age steppe cultures extending across Central Asia and southern Siberia, often grouped under the label Andronovo horizon in later periods. The Andronovo label itself covers considerable internal diversity across a wide geographic area and a span of centuries, and specialists increasingly caution against treating it as a single uniform culture with a single origin or a single language. Historians should understand references to "the Andronovo culture" as a convenient scholarly shorthand for a related family of archaeological traditions, comparable in some ways to how "Indo-Iranian" itself functions as a linguistic label covering considerable internal diversity, rather than as evidence for a single unified ancient society.

Section 6.5

Horses, Chariots, Pastoralism, and Mobility

Among the technologies most closely associated with Sintashta and related steppe cultures is the light, horse-drawn, spoked-wheel chariot. Archaeological evidence from Sintashta-Petrovka burial contexts provides some of the earliest secure evidence anywhere in the world for this technology, dated to around the turn of the second millennium BCE. Chariots of this kind required specialized woodworking, harness technology, and trained horses, and their appearance in the archaeological record has attracted considerable scholarly attention because of possible connections to reconstructed Indo-Iranian and Indo-European vocabulary for wheels, axles, and related technical terms.

This evidence has sometimes been popularized, in less careful accounts, into a dramatic narrative in which Indo-Iranian or Indo-European speakers used chariots as a kind of ancient superweapon to conquer or displace earlier populations across vast distances. This textbook treats that narrative with considerable skepticism. Chariots were technologically significant and probably conferred real advantages in certain military, ceremonial, and prestige contexts, but no evidence supports a simple model in which chariot warfare alone explains the spread of Indo-Iranian languages across Eurasia. Language spread is a complex social process that can occur through conquest, but also through trade, intermarriage, the prestige of a particular culture or elite group, gradual demographic movement, and the assimilation of existing populations to a newly dominant language for reasons that have little to do with military technology specifically.

More broadly significant than chariots alone was the general pattern of mobile pastoralism associated with steppe societies of this period. Communities that relied substantially on herding cattle, sheep, goats, and horses, often practicing some form of transhumance, seasonal movement of herds and households between different pastures, possessed a degree of mobility that agricultural, permanently settled populations typically did not. This mobility does not by itself explain language change, but it plausibly facilitated the kind of sustained contact, exchange, and gradual population movement across large distances that comparative linguists infer must have occurred for Indo-Iranian languages to have spread as widely as they eventually did, from the Black Sea steppe in the west to the borders of China in the east and from Central Asia to the Iranian plateau and South Asia.

Students should retain from this section a general historical principle that recurs throughout this textbook: technology and economic organization create possibilities and advantages, but they do not by themselves determine historical outcomes. Horses, wheeled vehicles, and pastoral mobility made certain kinds of large-scale movement and contact more feasible for steppe populations of this period; they did not automatically produce any particular linguistic, political, or demographic result.

Section 6.6

The Bactria-Margiana Archaeological Complex

South of the steppe zone examined in the preceding two sections, in the river oases of what is now southern Central Asia, spanning parts of northern Afghanistan, southern Uzbekistan, and Turkmenistan, archaeologists have identified a sophisticated urbanized society conventionally called the Bactria-Margiana Archaeological Complex, often abbreviated BMAC and sometimes referred to as the Oxus Civilization, after the ancient name of the Amu Darya river along whose oases much of this civilization developed. Flourishing across roughly the twenty-third through seventeenth centuries BCE, on the most widely cited chronological estimates, BMAC sites feature monumental architecture, fortified enclosures, distinctive material culture including elaborate metalwork and stone seals, and evidence of long-distance trade connections reaching toward Mesopotamia, the Iranian plateau, and the Indus Valley civilization.

The relationship between BMAC and the steppe cultures examined in Sections 6.4 and 6.5 occupies a central place in current scholarly debate over Indo-Iranian origins, and this textbook treats that debate as genuinely open rather than settled. Several distinct possibilities have been proposed. Some scholars have suggested that Indo-Iranian speakers originated within, or were closely associated with, BMAC populations themselves. Others argue that Indo-Iranian-speaking steppe populations, associated archaeologically with Sintashta and Andronovo-related traditions, moved into and interacted with the BMAC world from the north, adopting elements of its material culture, religious practice, and possibly some of its vocabulary, without themselves originating there. Still others emphasize a more gradual, long-term pattern of exchange along the margins of the two zones, in which steppe and oasis populations influenced one another over an extended period without a single decisive moment of migration or conquest.

What all careful current interpretations share is a rejection of a simple, one-directional replacement model in which a wave of steppe invaders simply overran and displaced the earlier BMAC population. The archaeological evidence instead points toward interaction, exchange, cultural borrowing, and, increasingly, genetic mixture between steppe-related and BMAC-related populations, a pattern examined further from the genetic angle in Section 6.12. This more complicated picture is historically important beyond its narrow relevance to Indo-Iranian origins: it illustrates a pattern this textbook will observe repeatedly in later chapters, in which cultural and political change on the Iranian plateau and in Central Asia typically resulted from sustained interaction between different populations rather than from a single, clean substitution of one people for another.

Whether the specific language spoken within BMAC settlements themselves was Indo-Iranian, some other, now unknown language, or a mixture reflecting contact between multiple language communities, remains, at present, beyond what the evidence can conclusively establish. Archaeology has not yet recovered a decipherable BMAC script that would settle the question directly, and inferences based on borrowed vocabulary in later Indo-Iranian languages, while suggestive of significant BMAC influence on Indo-Iranian religious and technical terminology, cannot on their own identify BMAC's own spoken language with certainty. Students should treat the linguistic identity of BMAC itself as an open scholarly question, distinct from the somewhat better supported claim that BMAC populations interacted significantly, in one direction or several, with Indo-Iranian-speaking steppe populations during this period.

Section 6.7

From Indo-Iranian to Indo-Aryan and Iranian

At some point, or more accurately across some extended and uneven period, the linguistic community whose reconstructed ancestor is called Proto-Indo-Iranian differentiated into distinct branches: the ancestor of the Iranian languages, which this textbook follows in the chapters that lie ahead; the ancestor of the Indo-Aryan languages, whose most celebrated early representative is the language of the Rigveda, the oldest surviving text in the Indo-Aryan branch and one of the oldest connected texts in any Indo-European language; and the ancestor of Nuristani, a smaller branch whose precise relationship to the other two, as Section 6.2 noted, remains debated among specialists but which most classifications treat as a third, independently distinguishable line of descent from Proto-Indo-Iranian.

Students should resist picturing this differentiation as a single dramatic event, a moment when one unified Indo-Iranian tribe split cleanly into two groups that then marched off in different directions. Comparative linguistics reconstructs language differentiation as a gradual process, in which dialects within a single speech community diverge over generations as different groups lose regular contact with one another, adopt different innovations, and eventually become mutually unintelligible enough that linguists classify their descendants as separate languages or language branches. This process leaves no single date and no single founding event; it is better understood as an extended continuum along which distinctiveness gradually increased, rather than a fork in a road that a traveler crossed on a particular afternoon.

By the early second millennium BCE, on most scholarly reconstructions, Iranian and Indo-Aryan already represented distinguishable branches within the Indo-Iranian family, each following its own subsequent historical trajectory. The Indo-Aryan branch's early speakers eventually moved into, or emerged within, regions associated with the northwestern reaches of the Indian subcontinent, producing the linguistic ancestors of Sanskrit and the eventual family of Indo-Aryan languages spoken today across much of northern and central South Asia. The Iranian branch's early speakers followed a different trajectory, one this textbook traces in the remaining sections of this chapter, eventually producing the ancestors of Avestan, Old Persian, Median, and the many other Iranian languages examined throughout this series.

This branching should not be read as implying that Iranian and Indo-Aryan speakers immediately lost all contact or similarity after their languages began to diverge. Comparative evidence, examined further in the next two sections, shows substantial shared vocabulary, shared religious and social concepts, and, in some contexts, continued geographic proximity between early Iranian and Indo-Aryan populations for a considerable period after their languages had become linguistically distinguishable. The relationship between the two branches is therefore better understood as a long, gradually loosening connection rather than a sudden and total separation.

Section 6.8

Evidence for Early Indo-Aryan Movements

This textbook is a history of Persian and Iranian civilization, not of South Asia, and it introduces Indo-Aryan evidence in this section only to the extent that this evidence illuminates the shared Indo-Iranian background from which Iranian languages and peoples also descend. Readers seeking a full history of Indo-Aryan migration, Vedic religion, or early South Asian civilization should consult this series' companion volumes on South Asian history; this section deliberately remains brief.

The most striking piece of indirect textual evidence for early Indo-Aryan history outside South Asia itself comes not from India but from the Near East. Documents associated with the kingdom of Mitanni, which flourished in northern Mesopotamia and Syria during the mid-second millennium BCE, preserve a number of personal names, deity names, and specialized technical vocabulary, particularly terms connected with horse training, that scholars widely recognize as Indo-Aryan in origin rather than belonging to the region's dominant Hurrian or Semitic languages. The evidence does not show that Mitanni's population generally spoke Indo-Aryan. Hurrian was the kingdom's predominant language, while Akkadian also served important written diplomatic and administrative functions within the wider Late Bronze Age Near Eastern world. Indo-Aryan elements are concentrated in particular personal and divine names and specialized equestrian vocabulary, suggesting a limited elite or specialist linguistic stratum rather than a population-wide language. What the evidence more plausibly suggests is that a ruling or specialist group associated with Mitanni, perhaps connected to horse-training expertise, retained certain Indo-Aryan names, terms, and possibly religious formulas as a kind of specialized or prestige vocabulary, even while the kingdom's ordinary administrative and diplomatic life proceeded in Hurrian and Akkadian.

The significance of this evidence for this chapter's purposes is primarily comparative and chronological. It demonstrates that Indo-Aryan linguistic elements had reached the Near East, far to the west and south of South Asia, by the middle of the second millennium BCE, providing an important chronological anchor for estimating how early Indo-Iranian differentiation, and Indo-Aryan movement specifically, must have already been underway. It also offers historians rare, if narrow, textual confirmation that Indo-Iranian-related populations were mobile across a very wide geographic range during this period, corroborating, from an entirely different kind of evidence, the broader picture of steppe and Central Asian mobility examined in Sections 6.4 through 6.6.

Students should draw one further methodological lesson from this section: the Indo-Aryan branch's early history cannot be reconstructed from later Iranian evidence alone, and, conversely, the Iranian branch's early history cannot be fully understood without at least some reference to Indo-Aryan evidence, since both branches descend from the same reconstructed Proto-Indo-Iranian ancestor and preserve, in different ways, related fragments of that shared inheritance. This chapter uses Indo-Aryan evidence, including the later comparison with Avestan examined in Section 6.11's companion Primary Source Window, strictly as a tool for illuminating the shared Indo-Iranian past, not as a substitute for the Iranian-focused narrative this textbook otherwise pursues.

Section 6.9

The Formation of Proto-Iranian and Early Iranian Dialects

Having followed the Indo-Aryan branch briefly to establish its comparative and chronological significance, this chapter now turns back firmly to the Iranian branch that is this textbook's central concern. Comparative linguists reconstruct Proto-Iranian as the common ancestor of every historically known Iranian language, including directly attested Old Persian and Avestan, the only indirectly and fragmentarily known Median language, recovered chiefly through proper names and loanwords preserved in other languages rather than through surviving Median texts, and the considerably larger family of Middle and Modern Iranian languages examined at various points throughout this textbook's later volumes.

Proto-Iranian itself subsequently differentiated into a range of early dialects, a process that, like the earlier Indo-Iranian to Iranian and Indo-Aryan split, occurred gradually and unevenly rather than at a single moment. Historical linguists conventionally group later Iranian languages into broad Western and Eastern Iranian categories, a classification based primarily on shared linguistic innovations rather than on simple geography, though the classification does correlate loosely with the historical geographic distribution of Iranian-speaking populations, with Old Persian and Median generally treated as Western Iranian languages and Avestan and many Central Asian Iranian languages generally treated as Eastern Iranian.

This chapter must immediately qualify that convenient classification, because it is easy for students to over-apply it. The Western and Eastern Iranian labels describe patterns of linguistic innovation identified by modern scholars working retrospectively across the full span of attested Iranian language history; they should not be projected rigidly backward onto every earlier period as though ancient speakers themselves recognized a clean, binary division. Avestan itself, the oldest attested Iranian language, does not straightforwardly display every feature that later scholars use to diagnose "Eastern Iranian" languages, a fact that complicates any simple claim that Avestan simply represents an early form of a fully formed Eastern Iranian dialect group. Historical linguists continue to refine how the earliest attested Iranian dialects relate to later, more clearly differentiated Western and Eastern branches, and this textbook presents the Western and Eastern Iranian classification as a useful organizing tool rather than as a precise, unambiguous map of the earliest Iranian dialect landscape.

What can be said with somewhat greater confidence is that by the time Iranian-speaking peoples become visible in outside written sources, examined in Section 6.13 and developed fully in Chapters 7 and 8, they already spoke a range of related but distinguishable Iranian languages and dialects rather than a single uniform tongue. This internal linguistic diversity among early historically attested Iranian peoples is itself evidence that the differentiation processes examined in this section had already been underway for a considerable period by the time Medes, Persians, and other Iranian-speaking groups entered the historical record.

Section 6.10

Moving South: Central Asia and the Iranian Plateau

How did Iranian-speaking communities move from the Bronze Age steppe and Central Asian oasis zones examined earlier in this chapter toward the areas later known as Khurasan, Fars, and Azerbaijan, and the wider plateau that Chapter 1 introduced as this textbook's central geographic subject? This section addresses that question directly, while insisting throughout on a principle already established in earlier sections: this movement should be understood as a set of multiple, gradual, and imperfectly documented processes occurring over an extended period, rather than as a single migration event that historians could trace with a single arrow on a map.

Older scholarly models, particularly influential through much of the twentieth century, sometimes proposed relatively direct migration routes for Iranian-speaking populations, in some versions moving from the steppe around the western side of the Caspian Sea and through the Caucasus into western Iran, and in other versions moving through Central Asia and approaching the plateau from the northeast. More recent scholarship, drawing on an increasingly detailed archaeological record across Central Asia, the Caucasus, and the Iranian plateau itself, has tended to favor a more complex picture involving movement along multiple corridors over an extended period, consistent with this textbook's Chapter 1 emphasis on the plateau's position at the intersection of several distinct geographic corridors rather than as the endpoint of a single route.

Several considerations support this more complex, multi-route picture. First, the sheer geographic range across which Iranian languages were eventually spoken by the time they become historically visible, from the Black Sea steppe and the Caucasus in the west, through Central Asia, Afghanistan, and the Iranian plateau, to the borders of China in the east, is difficult to explain through a single migration corridor and more plausibly reflects movement, over time, along several different routes by different Iranian-speaking groups. Second, archaeological evidence for population movement and cultural exchange across this period appears at multiple points along the plateau's northern and northeastern margins, in the Caucasus, and in Central Asia, rather than being concentrated along a single traceable corridor. Third, the internal linguistic diversity among early historically attested Iranian languages and dialects, discussed in the preceding section, is more easily explained by a prolonged process of dispersal along multiple routes than by a single, relatively rapid migration event.

This textbook accordingly resists the temptation, common in older popular accounts and in some outdated textbooks, to draw a single thick arrow across a map labeled "the Aryan migration" and treat the question of Iranian origins as settled by that image. Students should instead picture a gradual, uneven, multi-generational process, involving movement along several corridors, interaction and mixture with existing populations at every stage, and considerable regional variation in timing and circumstance, that only gradually produced the geographic distribution of Iranian-speaking peoples that Chapters 7 and 8 will describe as historically documented.

Section 6.11

The Avestan World and the Problem of Geography

The Avesta, the collection of sacred texts associated with the Zoroastrian religious tradition revisited in its late antique institutional context in Chapter 24, preserves the oldest surviving connected body of Iranian-language material, composed in the language scholars call Avestan. The Avesta is not linguistically or chronologically uniform: scholars distinguish an older linguistic layer, Old Avestan, represented chiefly by the Gathas, from a considerably larger body of later material, Young Avestan, both transmitted orally for a long period before being fixed in later written form. Because the text preserves place names and geographic references, scholars have long examined it for clues about the world its composers and audiences inhabited.

The detailed geographic evidence bearing on this question comes especially from the Young Avestan material, most notably the first chapter of the Vidēvdād and passages within several of the Yashts, rather than from the older Gathas, which do not themselves supply a comparable list of named regions. This Young Avestan geographic horizon points strongly toward eastern Iranian lands and adjoining parts of Central Asia, including regions later associated with Bactria, Sogdiana, and neighboring territories, rather than toward the southwestern Iranian region of Fars from which the Achaemenid dynasty later arose. This eastern and northeastern geographic orientation is historically significant: it suggests that the specific religious and linguistic tradition preserved in the Avesta took shape initially among Iranian-speaking communities in Central Asian and eastern Iranian regions. This textbook does not, however, claim that the evidence establishes a simple, well-documented westward transmission of that tradition into the later Achaemenid heartland; the relationship between the Avesta's eastern religious world and religious practice in western Iran under the Achaemenids is a separate and considerably more complicated historical question, addressed as the evidence allows in later chapters.

Students must nonetheless treat this geographic evidence with considerable methodological care, for several reasons. First, the Avesta is a religious and liturgical text, not a geographic survey or a historical chronicle, and its references to places and landscapes serve religious and literary purposes that do not necessarily provide a systematic or complete geographic picture. Second, the Avesta's oldest layers were transmitted orally for an extended and only approximately known period before being committed to the written form in which they eventually survive, meaning that place names and geographic references may reflect a composite picture built up over generations rather than a single snapshot of one moment in time. Third, dating the Avesta's oldest material remains a genuinely difficult and debated scholarly problem, with proposed dates for its earliest layers ranging across several centuries; this textbook accordingly avoids assigning the Avesta's composition to a single precise date and instead treats it as a composite textual tradition whose oldest material likely originated sometime in the later second millennium or earlier first millennium BCE, without further false precision.

What students should retain from this section is a historically defensible middle position: the Avesta provides valuable, if imperfect and indirect, evidence that an important early Iranian religious and linguistic tradition took shape in eastern Iranian and Central Asian regions, evidence that meaningfully supplements the archaeological and comparative linguistic material examined earlier in this chapter, while falling well short of a precise, datable historical map of early Iranian settlement.

Section 6.12

Archaeology, Ancient DNA, and the Limits of Ethnicity

Over the past two decades, the analysis of ancient DNA extracted from human skeletal remains has transformed scholarly understanding of prehistoric population movement across Eurasia, including the steppe and Central Asian regions central to this chapter. The genetic picture that has emerged is more precise, and more interesting, than a simple story of general mixture. Large-scale ancient genome studies have found that the main population buried at Bactria-Margiana Archaeological Complex sites shows little or no detectable steppe-related ancestry, indicating that the core BMAC population was not, on the whole, genetically transformed by contact with steppe groups. At the same time, a smaller number of individuals recovered from BMAC-associated sites and dated to roughly the twenty-second through seventeenth centuries BCE do show steppe-related ancestry, appearing as distinguishable outliers within an otherwise genetically consistent BMAC population. This pattern indicates real, datable contact between steppe and oasis populations, and, in at least some cases, subsequent genetic mixture involving specific individuals or family lines, without implying that the general BMAC population was substantially admixed. Genetic evidence of more widespread steppe-related admixture becomes clearer only in populations examined at later sites and periods, discussed further in relation to Central Asian history in Chapter 26.

This evidence is genuinely valuable, and this textbook treats archaeogenetics as an important and increasingly indispensable tool for reconstructing prehistoric population history. However, this section exists specifically to insist on a methodological principle that students must carry forward, not only through the remainder of this chapter but through their encounter with archaeogenetic evidence anywhere else in their historical education: genetic ancestry, archaeological culture, and language are three distinct kinds of evidence, and none of them can simply be read off from either of the others.

Consider what each kind of evidence can and cannot establish. Ancient DNA can reveal that an individual's ancestors included people from a genetically distinguishable steppe population and people from a genetically distinguishable southern Central Asian population, and it can estimate, with increasing precision, when and in roughly what proportions such admixture occurred. It cannot reveal what language that individual spoke, what he or she called themselves, what political community he or she considered themselves part of, or what religious beliefs he or she held. A population can undergo substantial language change while remaining genetically continuous with its ancestors, as when a community gradually adopts the language of politically or culturally dominant neighbors without significant intermarriage; conversely, a population can experience substantial genetic admixture while maintaining linguistic continuity, as when a smaller migrating group is genetically absorbed into a larger existing population while that population nonetheless adopts, or retains, a particular language for social or political reasons. Archaeological culture presents similar problems: shared pottery styles, burial customs, or settlement patterns can spread through trade, imitation, or the movement of ideas and specialists without necessarily indicating that everyone who used a particular pottery style spoke the same language or considered themselves part of the same people.

This methodological caution carries particular historical weight in the specific case of Indo-Iranian studies, because of the serious historical misuse this field's terminology has suffered. This chapter's Historical Vocabulary feature on "arya" and its later distortion into modern racial "Aryan" ideology addresses this misuse directly; this section's task is to make clear that archaeogenetic evidence provides no support whatsoever for treating ancient Indo-Iranian-speaking populations as a genetically or racially uniform group, nor for treating any modern population as the genetically pure or exclusive inheritor of an ancient Indo-Iranian identity. The genetic evidence instead consistently reveals populations formed through repeated mixture and interaction, a finding entirely consistent with this chapter's broader emphasis on interaction, exchange, and gradual, uneven historical processes rather than the movement of pure, unmixed peoples across a supposedly empty landscape.

Section 6.13

Iranian Speakers Reach the Historical Horizon

This chapter closes at the point where the processes it has examined, linguistic differentiation, steppe and oasis interaction, gradual movement along multiple corridors, and the formation of early Iranian dialects, begin to intersect with written history. Assyrian royal inscriptions name a land or people called Parsua, or Parsuwash, in the northwestern Zagros region as early as 843 BCE, and the Medes appear in Assyrian records shortly afterward, by 836 BCE. Historians conventionally connect these two names to the later Persians and Medes examined throughout the remainder of this textbook, but the relationship deserves qualification rather than simple identity: the precise continuity between this early, northwestern Parsua/Parsuwash and the Persians later associated with Fars, far to the south, remains a debated question that Chapter 7 examines directly, rather than a straightforward, settled fact this chapter can assume.

This transition deserves careful framing. The peoples named in these Assyrian records did not suddenly appear from nowhere in the ninth century BCE; they represent, in all likelihood, communities descended from the long and only partially reconstructable processes examined throughout this chapter, communities whose Iranian language had continued developing, whose populations had continued interacting with neighboring peoples including Elamites and others already established in western Iran, examined in Chapter 5, and whose political organization had continued evolving in ways this chapter's evidence cannot directly access. What changes in the ninth century BCE is not the peoples themselves but the historian's evidentiary situation: for the first time, outside observers with writing systems and administrative traditions capable of naming specific groups begin to do so, allowing historians to move from the general, reconstructed, and heavily qualified account this chapter has provided toward the more specific, if still incomplete, political history that Chapters 7 and 8 will examine.

Students should not expect Chapter 7 to answer every question this chapter has left open. The precise relationship between the archaeologically and linguistically reconstructed populations examined in this chapter and the specific groups named "Medes" and "Persians" in Assyrian records remains, in important respects, a matter of scholarly inference rather than direct documentation, since no source bridges the two kinds of evidence explicitly. What Chapter 7 can do, and what this chapter has been designed to make possible, is examine the Medes, Persians, and related Iranian-speaking peoples as historically identifiable political and social actors, drawing on written evidence, archaeological evidence from the plateau itself, and the broader linguistic and cultural background this chapter has established, without needing to re-derive that background from first principles.

The transition from reconstructed Indo-Iranian prehistory to documented Iranian political history is therefore not a sharp break but a gradual clarification, in which the same underlying peoples and processes become progressively more visible as the surviving evidence improves. Chapter 7 turns to that next problem directly: the Medes, Persians, and other Iranian-speaking peoples who emerge into the written record of the plateau, examined now as specific historical communities rather than as the general, reconstructed subject of this chapter.

Historical Vocabulary
Indo-Iranian

Definition: A branch of the Indo-European language family comprising the Iranian languages, the Indo-Aryan languages, and, as a closely related but distinct third branch whose precise classification remains debated among specialists, Nuristani. The term also refers, by extension, to the reconstructed speech community, conventionally dated to the Bronze Age, whose language, Proto-Indo-Iranian, was ancestral to the Iranian and Indo-Aryan branches and, as most classifications hold, to Nuristani as well, though Nuristani's precise branching position continues to be debated.

Historical context: Comparative linguists identified the Indo-Iranian branch through systematic comparison of Old Persian, Avestan, Sanskrit, and their many descendant languages, recognizing shared vocabulary, grammar, and sound patterns that point to a common ancestor distinct from, though related to, other Indo-European branches such as Greek, Latin, or Germanic. The archaeological and genetic background of this speech community is examined in Sections 6.4 through 6.6 and 6.12 of this chapter.

Why it matters: Establishing Indo-Iranian as a linguistic classification, rather than an ethnic, racial, or political category, is essential for avoiding serious historical errors, including the racialized misuse of related terminology examined in this chapter's companion vocabulary feature on "Arya."

Potential misunderstanding: Students should not assume that "Indo-Iranian" names a single ancient people, tribe, kingdom, or genetically uniform population. It names a language classification and, more loosely, the reconstructed speech community associated with that language's earliest stage, a community whose genetic and cultural composition, as Section 6.12 explains, was itself likely formed through mixture rather than uniform, isolated descent.

Historical Vocabulary
Arya / "Aryan"

Definition: Arya is an ancient Indo-Iranian self-designation, attested in both early Iranian sources, including Old Persian royal inscriptions examined in Chapter 12, and early Indo-Aryan sources, including the Rigveda, where it functions as a term of ethnocultural, linguistic, and religious self-identification among Indo-Iranian-speaking communities, distinguishing those communities from outsiders in their own conception. The modern English word "Aryan" derives from this ancient term.

Historical context: Within its authentic ancient Indo-Iranian context, arya designated something closer to a shared cultural, linguistic, and religious identity among certain Indo-Iranian-speaking communities than a racial or biological category in any modern sense; ancient Indo-Iranian societies had no concept resembling the nineteenth- and twentieth-century pseudoscientific idea of race. In the nineteenth century, European philologists studying Indo-European languages sometimes used "Aryan" as a general term for the entire Indo-European language family, a usage now abandoned by serious scholarship because it conflated language with biology and because it was subsequently seized upon and radically distorted by racial theorists.

Why it matters: In the late nineteenth and especially the early twentieth century, racial ideologues, most catastrophically National Socialist ideology in Germany, appropriated the term "Aryan" to construct a supposed biological "Aryan race," falsely claimed to be racially superior and falsely identified with northern European populations, a construction with no basis in the actual linguistic or historical evidence examined in this chapter and one that provided pseudo-historical justification for genocide. This textbook uses "Arya" only in its legitimate ancient Indo-Iranian linguistic and cultural sense, and it explicitly and permanently rejects any racial interpretation of the term.

Potential misunderstanding: Students should understand clearly that the ancient term arya, as used by Old Persian kings or in the Rigveda, refers to a linguistic and cultural self-identification among certain ancient communities and provides no support whatsoever for later racial theories, for claims about the biological purity of any ancient or modern population, or for treating any modern nation, ethnicity, or people as the exclusive or superior inheritor of an ancient Indo-Iranian identity. The archaeogenetic evidence examined in Section 6.12, showing repeated population mixture rather than isolated, unmixed descent, further undermines any such claim.

Historical Method

Can Archaeology Identify a Language?

This chapter has repeatedly drawn on archaeological evidence, the Sintashta horizon, chariot burials, the Bactria-Margiana Archaeological Complex, while cautioning, just as repeatedly, that none of this evidence can directly identify the language its makers spoke. This feature explains that caution more systematically, because it is one of the single most important methodological lessons this chapter has to teach.

Archaeological cultures are defined by patterns of material remains, pottery styles, burial customs, architecture, tools, and settlement organization, that archaeologists observe recurring together across a defined region and period. These patterns can spread for many reasons entirely unconnected to language: through trade in finished goods, through the movement of skilled craftspeople who carry techniques without carrying whole populations, through intermarriage and cultural exchange between neighboring groups who each retain their own language, through the prestige imitation of a fashionable style by people who otherwise have no connection to its originators, or through the movement of ideas alone, without any accompanying movement of people at all. A shared pottery style or burial custom found in two distant locations might indicate that a single population moved between them, but it might equally indicate trade, imitation, or coincidence, and archaeology alone frequently cannot distinguish among these possibilities with confidence.

Language, by contrast, is a social behavior that individuals learn from their communities and that changes according to its own internal linguistic processes, only loosely tied to material culture. Populations have repeatedly changed language while retaining much of their existing material culture, and populations have repeatedly retained their language while adopting substantial new material culture from neighbors or conquerors. This means that even a well-documented archaeological culture, however distinctive its pottery or burial customs, cannot be assumed to correspond neatly to a single language community.

This does not mean archaeology is useless for questions of linguistic history. When archaeological evidence for population movement, chronology, and geographic distribution converges plausibly with independent linguistic reconstruction, as in the Sintashta case examined in Section 6.4, the resulting correlation is genuinely informative and worth taking seriously. The discipline required is to describe such cases honestly, as plausible, evidence-based correlations supported by multiple independent lines of reasoning, rather than as settled identifications. Historians should always ask, when archaeological and linguistic evidence are combined: what independent evidence supports the specific correlation being proposed, and what would we expect to see if the correlation were false? A responsible historical account, of the kind this chapter has tried to model throughout, states its confidence level honestly rather than allowing an appealing correlation to harden into an unwarranted certainty.

Paraphrase: Comparative study of the Avesta and the Rigveda reveals striking correspondences: cognate deity and concept names, including a shared underlying term for cosmic or ritual order, cognate ritual vocabulary connected with fire and with a sacred plant-based ritual drink, and shared poetic and metrical conventions. These correspondences are far too systematic and numerous to be explained by chance or by casual borrowing between unrelated neighbors, and specialists interpret them as evidence of a genuinely shared Indo-Iranian religious and poetic inheritance that later diverged as the Iranian and Indo-Aryan branches developed independently.

Analysis: This comparison provides some of the strongest available evidence, alongside the purely linguistic reconstruction discussed in Section 6.3, for the reality of a shared Indo-Iranian cultural and religious world prior to the branches' full differentiation. It allows historians to reconstruct, with reasonable confidence, certain elements of early Indo-Iranian religious vocabulary and practice even though no direct Proto-Indo-Iranian religious text survives.

Limitations: Both texts were composed considerably after the period of shared Indo-Iranian unity they indirectly illuminate, and both underwent extended oral transmission before being fixed in writing, during which change, elaboration, and reinterpretation certainly occurred. Neither text is a historical record of the Indo-Iranian period itself; both are later religious compositions that happen to preserve, alongside much that is specific to their own separate traditions, certain older shared elements that comparative analysis can identify.

Perspective: Both texts represent the perspective of specialist religious reciters within their respective traditions, concerned with ritual accuracy and religious meaning rather than historical or geographic documentation; neither speaks directly to the experience of ordinary pastoralists, farmers, or the wider population of the communities that produced them.

Questions for students:

  1. What kind of evidence do the Avesta and the Rigveda provide for the shared Indo-Iranian past, and what kind of evidence do they not provide?
  2. Why does the existence of systematic correspondences, rather than a handful of isolated similarities, strengthen the case for genuine shared inheritance rather than coincidence or later borrowing?
  3. Why must historians be cautious about treating either text as a direct window onto the Indo-Iranian period itself, given how much later both were likely composed and fixed in writing?
  4. How does this comparison complement the archaeological and genetic evidence examined elsewhere in this chapter, and what can it establish that those other kinds of evidence cannot?
Historical Debate

Where and How Did Indo-Iranian Languages Form?

The question: Did the Indo-Iranian speech community form primarily among steppe populations associated with the Sintashta and Andronovo-related archaeological traditions, primarily through interaction with, or within, the oasis societies of the Bactria-Margiana Archaeological Complex, or through some combination of the two that current evidence cannot yet fully disentangle?

Interpretation A — A steppe-centered origin with southward movement into Central Asia: Many specialists emphasize the Sintashta and Andronovo archaeological record, along with reconstructed Indo-Iranian vocabulary connected to horses, wheeled vehicles, and pastoral life, as evidence that the Indo-Iranian speech community formed primarily among steppe populations, who subsequently moved south and interacted with, and in some interpretations significantly influenced, the existing BMAC world.
Interpretation B — Substantial BMAC contribution to Indo-Iranian religious and cultural formation: Other specialists point to apparent BMAC-derived vocabulary and religious concepts within later Indo-Iranian tradition, arguing that sustained interaction with, or absorption of elements from, the sophisticated BMAC oasis world played a formative role in shaping Indo-Iranian religious and cultural identity, whatever the ultimate linguistic origin of the steppe populations involved.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on overlapping bodies of evidence: Sintashta and Andronovo archaeological remains, BMAC material culture and settlement patterns, reconstructed Indo-Iranian vocabulary, and, increasingly, archaeogenetic data on population admixture between steppe- and BMAC-related populations. They differ chiefly in how much explanatory weight to assign to steppe origins versus oasis interaction, and in how directly to read borrowed vocabulary as evidence of one population's specific contribution to another.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement concerns primarily interpretation and relative emphasis rather than a fundamental dispute over the underlying data; specialists broadly agree that both steppe and oasis populations were involved in some capacity, but disagree about the direction, scale, and mechanism of their interaction, and about whether a single point of origin can meaningfully be identified at all.

Why it matters: How historians resolve, or decline to resolve, this debate shapes broader claims about the geographic and cultural origins of later Iranian and Indo-Aryan religious and social institutions, and it illustrates, in a concrete case, the interpretive challenges this chapter has emphasized throughout regarding the relationship between archaeological evidence, linguistic reconstruction, and claims about ancient peoples.

Student task: Drawing on the evidence and reasoning presented in Sections 6.4 through 6.6 and 6.12, construct a case for each interpretation, and then explain what kind of additional evidence, archaeological, linguistic, or genetic, might help historians move toward a more confident resolution.

Multiple Perspectives

Living Through Movement: Perspectives on Bronze Age Mobility and Contact

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns archaeologists and historians have identified across many sites and periods. They are not quotations from any named historical individual, since no such individual left a surviving written record.

From the perspective of a steppe pastoral household on the southern Ural frontier: Seasonal movement of herds, access to horses and metal tools, and participation in a wide-ranging network of exchange and contact shape daily life and social status more directly than any fixed settlement or single homeland.
From the perspective of a resident of a fortified BMAC oasis town: Life centers on irrigated agriculture, craft production, and participation in long-distance trade networks reaching toward Mesopotamia and the Indus world, with periodic contact, exchange, and occasional tension with more mobile populations arriving from the northern steppe.
From the perspective of a specialist reciter of early religious poetry in an Indo-Iranian-speaking community: Preserving inherited ritual formulas, deity names, and poetic conventions with precision across generations of oral transmission is a matter of genuine religious and social importance, carrying forward a shared inheritance even as dialects and communities gradually diverge from one another.
From the perspective of a modern archaeologist or linguist reconstructing this period: Each new excavation, ancient genome, or refined linguistic reconstruction adds valuable evidence, but no single discovery, however striking, is likely to resolve every open question about Indo-Iranian origins; responsible scholarship requires holding several partially supported interpretations in mind simultaneously rather than declaring premature certainty.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Shared Vocabulary, Divergent Worlds

What Stayed the Same

  • Core Indo-Iranian vocabulary and grammatical structure persisted, in modified form, across both the Iranian and Indo-Aryan branches long after their languages became distinguishable
  • Certain shared religious concepts and ritual vocabulary, examined in the Primary Source Window comparing Avestan and Vedic evidence, persisted independently in both traditions
  • Patterns of mobility, exchange, and interaction between pastoral and settled populations recurred across the Bronze Age and into the periods examined in later chapters

What Changed

  • A single, reconstructed Indo-Iranian speech community gradually differentiated into mutually distinguishable Iranian and Indo-Aryan branches, and later into numerous individual Iranian languages and dialects
  • Iranian-speaking populations moved, gradually and by multiple routes, from steppe and Central Asian contexts toward the wide geographic distribution, including the Iranian plateau, in which they become historically visible
  • The evidentiary basis available to historians shifted, by the ninth century BCE, from reconstructed linguistic and archaeological inference toward increasingly direct written documentation

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the continuities and the changes across this long process prevents two opposite errors: imagining Indo-Iranian, Iranian, and Indo-Aryan peoples as entirely separate and unrelated from the outset, or imagining that shared ancient vocabulary and religious concepts mean that later Iranian and Indo-Aryan societies remained fundamentally identical to one another.

Cause and Effect

Why Indo-Iranian Speech Spread So Widely

Long-term causes

A mobile pastoral economy, associated with horse use and, at least in some contexts, wheeled-vehicle technology, that gave certain Bronze Age steppe populations an unusual capacity for sustained movement and long-distance contact.

Short-term causes

Specific episodes of interaction, exchange, and, in places, conflict or absorption between steppe populations and settled oasis societies such as the Bactria-Margiana Archaeological Complex, occurring at particular times and places that the archaeological and genetic record only partially preserves.

Effect

Iranian and Indo-Aryan languages, descended from a shared Indo-Iranian ancestor, came to be spoken across an extraordinarily wide geographic range, from the Black Sea steppe and Caucasus to Central Asia, the Iranian plateau, Afghanistan, and South Asia, by the time these populations become visible in written sources.

This textbook's response

Consistent insistence, throughout this chapter, that language spread resulted from a combination of mobility, interaction, absorption, and gradual demographic change across many generations, rather than from a single migration, a single technology, or a single conquering people.

Historical complexity: No single cause, chariot technology, pastoral mobility, or population replacement, adequately explains why Indo-Iranian languages spread as widely as they did; historians instead point to a combination of technological, economic, social, and demographic factors operating unevenly across many centuries and many distinct local circumstances.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households, Herds, and Craft in a Bronze Age World

The historical developments examined in this chapter were experienced, in the first instance, not as grand migrations or linguistic transformations but as the ordinary rhythms of household and community life. A pastoral family on the southern Ural steppe organized its year around the movement of cattle, sheep, and horses between pastures, relied on skilled metalworkers within or near its community for bronze tools and weapons, and marked important deaths with burials that, in some cases, included horses and wheeled vehicles reflecting the household's resources and status. A family in a fortified BMAC oasis town organized its year instead around irrigated agriculture, craft production, and participation in exchange networks that brought goods, and sometimes people, from considerable distances.

Archaeology allows historians to reconstruct fragments of this everyday world with reasonable confidence, houses, workshops, tools, animal remains, and burial goods, even where the language, religious beliefs, and self-understanding of the people who used them remain far harder to recover. Genetic evidence, examined in Section 6.12, increasingly reveals that many such households and communities were themselves the product of generations of intermarriage and mixture between populations of different origins, a finding that should encourage students to picture Bronze Age steppe and oasis communities as genuinely mixed, evolving social worlds rather than as static, isolated, or ethnically uniform tribes.

Because no written testimony survives from any individual examined in this chapter, historians must reconstruct this social history entirely from material and, increasingly, biological evidence, a fundamentally different evidentiary situation from the textual richness available for many later chapters of this textbook. Students should recognize this difference explicitly: the absence of named individuals or datable political events in this chapter reflects the nature of the surviving evidence, not a lack of complex, meaningful social life among the communities this chapter examines.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has moved backward from Elam's later history, examined in Chapter 5, into the much older and more difficult evidentiary world of Indo-Iranian origins, reconstructing, as far as the evidence allows, how the linguistic and cultural background of the Medes, Persians, and their many Iranian-speaking relatives took shape before any of them appear by name in written history.

The chapter began by classifying Iranian and Indo-Aryan as branches of the Indo-Iranian family, itself a branch of the wider Indo-European language family, with Nuristani treated as a related but distinct third branch whose precise position remains debated. It carefully distinguished this linguistic sense of "Iranian" from citizenship, ethnicity, or race, a distinction of particular importance given the term's later historical misuse. It then explained how comparative linguistics reconstructs unattested languages such as Proto-Indo-European, Proto-Indo-Iranian, and Proto-Iranian through the identification of cognates and systematic sound correspondences, producing reconstructed forms that represent careful scholarly hypotheses rather than directly observed facts.

The chapter examined the Sintashta archaeological horizon on the southern Ural steppe as an especially plausible, though not certain, material context for early Indo-Iranian speech communities, and it considered the significance of horses, chariots, and pastoral mobility for Indo-Iranian dispersal while firmly rejecting any simple narrative in which chariot technology alone explains the spread of Indo-Iranian languages. It then turned to the Bactria-Margiana Archaeological Complex, an urbanized Central Asian oasis civilization whose interaction with steppe populations remains a central, genuinely debated question in current scholarship, and it explicitly rejected any simple model of steppe populations simply replacing an earlier BMAC population.

Students traced the gradual, uneven linguistic differentiation of Indo-Iranian into Iranian and Indo-Aryan branches, examined Mitanni evidence for early Indo-Aryan mobility in the Near East, and followed the subsequent formation of Proto-Iranian and its early dialect divisions, with appropriate caution about projecting later Western and Eastern Iranian classifications too rigidly onto the earliest attested Iranian evidence. The chapter then examined the debated, multi-route movement of Iranian-speaking peoples toward Central Asia and the Iranian plateau, explicitly rejecting older models that proposed a single migration corridor, and it considered what the Avesta's eastern and northeastern geographic horizon can and cannot establish about the early history of Iranian religious tradition.

A sustained methodological section addressed the limits of archaeogenetic evidence for questions of language and ethnicity, insisting that genetic ancestry, archaeological culture, and language are three distinct kinds of evidence that cannot simply be read off from one another, a principle with particular importance given the catastrophic historical misuse of Indo-Iranian terminology by twentieth-century racial ideology. The chapter's companion Historical Vocabulary features on "Indo-Iranian" and "Arya" developed this point further, distinguishing the legitimate ancient linguistic and cultural meaning of these terms from their illegitimate later racial distortion.

The chapter closed at the threshold of written history, at the point from 843 BCE onward when Assyrian records first name Parsua/Parsuwash and, shortly after, the Medes, groups conventionally, though not unproblematically, connected to the Persians and Medes examined in later chapters. This transition was presented not as a sudden appearance but as a gradual clarification of the historian's evidentiary situation, in which the same underlying peoples and processes examined throughout this chapter become progressively more visible as surviving evidence improves.

Throughout, the chapter modeled a historical method appropriate to prehistoric and proto-historic reconstruction: triangulating among linguistic, archaeological, and genetic evidence, stating confidence levels honestly, resisting simple migration narratives and monocausal explanations, and treating unresolved scholarly debate as an accurate reflection of the evidence's genuine limits rather than as a defect to be papered over. This method, and the substantive background it has established, prepares students directly for Chapter 7, which examines the Medes, Persians, and related Iranian-speaking peoples as historically identifiable actors for the first time in this textbook.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Indo-European
A large language family encompassing most languages of Europe, Iran, and South Asia, reconstructed through comparative linguistics as descending from a common ancestor, Proto-Indo-European.
Indo-Iranian
The branch of Indo-European comprising the Iranian, Indo-Aryan, and (as a related but distinct branch) Nuristani languages, descended from a reconstructed common ancestor, Proto-Indo-Iranian.
Proto-Indo-European
The reconstructed, unattested common ancestor of the entire Indo-European language family.
Proto-Indo-Iranian
The reconstructed, unattested common ancestor of the Iranian and Indo-Aryan languages and, as most classifications hold, Nuristani, associated by most specialists with Bronze Age Eurasian steppe-related communities.
Proto-Iranian
The reconstructed, unattested common ancestor of all historically known Iranian languages, including directly attested Old Persian and Avestan and the only indirectly, fragmentarily known Median language.
Indo-Aryan
The branch of Indo-Iranian ancestral to Sanskrit and the great majority of languages spoken today across northern and central South Asia.
Nuristani
A small group of languages spoken in northeastern Afghanistan, related to but distinct from the Iranian and Indo-Aryan branches within Indo-Iranian, with its precise classification still debated.
Cognate
A word in one language that shares a common origin with a word in a related language, rather than resulting from chance resemblance or direct borrowing.
Sound correspondence
A systematic, repeated pattern in how sounds in one language correspond to sounds in a related language, used by comparative linguists as evidence of common descent.
Reconstructed form
A hypothetical ancestral word or grammatical form, conventionally marked with an asterisk, inferred through comparative linguistics rather than directly attested in any surviving text.
Sintashta culture
A Bronze Age archaeological horizon on the southern Ural steppe, dated to roughly the twenty-first through eighteenth centuries BCE, frequently proposed as a strong candidate for the material context of early Indo-Iranian speech communities.
Andronovo horizon
A broad, internally diverse complex of later Bronze Age steppe archaeological cultures across Central Asia and southern Siberia, often discussed alongside Sintashta in connection with Indo-Iranian dispersal.
Chariot
A light, horse-drawn, spoked-wheel vehicle, some of the earliest secure evidence for which comes from Sintashta-Petrovka burial contexts around the turn of the second millennium BCE.
Bactria-Margiana Archaeological Complex (BMAC)
An urbanized oasis civilization in Bronze Age Central Asia, also called the Oxus Civilization, whose interaction with steppe populations is central to debates over Indo-Iranian formation.
Oxus Civilization
An alternate name for the Bactria-Margiana Archaeological Complex, derived from the ancient name of the Amu Darya river.
Mitanni
A mid-second-millennium BCE kingdom in northern Mesopotamia and Syria whose ruling or specialist elite retained certain Indo-Aryan names and technical terms, providing rare indirect evidence for early Indo-Aryan mobility.
Rigveda
The oldest surviving text of the Indo-Aryan branch, composed in an early form of Sanskrit and transmitted orally for a long period before later written redaction.
Avesta
The collection of sacred texts of the religious tradition examined in Chapter 24, composed in Avestan, the oldest attested Iranian language, and preserving the oldest surviving connected Iranian-language material.
Avestan geography
The pattern of place names and geographic references found especially in the Young Avestan material, notably the Vidēvdād and relevant Yashts, which points toward eastern Iranian and Central Asian regions rather than the later Achaemenid southwest.
Western and Eastern Iranian
A conventional scholarly classification of later Iranian languages based on shared linguistic innovations, useful as an organizing tool but not to be projected rigidly onto the earliest attested Iranian dialect evidence.
Arya
An ancient Indo-Iranian ethnocultural, linguistic, and religious self-designation, attested in both early Iranian and early Indo-Aryan sources, sharply distinguished in this textbook from its later, illegitimate racial distortion as "Aryan."
Archaeogenetics
The scientific analysis of ancient DNA to reconstruct patterns of biological ancestry, population movement, and admixture among past populations.
Admixture
Genetic mixture between previously distinct populations, documented increasingly through archaeogenetic study of Bronze Age steppe and Central Asian populations.
Transhumance
The seasonal movement of herds and households between different pastures, a common feature of mobile pastoral economies such as those associated with steppe archaeological cultures.
Ethnolinguistic identity
A form of identity based on shared language and culture, distinct from genetic ancestry, political citizenship, or modern racial categories, a distinction central to this chapter's methodological cautions.
Migration model
A scholarly framework proposing how and by what routes a population moved historically; this chapter favors multi-route, gradual migration models over single-corridor models for Iranian dispersal.
Linguistic paleontology
The method of using reconstructed vocabulary to infer aspects of the material culture, environment, or homeland of a reconstructed speech community, a method with genuine value but significant interpretive limitations.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has asked students to do something genuinely difficult: to take seriously a period of history for which no participant left a written word, and to build, from linguistic reconstruction, archaeological remains, and ancient DNA, a historically responsible account of where the Iranian-speaking peoples examined throughout the rest of this textbook ultimately came from. That account is necessarily more tentative, more heavily qualified, and more explicitly methodological than the narratives of later chapters, and that tentativeness is not a failure of this textbook but an honest reflection of what the evidence allows.

The habits of mind this chapter has modeled, distinguishing correlation from identification, refusing to equate language with archaeological culture or genetic ancestry, resisting single-cause explanations and single-route migration maps, and remaining alert to the historical misuse of terminology such as "Aryan," are not specific to Indo-Iranian studies alone. They are general tools of careful historical reasoning that students should bring to any encounter with prehistoric or proto-historic evidence, in any region of the world, for the remainder of their historical education.

This chapter has also established a substantive foundation that later chapters will build upon without needing to re-derive it. When Chapter 7 introduces the Medes and Persians as historically documented peoples, and when Chapter 8 examines their interaction with Assyria and Babylonia, students should carry forward an understanding that these peoples' Iranian language, and much of their cultural and religious inheritance, reflects a long, gradual, and only partially reconstructable prehistoric process, one involving steppe pastoralists, Central Asian oasis societies, gradual linguistic differentiation, and repeated population mixture, rather than a sudden arrival of a fully formed people onto an empty plateau.

The story this textbook tells continues, in Chapter 7, with the Medes, Persians, and related Iranian-speaking peoples as they finally become visible as named historical actors. That story will draw directly on the linguistic and cultural background this chapter has established, while turning, at last, to the kind of documented political history that written sources make possible. Understanding how historians build knowledge across the boundary between prehistory and history, carefully, cumulatively, and always attentive to the limits of their evidence, is what this chapter has been for.
