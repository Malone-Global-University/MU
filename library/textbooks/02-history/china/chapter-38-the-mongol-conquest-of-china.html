The Mongol Conquest of China

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

The Mongol conquest of China was not one invasion. It was a sequence of wars across nearly seventy years, fought against different states, led by different Mongol rulers, and carried out increasingly with the soldiers, engineers, ships, and administrators of the societies being conquered.

In the spring of 1279, in a narrow channel off the Guangdong coast near a place called Yamen, a fleet carrying the last Song claimant to the throne was destroyed by a force whose commander was a Han Chinese general serving the Mongols. The child emperor, aged about seven, is traditionally said to have died in the water, carried by a loyal minister. The scene is among the most remembered in Chinese history, and it is easy to read it as the final chapter of a single, unstoppable advance. This chapter argues against that reading. The road to Yamen began more than sixty years earlier on the grasslands of Mongolia and passed through the burning of northern cities, the destruction of the Jurchen Jin dynasty, a succession war among Mongol princes, a five-year siege on the Han River, and a naval war on the Yangtze that the Mongols were not originally equipped to fight.

This chapter studies that process. It begins with the political consolidation of the Mongols under Temüjin, later Chinggis Khan, and the military system that made Mongol armies unusually effective. It then separates two very different conquests. The conquest of the Jin dynasty in the north, completed in 1234, was a war against a mounted, fortified, bureaucratic state in the same northern plain where the Mongols could use their strengths. The conquest of the Southern Song, completed in 1279, was a war against a wealthy, densely populated, river-laced, navally capable state in a landscape that favored defenders. The two campaigns took different forms, were led by different people, and produced different outcomes for the societies they absorbed.

Why does this matter? The conquest ended the political order of the Song, the Jin, and, as earlier chapters showed, a long multi-state system in which no single regime controlled all of the lands once associated with Tang rule. The conquest ended the long political division between a northern Jin/Mongol sphere and the Southern Song and brought the northern and southern heartlands under one imperial government. The conquest also killed, displaced, and impoverished very large numbers of people, and it shaped the social world, the institutions, and the memory of the dynasty that followed. At the same time, it did not erase local elites, family structures, markets, religious communities, or administrative habits. Understanding what changed and what survived is the central task of the final sections of the chapter.

What continued? Villages kept farming. Lineages, temples, and schools survived in many regions, and many officials and local notables found ways to serve new masters. What changed? The political center moved north and east, military power passed to the Mongol ruling house and its allies, and the old division between a Jin north and a Song south disappeared. Who was affected? Everyone, but not equally: peasants in contested zones, townspeople in besieged cities, refugees fleeing south, Song soldiers and officials who chose between resistance and surrender, Han, Khitan, Jurchen, and Central Asian commanders who rose in Mongol service, and Mongol princes who fought each other for the throne.

The evidence is uneven and sometimes hostile. Mongol-era court records survive mostly through the Yuan shi, compiled under the Ming dynasty in 1370; the Secret History of the Mongols gives a Mongol-centered account of Chinggis Khan's rise; Song and Jin writers, Persian historians, and travelers such as Marco Polo preserve other views; and archaeology at siege sites, shipwrecks, and cities adds evidence that texts cannot. Each source has its own purposes and blind spots, and the chapter points them out as it goes.

This chapter follows directly from Chapter 37, which showed a Southern Song state whose wealth, port cities, shipping, and maritime connections remained formidable despite the loss of the north. Chapter 38 asks how the Mongols overcame that resilient state, and why doing so took decades of warfare, adaptation, siegecraft, river and naval operations, and the resources of peoples already incorporated into the Mongol Empire. It stops where military conquest ends. How the conquered lands were governed is the subject of Chapter 39, on the Yuan dynasty.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Temüjin consolidated Mongol political power and how the Mongol military system was organized.
  2. Analyze why Mongol armies were effective without reducing their success to cavalry archery alone.
  3. Trace the major phases of the conquest of the Jin dynasty from 1211 to 1234.
  4. Evaluate the role of defection, local Han, Khitan, and Jurchen commanders, and conquered specialists in Mongol success.
  5. Describe the first decades of Mongol rule in North China, including registration, taxation, appanages, and competing administrative policies.
  6. Explain the succession struggle between Khubilai and Ariq Böke and its significance for the conquest of the Southern Song.
  7. Compare the conquest of the Jin with the conquest of the Southern Song as distinct military and logistical problems.
  8. Assess the importance of Xiangyang, the Han and Yangtze waterways, and naval warfare in the final conquest.
  9. Distinguish between well-supported claims and uncertain ones regarding siege technology, casualties, and population loss.
  10. Analyze the human consequences of conquest for civilians, soldiers, officials, and local elites in different regions.
  11. Contextualize the conquest of China within the wider Mongol Eurasian empire.
  12. Identify what survived the conquest and what was transformed, avoiding both total-destruction and painless-succession narratives.

Historical Context

By 1200, the East Asian political world was divided among several states, as Chapters 35 and 36 described. The Jurchen Jin dynasty, founded in the early twelfth century, ruled the North China Plain, Shandong, much of Shaanxi and Shanxi, and a Manchurian homeland, from a central capital at Zhongdu (near modern Beijing) and a southern capital at Kaifeng. Its population, registered at about 53.5 million in the census of 1207, was largely Han Chinese farmers governed by a Jurchen military elite, with a bureaucracy that used examinations, written Chinese and Jurchen scripts, and a land-grant military system. The Southern Song, based at Lin'an (Hangzhou), ruled the Yangtze basin, the southeast, Sichuan, and the southern coast. The Tangut Western Xia dominated the Ordos and northwest, and a Tibetan, Dali, and many other polities lay to the southwest. These states had fought each other and negotiated for generations, and none expected a pastoral confederation from beyond the Gobi to alter their world.

North of the Jin lay the steppe, home to Mongolic and Turkic-speaking pastoral peoples: Mongols, Tatars, Kereyids, Naimans, Merkits, Önggüts, and others. These were not a single people. They were rival confederations led by kin-based aristocracies, bound by alliances and feuds, and tied to the settled world by trade in grain, textiles, and metal goods. For decades the Jin had managed the steppe by diplomacy, subsidies, and punitive raids, setting groups against each other. This policy worked until a leader emerged who ended the rivalry by absorbing the competitors.

The geography of the conquest matters. The North China Plain is open, with great walled cities and a dense network of roads and rivers; its northern edge is protected by the Yan mountains, passes, and the old frontier works. The Han River valley in Hubei links the Central Plains to the middle Yangtze by way of the twin cities of Xiangyang and Fancheng. The Yangtze, the Huai, and a web of canals, lakes, and rice paddies lie south of the Huai. These landscapes made war in the south slower, wetter, and more reliant on boats and fortified positions. Climate also mattered: northern horses suffered in humid, disease-ridden southern summers, and Mongol commanders regularly pulled forces back to avoid them.

The Song state at this time was not weak by every measure. Chapters 32 and 37 showed that its commerce, taxation, paper currency, ports, and shipyards sustained a large treasury and a navy on the Yangtze and the coast. Its weaknesses lay elsewhere: a long history of civilian control over generals that sometimes frustrated military initiative, financial strain from maintaining large armies, factional politics at court, and a reliance on frontier garrisons whose commanders could become semi-autonomous. The Mongols did not invade a collapsing state, and the Song did not expect to be conquered.

Finally, students should remember that "China" is not a safe label for the territory conquered. The Mongols fought a Jurchen dynasty with a Han majority population, a Han-led southern dynasty, a Tangut kingdom, and other states, as part of a larger project of Eurasian expansion that also encompassed Central Asia, Persia, Russia, and Eastern Europe. China was one theater in a world war fought on several fronts, and Mongol decisions about China were shaped by events as far away as Iraq and Hungary.

Historical Evidence

No single source provides a neutral account of the Mongol conquest. The Secret History of the Mongols, composed in Mongolian in the thirteenth century, gives a Mongol-centered narrative of Temüjin's rise, but says little about the details of Chinese campaigns and was shaped for the ruling house. The Yuan shi (History of the Yuan), compiled in 1369–70 by Ming scholars working quickly with Yuan court records, preserves biographies of commanders and the annals of reigns. It is indispensable but written by the dynasty that overthrew the Mongols, and it is selective, sometimes compressing the periods of conquest. Persian histories such as those by Juvayni and Rashid al-Din view the empire from the Ilkhanate and the Islamic world, and they contain valuable details on Mongol organization, but they rely on reports and may exaggerate casualties.

Contemporary Song and Jin sources include envoy reports such as Zhao Hong's account of a Song mission to the Mongols in the 1220s, notes by Jin officials, local gazetteers, and later biographies and funerary inscriptions of figures such as Wen Tianxiang. These preserve voices of resistance and loyalty, and they are often shaped by the moral frameworks of the Confucian tradition, which valued loyalty to a fallen dynasty. Epitaphs and stele inscriptions in the north record how local strongmen negotiated with Mongol commanders. Travelers' accounts, notably Marco Polo's, add outside perspectives but must be handled carefully, as discussed in section 38.8.

Archaeology adds an independent check. Excavations at Diaoyu Fortress, sunken shipwrecks off the south China coast, and urban sites show the scale of fortification, the technology of ships, and the destruction and reoccupation of cities. Household registers and census figures are essential for demography, but conquest-era registers are incomplete and politically shaped. For all of these, students should ask: who wrote this, for whom, how long after the events, and what would the author have wanted readers to believe?

Several named works are especially useful. Zhao Hong's report of a Song mission to the Mongols in the 1220s and Xu Ting's account of a Song embassy in the 1230s describe Mongol customs and armies from an outsider's view. Li Zhichang's record of the Quanzhen master Qiu Chuji's journey to Chinggis Khan describes the steppe and Central Asia. The physician Li Gao's medical writings describe the Kaifeng epidemic from direct experience. Rashid al-Din's universal history, compiled in the Ilkhanate in the early fourteenth century, draws on Mongol court sources. Each reflects its author's position: envoys and monks needed to please or inform their sponsors, physicians recorded what they saw, and court historians shaped events to fit the dynasty's interests.

Section 38.1

The Rise of Chinggis Khan and the Mongol Empire

Temüjin was born, probably in the early 1160s (sources and scholars disagree on the exact year), into a lesser aristocratic Mongol lineage on the steppe near the Onon and Kherlen rivers. Our main account, the Secret History of the Mongols, presents a youth of poverty, kidnapping of his wife Börte, and shifting alliances. It is a heroic narrative and should not be treated as a transcript, but it conveys how steppe politics worked: power rested on personal followings, kinship, oaths of loyalty, and the ability to deliver victories and plunder. Temüjin defeated rival leaders, including former allies, over some two decades. Through the 1190s and early 1200s he overcame the Tatars, Kereyids, and Naimans. In 1206 an assembly of chiefs on the Onon proclaimed him Chinggis Khan, a title whose exact meaning remains debated.

The key to his success was less conquest itself than what he did with the people he defeated. Instead of leaving defeated confederations intact, he broke up the old tribal units and redistributed their members among new military formations that cut across kinship lines. He promoted able followers regardless of birth, created a personal guard, the keshig, that drew sons of commanders into his household, and placed loyal commanders over groups of households. A written script, adapted from the Uyghurs, was introduced for administration, and a legal and customary code, often called the yasa (the scholarship on how much of it existed as a formal text is divided), regulated discipline, hunting, and succession.

The new Mongol state was not a bureaucratic empire like the Jin or Song. It was a household-based military polity in which sons, brothers, and favored commanders held shares of people and pasture. This structure helps explain much of the conquest. It made rapid mobilization possible, but it also meant that every succession was a crisis, that princes held semi-independent forces, and that the empire tended to split into lineage-based domains. These features shaped the war against the Jin, the delay in attacking the Song, and the civil war of 1260–64.

Chinggis Khan's motives for attacking the Jin combined several elements. The Jin had long dominated the steppe by manipulating tribal rivalries and demanding tribute and submission. The wealth of the northern plain, with its cities, textiles, grain, metals, and craftsmen, was attractive. Alliances with disaffected groups on the Jin frontier, particularly the Khitans, who remembered Jurchen overthrow of the Liao, offered political opportunities. Plunder and tribute were also how a steppe ruler paid his followers. A history that stresses only a thirst for conquest, or only grievance, misses the way these motives reinforced each other.

Students should place these events in a Eurasian frame. After 1206, Chinggis Khan fought in several directions. He campaigned against Western Xia in 1209–10 and forced its ruler into submission (the Mongols did not finally destroy the Tangut state until 1227), attacked the Jin from 1211, and from 1219 led a massive campaign against the Khwarazmian Empire in Central Asia, an operation that devastated cities such as Bukhara, Samarkand, and Nishapur. Chinese warfare was therefore often a secondary front, managed by subordinates such as Muqali, while the Great Khan was elsewhere. After his death in 1227, the empire was partitioned among his sons' lines, and his successors, beginning with Ögödei, directed campaigns against the Jin, Song, Korea, Russia, Eastern Europe, and Persia at once.

The Jin lacked the means to hold the steppe, and its policy depended on keeping steppe leaders divided. Jin rulers conferred titles and gifts on friendly leaders, backed some against others (the Jin and Temüjin cooperated against the Tatars in the 1190s), and mounted periodic expeditions to thin the nomads' herds and numbers. Jin authorities also built border ramparts and ditches and maintained garrisons to deter raids. Chinggis Khan inherited both the memory of this domination and the knowledge it gave him of Jin frontier defenses.

Reporting on Mongol origins relies heavily on the Secret History, which dates his rivals' defeats loosely: the Naimans in 1204 and the Merkits in 1205 appear in the sequence that culminated in 1206. A later tradition, reported in Mongol-aligned and Chinese sources, says that in 1210 he refused to submit to the new Jin emperor. Whether or not the anecdote reproduces actual words, it reflects how the Mongols framed the war as a break with Jin overlordship.

Section 38.2

Mongol Warfare and Expansion

It is tempting to explain Mongol success by the image of the horse archer, and mounted archery was indeed central. Steppe peoples grew up riding and hunting, and the composite bow, made of horn, wood, and sinew, was powerful and compact enough to use from the saddle. Each warrior typically traveled with several horses, which allowed long marches and rapid maneuvering. But these features were shared by many steppe armies defeated by the Mongols. What distinguished Mongol armies was organization, discipline, information, and adaptability.

Organization. The army was arranged on a decimal basis: units of ten (arban), one hundred (jaghun), one thousand (mingghan), and ten thousand (tümen), each with commanders who answered upward. In practice, the numbers were approximate and strengths varied. The system tied households to military service, cut across older tribal loyalties, and allowed planning and rotation. Discipline was enforced by collective responsibility, severe punishments for desertion or disobedience in battle, and by the prestige of the leader.

Intelligence and mobility. Mongol commanders gathered information from merchants, defectors, scouts, and prisoners, and used columns advancing on separate routes that converged on a target. A relay system of mounted couriers and stations, the yam, moved messages and officials. Armies used feints, false retreats, and ambushes. They also used the seasons: operations in the north avoided the heat of summer and exploited frozen rivers in winter.

Terror and negotiation. Mongol commanders offered cities the chance to surrender and be spared, with demands for submission, supplies, soldiers, and hostages. Cities that resisted and were stormed might be sacked, with inhabitants killed, enslaved, or forced into labor. This policy was calculated: a few destructions could encourage others to submit. Reputation therefore mattered, and Mongol-aligned authors sometimes magnified it. At the same time, selective mercy was also common, particularly for craftsmen, scribes, physicians, astrologers, and engineers, who were valuable and often transferred to Mongol service. The practice should not be reduced to indiscriminate slaughter, and it cannot be excused as merely strategic.

Siege adaptation. Steppe cavalry had little tradition of siege warfare, and early campaigns against walled northern cities struggled. The Mongols quickly learned. They recruited Khitan, Jurchen, Han, and Central Asian engineers, built catapults and ladders, used battering rams, mined walls, diverted rivers, forced local people to fill moats or carry earth, and used prisoners as shields and laborers. By the 1230s, Mongol armies besieged major fortified cities with confidence. Both sides used gunpowder weapons, such as fire lances, incendiaries, and iron-cased explosive bombs, in the Jin–Mongol war; these are discussed further in section 38.4 and in the Song technology of Chapter 33.

Recruitment of conquered people. The Mongol army was always more diverse than its popular image. As campaigns advanced, units of Khitans, Jurchens, Han Chinese, Turks, Tanguts, Uyghurs, and others joined, sometimes under their own commanders. By the time of the Song war, much of the Mongol force consisted of non-Mongol infantry and specialized troops. This flexibility is one of the central lessons of the chapter: the Mongol army that conquered the Song was not the same as the one that crossed the Gobi in 1211.

Finally, the sources warn against exaggeration. Armies of "hundreds of thousands" appear in some texts, but reliable counts are difficult. Opponents sometimes inflated Mongol numbers to explain defeat, and Mongol-aligned sources sometimes inflated the numbers killed. Students should treat figures cautiously and look for patterns of operation rather than relying on single numbers.

Logistics and remounts. A large mounted army needs grass and water. Mongol forces moved with herds of spare horses, sheep, and mares, and campaigns were timed to the availability of pasture. This limited how long and how far a large force could stay in one place, particularly in the wooded, irrigated, and heavily farmed landscapes of China, where fields reduced pasture and Mongol animals competed with farmers' needs. Armies often requisitioned grain and fodder from conquered districts, which added to the burden on civilians. When Mongol commanders judged that a region could not support their horses, they withdrew, which helps explain why many early conquests were raids rather than occupations.

Command and communication. Commanders coordinated columns through couriers, scouts, signals, and prearranged meeting points. Large hunts, in which lines of horsemen drove game toward a center, served as training for encirclement. Discipline in battle depended on subordinate officers' ability to act within the commander's plan without constant orders.

Surrender and administration. A city that submitted typically gave hostages, supplies, and troops, and accepted officials, often called darughachi, who supervised the local administration and census. The surrender policy was thus an administrative method as well as a military one. It created a path for local leaders to keep status, which is one reason defection spread, and it made the choice between resistance and submission a deeply local one.

Section 38.3

Campaigns in North China

Mongol attacks on the Jin began in 1211, after a period of raiding and negotiations. In that year a Mongol army entered Jin territory and defeated a large Jin force at Yehuling, in the area of present-day Zhangjiakou, Hebei, in a battle that destroyed much of the Jin field army. The Mongols then raided widely across Hebei, Shanxi, and Shandong in the following years, avoiding sieges of the strongest cities when they could and ravaging the countryside. Jin defenses were organized around garrison cities and frontier works, which could not stop highly mobile columns. Jin government also suffered from internal conflict: in 1213 a commander named Hushahu killed the reigning emperor and placed Xuanzong on the throne, an example of how pressure from outside intensified court instability.

Defection was a major factor. Khitans, who had formed the previous Liao dynasty and lived under Jin control, defected in significant numbers, and some Khitan leaders, including Yelü Liuge, aided or allied with the Mongols. Han Chinese commanders and local strongmen in Hebei and Shandong, such as Shi Tianze, Zhang Rou, Liu Heima, and Yan Shi, also submitted, often bringing their forces and territories. They did so for varied reasons: fear, local interests, resentment of Jin rule, the hope of gaining rank, or the judgment that the Jin could not protect them. Their defection helped the Mongols hold ground they could not otherwise have occupied. These men were not simply "collaborators" or "patriots." They were regional leaders making hard choices in a collapsing system, and they later became pillars of Mongol rule in the north.

In 1214 the Mongols besieged Zhongdu, and the Jin agreed to a settlement involving tribute, a princess, and goods. Shortly afterward, the Jin court moved its capital south to Kaifeng. The Mongols interpreted this as bad faith, returned, and captured Zhongdu in 1215. The city was sacked and burned, and sources describe widespread destruction. This fall effectively ended Jin control of the northeast and much of Hebei. Chinggis Khan then turned west and left operations in China to the commander Muqali, who in about 1217 was given authority over the conquered territories in North China. Muqali's forces, with large Khitan, Han, and other contingents, extended control over Hebei, Shanxi, Shandong, and parts of Shaanxi by his death in 1223.

Control during this phase was uneven. Mongol and allied forces held key cities and roads, while Jin troops, rebel bands, local militias, and self-defense groups held other areas. Cities changed hands repeatedly. A cohort of Han strongmen became semi-autonomous commanders under Mongol authority. In some regions, such as Shandong, a series of competing powers, including the "Red Coat" rebels, who resisted both the Jin and the Mongols, and local warlords, created a patchwork. In others, such as the Jin heartland around Kaifeng and Henan, the Jin government survived for two more decades.

For ordinary people, this phase was marked by flight, raiding, forced requisitions, and fear. Reports from Song envoys and Jin writers describe depopulated areas, ruined villages, and crowds of refugees heading south or into the mountains. Peasants moved into walled refuges or hill strongholds. Prisoners were taken as slaves or laborers, and some skilled people were moved far away. The Jin government itself added to the burden by increasing taxes and conscripting soldiers to meet the threat, and by issuing inflated paper currency. In the period 1217–1224 the Jin also fought the Song to the south, a war that weakened both and demonstrated how the Jin tried to compensate for lost territory in the north.

Regional detail shows how varied this phase was. Hebei and the northeast came under Mongol-aligned commanders earliest, including Khitan officers and the Han leaders Shi Tianze and Liu Heima. In Shandong, the picture was more fragmented. Local strongmen and the "Red Coat" bands, who had risen against Jin rule, shifted allegiances; one of their leaders, Li Quan, moved among Jin, Song, and Mongol sides before submitting to the Mongols, and his son Li Tan's revolt in 1262 (section 38.6) shows how long such houses retained their local power. Yan Shi of Dongping submitted in 1219 and held a semi-autonomous domain. Shanxi and Shaanxi saw repeated fighting among Jin garrisons, local forces, and Mongol detachments.

The Jin response included moving large numbers of military households south of the Yellow River after 1214, which concentrated defenders in Henan while leaving the north to be contested by others. It also raised problems of supply and trust, since the Jin leadership distrusted its Khitan and Han subjects. For villagers, the shift of the court meant that Jin protection receded, and local armed leaders became the main source of order, tax collection, and protection.

Section 38.4

The Destruction of the Jin Dynasty

After Chinggis Khan destroyed Western Xia and died in 1227, Mongol attention returned to the Jin. Ögödei, elected Great Khan in 1229, launched a coordinated campaign beginning in 1230. His strategy was to avoid a direct assault on the Jin defenses along the Yellow River and the Tong Pass, and instead to send a force through the territory of the Southern Song, through the Hanzhong region and the Han River valley, to approach Henan from the southwest. The Mongols requested passage through Southern Song territory, but the Song court refused. Tolui nevertheless forced his army through Song territory in late 1231, raiding and provisioning as it moved before reentering Jin territory from the southwest. The episode shows how the Mongols used logistics and coercion as tools of war, and it means that armed Mongol–Song friction predated the invasions of 1235.

In January 1232 a Mongol army defeated a major Jin force at Sanfengshan in Henan, destroying the Jin's best remaining field army. The Mongols, under Sübötei, then besieged Kaifeng, the Jin capital. The defenders of Kaifeng used gunpowder weapons, notably iron bombs, described in sources as "thunder-crash bombs," and fire lances, with some effect. The Mongols responded with their own engines, and with attacks on the countryside to cut supplies. A severe epidemic broke out in the overcrowded city. The physician Li Gao reported more than a thousand deaths per day for nearly three months at its height. Some modern historians have identified this outbreak with plague, but the diagnosis remains debated. Emperor Aizong left Kaifeng in 1233 and fled north and then south, ultimately to Caizhou; the city surrendered later that year after a coup by a Jin commander. Notably, the surrender of Kaifeng involved negotiation rather than the massacre that resistance might have brought, and later tradition credits the Khitan official Yelü Chucai with arguing against the destruction of its people and craftsmen. That story reflects a real policy debate but may be embellished.

At Caizhou, Aizong faced a combined attack. The Song, whose state had lost its northern territories to the Jin in 1127 and which had been forced to pay tribute and, at times, fight them, agreed to join the Mongols. Song armies under Meng Gong supplied troops and grain, and in early 1234 the two forces took Caizhou. Aizong died, traditionally by suicide, as the city fell, and a final claimant was killed. The Jin dynasty ended.

The Song decision is among the most debated of the era. From a Song perspective, joining the Mongols was a chance to avenge the humiliation of 1127 and to recover Henan, including the old capitals of Kaifeng and Luoyang. The alliance appeared to offer a favorable bargain. In retrospect it destroyed a buffer state that had absorbed the Mongol attack, leaving the Song borders directly exposed. Some Song officials warned of this at the time, citing the old saying that when the lips are gone, the teeth are cold. Other Song leaders were more optimistic, and some Mongol statements seemed to promise territory in Henan. Judging the decision requires remembering that the Song did not know the Mongols were about to attack them, and that diplomacy with powerful northern neighbors had a long history of both alliance and betrayal. Chapter 36's discussion of the Song–Jin alliances against the Liao and the result in 1127 offers a parallel.

The consequences came swiftly. In the summer of 1234, a Song force advanced to retake Kaifeng, Luoyang, and Nanjing (Guide) in an operation that ended in hunger, disease, and defeat when Mongol forces counterattacked. Mongol leaders cited the incursion as a violation of an agreement, and in 1235 they launched their first large-scale invasion of Song territory. The war that began then would last, with intervals, for over four decades. The destruction of the Jin was, in this sense, the first stage of a longer conquest, not its completion.

The campaign around Kaifeng illustrates the interplay of siege, politics, and logistics. Sources report that the Jin killed Mongol envoys sent to negotiate in 1232, which hardened Mongol commanders' attitudes. Inside the city, food, fuel, and housing were strained by refugees; the Jin court at times depended on contributions from wealthy residents and conscription of urban populations. After the epidemic and the coup of early 1233, in which a Jin officer, Cui Li, handed the city over to the Mongols, the surrender was negotiated rather than stormed. Tradition credits Yelü Chucai with arguing against a massacre; whatever his role, the fact that the city's population and craftsmen were largely preserved reflects a pragmatic Mongol calculation.

The Song's decision to join the attack on Caizhou should also be seen in this setting. Song sources report that the Song supplied tens of thousands of troops and large quantities of grain (figures vary), and that Mongol negotiators indicated that Henan could pass to the Song; the reliability of those understandings is uncertain. The Song hoped to regain territory and wipe out the memory of 1127, and some officials believed that cooperation could secure a new northern frontier. In hindsight, the destruction of the Jin removed the buffer, but at the time it appeared to be a rare chance to reverse a long humiliation.

Section 38.5

Mongol Rule in North China and the Wider Eurasian Empire

After 1234 the Mongols held most of northern China, but they had no ready-made system for governing it. The Mongol state was built to organize pastoral households and armies, not millions of tax-paying farmers. Over the following years they experimented, and the result was a mixture of approaches that varied by region and over time.

Registration and taxation. In 1235–36, under Ögödei, a census of households was carried out in North China. Yelü Chucai, who had served Chinggis Khan and Ögödei, argued for a system of regular taxes on households and land, with offices to collect them, as a more reliable source of revenue than plunder. Jin registers had listed about 53.5 million people in 1207; Mongol-era counts recorded far fewer, a gap that reflects deaths and flight but also concealment, incomplete registration, and the movement of people into the households of powerful patrons. Historians do not treat the difference as a precise measure of loss.

Competing policies. Traditional accounts describe a debate at court: whether to treat the north as a source of plunder and pasture, or as a taxable agricultural society. A famous anecdote has Yelü Chucai persuading Ögödei by showing how much revenue could be raised from the Chinese population. This story appears in the Yuan shi and should be read as a moral tale, though it reflects a genuine tension. In 1239 a Central Asian Muslim merchant named Abd al-Rahman won the right to farm taxes in North China, offering higher revenues in exchange for a contract; such tax farming, common in the Mongol empire, often led to heavy exactions, and the system aroused criticism. Officials later reformed these practices under Möngke and Khubilai.

Appanages and military households. In 1236 Ögödei allocated households and territories in North China to Mongol princes, commanders, and nobles as appanages. These holders had rights to revenues and sometimes appointed officials, producing a layered system in which central authority competed with princely interests. Military households, drawn from the Han and other populations, were registered to provide soldiers and were exempt from some other burdens. Artisans were registered separately and sent to work in state workshops. Ordinary peasant households carried tax and labor obligations and could be assigned to the support of princes, commanders, or temples.

Local power. Mongol officials were few. In the early decades, local government rested heavily on Han strongmen with armies and territory, such as Shi Tianze in Hebei and Yan Shi in Shandong. These houses passed positions to sons and ran largely autonomous domains. Their services included restoring order, rebuilding towns, protecting scholars and temples, and raising troops. They also exploited their position, acquiring land and labor. Meanwhile, other individuals, such as the Khitan-descended Yelü family, Jurchen officials, and scholars who survived the wars, found places as advisors and clerks. Confucian scholars, many of them displaced from Jin service, tried to persuade Mongol rulers to adopt Chinese institutions, with mixed results.

Religion and culture. The Mongols were generally tolerant of religious communities, and many received exemption from taxes and labor. The Quanzhen Daoist leader Qiu Chuji (Changchun) traveled across Eurasia to meet Chinggis Khan in 1222–24 and his order gained significant influence in the north, a source of later conflict with Buddhists. Buddhist monks, Muslim and Christian communities, and Confucian scholars also negotiated their status. This pattern prefigures the multi-religious landscape discussed in Chapter 39.

Reconstruction and devastation. The early decades of Mongol rule were not simply ruin. Some Han commanders rebuilt walled towns and encouraged resettlement, markets reopened, and tax registers gradually expanded. But recovery was uneven, and wars continued against the Song in the 1230s and 1250s. Many areas, particularly those near the Yellow River, remained depopulated or insecure for years. Historians increasingly emphasize regional differences: Shandong under Han warlords, Shanxi, which suffered less, and Henan and Shaanxi, which suffered more.

A larger empire. North China was part of a larger empire, and conditions there depended on decisions made in Mongolia and elsewhere. Ögödei built a capital at Karakorum in Mongolia. Revenues from China helped pay for the empire's armies in the West, and decisions about the region were shaped by rivalries among princes. After Ögödei's death in 1241, a period of regency and weak authority followed until Möngke's election in 1251. Möngke centralized the empire's finances, ordered new censuses, restricted abuses by princes, and entrusted his brother Khubilai with oversight of the Han lands. This brings us to the political question of 38.6.

Section 38.6

Khubilai and the Struggle for Mongol Power

Khubilai, born in 1215, was a grandson of Chinggis Khan and a son of Tolui. His mother, Sorghaghtani Beki, a Christian from the Kereyid people, managed the family's estates and encouraged her sons to learn about the societies they ruled. In 1251, when Möngke became Great Khan, Khubilai was assigned authority over the Han lands. He gathered advisors: Buddhist and Daoist monks such as Liu Bingzhong and Confucian-educated men like Yao Shu, Dou Mo, and Xu Heng, and he studied how to restore agricultural production and revenue in the north. His territories in Henan and Shaanxi were built up through colonies, farmland, and local officials, and he constructed a base at Kaiping in 1256, later known as Shangdu, north of the Great Wall.

Under Möngke, the Mongols adopted a broader strategy toward the Song. Rather than attacking only along the Huai and Yangtze, they sought to outflank the Song from the west. In 1253–54 Khubilai and the commander Uriyangqadai conquered the Dali kingdom in Yunnan, giving the Mongols a base on the Song's southwestern side. Möngke himself led a campaign into Sichuan in 1258, besieging fortified positions, among them Diaoyu Fortress near modern Chongqing. In 1259 he died there, from disease or wounds (sources disagree), which halted the campaign. Other forces, including those under Khubilai, were advancing in the middle Yangtze. Khubilai crossed the river and besieged Ezhou (near Wuchang) when news arrived.

The death of a Great Khan opened a succession crisis. Under Mongol custom, the heirs were chosen at a quriltai, but several candidates existed, and different princes and commanders had their own interests. Khubilai's younger brother Ariq Böke, who held the Mongol homeland, was proclaimed khan by an assembly at Karakorum. Khubilai, supported by his Han and Mongol commanders and his base in North China, had himself proclaimed khan at Kaiping in 1260. Two rival Great Khans existed. Khubilai negotiated a truce with the Song chief councilor Jia Sidao, who had been defending Ezhou, and withdrew north. The Song later portrayed this as a victory, though the terms were secret and Jia Sidao later held Khubilai's envoy, Hao Jing, in detention for many years, an episode that Khubilai remembered.

The war between Khubilai and Ariq Böke lasted until 1264. Khubilai controlled the revenues and manpower of North China, which he used to cut grain supplies to Mongolia, while Ariq Böke's resources were limited. In 1262 a Han warlord, Li Tan, rebelled in Shandong against Khubilai, who suppressed the rebellion and executed his father-in-law, the powerful minister Wang Wentong; the episode produced lasting caution among Khubilai's officials about the independent power of Han commanders and led to reforms that separated civil and military authority. In 1264 Ariq Böke surrendered. Khubilai's position was still contested by other Mongol rulers, notably Qaidu in Central Asia, whose resistance continued for decades, and by the Chinggisid khans in the west, who recognized the Great Khan's title less and less. These conflicts shaped how much attention and manpower Khubilai could devote to the Song.

Khubilai's victory mattered for the conquest in several ways. First, it secured his control of North China's economy, which supported the campaign. Second, it made his government dependent on Han and other non-Mongol advisors and officers, and committed him to a policy of restoring revenues in the agricultural north. Third, it created a ruler with a personal stake in a decisive victory over the Song, whose conquest would reinforce his claim to be the legitimate heir of Chinggis Khan and a universal ruler. The political bridge to Chapter 39 is that the same man who completed the conquest also founded the Yuan state, adopting the dynastic name in 1271 and moving his capital to Zhongdu, renamed Dadu in 1272.

Historical Vocabulary
Quriltai

A quriltai (also spelled khuriltai) was an assembly of Mongol princes, commanders, and notables that discussed major questions such as war, law, and the choice of a ruler. Temüjin's proclamation as Chinggis Khan in 1206 and Ögödei's election in 1229 took place at such gatherings. In theory, a quriltai gave consent to a new khan; in practice, attendance, timing, and outcomes were influenced by the power of candidates and their supporters. In 1260, rival assemblies at Karakorum and Kaiping each named a khan, showing that a quriltai could legitimate competing claims rather than settle them. Understanding this institution helps explain why succession was a recurring source of crisis in the Mongol Empire and why Khubilai's election was challenged.

Section 38.7

The Long War Against the Southern Song

The Mongols attacked the Song from 1235, using several columns: one into Sichuan, another toward the Han River and Xiangyang, and others along the Huai. They captured Chengdu in 1236 and ravaged Sichuan, one of the Song's richest regions, though they were unable to hold it. Over the next decades, raids and counterraids, sieges, truces, and negotiations alternated. Why did the Song survive so long?

Geography and terrain. South of the Huai, rivers, lakes, canals, and paddy fields hindered cavalry movement. Roads were limited, and armies depended on boats. Southern summers brought disease. The mountains of Sichuan and the Han River valley offered defensive positions that fortified cities and mountain strongholds exploited. Diaoyu Fortress, built on a high promontory with steep sides and strong walls, held out for decades after 1243 under reinforced defenses and became a symbol of resistance.

Resources and population. The Song ruled a large share of the empire's farmland and an even larger share of its commerce. Taxes, paper money, and the profits of maritime trade gave it the means to maintain armies, build fortifications, and construct ships. The population was far larger than that of Mongol-held North China, and the cities were large and well supplied. Conquering such a state required occupying and administering a populous region, not merely defeating an army.

Navy and defense in depth. As Chapter 37 showed, the Song had one of the most sophisticated navies in the world, including river and sea craft armed with trebuchets and fire weapons, supported by shipyards and ports. Its strategy emphasized defense at key river crossings and fortress cities, backed by garrison troops and local militias. Commanders who were successful, such as Meng Gong in the 1240s and Yu Jie in Sichuan in the 1250s, held the line for years.

Mongol constraints. The Mongols were stretched. In the 1240s, their armies were engaged in Europe, the Middle East, and Korea, and succession struggles absorbed energy. Ögödei's death in 1241 and the following regency reduced coordination. In the 1250s, Möngke's campaigns focused on a new approach, but his death in 1259 and the civil war delayed the campaign for a decade. Southern campaigns also posed logistical problems: supply lines were long, summers were deadly, and Mongol horses were poorly suited to the humid south.

Song politics. The Song court was not passive, but it was divided. Emperor Lizong (r. 1224–1264) and his successor Duzong (r. 1264–1274) were largely dominated by ministers. Jia Sidao, who rose through the favor of Lizong's consort and became chief councilor, held power in the 1260s and early 1270s. He is a controversial figure. Traditional histories, written under the Yuan and Ming, blame him as a corrupt and arrogant favorite who hid bad news from the emperor, and he was involved in the 1263 "public land purchase" policy, which bought up large estates to supply military rations and aroused opposition from landowners. Modern historians often see the policy as an attempt to solve a genuine fiscal problem, and they caution that the later portrayal of Jia as the cause of the fall exaggerates one man's role. Factionalism, mistrust between civil officials and military commanders, and the strain of paying for frontier armies all weakened the response.

Collaboration and resistance. As the war lengthened, Song commanders and officials made choices. Some resisted to the death; some surrendered when their situation was hopeless, or when they felt betrayed by the court; and some defected earlier, bringing troops and local knowledge. A notable example is Liu Zheng, a Song general in Sichuan who defected to the Mongols and advised them on attacking Xiangyang and developing naval forces. Later, Lü Wenhuan surrendered Xiangyang in 1273 and entered Mongol service, providing another important example of how former Song commanders contributed to the conquest. These choices were shaped by local circumstances, ties to commanders, the behavior of court officials, and personal calculation. Over time, the Mongols incorporated Song soldiers into the xinfujun, "newly submitted army," and used them in the final campaigns.

Historical Vocabulary
Hanjun and Xinfujun

Hanjun (漢軍, "Han army") refers to units of northern Chinese soldiers, often organized as hereditary military households, that served the Mongols after the conquest of the Jin. Xinfujun (新附軍, "newly submitted army") refers to Song soldiers who surrendered and were incorporated after 1276. These categories show how the Mongols turned conquered populations into manpower and how this affected the conquest itself: Mongol armies attacking the Song were composed substantially of Han infantry, engineers, and naval crews. In later Yuan administration the terms had legal and social weight, as Chapter 39 explains. The names are not ethnic labels in the modern sense; they reflect the Mongol practice of classifying people by when and how they submitted.

Section 38.8

Xiangyang, Naval Warfare, and the Final Conquest

The siege of Xiangyang and Fancheng (1268–1273). Xiangyang, on the south bank of the Han River, and Fancheng, on the north bank, formed a paired fortress guarding the route from the Central Plains to the middle Yangtze. The cities were heavily fortified, stocked with supplies, and linked by a floating bridge and river craft. Whoever held them controlled the Han River corridor and the approach to Wuchang and the lower Yangtze. Liu Zheng argued that taking them would open the Song's central defenses, and the Mongol command under Aju and Liu Zheng decided to besiege them rather than bypass them. Beginning in 1268, they built fortified lines around the cities, cut land access, and blockaded the river with ships. The commander of Xiangyang, Lü Wenhuan, a member of the family of the earlier Song frontier commander Lü Wende (who died around 1269–70, before the siege ended), held out for five years.

The Song tried to relieve the siege. Boat convoys broke the blockade in 1272, led by commanders Zhang Shun and Zhang Gui, who ran supplies through at night; Zhang Shun was killed. Other relief attempts failed. The Mongols tightened their grip with a stronger navy, and in early 1273 they stormed Fancheng; the following months saw Xiangyang isolated. Its commander surrendered in the spring of 1273, after negotiations that spared the garrison. The Song court had not been able to rescue the defenders, a failure that damaged morale and demonstrated the limits of its central command.

Technology. The siege is often associated with the arrival of counterweight trebuchets from West Asia. The Yuan shi biographies of two engineers, Ismail and Ala al-Din, state that these West Asian Muslim engineers were sent from Abaqa's Ilkhanid realm, and that the Mongols built large catapults capable of destroying defenses at Xiangyang, whose fall came soon after they were used. Scholars generally accept the main outline, while noting uncertainty about details of date and effect. The story also shows how the Mongol empire moved expertise across Eurasia: the engineers were requested around 1271 and were operational by the final phase of the siege in 1272–73. Marco Polo's later claim that he and his father and uncle built the siege engines cannot be correct, since the siege ended before their arrival in China (see the Primary Source and Debate features below). The Song had sophisticated siege technology of its own, including trebuchets, gunpowder weapons, and fortifications, and Xiangyang's fall owed as much to the blockade and exhaustion of supplies as to any single machine.

Building a navy. Xiangyang also demonstrated that Mongol success in the south depended on naval capability. Liu Zheng and other advisors proposed building a fleet and training crews. A later Chinese historical compilation reports 5,000 ships and 70,000 trained naval personnel; such large premodern figures should be treated cautiously, but they indicate the scale attributed to the buildup. Crews were drawn from northern and southern populations with experience in river and sea transport. Shipyards were set up along the Han and Yangtze, and defectors from the Song navy provided sailors, pilots, and knowledge of local waters. By the early 1270s, the Mongols had a force capable of fighting on the Yangtze. This was a significant change: an army that had begun as steppe cavalry now included large fleets crewed by its former enemies. The Song's earlier maritime and naval strengths, described in Chapter 37, were not enough to compensate for poor coordination and the defection of experienced commanders.

Historical Vocabulary
Counterweight Trebuchet

A counterweight trebuchet is a siege engine that uses a heavy weight on one end of a beam to hurl a projectile from a sling on the other. It could throw heavier stones than the older human-powered or traction trebuchets that were common in East Asia, and could do so with more accuracy. In Chinese sources such engines are often called huihui pao ("Muslim catapults") or xiyu pao ("western region catapults"), reflecting their perceived origin. Their use at Xiangyang is one of the best-known examples of technology transfer within the Mongol Empire, though historians debate how decisive it was relative to blockade and starvation.

The Yangtze campaign (1274–1276). In 1274 Khubilai appointed Bayan of the Baarin to lead the main army, assisted by Aju. The timing was favorable: the Song emperor Duzong died in 1274 and was succeeded by a child, Gongdi, with the Empress Dowager Xie and ministers governing. Bayan advanced down the Han River and into the Yangtze, taking Hanyang and Wuchang. Many Song commanders and garrisons surrendered, and the Song fleet's resistance weakened. In early 1275 Jia Sidao took the field in person with a large army and fleet, but his force was routed at Dingjiazhou on the Yangtze in the spring of 1275. Jia was dismissed and exiled, and he was killed by an escort on the way to his place of banishment. The Mongols continued downriver, taking Jiankang (Nanjing) and other cities.

Resistance was not absent. Some cities held out and paid a heavy price. Changzhou, in the Lower Yangtze, resisted in late 1275 and was stormed with widespread killing, an event that Mongol commanders used to encourage surrender elsewhere. Yangzhou, defended by Li Tingzhi, held for months in 1275–76. Other commanders, including those at Chongqing and Diaoyu in Sichuan, continued to fight, and the last Sichuan strongholds did not surrender until 1279. The war for the south was therefore a patchwork of sieges, surrenders, and local decisions rather than a single drive.

The fall of Lin'an (1276). By early 1276 Mongol columns were converging on Lin'an. The Song chief ministers fled or resigned, and the court, led by the Empress Dowager Xie, negotiated. Wen Tianxiang, a scholar-official who had raised troops, was sent to negotiate with Bayan, who detained him; he later escaped. In February 1276, the court surrendered the city, the seals of state, and the child emperor Gongdi, along with members of the imperial family, who were sent north. Lin'an, a city of perhaps a million people, was not sacked, a decision that preserved its population, though its palaces and treasuries were taken and the court's holdings were seized. Gongdi was later given a Tibetan Buddhist education and became a monk, one of the more remarkable fates of a deposed ruler.

The loyalist courts and Yamen (1276–1279). Two other sons of Emperor Duzong, Zhao Shi and Zhao Bing, had escaped south. Loyalists proclaimed Zhao Shi emperor (Duanzong) at Fuzhou in June 1276, with Zhang Shijie, Lu Xiufu, and Chen Yizhong among the leaders. The court moved by sea, harried by Mongol forces. In Quanzhou, the maritime trade commissioner Pu Shougeng, a Sino-Muslim merchant-official from a family of foreign maritime background, closed the port to the loyalists and surrendered to the Mongols. Later accounts associate Pu and his supporters with the killing of large numbers of Song imperial clansmen in Quanzhou, traditionally about 3,000, although the sequence, responsibility, and details remain debated. The fleet moved down the coast, and Duanzong died in 1278. His half-brother Zhao Bing, about seven, was enthroned. Wen Tianxiang, who had continued to organize resistance in Jiangxi and Guangdong, was captured in late 1278. He refused to submit and was held in Dadu until his execution in 1283, and became one of the most celebrated figures of loyalty in Chinese memory.

The last battle took place at Yamen in early 1279 (traditionally dated to March 19). The Song fleet, which sources describe as large, anchored in a narrow channel with its ships chained together as a floating fortress. The Mongol force under Zhang Hongfan, a Han Chinese commander, blocked access to fresh water, attacked the fleet's flanks, and, at the decisive moment, broke the line. Defeat produced mass death. Lu Xiufu is traditionally said to have taken the young emperor into the sea, drowning with him. Zhang Shijie escaped but died in a storm soon afterward. The Southern Song was finished, and the Mongols controlled the entire territory.

Yuan shi biographies of Muslim siege engineers, and Marco Polo's claim

Creator: Ming-dynasty compilers of the Yuan shi (1369–70), drawing on Yuan court records; Marco Polo and his collaborator Rustichello da Pisa (c. 1298–99) · Type: Official dynastic history (biographies) and a travel narrative · Region: Xiangyang, Hubei, early 1270s

Context: The Yuan shi includes biographies of engineers who came to Khubilai's court from the Ilkhanate. It reflects a Mongol court interest in showing its ability to bring in outside expertise. Polo's account, though rich in other respects, conflicts with the chronology: the siege ended in 1273, and most scholars date the Polos' arrival at Khubilai's court to about 1275. Section 38.9

Destruction, Continuity, and Transformation

What did the conquest do? A fair assessment must avoid two errors: treating it as total annihilation, and treating it as a painless change of rulers. The evidence shows both severe destruction and substantial continuity, distributed unevenly across time and space.

Human loss. The north suffered the longest and most destructive wars. Large numbers of people died in sieges, massacres, famine, epidemics, and flight between 1211 and the 1240s. The south suffered less in some regions, but heavily in contested zones, especially Sichuan, the Huai region, and the middle Yangtze. Registers suggest steep declines in recorded population, but conquest-era registers are unreliable, as the Historical Debate below explains.

Historical Debate

How Large Was the Population Loss in the Mongol Conquest?

The question: The Jin census of 1207 recorded roughly 53.5 million people in the north. Registers from the early Mongol period and the Yuan census of 1290 show far lower numbers for the north, and the Song's registered population also declined after the 1220s. How much of the difference represents deaths?

Interpretation A — Catastrophic loss: Some scholars argue that war, massacre, famine, and disease killed a very large share of the population, with some estimates placing losses in the tens of millions. They point to descriptions of empty countryside, abandoned cities, and low registered figures, and to evidence of the destruction of irrigation and farmland.
Interpretation B — Large but overstated by registers: Others argue that registration failure explains much of the gap. People fled, hid from tax collectors, entered the households of powerful patrons or religious institutions, or were omitted from Mongol registers that used different categories. Moreover, Jin and Mongol registers differed in coverage and administrative categories, so their totals are not directly comparable. Some regions recovered quickly and population growth in the south continued. On this view, losses were severe but smaller than the raw figures suggest.

Evidence: Both views draw on census figures, local gazetteers, narrative sources, and archaeology. They differ in how they treat the reliability of the registers, how they read rhetorical descriptions of devastation, and whether they emphasize regional variation. Climatic and epidemic factors in the same period complicate attribution.

Why it matters: The answer shapes judgments about the scale of the conquest's impact, the extent of recovery under the Yuan, and comparisons with other crises.

Student task: What kinds of evidence would best distinguish actual deaths from changes in registration? How should a historian treat a source that describes a region as "empty"?

Cities and land. Zhongdu was burned in 1215 and later replaced by a new city, Dadu. Kaifeng, devastated by siege and plague, remained a diminished regional center. Cities that surrendered, such as Lin'an, kept much of their population and urban fabric, though they lost their political role. Farmland in contested zones was neglected or abandoned, irrigation works fell into disrepair, and animals and tools were requisitioned. Yet the Yangtze delta and much of the southeast escaped prolonged fighting, and their agricultural and commercial base survived.

Surrender and collaboration. The conquest relied on defection. Large numbers of Jin and Song commanders, officials, and soldiers served the Mongols, and entire units were incorporated. Motives varied. Some acted from fear or opportunism; some believed the dynasty had lost legitimacy; some saw service as a way to protect their communities. Confucian ideals of loyalty to a dynasty made such choices morally charged, and later writers honored martyrs such as Wen Tianxiang while criticizing defectors. Others refused to serve the new rulers and withdrew into private life, scholarship, or religion. Historians increasingly treat the full range of choices without reducing them to "traitor" or "patriot."

Elites and institutions. Local elites often survived. Landowning families, lineages, schools, Buddhist and Daoist monasteries, and merchants adjusted, especially in the south, where the Mongols took over the existing tax and administrative structures after 1276 and relied on Song officials for local administration. The Song's paper currency, tax registers, and commercial regulations were in part adopted. Some Song scholars continued to teach, write, and run academies, and the Neo-Confucian tradition described in Chapter 34 survived and later regained influence. Examinations were suspended for decades, closing a main route to office, a change that affected the career strategies of educated families.

Cause and Effect

Why the Southern Song Fell in 1276–1279

Causes

Long-term: loss of the north in 1127 and the later buffer role of the Jin; costly frontier armies and fiscal pressure; tension between civilian control and military initiative; landholding and tax disputes; factional court politics. Short-term: the 1234 alliance that destroyed the Jin; Mongol adoption of siege and naval techniques; defection of commanders and units; the accession of a child emperor in 1274.

Event or transformation

After the five-year siege of Xiangyang and Fancheng, Mongol forces moved down the Han and Yangtze, defeated the Song army at Dingjiazhou (1275), took the capital at Lin'an (1276), and destroyed the loyalist fleet at Yamen (1279).

Effects

End of the Song state; unification of north and south under Mongol rule; incorporation of Song soldiers, officials, and shipbuilders into Mongol service; loss of life and displacement in contested zones; survival of many local elites and institutions.

Historical complexity

The Song resisted for decades and might have held longer had Xiangyang held, had the court responded more coherently, or had the Mongols remained divided; Mongol success depended on contingent events, including Möngke's death, Khubilai's victory, and the availability of defectors and engineers. A "lost Mandate" narrative cannot replace this analysis.
Multiple Perspectives

The Surrender of Lin'an, 1276

Note: these perspectives are reconstructed from patterns in the sources, not quotations from named individuals.

The Song court: The Empress Dowager and ministers faced a hopeless military situation. Surrender promised to protect the city and the imperial family, but it also meant the end of the dynasty. Contemporary and later writers judged this decision differently, with some seeing prudence and others abdication.
Loyalist officials and soldiers: Figures such as Wen Tianxiang and Zhang Shijie saw surrender of the court as no reason to stop fighting. They rallied the remaining princes, and they paid with their lives. Their fidelity became a moral standard for later generations.
Mongol and allied commanders: For Bayan and his officers, the capital's capture without a sack was a strategic success, preserving revenues and artisans. Many of their troops and sailors were Han Chinese, who had their own reasons for serving.
Townspeople and merchants of Lin'an: For many residents the immediate concern was safety, food, and property. The city's survival without a sack spared them the fate of Changzhou, but they lost the court that had supported their trade and employment.
Continuity and Change Over Time

North and South Under Conquest

What changed?

  • Political authority passed to a Mongol ruling house, and the Jin/Song division ended
  • Military power relied on Mongol commanders and non-Mongol allies; many people were reclassified as soldiers, artisans, or dependents
  • Examinations were suspended, disrupting routes to office
  • Large areas of the north and Sichuan were depopulated or reorganized

What stayed the same?

  • Peasant farming, household production, and village life in most regions
  • Local elites, lineages, temples, and academies, especially in the south
  • Taxation of land and households, paper currency, and many administrative practices
  • Commercial networks and ports

Who experienced change most directly?

Residents of besieged cities, northern and Sichuan peasants, Song and Jin officials and examination candidates, and soldiers on both sides.

Who experienced continuity?

Many southern villagers, landholding families, and merchants in cities that surrendered, such as Lin'an.

Why the pattern matters

The mixture of rupture and survival explains why the Yuan state could rely on existing institutions while remaining a very different regime.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living Through Conquest

For peasants in northern Hebei and Shandong in the 1210s and 1220s, conquest meant repeated passage of armies that requisitioned grain and animals, burned villages, and carried off people. Families fled to walled enclaves or hill refuges, or joined local militias and bandit groups. Women and children were at particular risk of capture and enslavement, and many were separated from their families; some were sold or given to commanders. Skilled artisans, physicians, and clerks were frequently moved to workshops and offices far from home, creating new communities in places such as Karakorum and Central Asia.

In besieged cities, hunger, disease, and fire killed many noncombatants. Kaifeng's epidemic in 1232–33 is among the best-documented examples. In the south, the war was a series of local disasters rather than a single catastrophe. In Sichuan, repeated invasions, famine, and flight reduced the population and agricultural base. In the Lower Yangtze, most cities surrendered, and daily life largely continued under new officials. Refugees from the north had been arriving in the south for decades, and after 1276 some southerners fled further into Fujian, Guangdong, and overseas. After the war, taxation, soldier registration, and forced labor placed new burdens on households, while some people benefited as soldiers, officials, or merchants. Evidence for these experiences comes mainly from elite writers, official records, and archaeology, so the voices of ordinary people are often indirect.

Chapter Review and Mongol Conquest Map Study

This chapter has traced a conquest that unfolded in stages: the creation of the Mongol state and army; the wars against the Jin from 1211 to 1234; the establishment of Mongol authority in the north; the succession struggle that brought Khubilai to power; and the war against the Southern Song from 1235 to 1279, culminating in the siege of Xiangyang, the Yangtze campaign, the fall of Lin'an, and the final battle at Yamen. The Jin and Song wars were different problems, and the Mongol army changed greatly between them. The conquest depended on defection, conquered specialists, and Han, Khitan, Jurchen, Central Asian, and Muslim contributions, and it imposed heavy costs on civilians, though not uniformly.

Regional and Frontier Context

Conquest differed by region. In Shandong and Hebei, Han strongmen mediated between Mongol commanders and local society. In Shaanxi and Gansu, the end of Western Xia and prolonged war left deep marks. Sichuan was devastated by decades of campaigning and fortress warfare. Yunnan's Dali kingdom, a Buddhist state with its own ruling house, was incorporated in the 1250s with continuity among local rulers. Tibetan lamas, particularly of the Sakya order, established ties with the Mongol court during this period, with consequences taken up in Chapter 39. The Lower Yangtze, with its large cities, largely surrendered. Fujian and Guangdong, the base of maritime trade, saw conflict between loyalists and Mongol-aligned officials, including Pu Shougeng and Muslim merchants. Frontier peoples in the southwest, the Khitan and Jurchen communities of the northeast, and Korean and Tangut populations experienced the Mongol Empire in their own ways, not simply as an extension of Chinese history.

China and the Wider World

The conquest of China took place within a Eurasian empire. Mongol forces that fought the Jin were also fighting in Central Asia, Persia, and Russia. Engineers and specialists traveled from West Asia to Hubei; Chinese engineers and physicians traveled westward. Korea, which the Mongols attacked repeatedly from the 1230s, became a subordinate ally. After 1274 and 1281, Khubilai launched naval expeditions against Japan, using Korean and Chinese resources, which failed; they demonstrate how the Mongol navy built for the Song war was redeployed. The Song, for its part, had been tied to world trade, and its fall disrupted southeastern ports, though maritime commerce continued under Mongol rule. Chinese diplomacy, technology, and migration were active elements, not only objects of foreign action. As the Yuan state emerged, Chapter 39 will show, its connections to the Ilkhanate, the Chagatai and Golden Horde, and the Indian Ocean world were central to its identity.

The Mongols had conquered the territories of the Jin and Southern Song, but military conquest did not itself answer how those territories would be governed. Chapter 39 turns from conquest to the Yuan dynasty and the problem of ruling a vast, socially diverse empire.

Chapter Summary

The Mongol conquest of China was a prolonged, multi-stage process, not a single invasion. It grew out of Chinggis Khan's consolidation of steppe peoples into a new military polity in 1206, a state organized for mobilization and conquest but dependent on kinship and succession politics.

Mongol military success rested on organization, discipline, intelligence, mobility, and a policy of combining terror with negotiated surrender. It did not rest on horse archery alone. Over time, the Mongols incorporated Khitan, Jurchen, Han, Central Asian, and Persian specialists, who provided siege engineering, administration, and later naval power.

The war against the Jin from 1211 to 1234 was fought largely on the northern plains. It destroyed Zhongdu, drove the court to Kaifeng, relied on Khitan and Han defections, and ended with the fall of Caizhou after a Song–Mongol alliance. That alliance helped to destroy a buffer state and exposed the Song to attack, though the Song could not have known the consequences.

In the north, the Mongols created systems of registration, taxation, appanages, and military households, often through Han strongmen and a few non-Mongol advisors such as Yelü Chucai. Debates over plunder versus taxation, the abuses of tax farming, and the autonomy of local commanders marked the early decades. Devastation and reconstruction coexisted, varying by region.

Khubilai's rise, after the death of Möngke in 1259 and the war against Ariq Böke, secured a North China base and linked his legitimacy to the conquest of the Song. Rival Mongol princes continued to challenge him, which affected the pace of operations.

The Southern Song was a harder target. Rivers, fortresses, dense population, large revenues, and a capable navy allowed it to resist for over four decades. Its weaknesses included fiscal strain, factional politics, and friction between civilian and military authorities. Collaboration and defection by Song commanders, together with Mongol adoption of naval power, shifted the balance.

The siege of Xiangyang and Fancheng from 1268 to 1273 was the decisive struggle for the Han River corridor. The use of imported West Asian engineering, a blockade, and the construction of a fleet illustrate Mongol adaptation. The Yangtze campaign of 1274–76, the defeat at Dingjiazhou, the destruction of Changzhou, and the surrender of Lin'an led to the collapse of Song central government.

Resistance continued under loyalist courts until the battle of Yamen in 1279, which ended organized opposition. The events involved a range of responses, from martyrdom to surrender to collaboration, and the choices of individuals such as Wen Tianxiang, Lü Wenhuan, Pu Shougeng, and Zhang Hongfan show the complexity of loyalty.

The human consequences were serious: deaths from siege, massacre, famine, and disease; displacement; enslavement; and ruined farmland. Yet scholars debate the scale of population loss, and regional variation was large. Elites, lineages, and institutions survived in many places, especially the south.

The conquest ended the Jin/Song division and brought the northern and southern heartlands under one imperial government, headed by a ruling house of steppe origin and drawing on resources throughout the Mongol world. It ended the multi-state system of the preceding centuries. How that state governed the conquered territories is the subject of Chapter 39.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows the end of one political world and the beginning of another. The multi-state order of the Song, Liao, Western Xia, and Jin ended with the Mongol conquest, and a single regime came to rule from Mongolia to the South China Sea. The conquest did not follow a script. It depended on contingent events, military adaptation, and the decisions of thousands of people whose motives ranged from loyalty to survival.

The conquest shaped later Chinese history in many ways. It determined the northern capital, the composition of the military, the status of examinations, the relation between Han elites and a non-Han ruling house, and the memory of loyalty and resistance that later writers invoked. It also demonstrated that Chinese states could be conquered by outsiders who learned quickly and used local resources, a theme that returns with the Manchus in Chapter 45.

The chapter also connects China to Eurasia. The war involved engineers from West Asia, Korean and Central Asian troops, and decisions made at assemblies far from the Yellow River. It shows that China's history cannot be separated from that of the steppe, Central Asia, and the Indian Ocean, and that conquered peoples were active participants in the process.

Understanding the conquest helps students see Chinese history as diverse, interconnected, contested, adaptive, and unfinished. Chapter 39 now turns to what the victors built: the Yuan dynasty, its institutions, its social categories, and its place in the Mongol world.
