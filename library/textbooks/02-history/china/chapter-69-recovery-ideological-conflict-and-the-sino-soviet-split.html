Part XI — Maoist China

Recovery, Ideological Conflict, and the Sino-Soviet Split

China: A History of a Civilization · Principal chronology: 1962–1965

Chapter Opening

In early 1962, thousands of officials gathered in Beijing to discuss a catastrophe that had killed millions of their fellow citizens. The question before them was not only how to feed the country again, but who would bear responsibility, what socialism ought to mean, and whether the Party could admit error without weakening itself.

From January 11 to February 7, 1962, roughly seven thousand Party cadres from the central government, provinces, prefectures, counties, major enterprises, and the army assembled in the capital. Their meeting, remembered as the Seven Thousand Cadres Conference, was unusually large and unusually candid for a Communist Party gathering. Delegates heard a report that attributed the disaster of the previous three years in substantial part to errors in policy and work, not merely to bad weather. Before the meeting ended, Mao Zedong himself had offered a self-criticism, though one framed within the limits of Party unity. How far such candor could go, and what would follow from it, shaped the politics of the following four years.

This chapter studies the period between the Great Leap Forward and the Cultural Revolution, roughly 1962 to 1965. It has three connected themes. The first is economic recovery: how a state that had presided over famine reduced unrealistic targets, reorganized the people's communes, restored limited incentives, imported grain, and rebuilt industry on more cautious foundations. The second is a struggle over political and ideological authority within the Chinese Communist Party (CCP), as leaders who agreed on the need for recovery disagreed about what recovery meant for socialism. The third is China's growing strategic independence, as the alliance with the Soviet Union collapsed into public rivalry, a border war with India was fought, a nuclear weapon was tested, and Beijing sought new partners in Asia, Africa, and Europe.

The period matters because it is easily misread in two opposite ways. One reading treats it as a calm interlude of pragmatism before the storm of 1966, in which sensible administrators repaired Mao's mistakes. Another treats it as a simple prelude in which every development pointed toward the Cultural Revolution. Neither is adequate. Recovery was real, measurable, and important to millions of households, yet it was uneven, and it left rural society and the Party altered by what had happened. Disagreements were real, yet Mao and his colleagues cooperated on many matters, and their positions shifted as events unfolded. The outbreak of the Cultural Revolution in 1966 was not inevitable in 1962. It followed from particular decisions made by particular leaders in response to particular pressures.

What changed during these years? The state abandoned the most extreme claims of the Great Leap: that willpower could substitute for material conditions, that communal kitchens and giant accounting units represented imminent communism, and that steel output could be raised by mass campaigns in village furnaces. Production teams, private plots, rural markets, technical specialists, and cautious investment returned to prominence. At the same time, Mao reasserted ideological priorities, warning of class struggle and capitalist restoration, and used the Socialist Education Movement to turn debates about rural corruption into a contest over who in the Party was reliably socialist.

What continued? The People's Republic remained a one-party state under a leadership that valued planning, collective ownership, and Party supremacy. Neither Liu Shaoqi, Deng Xiaoping, Chen Yun, nor Zhou Enlai advocated political pluralism; they wished to make the existing system work. The household registration system, grain procurement, and urban rationing continued to divide rural from urban citizens. The commune structure endured, in modified form, until the early 1980s. Mao remained chairman of the Party and retained the authority to shape ideology and launch campaigns.

Who was affected? Farmers recovering from hunger, urban workers sent back to villages, factory managers and engineers, rural cadres suspected of corruption or accused of ideological error, villagers pressed into public meetings, scientists building a weapon, Kazakh and Uyghur families crossing the Xinjiang border, soldiers on the Himalayan frontier, diplomats in African capitals, and students and writers in a culture increasingly subject to political criticism. The evidence historians use is correspondingly varied: Party directives and speeches, official statistics, provincial archives, memoirs of officials and villagers, published polemics, diplomatic records from Moscow, Beijing, New Delhi, and Washington, and oral histories. Each has limits, and the chapter will return repeatedly to the question of how much can be established and how much remains debated.

Finally, the chapter connects China's domestic history to the wider Cold War. The Sino-Soviet split divided the Communist world, altered the strategic calculations of the United States, and made China a rival claimant to leadership of revolutionary movements in the developing world. The nuclear test of October 1964 and the war with India in 1962 changed how neighbors and great powers regarded the People's Republic. These external developments cannot be separated from domestic politics, since both sides in the leadership debates interpreted international events as evidence for their views about revisionism, self-reliance, and vigilance.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  2. Describe the reorganization of the people's communes, including the production team, the Sixty Articles, and the role of private plots.
  3. Distinguish between indicators of recovery, such as national grain output, industrial production, food availability, and household welfare, and explain why they did not move together.
  4. Analyze the roles of Liu Shaoqi, Deng Xiaoping, Chen Yun, and Zhou Enlai in economic administration, and distinguish economic pragmatism from political liberalization.
  5. Assess Mao Zedong's position after the Great Leap, distinguishing reduced involvement in daily administration from loss of political authority.
  6. Trace the development of the Socialist Education Movement and the Four Cleanups from the Early Ten Points of 1963 to the Twenty-Three Articles of 1965.
  7. Interpret the vocabulary of class struggle, revisionism, and "taking the capitalist road" in its contemporary political context.
  8. Explain the stages of the Sino-Soviet split through ideology, security interests, economic relations, and competition for leadership.
  9. Evaluate competing interpretations of the Sino-Indian War of 1962 and the surrounding documentary uncertainties.
  10. Connect China's nuclear program, Third Front construction, and diplomacy in Asia and Africa to the search for strategic independence.
  11. Contextualize the precursors of the Cultural Revolution without treating its outbreak as inevitable.

  2. Why could the Party leadership acknowledge policy errors in 1962 yet remain unable to resolve who was responsible for them?
  3. In what ways did policies of readjustment depart from the Great Leap, and in what ways did they remain within the framework of socialist planning and collective ownership?

  5. How did a campaign aimed at rural corruption become a conflict over authority within the Party?
  6. Why did an alliance that had been central to Chinese security and industrialization in the 1950s become a source of rivalry?
  7. How did the experiences of 1962 to 1965 shape the strategic choices China made about nuclear weapons, defense, and diplomacy?
  8. What evidence allows historians to reconstruct debates within a secretive political system, and what are the limits of that evidence?

Historical Context

The People's Republic of China was founded in 1949 after decades of war and revolution (Chapters 64–66). During the early 1950s the new government carried out land reform, restored the economy, intervened in the Korean War, and, drawing on Soviet assistance, launched the First Five-Year Plan (Chapter 67). Agriculture was collectivized in stages between 1953 and 1956, and private industry and commerce were absorbed into state or joint ownership. In 1957, after the Hundred Flowers Movement, the Anti-Rightist Campaign punished hundreds of thousands of intellectuals, officials, and others who had voiced criticism or were subsequently classified as Rightists. These years bound the Party's prestige to rapid industrial growth and to the claim that China could pass quickly to a more advanced socialism.

In 1958 that claim produced the Great Leap Forward and the people's communes (Chapter 68). Giant communes combined local government, agricultural production, small industry, and communal dining. Inflated production reports, heavy grain procurement, diversion of labor into industry and water-conservancy projects, and the political danger of contradicting superiors produced a famine between 1959 and 1961. Estimates of excess deaths vary widely, commonly ranging from the high teens of millions to more than forty million, and the debate over scale and responsibility is addressed in Chapter 68. At the Lushan Conference of 1959, Marshal Peng Dehuai's criticism of the Leap led to his dismissal, which discouraged other leaders from speaking frankly for the next two years.

By 1960 the economy was in deep trouble. Grain output had fallen sharply, heavy industry contracted after being overextended, and the Soviet Union withdrew its technical advisers in the summer. During 1961 the leadership began a retreat: communal dining was loosened, the basic unit of ownership and accounting moved down from the commune toward smaller units, and the Ninth Plenum in January 1961 endorsed a policy of readjustment. Investigations of rural conditions conducted by Liu Shaoqi, Zhou Enlai, Chen Yun, Deng Xiaoping, and others in the spring of 1961 convinced many leaders that conditions were worse than official reports had suggested. These changes form the background of this chapter, which begins with the aftermath rather than retelling the famine.

Geographically, the period involved very different spaces. Recovery was lived in the grain-growing villages of the North China Plain and the Yangtze basin, in hard-hit provinces such as Anhui, Henan, Sichuan, Gansu, and Guizhou, and in industrial cities from Shenyang to Shanghai. The Sino-Soviet dispute and the war with India were conducted along frontiers in Xinjiang, Inner Mongolia, the northeast, Tibet, and the Himalayas, where communities with their own languages and religious traditions lived close to disputed lines. Strategic planning shifted industrial investment toward the mountainous interior of the southwest and northwest. The "China" described in this chapter was thus a state with a coastal industrial core, a vast rural majority, and contested borderlands, not a uniform national space.

Students should keep in mind the institutional structure. Mao Zedong was Chairman of the CCP Central Committee. Liu Shaoqi had been Chairman (head of state) of the People's Republic since April 1959, succeeding Mao in that state office. Zhou Enlai was Premier, Deng Xiaoping General Secretary of the Party Secretariat, and Chen Yun a senior economic planner. Lin Biao replaced Peng Dehuai as Minister of Defense in 1959. Policy was made in the Politburo and its Standing Committee, at central work conferences and plenums of the Central Committee, and through a bureaucracy of ministries, planning bodies, and provincial Party committees. Understanding these overlapping offices is essential for reading the leadership debates that follow.

Division I — The Aftermath of Famine and the Return to Economic Readjustment, 1961–1963
Section 69.1

China After the Great Famine

By 1961 the human and material damage of the Great Leap Forward was evident across much of the country, though its full scale would take decades to establish. Grain output had fallen well below its 1958 level; draft animals had been slaughtered or had died of neglect; farm tools had been melted or lost; trees had been cut for fuel; and irrigation works built in haste were in some places useless or harmful. Many villages had lost a substantial share of their working-age adults. The birth rate had fallen sharply and mortality had risen, leaving a visible gap in the population pyramid. Households that survived did so by eating bark, chaff, and wild plants, selling possessions, or leaving for other regions.

The state itself was depleted. Grain reserves had been drawn down, and exports of grain made in 1958–1960 to honor foreign commitments and earn foreign exchange had worsened shortages. Finances were strained by a large budget deficit and by Soviet debts incurred for industrial and military purchases. Factories lacked inputs, since agricultural raw materials such as cotton and oilseeds had declined along with grain. Statistical reporting was unreliable: inflated figures from 1958–1959 had corrupted the data on which planners relied, so one early task of recovery was simply to find out what was true.

Restoring confidence was a political as well as an economic problem. Farmers had learned that the state would take grain even when the harvest was poor, and that officials who reported honestly might be punished. Cadres had learned the opposite lessons at different moments: that overreporting was rewarded in 1958 and that candor was dangerous after Lushan. For the Party to govern effectively, it needed local officials who would report accurately and villagers who would work for the collective. Both depended on visible changes in policy that relieved immediate pressure on the countryside.

The recovery that followed was not a return to the situation of 1957. Rural society had been altered by communal dining, by the mobilization and exhaustion of labor, by deaths that removed members of many families, and by the memory of hunger. Relations between villagers and cadres were strained, and in some areas cadres who had enforced harsh procurement faced resentment or retaliation. At the level of the Party, leaders who had witnessed conditions firsthand or heard accounts from their home provinces carried those experiences into subsequent debates about what socialism required.

Section 69.2

The Seven Thousand Cadres Conference of 1962

The Seven Thousand Cadres Conference, held in Beijing from January 11 to February 7, 1962, was formally an enlarged central work conference. Its unusually large attendance, which extended beyond the Central Committee to county-level and enterprise leaders, was intended to carry the lessons of the previous three years down through the Party hierarchy. The leadership had already adopted readjustment measures during 1961; the conference's purpose was to secure agreement about the causes of the crisis and to encourage honest communication between levels of the Party.

Liu Shaoqi delivered a written report, circulated in draft and revised after discussion, which assessed the Great Leap period more critically than earlier official statements. In remarks to the assembly he reportedly argued that the difficulties had been caused in substantial part by errors in work, with natural disasters a lesser factor, a formulation sometimes summarized as a 30/70 division between natural and human causes. In his January 27 oral speech, Liu explicitly attributed the approximate 30/70 comparison to peasants he had interviewed in Hunan; he did not present the percentages as a precise national measurement. His published words should be distinguished from later summaries of them. What is clear is that the leadership accepted that policy mistakes, not only weather, had contributed to the crisis, and that this acknowledgment was extended to the provincial and local level.

Mao Zedong spoke on January 30 about democratic centralism and offered a self-criticism, accepting responsibility for errors while framing the issue as a collective one and emphasizing the need to learn from experience. Lin Biao, by contrast, praised Mao's leadership and argued that setbacks had arisen when his ideas were not followed, a line that anticipated later uses of Mao's authority. Zhou Enlai and Deng Xiaoping also spoke, and Deng supported the call for more open discussion within the Party. Some provincial leaders used the occasion to describe local conditions bluntly.

Historians such as Roderick MacFarquhar have argued that the conference was important because it translated reforms developed during 1961 into broader Party practice, and because it displayed both the possibility and limits of candor. The leadership could not easily go further, for several reasons. Mao's status as founder and chairman made it difficult to assign responsibility to him directly. The Party's legitimacy rested on its claim to lead socialist construction successfully. And some leaders worried that open discussion of errors might weaken discipline or encourage criticism of the system itself. The result was an acknowledgment of mistakes combined with continued emphasis on unity, a combination that left the question of responsibility unresolved.

Creator: Liu Shaoqi, on behalf of the Central Committee · Date: January 1962 · Type: Party conference report and accompanying oral remarks · Region: Beijing, addressed to cadres from across the country

Context: Drafted by a committee under Liu's direction after discussions among central leaders, and revised after delegates commented on the draft, the report was designed to be read to large audiences of officials. It was an internal Party document, not a public statement.

Paraphrase: the report reviewed the achievements and shortcomings of the preceding years, acknowledged serious errors in planning and in rural work, called for restoring democratic centralism and for honest reporting from lower to higher levels, and stated that experience gained at a heavy price should be used to correct policy.

Analysis: The report shows a leadership willing to say, within the Party, that policy had gone wrong. It reveals the vocabulary of acceptable criticism: mistakes were attributed to "shortcomings and errors in work" and to lack of experience rather than to the conception of the Leap itself. It was addressed to cadres, so it tells historians more about how the leadership wanted officials to understand the crisis than about how villagers experienced it. Villagers' voices are absent. Later accounts differ on exactly what Liu said orally, and Party archives for the period remain incompletely accessible.

Section 69.3

Liu Shaoqi, Deng Xiaoping, Chen Yun, and the Administration of Recovery

Responsibility for managing recovery was shared among several senior leaders. Liu Shaoqi, as head of state and the Party's second-ranking figure, presided over many central work conferences and led investigations in his home province of Hunan in 1961, where he heard villagers' accounts of hunger and of the failures of communal dining. Zhou Enlai, as Premier, coordinated the State Council and its ministries and worked to reduce industrial demands on the countryside. Deng Xiaoping, as General Secretary, ran the Party's central secretariat, and was involved in drafting policy documents and coordinating Party organization.

Chen Yun, who had been sidelined during the Leap after warning of its risks, returned to a central economic role. In early 1962 he argued before senior colleagues that the financial and economic situation was more serious than many had acknowledged, that the budget deficit and money supply needed to be controlled, and that urban populations and capital construction would have to be reduced. A central finance and economic group was reestablished with Chen Yun prominent in it. He supported measures that many in the Party found uncomfortable, including grain imports and more flexible arrangements in agriculture.

These leaders shared a practical concern: restoring production and stabilizing the state. They did not necessarily share identical views, and students should avoid describing them as a single faction. Some, like Deng Zihui in the Party's rural work department, went further in favoring household-based production arrangements, and were rebuked by Mao when he learned of them in 1962. Others were cautious about such steps. In his July 7, 1962 speech on restoring agricultural production, Deng Xiaoping cited a Sichuan proverb used by Liu Bocheng: whether a cat was yellow or black mattered less than whether it caught mice; the phrase later became famous, though in 1962 it drew criticism from Mao's supporters.

The relationship between collective leadership and Mao's authority was central. Mao had stepped back from daily administration after 1958–1959, a retreat he later described as moving to a "second line," and his colleagues handled many decisions without consulting him on every detail. Yet they remained aware that his approval mattered on matters of principle, and several of the most contested decisions of 1962 turned on whether Mao would accept them. The administrators' pragmatism operated within limits set by his willingness to tolerate it.

Section 69.4

Readjustment, Consolidation, Filling Out, and Raising Standards

The policy known as readjustment was summarized in an eight-character slogan adopted at the Ninth Plenum in January 1961: tiaozheng, gonggu, chongshi, tigao (调整、巩固、充实、提高), commonly rendered "readjust, consolidate, fill out, and raise standards." Its meaning developed through 1961–1962. Readjusting meant correcting imbalances among agriculture, light industry, and heavy industry, and reducing the scale of capital construction. Consolidating meant stabilizing enterprises and institutions. Filling out meant completing unfinished projects and supplying missing inputs. Raising standards meant improving quality and efficiency.

In practice, readjustment meant sharp cuts. Targets set during the Leap were abandoned or sharply reduced. Investment in capital construction was cut substantially, and hundreds of thousands of projects were halted or postponed. Many enterprises established during 1958–1960 were closed or merged, especially small, inefficient local factories. Heavy industrial output, including steel, fell significantly from its 1960 peak before recovering. The priority of agriculture was restated: the economy was to be organized with agriculture first, light industry second, and heavy industry third, reversing the emphasis of the first Five-Year Plan.

The government did not repudiate planning or abandon socialism. State ownership of industry, state procurement of grain, and collective ownership in the countryside continued. What changed was the pace, the realism of targets, and the willingness to use economic incentives and technical expertise. Historians distinguish between this readjustment, which operated within the planned economy, and the market-oriented reforms of the post-Mao era (Chapters 71–72). The comparison is useful but should not be pressed too far: readjustment was a temporary and partial adjustment, not a different economic system.

Historical Evidence

Recovery Indicators After the Great Leap

Historians reconstruct recovery from several kinds of evidence, each with different strengths. National statistics published by the State Statistical Bureau give aggregate figures for grain, steel, and other products, but 1958–1960 data were distorted by overreporting, and later revisions and reconstructions differ. Provincial and county archives, increasingly used by scholars, record local procurement, population, and conditions, though access is uneven. Memoirs and oral histories convey household experience but are shaped by memory and by later political climates. Foreign sources, such as trade statistics from Canada and Australia on grain sales, provide independent checks on imports.

These sources show broadly that official grain output reached a low around 1960–1961 and recovered in stages, approaching the 1957 level by 1965 and clearly surpassing it in 1966 in the official aggregate grain series. But aggregate grain output is not the same as per-capita availability, since population continued to grow, nor as rural welfare, since procurement and local distribution determined what households actually received. Students should treat figures as estimates and compare indicators rather than relying on any single number.

Section 69.5

Reorganizing the People's Communes and Rural Production

The reorganization of the communes was the most consequential change of the period for rural households. In 1958, the commune had been envisioned as a very large unit encompassing thousands of households, with ownership and accounting at the commune level and communal dining as a sign of progress toward communism. By 1960–1961, this structure had proved unmanageable. Distant leaders could not allocate labor and resources sensibly, and egalitarian distribution across large units weakened incentives, since hard work in one village might benefit another.

Successive drafts of regulations on rural people's commune work, widely called the Sixty Articles after their number of clauses, were issued in 1961 and revised in September 1962, when the Tenth Plenum approved the revised draft. The regulations established a three-level system of ownership and accounting: commune, production brigade, and production team. Crucially, the production team, typically a small group of neighboring households numbering a few dozen families, became the basic accounting unit, responsible for planning and distributing its own output. This was intended to remain stable for a long period. Communal dining halls were abandoned or made voluntary, and the "supply system" of free distribution was ended in favor of distribution according to labor, measured in work points.

Private plots, small areas of land allotted to households for their own cultivation, were restored or expanded, generally limited to a small percentage of cultivated land, and households were allowed to raise pigs, poultry, and other sideline products. Rural markets were reopened so that farmers could sell surplus produce. These measures were defended as supplements to the collective, not alternatives to it, and they were hedged with restrictions. Some localities went further. In Anhui, Sichuan, Gansu, and elsewhere, experiments in assigning fields or output quotas to households appeared, sometimes under local officials' sponsorship. Such "contracting to the household" was criticized by Mao in 1962 and was curtailed, though it left precedents later invoked in the reforms of the late 1970s.

Context: The regulations were drafted after rural investigations in 1961 and consolidated lessons from local experiments. Mao took an interest in the drafting but also later criticized some tendencies toward household-level production.

It is a statement of intended policy, not a description of practice: implementation varied by region, and local officials sometimes departed from or extended its provisions. It reflects the perspective of central policymakers and tells us little directly about how farmers regarded the changes, though other sources, including local archives and interviews, can supplement it.

Division II — Economic Recovery and the Reconstruction of Everyday Life, 1962–1965
Section 69.6

Agricultural Recovery, Food Supplies, and Rural Incentives

Agricultural recovery proceeded from 1962. Official grain output rose from the low of 1960–1961 to roughly 160 million tons in 1962 and to higher levels in subsequent years, approaching the 1957 total by 1965 and exceeding it in 1966 in the official series; the reliability and comparability of historical statistics still require scrutiny. Cotton and oilseed production also recovered. Several factors contributed: better weather in many regions, restored incentives, the return of labor to fields, reduced procurement pressure, more realistic planning, and increased supplies of chemical fertilizer, partly through imports of fertilizer plants. The government also began importing grain from Canada, Australia, and other countries in 1961, on the order of several million tons annually, which helped feed cities and coastal regions while allowing procurement from the countryside to be moderated.

Rural incentives mattered. Work points were again tied more closely to labor, small teams could organize farm tasks, and private plots and sidelines supplemented household diets and incomes. Free markets in some localities allowed farmers to sell vegetables, poultry, and pork. The state maintained a monopoly on major grains and set procurement quotas, but quotas were stabilized and, in places, reduced. These measures did not abolish rural poverty or the gap between rural and urban living standards, and they reflected a limited and revocable tolerance, not a new principle.

Students should distinguish among indicators. National output was one measure; grain per person was another, since population resumed growth after the famine; and what a particular household actually consumed depended on its team's harvest, its size and dependency ratio, and local distribution decisions. Areas that had suffered most, such as parts of Anhui, Henan, Sichuan, Gansu, and Guizhou, took longer to recover than others, and in many villages the early 1960s were still years of scarcity.

Consider a representative farming household in a village in the North China Plain around 1963. The description below is a composite drawn from patterns documented in oral histories and local studies, not a specific family. Its members, perhaps two adults, three children, and a grandparent, belong to a production team of some twenty-five households. The team decides which fields to plant with wheat, millet, or cotton, and each adult earns work points for labor, recorded by a team accountant. At the end of the year, grain is distributed according to points and household size.

The family has lost a relative during the famine, and the household has fewer able-bodied workers than before. The communal kitchen has closed; the family cooks at home again, a change many welcomed, though what they cook depends on rations and on what their private plot yields. The plot provides vegetables, and the household keeps a pig for the winter. On market days the husband sometimes sells eggs, a practice permitted but regarded warily by cadres who might later accuse villagers of "spontaneous capitalism." Women work in the fields and also spin, weave, and prepare food; in many teams, women earned fewer work points than men for comparable days. Children, if the family can afford it, attend the village primary school; many girls remained at home.

Daily life was shaped by the proximity of memory. People remembered who had died, who had received relief, and which cadres had taken more than their share. These memories affected how villagers responded when cadres later sought to mobilize them in political campaigns.

Section 69.7

Industrial Retrenchment and the Restoration of Production

Industrial retrenchment was as important as agricultural reform. During the Leap, heavy industry had expanded explosively, with new steel mills, mines, and machine-building plants started simultaneously across the country. By 1960 many lacked raw materials, power, or transport, and the Soviet withdrawal deprived key projects of technical help and spare parts. In 1961–1962 the government cut capital construction sharply, closed or merged thousands of enterprises, and reduced the industrial workforce. Heavy-industrial output fell steeply from its peak, with steel production in 1962 well below the level claimed for 1960.

Restoration proceeded by reorienting priorities. Planners emphasized agriculture and the industries that supported it, including chemical fertilizers, farm machinery, and consumer goods. Enterprises were reorganized and some were given clearer rules governing management, quality control, and responsibility, with experts and engineers regaining roles that had been challenged during the Leap. The government also relied on imports, including entire plants for fertilizer and synthetic fibers purchased from Western Europe and Japan in the mid-1960s, a notable departure from the exclusive dependence on the Soviet bloc of the 1950s.

Retrenchment had human costs. Workers were laid off or sent to the countryside, and managers who had pursued Leap targets faced reassessment. Yet industrial output recovered, and by 1964–1965 many sectors had regained or exceeded earlier levels, laying a more sustainable foundation than the Leap's improvisation.

Cause and Effect

Retrenchment and Industrial Recovery

Causes

Long-term: overextended investment, imbalance between heavy industry and agriculture. Short-term: famine-reduced raw material supplies, loss of Soviet technical support, fiscal deficit, and a political reassessment after 1961.

Transformation

Capital construction cut, enterprises closed or merged, workers reduced, agricultural support industries prioritized, specialists restored to authority, selective imports of foreign plants.

Effects

Immediate: unemployment and dislocation for many workers. Longer-term: improved balance among sectors, restored output by the mid-1960s, and a smaller but more sustainable industrial base.

Historical complexity

Retrenchment cannot be explained by the Leap's failure alone: it also reflected political decisions about reassessment, technical judgments by planners, external pressures, and the limits of what the state could afford. It was also contested, since some leaders feared it signaled retreat from socialist goals.
Section 69.8

Fiscal Stabilization, Planning, and Technical Expertise

Restoring fiscal order was a precondition for recovery. During the Leap, local governments and enterprises had expanded credit and spending, and the central budget ran large deficits. Chen Yun and finance officials worked to reduce spending, curb the money supply, and restore the authority of planning bodies. The government also took steps to reduce price distortions in some markets and to withdraw excess currency, notably by selling high-priced goods at premium prices in some cities in 1961–1962, a measure intended to absorb purchasing power.

Planning was reconstructed on more realistic foundations. Targets were calculated from verified capacity and supplies; statistical offices were reinvigorated; and annual and medium-term plans were formulated with attention to balance among sectors. The Third Five-Year Plan, drafted during 1963–1965, was originally to emphasize agriculture and consumer goods, though it was later reoriented toward defense and the Third Front (Section 69.29).

Technical expertise regained standing. Engineers, scientists, economists, and managers who had been criticized as "experts" in contrast to "reds" in earlier campaigns were consulted again. In 1962 several leaders, including Zhou Enlai, spoke at conferences with scientists and intellectuals, affirming their contribution to socialist construction and relaxing some political pressure. This did not mean a liberalization of political control, but it opened space for professional judgment in economics, science, and education. That space would prove vulnerable when political priorities shifted.

Section 69.9

Cities, Employment, Migration, and Rationing

Urban policy was a major instrument of recovery. During 1958–1960, tens of millions of people had moved from the countryside into cities and industrial jobs. In 1961–1963, the government reversed this flow. Estimates suggest that on the order of twenty million urban workers and their dependents were sent or returned to rural areas, though figures vary by source. This reduced the number of people dependent on state-supplied grain, relieving pressure on supplies, and returned labor to farms. The household registration system (hukou) established in 1958 gave the state a means to control such movement by classifying people as agricultural or non-agricultural and tying grain rations and urban services to registration.

For those who remained, urban life was shaped by rationing. Grain, cooking oil, cotton cloth, and other staples were distributed through ration coupons, and quantities varied by occupation, age, and city. Workers in heavy industry received larger allowances than office workers or dependents. Wages were largely frozen, and housing was provided or controlled through work units (danwei), which also provided medical care, schooling, and in some cases food. These units made the workplace the center of urban social life and of political supervision.

Those sent to the countryside experienced displacement: some were villagers who had been recruited into factories during the Leap and now returned to farming; others were urban residents with no rural ties. Cities remained privileged relative to the countryside, a gap sustained by the combination of state subsidies, rationing, and hukou restrictions. This urban-rural divide would persist long after the 1960s and became a central subject of later reforms (in the later discussion of post-Mao rural and urban reforms).

Section 69.10

Regional Differences and the Social Consequences of Recovery

Recovery was geographically uneven. Provinces that had suffered the worst famine mortality, such as Anhui, Sichuan, Henan, and Guizhou, faced longer rebuilding, with depleted populations and damaged farmland. Others, including coastal provinces and some northern areas, were less severely affected and rebounded sooner. Provincial leaders' responses also differed: some, like Anhui's Zeng Xisheng, had championed Leap policies and later faced criticism, while others adapted more quickly to readjustment. Regional variation in climate, soil, and local administration meant that a national policy of readjustment worked out differently in different places.

The social consequences were deep. Women and children had been disproportionately affected by hunger in some regions, and the loss of adult family members changed household structures. Orphaned and abandoned children, widowed women, and elderly people without support depended on relatives, teams, or relief. Health services, especially in rural areas, slowly recovered; in 1965 Mao criticized the Ministry of Health for neglecting the countryside, which helped prompt the later expansion of rural medical workers. Education was restructured: part-work, part-study schools and a two-track system of full-time and half-day schooling were promoted to extend schooling to rural children cheaply, though quality varied.

In minority and frontier regions, recovery intersected with other pressures. In Tibetan areas, the suppression of the 1959 uprising and subsequent reforms had altered social structures; in Xinjiang, Inner Mongolia, and other borderlands, famine and policy changes affected pastoral and agricultural communities, and border tensions with the Soviet Union complicated local life (Section 69.25). Documentation for these regions is thinner and more politically sensitive, and historians caution against generalizing from central policy to local experience.

Division III — Mao, the Communist Leadership, and the Struggle over China's Political Direction
Section 69.11

Mao Zedong's Position After the Great Leap Forward

Mao's position after 1959 was neither one of retirement nor one of unchallenged control. He remained Chairman of the Party's Central Committee and its Military Commission, positions that carried authority over ideology, personnel, and the armed forces. He had given up the chairmanship of the state to Liu Shaoqi in 1959 and withdrew from routine decision-making, spending long periods outside Beijing, reading, and consulting provincial leaders. His colleagues continued to defer to him on principle, and he remained able to intervene decisively when he chose.

Assessments of his standing differ. Some historians stress that the Leap's failure, and the 1962 acknowledgment of error, weakened his prestige among senior leaders and provincial officials, many of whom had suffered under its consequences. Others emphasize that his authority in the army, his symbolic position as founder of the Republic, and his control over ideological language remained formidable. Both views can be correct: his legitimacy among some colleagues eroded while his institutional power persisted. The distinction between reduced operational involvement and the loss of political power is essential. Mao's later actions show that he regarded his retreat as tactical and was prepared to reenter policy when he judged that socialism was endangered.

What Mao concluded from the Leap is debated. Evidence suggests he accepted that some measures had been mistaken, but he did not conclude that the underlying aims were wrong. Instead he increasingly attributed difficulties to resistance by officials, to bourgeois influences within the Party, and to the persistence of class contradictions in socialist society. This interpretation guided his actions from the Tenth Plenum onward.

Section 69.12

The Tenth Plenum of 1962 and the Renewed Emphasis on Class Struggle

During the summer of 1962, leaders gathered at the seaside resort of Beidaihe, where Mao criticized what he saw as excessive pessimism about the economy and the drift toward household contracting. He warned that capitalism was reviving in the countryside and that the Party risked losing its revolutionary character. The Tenth Plenum of the Eighth Central Committee, meeting in Beijing from September 24 to 27, 1962, endorsed the revised commune regulations and agricultural priorities, but it also adopted Mao's emphasis on class struggle, urging the Party never to forget it.

The plenum thus combined two messages. Economic policy would continue to prioritize recovery and agriculture, and the production team's role was affirmed. At the same time, ideological vigilance was reasserted: classes and class struggle continued to exist under socialism, and the danger of capitalist restoration remained. The plenum also dealt with Peng Dehuai's attempt to be rehabilitated, which Mao rejected, and it criticized other leaders associated with proposals for household contracting. These actions signaled that Mao would permit pragmatic adjustment only within limits he defined.

The significance of the plenum lies in the coexistence of recovery and renewed ideological emphasis. Students should resist reading it as the beginning of the Cultural Revolution, since the policy consensus on readjustment held, and Mao did not yet attack Liu Shaoqi or the Party leadership by name. But it supplied vocabulary and a framework that later campaigns would use.

Section 69.13

Mao and Liu Shaoqi: Competing Approaches to Socialist Development

Mao and Liu had worked together since the 1930s and 1940s, and Liu had been a principal architect of Party organization and a loyal supporter of Mao's authority, including during the Leap. After 1961 their emphases diverged. Liu and like-minded colleagues wanted to restore production by relying on material incentives, specialized administration, and orderly Party discipline. Mao, while accepting readjustment, worried that these methods would encourage selfishness, bureaucratism, and a new privileged stratum, and he favored political education, mass mobilization, and the cultivation of revolutionary commitment.

The differences concerned several matters: how much room to allow private and household production; how to treat experts and cadres; how to understand the nature of contradictions in rural society; and who should lead campaigns to correct cadre abuses. They became sharper during the Socialist Education Movement, as the next division shows. But it is a mistake to describe the two men as leaders of permanently opposed factions. Liu continued to profess loyalty, and both spoke in the shared vocabulary of Marxism-Leninism. Many decisions, including the Ten Points and the Third Front, involved both. The deterioration of their relationship was gradual and contingent.

The disagreements were not simply about whether individual fields should be cultivated by families. They concerned the status of specialists, the authority of provincial and county officials to adapt policy, the uses of markets within a collective economy, and the political significance of incentives. Liu's emphasis on investigation and restoration of production did not imply the abandonment of collectivism. Mao feared that measures tolerated as emergency relief might establish a durable social constituency for private accumulation. Both sides could describe their positions as protecting socialism, while attaching different meanings to that claim.

A useful analytical distinction is between immediate disagreement and retrospective interpretation. Decisions about how much land to allot to private plots or how to pay production teams were made in specific meetings and through instructions that could later be modified. The later Cultural Revolution recast these policy differences as evidence of antagonistic political lines. Historians must compare contemporary records with those later accusations instead of reading the outcome of 1966 backward into every debate of 1962.

Historical Debate

The Origins of Mao–Liu Political Disagreement

The question: Did Mao's conflict with Liu Shaoqi arise from deep ideological differences present since the 1950s, from policy disputes after the Great Leap, or from Mao's perception of threats to his own authority?

Interpretation A — Differences of principle and policy: Some scholars argue that the two leaders held divergent visions of socialist development: Liu favored institutionalization, expertise, and incentives; Mao favored continuous revolution. The disputes of 1962–1965 over household contracting, work-team methods, and cadre policy are seen as expressions of these differences.
Interpretation B — Contingent conflict and questions of authority: Others stress that the leaders' positions overlapped until the early 1960s, that both supported the Leap initially, and that the break emerged from particular events: criticism of the Leap, Mao's sensitivity to perceived disloyalty, and disputes over how to run the Four Cleanups. On this view, Mao's concern for his standing and for the Revolution's direction interacted with institutional rivalry.

Evidence: Both views rely on Party documents, speeches, and memoirs. Many sources were produced or edited later, during the Cultural Revolution or in post-Mao assessments, and reflect the political purposes of those moments. Mao's recorded remarks are often reported secondhand.

Why it matters: The question shapes how historians explain the Cultural Revolution: as an expression of long-standing ideological conflict, or as a product of accumulating and contingent disputes.

Student task: Which kinds of evidence would best distinguish between the two interpretations? Why is retrospective testimony problematic for this question?

Section 69.14

Deng Xiaoping, Party Organization, and Administrative Discipline

As General Secretary, Deng Xiaoping oversaw the Party's central Secretariat, which handled daily coordination of Party work. He worked with Peng Zhen, Beijing's Party leader, and other officials to manage Party meetings, draft documents, and supervise cadres. In the early 1960s he was involved in rebuilding Party organization damaged by the Leap and in setting rules to improve administrative discipline, including restoring regular reporting, collective decision-making, and attention to cadre conduct.

Deng's role in rural policy included drafting the Later Ten Points of 1963, which tried to place limits on the Socialist Education Movement and to channel it through orderly procedures. His practical outlook was visible in his support for readjustment and technical expertise, but students should be cautious about retrospective portraits. His support for effective administration did not imply support for political liberalization: he operated within, and strongly supported, Party authority. His later reputation as a reformer, formed after 1978, should not be read back into his actions in 1962–1965.

Party administrative reform required careful attention to the relationship between the central Secretariat, provincial committees, and economic ministries. A decision could be formally approved at the center yet interpreted differently by the commune or factory that carried it out. Deng and other administrators sought clearer reporting, more predictable personnel assignments, and instructions that could actually be implemented. These priorities reflected the experience of the Leap, when inflated targets and fear of disapproval had made truthful reports difficult to obtain.

The practice of democratic centralism did not remove tensions between collective discussion and obedience to the highest authorities. Party meetings could expose errors while leaving the leadership's basic political hierarchy intact. This meant that better information and tighter administrative discipline were not necessarily remedies for the political risk of disagreeing with Mao. The distinction helps explain why administrative recovery coexisted with new campaigns directed against alleged ideological deviation.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Socialist Governance Before and After the Great Leap

What changed?

  • Targets became more realistic and planning more cautious.
  • The accounting unit in the countryside moved down to the production team.
  • Private plots, rural markets, and technical expertise regained legitimacy.
  • Candor within the Party was encouraged, at least in principle.

What stayed the same?

  • One-party rule, state ownership, and collective agriculture.
  • State procurement of grain and the hukou system.
  • The Party's claim to lead and its use of campaigns.
  • Mao's position as the final arbiter of ideology.

Who experienced change most directly? Rural households and team cadres saw altered rules about land, labor, and markets; urban workers faced layoffs and relocation. Who experienced continuity? Senior Party leaders and the structure of central authority. Why it matters: Recovery modified the system without changing its foundations, which left unresolved the question of how far modification could go.

Section 69.15

Socialist Exemplars, Political Culture, and Revolutionary Education

While economic administrators restored incentives, the Party also promoted models of selfless commitment. In March 1963 Mao's call to learn from Lei Feng, a young PLA soldier portrayed as a paragon of service and devotion, launched a national campaign, though historians note that Lei Feng's published diary and image were carefully constructed by propaganda organs. In 1964 the Party urged industry to "learn from Daqing," the oilfield in Heilongjiang developed under conditions of hardship and celebrated for self-reliance after Soviet oil supplies ended, and agriculture to "learn from Dazhai," a mountain brigade in Shanxi led by Chen Yonggui that was presented as having transformed barren terrain through collective effort.

The PLA, under Lin Biao, became a source of political models. A campaign to learn from the army promoted political study, the reinstatement of political commissars, and the use of short quotations from Mao, compiled in 1964 in a volume that later became known as the "Little Red Book." These campaigns formed part of a political culture that emphasized ideological commitment alongside, and sometimes against, technical and material considerations. They also provided Mao and his allies with institutions and symbols that would be mobilized later.

Historians treat the models critically. Daqing's achievements were real, but its portrayal minimized the role of planning, imported equipment, and state support. Dazhai's record was later challenged by evidence of exaggeration and of reliance on state aid. The exemplar campaigns show how the state used propaganda to shape expectations and how ideological conformity was pressed even as recovery proceeded.

Division IV — The Socialist Education Movement and the Four Cleanups, 1963–1965
Section 69.16

The Origins of the Socialist Education Movement

The Socialist Education Movement grew from Mao's concern, expressed from the Tenth Plenum onward, that class differentiation, cadre corruption, and "spontaneous capitalist tendencies" were reviving in the countryside. Reports from the field provided material for his worries: some cadres had misappropriated grain or funds, some villagers practiced geomancy, held clan rituals, or engaged in private trade, and some teams had leaned toward household contracting. Investigations suggested that rural administration was in disrepair after the Leap, and that cadres' standing with villagers was low.

Differences existed from the start about the movement's purpose. For some leaders, it was a means to repair rural administration by auditing accounts and disciplining abusive officials. For Mao, it increasingly became a means to educate cadres and peasants in class awareness and to detect those undermining socialism. The movement's name, "socialist education," reflected this broader aim, while its popular label, the "Four Cleanups" (siqing), initially referred to a narrower audit.

The movement was connected to the social consequences of the famine. Villagers complained about unequal access to food, the appropriation of collective property, favoritism in allocating work points, and officials who had used coercion during procurement. These complaints did not all point in the same ideological direction: a villager demanding repayment of stolen grain might favor fairer collective management rather than private farming. A campaign that encouraged such grievances could, however, generate accusations that reached well beyond accounting problems.

The timing also mattered. Recovered agricultural output and reduced procurement pressure gave officials some room to revisit local institutions, but the leadership had not agreed on how to assess the political lessons of the Leap. Mao interpreted enduring administrative abuses as evidence that class struggle continued under socialism. Other officials were more concerned with competence and a limited restoration of trust. The Socialist Education Movement became a setting in which these overlapping concerns could be mobilized for different purposes.

Historical Vocabulary
Class Struggle, Revisionism, and Socialist Education

Class struggle (jieji douzheng) was a Marxist concept applied in China to conflicts among social classes, defined in the revolution by relationship to land and capital. After land reform and collectivization, Mao argued that classes and class struggle persisted in socialist society, including through former landlords, "rich peasants," and bourgeois ideas within the Party. This was a contested extension of the term, since many colleagues believed the main task was development.

Revisionism referred to deviation from what the Party considered correct Marxism-Leninism, in the Chinese view exemplified by the Soviet leadership under Khrushchev; it was also used domestically to describe tendencies to restore capitalism. Socialist education meant political education intended to strengthen collective values and resist capitalist influences. Students should read these terms in their political context: they were instruments in disputes, not neutral descriptions, and their meaning shifted with the speaker's aims.

Section 69.17

The Early and Later Ten Points of 1963

In May 1963, a central meeting in Hangzhou, with Mao's guidance, produced a draft decision on certain problems in current rural work, known as the Early Ten Points. It described class struggle in the countryside, called for organizing poor and lower-middle peasants into associations, and set out methods for educating and checking cadres. In September 1963 the Later Ten Points, drafted under Deng Xiaoping's supervision, provided more detailed policy provisions. They distinguished among types of contradictions and urged care to avoid excess, for instance by stressing the need to unite with most cadres and to treat mistakes as contradictions among the people unless clear evidence showed otherwise.

The two documents illustrate a pattern: Mao's emphasis on class struggle and the practical concerns of administrators to limit excess. Their texts were not identical in tone, and the movement's course depended on how local officials, work teams, and higher bodies interpreted them. A revised version of the Later Ten Points issued in September 1964 added further instructions, reflecting the influence of work-team experiments led by Liu Shaoqi's circle (Section 69.19).

The Early Ten Points and Later Ten Points should be read as steps in a changing campaign, not as a pair of fixed constitutional rules. Local investigators needed to decide which households counted as reliable poor and lower-middle peasants, how to separate actual misuse of public property from differences over policy, and what evidence justified accusations against a cadre. Such decisions rested on local histories shaped by land reform, collectivization, and famine. A phrase like 'contradictions among the people' could protect a person from the most severe treatment in one place, while another team might classify similar conduct politically.

The 1964 revision and the January 1965 Twenty-Three Articles made the campaign's political implications clearer. Students should compare the successive documents by asking who was authorized to investigate, which level of official was treated as the principal problem, and whether a remedy meant restitution, administrative discipline, or ideological remolding. A comparison of dates is essential, because later interpretations of the movement can obscure how its stated aims shifted during implementation.

Section 69.18

The Four Cleanups and the Investigation of Rural Administration

The "Four Cleanups" first meant auditing four things in rural collectives: accounts, granaries, property, and work points (qing zhangmu, qing cangku, qing caiwu, qing gongfen). Teams of investigators examined records to detect embezzlement, hidden grain, and favoritism in distributing work points. In many places this exposed genuine abuses, and some villagers welcomed the opportunity to hold cadres accountable.

By 1964, the formula was reinterpreted to mean cleaning up politics, economy, organization, and ideology (qing zhengzhi, qing jingji, qing zuzhi, qing sixiang). This broadened the target from financial irregularity to political and ideological error, and shifted the movement toward evaluating the political reliability of officials. The change shows how a campaign against corruption could turn into a campaign for ideological purification, and why historians treat the movement's chronology carefully: a document or experience from 1963 should not be read as if it reflected the objectives of 1965.

The relationship between reform and coercion was complex. The investigations could correct abuses and restore some accountability, but they also relied on public accusation, forced confessions, and sometimes physical abuse. Local variation was great: some areas emphasized economic audit and restitution, while others pursued harsh class labeling.

Accounts and work-point books were especially important because they linked collective production to the grain or cash distributed to individual families. A false entry could deprive a household of subsistence or reward politically connected relatives. Yet an inquiry could be distorted if it assumed in advance that all shortages resulted from individual wrongdoing. Some missing goods reflected production collapse, administrative confusion, and coercive quotas imposed from above rather than personal theft by the village official being investigated.

The widening of the Four Cleanups from economic accounts to political conduct also changed who could speak safely. A complaint about rationing or the use of collective animals might become an accusation about a cadre's class outlook. In some villages, the campaign briefly opened opportunities for peasants to question officials; in others, visiting work teams controlled proceedings and decided which testimony counted. That duality is central to evaluating the movement as both an attempt to remedy abuses and a mechanism of political control.

Section 69.19

Work Teams, Local Officials, and the Politics of Rural Rectification

Work teams of outside cadres, often drawn from higher-level offices, cities, and schools, were sent to villages to conduct the investigations. Their methods differed. In 1963–1964, Wang Guangmei, Liu Shaoqi's wife, joined a work team at the Taoyuan (Peach Garden) brigade in Hebei, and its report, endorsed by Liu, advocated large, concentrated work teams that would "dig out roots" and rely on investigation conducted largely by outsiders, with local cadres sidelined. Mao and others criticized aspects of this method, arguing it distrusted the masses and local activists and focused excessively on lower-level cadres.

Local cadres faced a difficult position. They had implemented procurement and Leap policies under pressure from above and now found themselves investigated for the results. Some were genuinely corrupt; others were scapegoated. Villagers, for their part, might support, resist, or exploit the process, settling personal or clan grievances. The campaign's effects included the removal of many local officials and the punishment of some, followed in places by reinstatement.

The Taoyuan example became influential because it offered an apparently systematic technique: outsiders would live in the locality, collect testimony, investigate assets and behavior, and reorganize the Party branch. Such concentration of authority could bypass village networks and uncover entrenched favoritism, but it could also encourage investigators to classify ambiguous personal conflicts as evidence of an organized political enemy. The report's later prominence makes it important to distinguish the claims of a celebrated model investigation from the diversity of actual village campaigns.

Work teams were not neutral observers. Their careers depended partly on persuading superiors that the campaign had made progress; villagers were sometimes asked to recount hardships in settings dominated by officials who could award or withdraw political labels. On the other hand, some teams identified real theft and coercion. The historian's task is to compare central instructions, reports from investigators, local account books, and villagers' recollections, recognizing the imbalance of power between those giving and those receiving testimony.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Village Undergoing the Four Cleanups

The following is a composite of patterns reported in memoirs and local studies, not a specific village. When a work team of two dozen outsiders arrives, they lodge with poor peasant families and begin meetings. Team accounts are laid out; ledgers are compared with grain stores. Villagers are asked to "pour out bitterness" about cadre behavior, and the production brigade leader, who had supervised procurement during the famine, is called to explain discrepancies.

Some villagers are eager to speak; others are wary, since class labels assigned in land reform affect families' status, and a person who speaks may later face retaliation. The brigade leader's relatives are pressed to return goods. Evening study sessions require participants to read documents and discuss their meaning. For the duration, normal work continues but is shadowed by meetings. After months, some cadres are demoted, some replaced, and some quietly reinstated; relationships within the village remain strained long after the team leaves.

Section 69.20

Socialist Education in Cities, Factories, and Cultural Institutions

The movement extended into cities as the "Five Antis" in enterprises and offices, targeting corruption, waste, and bureaucratism, and into schools, research institutes, and cultural organizations. In factories, it emphasized discipline, political study, and cadre participation in labor. In the arts, Mao in December 1963 and June 1964 criticized the Ministry of Culture and the cultural establishment for neglecting workers, peasants, and soldiers and for promoting traditional and foreign themes; he suggested that without change the ministry might become one for emperors, kings, and scholars. A rectification of literary and art circles followed, and Peking-opera festivals in 1964 promoted contemporary revolutionary themes.

Intellectual debates were also politicized. In 1964, philosophical discussions, such as Yang Xianzhen's thesis that two aspects of a contradiction could "combine into one," were attacked as revisionist. A Group of Five led by Peng Zhen was formed to oversee cultural rectification. Intellectuals who had regained some professional latitude after 1962 saw it narrow again, and the standards of acceptable cultural expression became increasingly tied to political loyalty.

In urban workplaces, the targets and evidence differed from those in agricultural communes. Factory managers could be questioned over material losses, production accounts, purchasing arrangements, or misuse of enterprise property; employees might be asked to criticize supervisors while still depending on those supervisors for wages, housing, and promotion. Party organizations conducted political study sessions alongside technical work. Campaigns that treated failures of management as ideological weaknesses could make qualified engineers and administrators reluctant to report problems candidly.

Universities, publishing houses, and theaters faced related pressures, although their output could not be measured like grain or steel. Criticism could focus on a writer's choice of historical subject, the reputation of a teacher, or the political implications of a scholarly argument. Officials did not apply a single uniform policy: some promoted technical and cultural work, while others demanded public demonstrations of orthodoxy. This tension between expertise and ideological loyalty became increasingly significant as political conflict moved toward the cultural sphere in 1965.

Section 69.21

The Twenty-Three Articles of 1965 and the Conflict over Party Authority

In January 1965, a central work conference issued a directive, usually called the Twenty-Three Articles, on certain current problems in the rural socialist education movement. It reframed the movement's focus: the main target was no longer the lower-level cadres or "four unclean" practices alone, but "those people in authority within the Party who are taking the capitalist road." The directive also criticized the use of large outside work teams in the manner of Taoyuan and stressed reliance on the masses and on local cadres who were willing to correct mistakes.

Reports of the meeting suggest sharp exchanges between Mao and Liu Shaoqi about the nature and targets of the movement, with Mao rejecting formulations he thought obscured the main question of political line. The record of these exchanges comes largely from later accounts and should be treated cautiously. What is clear is that the directive made officials in leadership positions, not only village cadres, potential objects of criticism.

Students should analyze the phrase in its 1965 context. At that time it had no settled reference to specific individuals; it was a broad formula whose application remained undefined. After 1966 it was used to attack Liu Shaoqi and others. Reading it only through that later use would anachronistically make the Cultural Revolution seem already underway.

The Twenty-Three Articles did not merely restate the older demand for honest accounting. They made political authority itself a central object of inquiry. A directive naming cadres in authority who were allegedly following a capitalist road could direct attention toward increasingly senior officials, although its immediate implementation remained varied and the text did not itself announce the Cultural Revolution. The phrase gained further and more destructive uses in the years after 1965; its meaning in this document must be established from the document's own context.

Liu Shaoqi's association with large external work teams and Mao's criticism of some of their procedures show that rival methods could coexist within a movement nominally serving the same Party goals. Historians should not assume that all local investigators already understood the campaign as a struggle against national leaders. The transition from local rectification to disputes over the political direction of the CCP was contingent, and it unfolded through decisions about targets, evidence, and who could supervise whom.

Division V — The Sino-Soviet Split and the Crisis of International Communism
Section 69.22

The Deterioration of the Sino-Soviet Alliance, 1956–1960

The Treaty of Friendship, Alliance, and Mutual Assistance of 1950 had bound the two largest communist states. Soviet loans, equipment, and thousands of advisers had supported the First Five-Year Plan (Chapter 67). Yet tensions existed from the start, over Soviet terms, over Mao's treatment during his 1949–1950 Moscow visit, and over relative status in the communist world. After Stalin's death in 1953, Mao regarded himself as the senior living Marxist leader, a self-image that sat uneasily with Soviet primacy.

Nikita Khrushchev's secret speech of February 1956, denouncing Stalin, shocked Chinese leaders, who saw it as undermining the communist movement and as delivered without consultation. Mao accepted some criticism of Stalin's errors but defended his legacy. Khrushchev's policy of peaceful coexistence with the West, including his September 1959 visit to the United States, conflicted with Mao's view that imperialism remained aggressive and that revolution should be supported. The Soviet decision in June 1959 to cancel an agreement to supply a sample atomic bomb and technical data, and Soviet neutrality in the Sino-Indian border incidents that year, deepened Chinese resentment. Disputes over Soviet proposals in 1958 for a long-wave radio station and joint submarine fleet on Chinese territory raised sovereignty concerns, and the Taiwan Strait crisis of 1958 revealed divergent risk calculations.

These strands show that the split cannot be reduced to one cause. Ideology, national interests, nuclear policy, leadership rivalry, and different assessments of the Cold War interacted. By 1960 the dispute was becoming open within the communist movement, as at the Bucharest meeting in June, where delegations traded criticisms.

Security disagreements compounded the ideological quarrel. Moscow and Beijing did not share the same assessment of risk in the Taiwan Strait, especially during the crisis of 1958, or of the wisdom of confrontation with the United States. Soviet leaders were concerned that Chinese decisions might entangle the USSR in a conflict it could not control. Chinese leaders, meanwhile, suspected that Soviet restraint sacrificed China's security and revolutionary objectives to a superpower understanding. These differences made treaty obligations harder to interpret even before both parties publicly attacked each other's theory.

The chronology must distinguish the rise of tensions after the Soviet Party's Twentieth Congress in 1956, arguments over nuclear cooperation in 1957–1959, the withdrawal of assistance in 1960, and the public party-to-party polemics after 1963. Later border confrontation was not simply an immediate consequence of Khrushchev's criticism of Stalin. The split was cumulative, combining conflicting strategic interests, ideological claims, expectations of partnership, and competition for status within the international Communist movement.

Section 69.23

The Withdrawal of Soviet Advisers and the Consequences for China

In July 1960 the Soviet government notified China that it would recall its specialists, and about 1,390 Soviet experts were recalled, according to the contemporary Chinese government’s account; independent summaries commonly round the number to about 1,400. This disrupted access to personnel, designs, and technical cooperation. Chinese sources state that hundreds of contracts and many scientific and technical cooperation projects were terminated. Soviet explanations emphasized Chinese hostility and the treatment of its experts; Chinese accounts portrayed the withdrawal as a deliberate attempt at pressure during the famine.

The consequences were significant but should not be overstated. Projects in industry, mining, and defense were delayed or disrupted, and some plants remained unfinished. The withdrawal came as the Leap's own failures were already crippling the economy, so the effects were compounded. China also repaid its Soviet debts, completing repayment by about 1965, which strengthened a narrative of self-reliance (zili gengsheng). The episode fostered attention to domestic scientific and technical training, and to diversifying economic ties, including purchases from Japan and Western Europe.

Memory of the withdrawal became politically important. In Chinese official narratives, it symbolized Soviet unreliability; in Russian accounts, it highlighted Chinese ingratitude. Students should compare sources on both sides and note the limits of figures.

The 1,390 figure was deployed in China's public case against Moscow; it measures personnel recalled according to that source, not an independently reconciled total of all specialists who had ever worked in China. The damage depended heavily on the stage of each project. Plants already commissioned could sometimes continue using Chinese engineers and documentation, whereas experimental equipment, incomplete designs, and specialist-dependent installations faced delays. Interruptions were also different in civilian industry, military technology, and higher education, where experienced personnel could not easily be replaced.

The withdrawal did not erase all earlier Soviet assistance. A generation of Chinese technicians had trained with Soviet experts, and equipment already installed continued to shape industrial production. Chinese leaders drew a political lesson of self-reliance from the dispute, yet practical dependence on imported machinery, materials, and technical information persisted. Students should separate China's contemporary accusation that Moscow was deliberately undermining development from the demonstrable interruption of specific projects and from the broader effects of famine and domestic mismanagement on the economy.

Section 69.24

The Great Ideological Polemics of 1963–1964

The dispute became public in stages. At the Twenty-Second Congress of the Soviet Communist Party in October 1961, Zhou Enlai objected to public attacks on Albania's leadership and left early. The Cuban Missile Crisis of October 1962 led Chinese commentators to denounce Soviet behavior, first as adventurism in placing missiles and then as capitulation in withdrawing them. In 1963, an exchange of letters set out positions. China's letter of June 14, 1963, "A Proposal Concerning the General Line of the International Communist Movement," argued for revolutionary struggle against imperialism, support for national liberation movements, and rejection of what it called revisionist peaceful transition and coexistence as universal policy. The Soviet Union replied with an open letter in July 1963.

The Partial Test Ban Treaty, signed by the United States, Britain, and the Soviet Union in August 1963, was denounced by China as a measure to preserve a nuclear monopoly. Between September 1963 and July 1964, the Chinese press published nine commentaries on the Soviet open letter, culminating in a critique of Khrushchev's "phony communism." These texts used the language of Marxism-Leninism to dispute questions of war and peace, the transition to socialism, and the character of the Soviet state. They also implied that the Soviet leadership had abandoned revolution and that China was the guardian of orthodoxy. In October 1964 Khrushchev was removed from office; Zhou Enlai visited Moscow the following month, but reconciliation did not follow.

The polemics had immediate organizational consequences. Communist parties outside the two states had to decide whether to align publicly with Beijing, Moscow, or neither. Some parties split, and others attempted neutrality while receiving competing ideological literature and assistance. For leaders in Beijing, charges of 'modern revisionism' also had a domestic function: a condemnation of compromise in international affairs could be applied to officials advocating flexibility in economic management at home. This did not mean domestic policy was wholly determined by foreign disputes, but the language of both conflicts increasingly overlapped.

Documentary comparison is essential. China's June 1963 General Line proposal, the Soviet replies, and subsequent editorials were persuasive public texts addressed to Communist audiences, not neutral chronicles of events. Students should identify whether each claim concerns nuclear strategy, attitudes to imperialism, national sovereignty, the role of peaceful coexistence, or ideological orthodoxy. An argument that one side had betrayed Marxism cannot establish by itself that the same side had violated a particular agreement or caused a specific economic disruption.

Section 69.25

Border Disputes, Xinjiang, and Strategic Tensions with the Soviet Union

The Sino-Soviet border, among the longest in the world, was defined by nineteenth-century treaties imposed on or negotiated with the Qing, including those of Aigun, Beijing, and Ili, and the 1881 Treaty of Saint Petersburg. Chinese leaders regarded some of these as unequal, though in the 1950s they did not press territorial claims. As relations worsened, border questions resurfaced. In July 1964 Mao told a Japanese delegation that Russia had taken vast territories, remarks that Khrushchev publicly rejected. Border talks in Beijing in 1964 failed to settle the line. Both sides increased troop deployments in later years (Chapter 70).

In April–May 1962, tens of thousands of residents of the Ili and Tacheng districts of Xinjiang, many of them Kazakh and Uyghur, crossed into Soviet Kazakhstan, commonly estimated at around sixty thousand. Causes included the hardships of famine and policy in Xinjiang, resentment of controls on religion, trade, and movement, and Soviet consular encouragement of emigration, as well as kinship ties across the border. Chinese officials accused the Soviet Union of subversion; Soviet officials described the movement as a response to Chinese policy. China then tightened border controls. The episode illustrates how the dispute involved frontier peoples with their own motives, not only states.

The borderland had social histories that could not be reduced to rival maps. Kazakh and Uyghur communities maintained kinship, language, and trade links across frontiers that had become much harder to cross under Soviet and Chinese systems of security. Restrictions, shortages, and perceived discrimination affected decisions to leave in 1962. Soviet consular activity and Chinese accusations of deliberate recruitment formed part of the diplomatic dispute, but archival research also shows that Chinese officials recognized serious domestic pressures behind the migration.

The territorial issue was different from the population exodus. Nineteenth-century agreements, river channels, islands, and the geography of border markers raised questions over where sovereignty should be exercised. These disputes escalated in subsequent years, and Chapter 69 should not import the battles of 1969 into the period under study. The immediate point is that deteriorating diplomacy turned local frontier problems into tests of national security while the lives of minority communities remained central to events.

Section 69.26

China and the Contest for Leadership of World Communism

As the split deepened, Beijing and Moscow competed for the allegiance of communist parties and national liberation movements. Albania aligned with China; many parties in Asia, including those of Japan, Indonesia, and Vietnam, navigated between the two, and some parties in Europe and elsewhere divided into pro-Soviet and pro-Chinese factions. China offered support, including training and limited aid, to some revolutionary and independence movements, particularly in Africa and Southeast Asia, emphasizing armed struggle and rural base areas drawing on its own experience.

The outcome was mixed. China gained influence in some places, but it also encountered limits: many governments preferred Soviet or Western aid, and Chinese methods did not always translate. The split also fragmented the communist world and offered opportunities to the United States and others, though American policy toward China remained hostile during these years, including discussion in Washington of options against China's nuclear facilities. The consequences for international communism were lasting: it ceased to be a single bloc.

Competition for leadership was neither uniform nor confined to the two largest Communist parties. Albania aligned closely with Beijing, while other parties and governments weighed ideological affinity against trade, military assistance, and their relationships with neighboring states. The Soviet Union retained the stronger network of formal alliances and institutional relationships across Eastern Europe; China's appeal was particularly visible in arguments that revolutionary struggles in Asia, Africa, and Latin America differed from the conditions of industrial Europe.

Beijing's position was also constrained by the choices of other governments. Support for national liberation did not ensure that newly independent states would accept Chinese guidance, and communist movements could disagree with Moscow without endorsing every Chinese policy. Chinese leaders emphasized anti-imperialism and rural revolutionary experience, but the message competed with Soviet material support and with local political priorities. This division of world communism should therefore be analyzed as a contest over international influence, ideology, and national interests rather than as a global alignment of countries into two obedient camps.

Division VI — Strategic Independence, Foreign Policy, and the Approach to Political Upheaval
Section 69.27

The Sino-Indian War of 1962 and China's Southwestern Frontier

The Sino-Indian border had never been fully demarcated. In the west, the Aksai Chin plateau, crossed by a road China built in the 1950s linking Xinjiang and Tibet, was claimed by India as part of Ladakh. In the east, the McMahon Line, drawn at the 1914 Simla Conference between British India and Tibetan representatives, was regarded by India as the boundary but not accepted by Chinese governments. Relations were cordial in the mid-1950s, symbolized by the Panchsheel principles of 1954, but deteriorated after the 1959 Tibetan uprising, when the Dalai Lama fled to India. Talks between Zhou Enlai and Nehru in 1960 did not settle the dispute.

From late 1961, India adopted a "forward policy," establishing posts in disputed areas; China responded with its own posts and warnings. Fighting began on October 20, 1962, with Chinese attacks in both sectors, and Chinese forces advanced rapidly. On November 21 China declared a unilateral ceasefire and later withdrew from areas in the east to positions behind the line it had claimed, while retaining Aksai Chin. Casualty figures differ by source; Indian official figures give over a thousand killed, Chinese figures several hundred. The war coincided with the Cuban Missile Crisis, which limited great-power attention.

Interpretations differ. Chinese accounts describe the war as a self-defensive counterattack after Indian provocations. Indian accounts hold that China planned the attack and was responsible for aggression. Scholars have argued variously about the role of the forward policy, Chinese perceptions of Indian involvement in Tibet, and the context of the Leap's aftermath. Key documents, including India's Henderson Brooks–Bhagat report, remain restricted, and Chinese military archives are largely closed, so uncertainties persist. The boundary dispute continues through the Line of Actual Control.

The boundary dispute had distinct western and eastern sectors. In Aksai Chin the road linking Xinjiang and Tibet was strategically important to Beijing, while India understood the plateau through its claims to Ladakh. In the east, the McMahon Line derived from the 1914 Simla negotiations between British Indian and Tibetan representatives; Chinese representatives did not accept the final arrangement. A student map should show both claim lines where possible and identify the difference between a military position and a legally agreed international boundary.

The war's short duration should not obscure its consequences. India's military reverses prompted major reassessment of defense arrangements and foreign assistance; China's victory did not resolve the boundary dispute or create an agreed frontier. Contemporary statements by each government assign blame differently, and sources must be dated carefully: a later official justification may portray earlier decisions as inevitable. Terrain, logistics, the presence of Tibetan refugees in India, and differences in strategic perceptions all need to be evaluated alongside the military chronology.

Section 69.28

China's Nuclear Program and the Atomic Test of 1964

China decided in the mid-1950s to develop nuclear weapons, spurred by American nuclear threats during the Korean War and Taiwan Strait crises. Soviet assistance after a 1957 agreement included technical aid and training, but ended in 1959–1960. The Chinese program continued under leaders including Zhou Enlai, Marshal Nie Rongzhen, and scientists such as Qian Sanqiang, Wang Ganchang, Deng Jiaxian, and Zhou Guangzhao, many trained abroad and returned after 1949. Uranium enrichment, bomb design, and testing facilities were built under conditions of economic hardship, requiring industry and mining in remote regions.

On October 16, 1964, at Lop Nur in Xinjiang, China detonated a uranium-based device from a tower, with an estimated yield of roughly 20–25 kilotons, depending on the technical assessment cited. The test came a day after Khrushchev's fall. It made China the fifth nuclear power. This first detonation differed from a deployable arsenal: delivery systems, warhead miniaturization, and thermonuclear capacity required further years of work, with a hydrogen bomb test in 1967 (Chapter 70). Testing at Lop Nur affected local environments and communities, and the extent of health effects is contested and imperfectly documented.

The nuclear program combined civil administration, military requirements, and scientific networks. Training, mining and isotope separation, the design of specialized instruments, and test-range construction demanded coordinated work over many years. The Soviet withdrawal created obstacles but did not leave China without trained scientists or industrial facilities. The 1964 test therefore should be explained as the outcome of both earlier foreign assistance and subsequent Chinese work, rather than as a simple demonstration that imported expertise was unnecessary.

Yield estimates for the first detonation vary among technical compilations, and the chapter uses a range rather than a single supposedly exact measurement. The event's international significance was immediate: it challenged the monopoly exercised by the existing nuclear-armed powers and allowed China to announce a no-first-use position. The government's published statement describes its declared intentions; independent analysis is necessary to evaluate future force development and strategy. The immediate test did not itself establish a deliverable thermonuclear arsenal.

Section 69.29

National Defense and the Beginnings of Third Front Construction

In 1964, concern about external attack, driven by the escalating war in Vietnam, tension with the Soviet Union, and the American presence around China, led Mao and the leadership to reorient industrial planning. The "Third Front" referred to the interior regions of the southwest and northwest, such as Sichuan, Guizhou, Yunnan, Shaanxi, and Gansu, to be developed as a secure base for heavy industry, defense production, and transport, as opposed to the coastal "first front" and the intermediate "second front." Projects included the Chengdu–Kunming railway, the steel complex at Panzhihua, and numerous factories placed in mountain valleys, often dispersed and hidden.

The Third Front diverted investment from consumer goods and agriculture, which some planners had prioritized, and it entailed inefficiencies, since remote sites were costly and difficult to supply. Millions of workers and technicians relocated, transforming parts of the interior economically while imposing hardship. It illustrates the tension between security requirements and economic efficiency, and it shows how foreign threats shaped domestic planning. The long-term legacy, including industrial infrastructure in the interior, remains debated.

The decision to develop inland defense industries reflected fears that concentrated coastal factories and transport links would be vulnerable in a major war. Site selection favored concealment, separation of production facilities, and access to valleys that could be defended, even when these advantages increased construction costs. Workers brought machinery and specialist knowledge into areas with limited infrastructure, and planners had to build roads, rail links, housing, and services alongside factories. The resulting projects changed local environments and economies as well as China's strategic industrial geography.

The chronology is important: Third Front construction began in 1964, but many facilities were completed later. Projects such as Panzhihua and the Chengdu–Kunming railway therefore illustrate an initiative whose implementation continued beyond this chapter. The initial choice was made while economic readjustment was still recent, producing tension between planners concerned with efficiency and officials responsible for defense. Students should assess both the new industrial capacity eventually created and the costs of remote siting without assigning those later results wholly to 1964–1965.

Section 69.30

China, the Developing World, and the Cold War in Asia

In the early 1960s China broadened its diplomacy. From December 1963 to February 1964, Zhou Enlai visited ten African countries, including Egypt, Algeria, Morocco, Tunisia, Ghana, Mali, Guinea, Sudan, Ethiopia, and Somalia, and in Ghana announced principles for Chinese economic aid, emphasizing equality, no political conditions, and low-interest loans. China offered technical assistance, medical teams, and support for liberation movements. In January 1964, France under de Gaulle recognized the People's Republic, an important step toward diplomatic breakthrough in the West, though the United Nations seat remained with the Republic of China on Taiwan.

In Asia, China signed a border agreement with Pakistan in 1963 and developed closer ties with it, partly in response to India. Relations with Indonesia under Sukarno warmed, though they would deteriorate after the events of 1965. As the United States escalated in Vietnam after the Gulf of Tonkin incident of August 1964 and the bombing campaign of 1965, China provided aid to North Vietnam and, from 1965, sent engineering and anti-aircraft units, while Lin Biao's 1965 article on people's war articulated a revolutionary strategy for the developing world. A planned second Afro-Asian conference in Algiers in 1965 was canceled, and China's efforts to organize a rival grouping to the Soviet-influenced bloc met limits.

The ten-country African tour should be seen as negotiations with governments possessing their own priorities. The diplomacy was accompanied by statements about sovereign equality and development assistance, but the resulting relationships differed. The January 1964 aid principles offered an important statement of Chinese policy; how aid was financed and implemented in each country requires separate evidence. Recognizing France in 1964 similarly mattered because it diversified international relations without ending Beijing's dispute over China's representation at the United Nations.

In Southeast Asia, the intensification of the Vietnam War created a more immediate security challenge. Beijing supported North Vietnam but also weighed the danger of direct military escalation with the United States. China’s activities must be distinguished from Hanoi's political and military decisions; support did not confer control over the Vietnamese war effort. Attempts to forge a broad diplomatic grouping at the proposed Afro-Asian meeting also showed the limits of Chinese influence, since participating governments did not necessarily agree about the Soviet Union or revolutionary struggle.

China and the Wider World

Diplomacy in Asia and Africa

Chinese foreign policy in these years was an active process, not simply a reaction to isolation. It involved aid, trade, medical and technical missions, ideological appeals, and bilateral negotiations. Chinese leaders sought partners among newly independent states while competing with both the United States and the Soviet Union. African and Asian governments pursued their own interests: some welcomed Chinese aid as an alternative; others were wary of Chinese revolutionary rhetoric and interference. The record shows both ambition and constraint, and the results varied by country.

Section 69.31

The Road Toward the Cultural Revolution

By 1965, several lines of tension converged. Economic recovery had succeeded, but Mao distrusted the pragmatic methods that produced it. The Socialist Education Movement had made officials at all levels potential targets. The polemics against Soviet revisionism provided a vocabulary that could be turned inward. The Third Front and the war in Vietnam heightened a sense of external danger. Lin Biao's army promoted political study as a model. In the cultural field, criticism intensified: Mao and his wife Jiang Qing, who had been active in reforming Peking opera, sought to reshape arts and criticized Beijing's cultural establishment.

In November 1965, the Shanghai newspaper Wenhui bao published an article by Yao Wenyuan attacking Wu Han's historical play Hai Rui Dismissed from Office, which dramatized the dismissal of a Ming official for criticizing an emperor. The article suggested that the play was an allegorical attack on Mao's dismissal of Peng Dehuai. It was published without full approval by Beijing's cultural leaders, and its escalation belongs to Chapter 70. In the same period, senior figures such as Luo Ruiqing, the army chief of staff, and Yang Shangkun were removed, signaling that Party and military leaders were vulnerable.

None of this made the outbreak of the Cultural Revolution inevitable. Mao could have been satisfied with a more limited reform; his colleagues could have responded differently; events in Vietnam or the economy might have altered priorities. The upheaval resulted from choices, especially Mao's decision to turn to mass mobilization outside Party channels, which Chapter 70 examines.

The cultural struggle over Wu Han's play had a specific institutional setting. Wu was a historian and Beijing official; Yao Wenyuan's published criticism was supported by political figures outside the Beijing cultural establishment. Whether readers understood the play as an allegory for Peng Dehuai's dismissal, as the attackers suggested, was itself contested. The significance of the criticism lies not in proving the author's intention but in the way a historical drama became a weapon in an internal struggle over ideological authority.

At the same time, criticism of senior security and military figures indicated that disputes were moving beyond the management of farms and factories. Yet it would be misleading to treat a 1965 newspaper article as the opening of the fully developed Cultural Revolution. The attack's subsequent political uses, and the decision to mobilize students and others outside the normal Party hierarchy, require the chronology and evidence of Chapter 70. Chapter 69 ends with mounting conflict and uncertainty, not with a foregone conclusion.

Division VII — Final Review
Section 69.32

Chapter Review and Post-Great Leap Checkpoint

This chapter has traced three connected developments between 1962 and 1965: economic recovery, political and ideological struggle within the Party, and the search for strategic independence. The central synthesis question is: How did China achieve significant economic recovery after the Great Leap Forward while simultaneously developing the political and ideological conflicts that would contribute to renewed upheaval? Recovery relied on readjustment, reorganized communes, restored incentives, retrenchment, and imports. The same measures raised questions about socialism and authority that Mao answered with campaigns on class struggle and revisionism. International rivalry reinforced both sets of concerns.

Regional and Frontier Context

The period's developments varied by region. In the North China Plain and Yangtze basin, recovery centered on grain and cotton. In Sichuan and the southwest, famine recovery coincided with Third Front construction that brought new industry and migrants. In Xinjiang, the Ili–Tacheng exodus, the Lop Nur test site, and Soviet tension made the region a security frontier; Kazakh, Uyghur, Hui, and Han residents experienced these changes differently, and the settlement of Han migrants and the Xinjiang Production and Construction Corps altered demography. In Tibet and the Himalayan borderlands, the aftermath of 1959 and the 1962 war reshaped local life and sovereignty claims, and in Inner Mongolia and the northeast, the Soviet dispute increased military presence. Sources for frontier peoples' own views are limited and often mediated by state or foreign accounts, and students should seek them where available.

Chapter Summary

After the catastrophe of the Great Leap Forward, the People's Republic entered a period of reconstruction, institutional adjustment, and rising political tension. The Seven Thousand Cadres Conference of early 1962 acknowledged errors but left the question of responsibility unresolved, illustrating both the possibility and limits of candor within a Leninist party.

Under the administrative leadership of Liu Shaoqi, Deng Xiaoping, Chen Yun, and Zhou Enlai, the government adopted readjustment: reducing targets, retrenching industry, reorganizing the communes around the production team, restoring private plots and markets, and importing grain. These measures contributed to recovery, but recovery was uneven among indicators and regions, and it did not erase the effects of famine on households, communities, and the Party's relationship with the countryside.

Economic pragmatism should not be confused with political liberalization. These leaders operated within, and supported, the Party's governing system. Mao Zedong, while stepping back from daily administration, retained authority over ideology and campaigns, and from 1962 emphasized class struggle and the danger of capitalist restoration. His relations with Liu Shaoqi deteriorated through specific disputes rather than from fixed opposition from the start.

The Socialist Education Movement and Four Cleanups were the central bridge between recovery and later conflict. Beginning as an audit of rural accounts and cadre abuses, the movement broadened into an ideological campaign, and by the Twenty-Three Articles of January 1965 identified Party officials taking the capitalist road as its main target. Work teams, local cadres, and villagers experienced the campaign differently, and its costs fell unevenly.

Meanwhile the Sino-Soviet alliance disintegrated through overlapping disputes about ideology, nuclear weapons, security, economic relations, and leadership of the communist world. The withdrawal of Soviet advisers in 1960 and the polemics of 1963–1964 marked distinct stages. Border tensions, including the Xinjiang exodus of 1962, added strategic distrust and showed the agency of frontier communities.

China's search for strategic independence included the 1962 war with India, whose causes and conduct remain interpreted differently by Chinese and Indian scholars and constrained by restricted archives; the nuclear test of October 1964; the Third Front's inland industrialization; and diplomatic outreach to Africa, Asia, and France.

By late 1965, China had achieved substantial recovery, expanded international relationships, and strengthened strategic capabilities. But unresolved disputes over economic policy, authority, and revolutionary ideology, together with the politicization of culture exemplified by the attack on Hai Rui, created the setting for the Cultural Revolution.

That outcome was not inevitable. It resulted from choices by Mao and others in response to accumulated tensions, including his decision to bypass Party institutions through mass mobilization. Chapter 70 examines that turn.

Documentary and Statistical References

Grain production and the timing of recovery
State Statistical Bureau historical grain series, reproduced in K. Ge, C. Chen, T. Shen, and S. Zhang, “Food Consumption and Nutritional Status in China,” Food, Nutrition and Agriculture (FAO), and in The Political Economy of Hunger, volume 3. The published series gives 160.00 million tonnes in 1962, 194.53 million in 1965, and 214.00 million in 1966, compared with 195.05 million in 1957; units and data revisions must be stated when comparing totals.
Liu Shaoqi's January 1962 oral remarks
“Speech at an Enlarged Working Conference of the Central Committee of the Party,” January 27, 1962, in Selected Works of Liu Shaoqi, vol. 2 (Beijing: Foreign Languages Press, 1991), p. 398. Liu attributed the roughly 30/70 natural/man-made comparison to peasants he questioned in Hunan; it was not a calculated national proportion.
Deng Xiaoping's July 1962 proverb
“Restore Agricultural Production,” July 7, 1962, in Selected Works of Deng Xiaoping, vol. 1 (1938–1965), English translation. Deng cited a yellow-cat/black-cat Sichuan proverb used by Liu Bocheng; the common later white-cat wording should not be substituted into a 1962 transcription.
Withdrawal of Soviet experts
1963 Chinese Communist Party correspondence with the Communist Party of the Soviet Union cited 1,390 withdrawn specialists and interrupted contracts. This is a figure advanced by a party to the dispute; independent documentary summaries commonly use approximately 1,400, and the two categories should not be treated as separately counted populations.
Ili–Tacheng exodus
Charles Kraus, “Laying Blame for Flight and Fight: Sino-Soviet Relations and the ‘Yi–Ta’ Incident in Xinjiang, 1962,” The China Quarterly (2019), estimates approximately 60,000 migrants and examines both local conditions and international accusations.
Urban population resettlement
Chronicle of the People's Republic of China (October 1949–September 2019), Institute of Party History and Literature of the CCP Central Committee, records 26 million urban residents resettled by June 1963 and a workforce reduction of 18.87 million. The two numbers describe different measures; neither should be conflated with another without explanation.
1964 nuclear yield
SIPRI Yearbook 1986 lists a 20-kiloton estimated yield; the US National Security Archive's 2014 documentary overview reports 22 kilotons, while other technical surveys give approximately 25. All are estimates for the October 16, 1964 tower test.
Socialist Education directive
The January 14, 1965 Twenty-Three Articles identified people in authority taking the capitalist road as a focus of rectification; distinguish this contemporary document from subsequent Cultural Revolution reinterpretations.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows a political system recovering from catastrophe while preparing a new one. The same years that restored harvests and built a bomb also saw the language of class struggle turned toward the Party itself. Recovery and radicalization were not opposites but intertwined processes.

The period shaped later Chinese history in several ways. The production team and private plot left precedents for later rural reform; the Third Front and nuclear program shaped defense and industrial geography; the Sino-Soviet split reordered Asian geopolitics and eventually made possible the opening to the United States; and the Socialist Education Movement prepared methods and vocabulary used in the Cultural Revolution.

It also reveals larger themes of the textbook: the tension between central authority and local practice, the role of evidence and memory in contested history, and China's active participation in Asian and global politics, from Africa to the Himalayas. Frontier peoples in Xinjiang and Tibet were participants, not only objects, of these events.

Studying these years helps students see Chinese history as regionally varied, politically changing, and unfinished. The outcome of 1966 was a product of choices made in a period when other choices were possible, and that recognition is itself a central lesson of historical thinking.
