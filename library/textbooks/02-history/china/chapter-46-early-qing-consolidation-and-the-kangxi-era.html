Part VIII — The Qing Imperial Order
46

Early Qing Consolidation and the Kangxi Era

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

Conquering Beijing in 1644 did not make the Qing an empire. That took another four decades of administration, coercion, bargaining, and war.

Chapter 45 asked how the crisis of the Ming became a Manchu conquest. This chapter asks a different question: how did the Qing turn conquest into a durable political order? Many seventeenth-century regimes, from rebel states to loyalist courts, rose quickly and vanished. The Qing endured. Understanding why requires looking beyond battlefields to registers, garrisons, examination halls, tax quotas, and negotiations with generals, scholars, and neighboring states.

The central argument is that the Qing neither simply replaced Ming institutions with Manchu ones nor adopted Chinese government unchanged. Early Qing rulers combined Banner military power, selective continuation of Ming bureaucracy, cooperation with Han officials, coercive measures, fiscal stabilization, suppression of regional rivals, frontier diplomacy, and patronage of Confucian scholarship. Under the Kangxi emperor (r. 1661–1722) these strategies produced a more secure imperial order, though conquest, resistance, ethnic distinctions, and frontier problems remained live issues.

Evidence for this period includes Qing court records and edicts, memorials, the Ming History project, Banner registers, loyalist essays and poetry, Dutch and Jesuit accounts, and local gazetteers. Each reflects a position: court sources stress order, loyalist writings stress moral refusal, and foreign accounts record what outsiders observed. The chapter connects to Chapter 47 (High Qing government) and Chapter 48 (Inner Asia and imperial diversity), which treat in depth what is introduced here only as it bears on consolidation.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the Qing inherited both a conquered empire and much of the Ming administrative machinery.
  2. Describe the Banner system as a military, social, and household institution.
  3. Analyze how accommodation and coercion worked together in Qing governance.
  4. Distinguish forms of Ming loyalism, from armed resistance to scholarly withdrawal.
  5. Evaluate why the Revolt of the Three Feudatories was not simply Han resistance to Manchu rule.
  6. Trace the Zheng regime and the Qing incorporation of Taiwan without assuming earlier administrative integration.
  7. Assess Kangxi as a ruler who performed several political identities at once.
  8. Connect taxation, examinations, and scholarship to regime durability.
  9. Compare explanations for Qing survival: military, institutional, fiscal, and ideological.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How much institutional continuity can exist across a dynastic conquest?
  2. Why were the Banners more than regiments?
  3. Why did some Ming officials serve the Qing, while others refused?
  4. How did resistance shape Qing political culture?
  5. Why did Han generals who helped conquer China later threaten the Qing?
  6. How did Taiwan come under Qing rule, and what populations lived there?
  7. How did Kangxi combine Confucian, Manchu, military, and Inner Asian roles?
  8. Why did Qing rule survive when so many seventeenth-century regimes did not?

Historical Context

By 1644 the Ming had been battered by fiscal collapse, climate stress, silver shortage, rebellion, and frontier war (Chapter 45). The Qing, a state built by the Jurchen-descended Manchus under Nurhaci and Hong Taiji in the northeast, entered Beijing after Li Zicheng's rebels took it and with the assistance of the Ming general Wu Sangui. Capturing the capital, however, left the south, the southwest, the southeast coast, and much of the countryside outside Qing control. The conquering population was small relative to the Han majority, and the newly claimed realm combined densely settled agricultural regions with frontier zones.

"China" in this period cannot be equated with the modern state. The Qing ruled as a Manchu house that also claimed Mongol allegiance and Confucian legitimacy, and its territory expanded over the following century. Students should treat the early Qing as an empire in formation, not as a completed national state.

Historical Evidence

Qing court sources, including edicts, palace memorials, and the Veritable Records, document policy but present the dynasty's view and were shaped by court editing. Banner registers record households and status but say little about daily experience. Loyalist essays, poems, and memoirs preserve moral opposition, though many were circulated or revised later. Local gazetteers, tax registers, and land records show how counties recovered, but were compiled by officials and gentry. Foreign evidence, such as Dutch company records on Taiwan, Jesuit letters, and Russian diplomatic reports, offers outside views with their own interests. Treaties exist in several languages and require comparison. Historians must ask who wrote each source and what it leaves out.

Section 46.1

Manchu Rule and Qing Institutions

The Succession Crisis and the Regency

The Qing entry into Beijing arose from a contingent opportunity that Dorgon and his advisers, among them the Han adviser Fan Wencheng, were already preparing to exploit. Hong Taiji, who had built the Qing state in the northeast, died suddenly in 1643 without a settled heir. After a tense negotiation among Manchu princes, his young son Fulin was enthroned, later known by the reign title Shunzhi, with two regents: Jirgalang and Hong Taiji's brother Dorgon. When the rebel Li Zicheng took Beijing in April 1644 and the Ming emperor died, Wu Sangui, commanding the pass at Shanhaiguan, turned to the Manchus rather than to the rebels. Dorgon led Banner forces through the pass, defeated Li Zicheng, and occupied the capital. The decision to move the court to Beijing and rule from there was Dorgon's, and it committed the Qing to governing a vast agrarian society rather than raiding it.

Dorgon quickly became the dominant figure, eclipsing his co-regent and taking titles that approached those of a sovereign. This created a problem that the Qing would face repeatedly: the child emperor's legitimacy was entangled with powerful princes whose own authority rested on Banner commands. Dorgon's death in December 1650 allowed the throne to reassert itself. In 1651 the young Shunzhi took personal rule, and Dorgon was stripped of honors, a posthumous reversal that shows how contested the early distribution of power was. The Shunzhi emperor ruled until his death from smallpox in 1661.

Inheriting the Ming Machinery

The Qing presented itself in the first proclamations as the avenger of the Ming house, which it claimed had been destroyed by rebels. The new rulers performed the funeral rites for the last Ming emperor, Chongzhen, and invited Ming officials to remain at their posts. The six ministries, the censorate, the provincial hierarchy of provinces, prefectures, and counties, and the Ming system of tax registration were kept. In 1644 the court announced the abolition of the extra levies imposed in the late Ming war years and returned nominally to the land tax rates of the Wanli reign, a move meant to win support from taxpayers, though the practical record shows that war needs made full implementation difficult. Provincial examinations resumed in 1645, followed by metropolitan and palace examinations in 1646, which offered educated men a familiar path to office.

At the same time, the Qing grafted Manchu institutions onto this structure. The Deliberative Council of Princes and Ministers allowed Manchu nobles to take part in major decisions. Senior ministries were typically headed by a Manchu and a Han officer in parallel, so that offices were shared in form while the Banner elite kept the decisive voice. The Imperial Household Department, staffed largely by bondservants of the Banner system, managed the court. Historians therefore describe the early Qing state as dual in character rather than as a Ming government with a new emperor.

How Much Continuity?

The institutional continuity can be exaggerated or minimized. At the level of counties and taxes, a peasant in 1650 might not experience a change of system, only a change of officials and the pressures of war. At the top, the ruling house, the Banner command, the use of Manchu in court business, and the nature of access to the emperor differed markedly from the Ming. The central question for the early Qing was not whether to adopt Ming institutions, since that was largely settled by necessity, but how to keep Manchu political control while doing so.

Section 46.2

The Banner System

Origins and Structure

The Banners were not simply army regiments. They organized people, households, privilege, identity, and political loyalty. Nurhaci created the system in the early seventeenth century by grouping Jurchen households into companies (niru) and larger units distinguished by colored banners. By 1635 Hong Taiji had added Mongol Banners, drawn from allied Mongol groups, and in 1642 Han martial Banners (Hanjun), drawn from Han soldiers and defectors who had joined the Manchus. The result was three sets of eight Banners, twenty-four in all, each hierarchically organized, with Manchu Banners holding the highest prestige. The use of banners as the basic unit of residence, military service, and administration made membership a legal and social status that passed from father to son.

Within the eight Manchu Banners a distinction mattered: after Dorgon's death the 1651 reorganization placed the "Upper Three" Banners under the emperor's direct control, while the other five remained under princes, which linked the Banner structure to the question of imperial control. The Banners also included bondservants (booi), who served Banner masters or the Imperial Household Department. Some became wealthy and influential as managers and officials, which shows how a servile status could coexist with real power.

Garrisons, Stipends, and Land

After 1644 the Banners were stationed in Beijing, where most were concentrated, and in strategic provincial cities such as Xi'an, Nanjing, Hangzhou, Jingzhou, and Guangzhou. Garrisons were frequently walled off from civilian districts, with their own administration, and bannermen were subject to Banner jurisdiction rather than ordinary county courts. In the years after the conquest, the Qing seized farmland around the capital and redistributed it to Banner households in the Banner land enclosures of the mid-to-late 1640s, which displaced many peasants and caused lasting resentment. Later bannermen mainly received stipends in silver and grain, and were forbidden from most trades, since their duty was military service.

This arrangement had advantages and costs. It allowed a small conquering population to remain together, trained, and loyal to the throne. But it also made bannermen dependent on state payments and set them apart legally and spatially from the people they were meant to control. Over time, as stipends failed to keep pace with the number of households, many bannermen fell into poverty, a development that belongs to later chapters.

Banners and the Green Standard Army

The Banners alone could not police the empire. Ming troops who surrendered, together with new recruits, were organized as the Green Standard Army, named for the color of its flags. It was a largely Han force, deployed in small garrisons across the provinces for policing, road and river security, and campaigning, and in total it outnumbered the Banners. Many Green Standard units fought for the Qing during the Three Feudatories war. The two forces were distinct: Banner troops formed the hereditary core of the conquest elite, while the Green Standard provided the routine armed presence in the localities. Understanding the pair is essential to understanding how the Qing could rule a large population with a small ruling group.

Not every Manchu was privileged. Poorly paid ordinary bannermen, bondservants, and Banner women, whose lives were shaped by the system though rarely documented in it, had very different experiences from princes and senior commanders. The Banner population itself was also not purely Manchu: Han martial bannermen and Mongols were part of it, and identity within the Banners changed over time.

Section 46.3

Governing a Conquered Empire

Accommodation

Qing government combined accommodation and coercion. Accommodation began with the reuse of Ming administrative geography and personnel. Counties, prefectures, and provinces continued, and Qing governors and governors-general supervised them. Magistrates remained the officials with the closest contact with ordinary people: they collected taxes, heard cases, kept registers, and worked through clerks, runners, and local gentry. The new dynasty could not replace this layer, so it relied on it. Han officials who had served the Ming, among them advisers such as Hong Chengchou who submitted earlier, were employed in high posts, and the examination system was rapidly restored, with provincial examinations resuming in 1645 and metropolitan examinations following in 1646, so that educated men could again pursue careers through the established degree system.

The court also used pardons for those who submitted and issued tax remissions for areas ravaged by war. These actions were calculated: reducing the burden of the worst-hit districts helped to restore cultivation, which in turn secured future revenue. Proclamations used the language of Confucian rulership, promising to restore peace and to protect the people from rebels and oppressive officials.

Coercion

Coercion was equally important. On July 21, 1645, the regency ordered all Han men to shave the front of the head and wear the rest of the hair in a queue, in the Manchu manner, under penalty of death. For many Han the queue was a violation of filial duty, as the body was thought a gift from the parents, and the order made submission visible daily. Conquest violence had already been demonstrated at Yangzhou in May 1645, before the general queue decree; the survivor Wang Xiuchu described the sack in a memoir, whose very large casualty claims require careful evaluation. After the July queue order, resistance intensified in Jiangnan places such as Jiangyin and Jiading, and the Qing responded with brutal force.

The state also disciplined elites. In 1661 the Jiangnan "tax arrears case" punished degree-holders for unpaid taxes, asserting that gentry privilege did not excuse tax evasion. In the early 1660s the Zhuang Tinglong case, in which a privately compiled Ming history was judged seditious and led to executions, showed that writing the past could be dangerous. These cases helped establish that Qing patronage of scholarship, discussed in 46.8, existed beside censorship.

Why Did People Serve?

Historians stress that no single motive explains collaboration. Some Ming officials viewed the Qing as having restored order after rebel chaos, and thought that serving the state protected the people. Others sought to protect families and landholding, and many simply needed a career. Still others served reluctantly and were criticized by contemporaries as having betrayed the Ming. The line between collaboration and loyalism could be blurred: a man might serve in office while privately mourning the Ming. Local elites bargained with the new regime, offering cooperation in tax collection and order in exchange for protection of status, and some used the new government to settle local rivalries.

The practical limits of this arrangement deserve emphasis. A county magistrate governed a population of many thousands, sometimes far more, with a small personal staff, and depended on clerks, runners, and local notables who knew the land and its people. The mutual-responsibility system of baojia was revived for policing and registration, but it worked unevenly. Degree-holding gentry enjoyed privileges, including some exemptions from labor service, and they acted as intermediaries between officials and villagers. The Qing therefore had to discipline this group, as in the tax arrears case, while also relying on it, which made the relationship between the court and local elites one of continuing negotiation.

Regional and Frontier Context

Beijing and the Banner garrisons: The capital and the garrison cities were the base of Banner power, with separate quarters and jurisdiction. Jiangnan: A rich, literate region where the queue order was fiercely resisted and the gentry were disciplined, but which the Qing needed for revenue. The southwest and south: Domains of the feudatories, militarized and semi-autonomous until 1681. The southeast coast and Taiwan: A maritime zone of Zheng power, evacuation, and later Qing incorporation. The north and northeast frontier: Mongol allies, the Amur region, and Russian contact, treated in more depth in Chapter 48.

These regions experienced consolidation at different times and in different ways, so the capital's experience cannot stand for the whole empire.

Section 46.4

Ming Loyalism and Resistance

The Southern Ming

Chapter 45 asked how resistance prolonged conquest. The question here is how continued resistance shaped Qing consolidation and political culture. After the fall of Beijing, Ming princes established regimes in the south: the Prince of Fu at Nanjing in 1644, which fell in 1645; the Longwu court in Fujian; and the Yongli court, which moved through Guangdong, Guangxi, and Yunnan. These courts were divided by factionalism and dependent on generals who sometimes bargained or defected. The Yongli emperor fled to Burma and was captured and executed in 1662 on the order of Wu Sangui, whose role is a reminder that the same commander whose alliance with the Qing at Shanhai Pass helped bring Qing forces into China proper also ended the last Ming court.

Forms of Loyalism

Loyalism (yimin, "leftover subjects") was more than armed resistance. Some men fought, in the Southern Ming armies, in local militias, or in maritime forces such as those of the Zheng family. Others took their own lives when the Ming fell. Many refused office under the Qing, living in seclusion, teaching, farming, or entering religious life. The painter Zhu Da, a descendant of the Ming imperial house who became a monk and artist, produced work that has often been read as expressing grief, alienation, and loyalist memory after the Ming collapse. Still others preserved the Ming's memory by writing histories, poems, and essays, and by observing mourning rites. Loyalism could also be ambiguous, as when a man whose family served the Qing privately honored the Ming.

Loyalist Scholars

Three scholars show the intellectual side. Gu Yanwu (1613–1682) traveled the north, studying geography, institutions, and philology, and argued that scholarship should serve practical ends; he declined Qing office. Huang Zongxi (1610–1695), who had fought for the Ming, wrote Waiting for the Dawn, a critique of absolute monarchy and Ming government, and refused service although his students helped the Ming History project. Wang Fuzhi (1619–1692) worked in seclusion in Hunan on philosophy and history. Their works were not simply political protest; they offered explanations for why the Ming fell and influenced the scholarship discussed in Chapters 47 and 50. Their reception was shaped by later generations, who read them in light of nineteenth- and twentieth-century concerns.

Qing Responses

The Qing met armed resistance with force and supported its claims with propaganda that portrayed Ming officials as having failed. Over time the emphasis shifted toward absorption. The Qing honored the Ming imperial tombs, and in 1679 the special examination invited eminent scholars, including known loyalists, to take part in the Ming History project. Some refused or attended without taking real office, which shows the limits of the policy. The persistence of loyalist memory forced the Qing to build legitimacy on more than conquest, and encouraged the use of Confucian scholarship as a political tool.

The memory of the Ming was not only a source of opposition but also a political resource. Qing rulers honored the last Ming emperor Chongzhen, who had taken his own life as Beijing fell, and Kangxi later made offerings at the Ming founder's tomb near Nanjing during his southern tours, a gesture directed at Jiangnan elites. The resistance itself varied by region: it was strongest in Jiangnan and the southeast in the first years, persisted in the far south and on the coast through the Zheng regime, and faded over the following generations as new cohorts entered examinations and office. By the late seventeenth century loyalism more often survived as a literary and moral tradition than as a military threat.

Section 46.5

The Revolt of the Three Feudatories

Origins of the Problem

The Qing had rewarded powerful Han generals with large regional commands. Wu Sangui held Yunnan and Guizhou, and the Qing also gave him authority over Sichuan affairs; Shang Kexi governed Guangdong; and the Geng family, passing from Geng Zhongming through Geng Jimao to Geng Jingzhong, held Fujian. These commanders had brought armies and local knowledge into the conquest, and the Qing needed them to subdue the south and southwest. But the arrangements created semi-autonomous military power, with troops paid from imperial revenue and commanders who appointed officials and controlled local resources. The costs were a heavy fiscal burden and a standing risk. The very collaborators who made conquest possible later became threats to centralization.

The Decision of 1673

In 1673 Shang Kexi, aging and burdened by conflict with his son, asked to retire to Liaodong. The court agreed and ordered his withdrawal, which raised the question of what would happen to the other two. Wu Sangui and Geng Jingzhong submitted requests that were meant as tests of imperial intent. Kangxi, then about nineteen, decided to order all three feudatories abolished, reportedly against the caution of many senior ministers who feared provoking war. Wu Sangui rebelled at the end of 1673, and Geng followed in 1674.

The War

Wu's forces moved quickly through Guizhou and Hunan to the Yangzi, and revolts spread. In 1675 Wang Fuchen rose in Shaanxi, and in 1676 Shang Zhixin joined, though his father Shang Kexi had stayed loyal. In 1674 the Zheng regime on Taiwan sent forces against the Fujian coast. Qing control of the middle Yangzi, including the stronghold of Jingzhou, prevented a decisive rebel crossing of the river, and Wu's coalition lacked a unified command. The Qing mobilized Banner commanders and especially Green Standard generals, many of them Han, and combined offensives with offers of pardon. Geng Jingzhong surrendered in 1676, Wang Fuchen that year, and Shang Zhixin in 1677. Wu Sangui proclaimed a new dynasty in 1678 and died later that year. His grandson held on in Yunnan until Kunming fell in 1681.

Why It Was Not Simply Han Resistance

Many who fought against the rebels were Han, and many Han elites in the affected regions chose neither side wholeheartedly. Some joined from ambition, fear, or local advantage, while others resisted Wu because they accepted Qing legitimacy or distrusted a man who had served both Ming and Qing. Wu's own appeals mixed Ming restoration language with personal dynastic claims. The consequence was a decisive consolidation: the removal of the feudatories put military and fiscal authority in the court's hands and showed that Qing rule could defeat a major internal challenge.

The scale of the problem explains the court's decision. Contemporary Qing officials complained that the costs of maintaining the feudatory armies consumed a very large share of imperial revenue, although the figures come from court rhetoric and should be treated with caution. Wu Sangui also controlled the resources of the southwest, including trade and mining, and his authority extended to appointing officials, a practice known as the "western selection," whose appointees held posts well beyond his own territory. These were the features of a regional power that was already functioning as a second government.

Evidence for the revolt includes Kangxi's edicts and the court's memorials, which present the rebels as traitors, and rebel proclamations, which present Wu as a defender of the Ming heritage. Neither can be taken at face value. Kangxi's own strategy was to hold the Yangzi line, avoid overextending Banner forces, and combine military pressure with offers of pardon, a pattern that explains why defections proved decisive.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Three Feudatories Rebel?

The question: Was the revolt mainly a defense of regional power or an attempt to overthrow the dynasty?

Interpretation A — Defense of autonomy: The court's decision to dismantle the feudatories threatened commanders' armies, revenue, and lineage fortunes, and Wu Sangui rebelled to protect that position.
Interpretation B — A challenge to the dynasty: Wu Sangui's proclamations invoked Ming restoration and later dynastic claims, and the scale of the war suggests the aim was to replace Qing rule, at least in the south.

Evidence: Edicts, memorials, rebel proclamations, and campaign records support both readings; each source has a political purpose, since the Qing sought to portray defectors as rebels and the rebels sought to justify their cause.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how historians understand Han collaboration and the limits of early Qing authority.

Student task: What evidence would show whether Wu Sangui's supporters were motivated more by local interests or by dynastic loyalty?

Section 46.6

Taiwan and Qing Expansion

The Zheng Family

Taiwan was not an administratively integrated Chinese province before the Qing. Its Indigenous peoples had their own communities, Dutch East India Company forces held a fortified colony in the southwest from the 1620s, and Chinese settlers had been arriving. The Zheng house built a maritime power on the Fujian coast. Zheng Zhilong, a trader and Ming admiral, surrendered to the Qing in 1646 and was held in Beijing, where he was executed in 1661. His son Zheng Chenggong (Koxinga) continued to fight under Ming loyalist banners, based at Xiamen and Jinmen, and in 1659 led a campaign up the Yangzi that failed to take Nanjing.

Taiwan Under Zheng Rule

In 1661 Zheng Chenggong landed on Taiwan, and after a long siege the Dutch surrendered Fort Zeelandia in early 1662. He died that year, and a succession struggle followed before his son Zheng Jing took over. The Zheng regime organized garrison farming, encouraged settlement and agriculture, and established Ming-style institutions including schools and examinations, under advisers such as Chen Yonghua. Trade linked the island to Japan, Manila, and Southeast Asia. Indigenous communities experienced taxation, land pressure, and conscription, and evidence on their experience is thin and mostly comes from Dutch and Chinese writers.

Coastal Evacuation and Maritime War

To cut off the Zheng from mainland supplies, the Qing ordered the evacuation of coastal populations from 1661, with repeated orders in following years, and the destruction of coastal facilities. The policy displaced many families and damaged local economies, and it was resented. The Qing also cooperated with Dutch naval forces against the Zheng in 1663–64. Zheng Jing joined the revolt of the Three Feudatories by attacking Fujian in 1674 but lost ground, and by 1680 had been forced back to Taiwan. He died in 1681, and the regime's leadership weakened.

Conquest and the Debate Over Retention

In 1683 Admiral Shi Lang, a former Zheng officer who had changed sides, defeated Zheng forces at the Penghu islands, and Zheng Keshuang surrendered. Court officials debated whether to keep the island. Some doubted its value, while Shi Lang and others argued that abandoning it would allow it to become a base for pirates or for foreign powers. In 1684 it was organized as a prefecture under Fujian, with restrictions on migration. The same year the court lifted major maritime restrictions and established the new maritime customs regime across 1684–85, linking Taiwan's incorporation to the reopening of maritime trade.

Zheng power depended on commerce as well as arms. The family organized trade networks that connected coastal China with Japan, Manila, and Southeast Asia, and drew on merchant agents on the mainland. On Taiwan, garrison farming took land that Indigenous communities had used, and Indigenous villages that had previously dealt with the Dutch company now faced Zheng demands, which some resisted. Because most written evidence comes from Dutch and Chinese authors, the Indigenous perspective is hard to recover.

After 1684 Qing Taiwan remained a frontier. Migration was restricted by permit requirements and rules against bringing families, yet settlers continued to arrive, especially from Fujian and Guangdong, and Indigenous land and status continued to change. The island exported products such as rice and sugar. Treating Taiwan as an already integrated province would therefore miss both the earlier layers of rule and the ongoing negotiation of Qing authority there.

Section 46.7

The Kangxi Emperor

Childhood and Regency

Kangxi came to the throne in 1661 at about age seven, with four Manchu regents, among whom Oboi became dominant. He formally assumed personal rule in 1667, but Oboi remained extraordinarily powerful. Only his arrest in June 1669, by traditional account carried out with the help of young guards, broke the principal constraint on Kangxi's effective authority. The episode matters because it shows that the throne's control was not automatic; it had to be won against a powerful Manchu minister.

Governing Priorities

Kangxi later said he had written three matters on a palace pillar: the Three Feudatories, river conservancy, and the grain transport system. The latter two concerned the Yellow River and the Grand Canal, on which the north's food supply and flood control depended. He appointed administrators such as Jin Fu to direct river works and inspected them personally. His six southern tours, beginning in 1684, allowed him to see these projects, meet officials and elites in Jiangnan, and visit the temple of Confucius at Qufu, where he performed rites of respect. The tours were also political theater, projecting both imperial concern and imperial power in a region that had resisted the conquest.

Learning and the Court

Kangxi studied the Confucian classics with tutors and in the 1670s created a study group of scholars at court. He also studied mathematics, astronomy, and music with Jesuits such as Ferdinand Verbiest, Jean-François Gerbillon, and Joachim Bouvet. In late 1668 and early 1669 Verbiest successfully challenged the calendar work of a rival at the Astronomical Bureau, and the Jesuits became useful at court as astronomers, cartographers, and interpreters. Kangxi's attitude to Christianity was practical and later grew more difficult, which Chapter 50 discusses. The court worked in several languages, including Manchu, Chinese, and Mongolian.

Military Ruler and Inner Asian Sovereign

Kangxi was also a military ruler. He led hunts at the Mulan grounds in the north, which maintained martial skills and ties with Mongol nobles, traveled to the Manchu homeland, and in the 1690s campaigned in person against the Zunghar leader Galdan, a subject that Chapter 48 treats in depth. His reign demonstrates how Qing rulers performed several political identities at once: Confucian monarch, Manchu khan, military ruler, patron of scholarship, and Inner Asian sovereign. His rule was not free of difficulty: in his later years a succession conflict over the heir Yinreng, who was twice deposed, damaged court politics.

Kangxi's personal governing practice shaped the later Qing court. He read and answered memorials directly and cultivated confidential correspondence with trusted officials, including bondservant managers in Jiangnan who reported on local conditions. He relied on a mix of Manchu nobles and Han scholar-advisers, such as those in his study group, and was alert to factions among them. River works illustrate his method. Jin Fu's projects included dredging and new channels, among them the Zhong River, completed by 1687 to divert grain traffic away from a dangerous stretch of the Yellow River. During his 1689 southern tour, Kangxi inspected the canal and discussed the project directly with Jin Fu. Such projects served the state's food supply as well as its claim to care for the people.

The southern tours served several political purposes at once. They let Kangxi inspect river works, hear complaints, and reward officials, and they brought him to Jiangnan, where memory of the conquest was strongest, as a ruler who honored Confucius at Qufu and the Ming founder's tomb. Trusted bondservant correspondents such as Cao Yin, who held the post of textile commissioner at Jiangning, sent confidential reports on officials, harvests, grain prices, and local opinion, giving the emperor information outside the regular bureaucratic channels. River management tied these themes together: a working grain-transport system fed the capital and the Banner garrisons, flood control protected the tax base, and visible success supported the claim to rule well. Kangxi's governing style thus combined personal supervision with reliance on institutions he had inherited.

Historical Vocabulary
Legitimacy

Legitimacy is the acceptance of a ruler's right to rule. Qing legitimacy was not one thing: it rested at once on Confucian ritual and scholarship, Banner military strength, Manchu and Mongol ties, and the results of effective government. Different audiences were addressed differently.

Section 46.8

Administration, Taxation, and Scholarship

Rebuilding Revenue

Military victory produces a regime; routine administration makes it durable. After the wars the Qing restored county government and rebuilt registers. The core of revenue was the land tax, built on Ming practice, including the Single Whip combination of land and labor levies, and on the revised tax schedules compiled in the 1650s. The state also drew on the salt monopoly, grain tribute, and, after 1684, maritime customs. The court granted tax remissions to disaster and war areas, which served both relief and recovery. Local magistrates carried out the work through clerks and gentry, which created chances for evasion and informal charges that the court tried, with mixed results, to regulate.

The 1712 Decision

In 1712 Kangxi declared that the ding quota would be fixed at the level of the 1711 assessment, and that newly born population would not raise it. This did not abolish taxes and did not mean the state stopped counting people. Ding had become increasingly a fiscal unit and not a simple head count, and the freeze fixed one component of the assessment. It brought short-term relief and political credit, but it complicated later efforts to match revenue to population, and Chapter 47 takes up the reforms by which the Yongzheng emperor later merged ding into the land tax.

Scholarship as Statecraft

The Qing also invested in scholarship. The Sacred Edict of 1670 distributed moral maxims to be read to communities, linking Confucian ethics with local order. In 1679 the court held a special examination for eminent scholars, selecting fifty men, including some with loyalist backgrounds, and assigned many of them to the Hanlin Academy to compile the Ming History, completed in 1739. The court sponsored dictionaries and other projects, among them the Kangxi Dictionary of 1716, and promoted Zhu Xi's interpretation of the classics. Patronage attracted talent, signaled orthodox rulership, and absorbed former Ming elites. Not everyone accepted: some declined to attend, and others served without giving up private loyalties.

Patronage was accompanied by coercion. The Dai Mingshi case began in 1711 after his Nanshan ji was denounced for politically sensitive treatment of the Southern Ming, including use of the Yongli reign title; Dai was imprisoned and executed in 1713. It showed that history remained politically sensitive. Scholarship, taxation, and censorship all served to stabilize the regime, though with different effects on different groups.

The connection among these policies is worth stating directly. Taxes funded garrisons and officials, examinations and patronage supplied educated men who staffed offices and mediated with local society, and censorship set the limits of acceptable memory. The court also encouraged the reclamation of abandoned land by offering tax holidays to cultivators, which tied fiscal recovery to agricultural recovery after the wars. Degree-holders, who held privileges and influence in local communities, became partners in this system. At the same time, informal fees collected by local officials, which the court only partly controlled, show how the formal tax structure was supplemented by practices that Chapter 47 examines.

The logic of tax remission illustrates the point. Remitting taxes in devastated districts reduced revenue in the short term, but arrears from ruined counties could rarely be collected, and relief encouraged peasants to return and reclaim land, restoring the base on which later revenue depended. Examinations had a similar function for elites. The rapid restoration of provincial examinations in 1645 and metropolitan examinations in 1646, together with generous early-Qing admission quotas, restored career incentives that tied educated local families to the new state, giving gentry who might otherwise have remained aloof reasons to invest in the new order. The Sacred Edict, the Ming History project, and censorship represented different sides of the same process: teaching approved ethics to villagers, enlisting learned men in shaping the past, and punishing writings that threatened the dynasty's account of itself.

The 1712 ding decision was politically attractive because it signaled benevolence at little immediate cost, since the quota was fixed rather than abandoned. Its fiscal consequences were larger. As population grew, the frozen base became less connected to the number of people, and local officials who needed funds sought surcharges outside the formal schedule, which became a problem for later reformers (Chapter 47).

Section 46.9

Consolidation of Qing Power

What Made Qing Rule Durable?

What made Qing rule durable? The answer combined Banner military organization, Han collaboration, Ming institutional continuity, suppression of rival military power, defeat of loyalist regimes, incorporation of Taiwan, fiscal stabilization, examination government, Confucian legitimacy, Manchu political identity, and frontier diplomacy. No single element was sufficient, and each depended on others. Fiscal stability paid the garrisons, secure garrisons protected the tax base, and scholarly patronage turned potential opponents into participants. The treaty at Nerchinsk in 1689 shows that consolidation also involved the northern frontier, where negotiation with Russia reduced the risk of a second front while the court dealt with Mongol and Zunghar questions.

Comparison clarifies this. The Ming had fallen amid interacting fiscal, environmental, military, political, and rebel crises (Chapter 45); the rebel regimes lacked administrative continuity, and the Southern Ming courts and the feudatories lacked unified command. The Qing combined a disciplined army, a functioning bureaucracy, and the flexibility to bargain, then eliminate, its rivals.

Not every problem was solved. The Zunghar threat continued, Banner finances were stretched, the 1712 freeze left a fiscal puzzle, the succession crisis of the late reign damaged the court, and ethnic distinctions remained sources of tension. By the end of Kangxi's reign in 1722, the Qing was no longer simply a conquest government holding Beijing. It had become a durable imperial state, though not every challenge was solved.

Comparison with other regimes of the period adds depth. The rebel governments of Li Zicheng and Zhang Xianzhong had not built durable administrations. The Southern Ming courts were weakened by factionalism and dependence on generals. The Zheng regime had commercial strength but a limited territorial base, and the feudatories relied on regional resources without a clear claim to the empire. By contrast, the Qing could draw on a hereditary military organization, an existing bureaucracy, and the experience of earlier conquest dynasties in the north, while presenting itself in Confucian terms. These advantages were not guaranteed; they depended on choices such as the 1673 decision to confront the feudatories.

Each comparison clarifies a different advantage. Li Zicheng's Shun regime held the capital for weeks, with little administrative base, and fell to the Banner and Wu Sangui forces at Shanhai Pass. The Southern Ming courts commanded real armies but divided over leadership and relied on generals who might bargain or defect. The Zheng regime had commercial wealth and a strong fleet, but its territorial base on the coast and Taiwan was too small to challenge the interior. The feudatories controlled armies and revenue, but lacked a shared program and a clear claim that could unite regional elites. The Qing, by contrast, combined a hereditary conquest army, surrendered Han military capacity, an inherited nationwide bureaucracy, a credible tax system, flexible incorporation of elites, multiple languages and imperial identities, and enough coercive strength to eliminate rival centers one at a time. Durability came from the combination, not from any one element.

Cause and Effect

Why Did Qing Rule Become Durable?

Causes

Ming collapse left a power vacuum; Banner forces and Han allies provided military strength; Ming bureaucratic structures remained usable.

Transformation

Conquest was converted into government through administration, repression, bargaining, and the defeat of feudatories and the Zheng.

Effects

A secure imperial center, restored revenue, a recruited scholarly elite, a settled coastal frontier, and a basis for eighteenth-century expansion.

Complexity

Resistance, ethnic distinction, fiscal limits, and frontier problems persisted, so consolidation was real but incomplete.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Ming to Qing Government

What changed: The ruling house, the Banner system, Manchu-Han dual appointments, the queue, and the distribution of power at the top.

What stayed the same: Counties, ministries, tax registers, examinations, and Confucian official culture.

Who experienced which: Peasants and clerks often saw continuity in local administration; Banner families, loyalists, and Han men subject to the queue experienced sharper change.

Everyday Life and Social History

For farming families, consolidation meant recovery from war: returning to fields, facing tax collectors, and benefiting where remissions applied. For coastal families, evacuation meant displacement. For men, the queue changed daily appearance and carried symbolic weight. In Banner garrison cities, bannermen and civilians lived side by side in segregated quarters, with distinct legal status. Evidence for ordinary experience is thinner than for officials, and comes through gazetteers, local records, and later memory.

China and the Wider World

Early Qing consolidation was tied to wider connections, and the Qing acted in them, not only under pressure from them.

  1. 1624–1662 — Dutch colony on Taiwan, a link to European maritime commerce, ended by Zheng Chenggong.
  2. 1636–1637 — Qing invasion made Joseon Korea a subordinate state; Joseon elites often kept private Ming sympathies, so relations with the Qing were shaped by both politics and memory.
  3. 1661–1683 — Zheng trade with Japan, Manila, and Southeast Asia sustained a maritime regime; the Qing then reopened coastal trade in 1684.
  4. 1669 onward — Jesuit astronomers and interpreters served Kangxi, carrying European knowledge into the court.
  5. 1689 — Treaty of Nerchinsk, a negotiated settlement with the Russian empire.

Section 46.10

Chapter Review and Early Qing Checkpoint

This chapter traced the Qing transformation of conquest into government: the 1644 entry into Beijing; military occupation; accommodation and coercion; Banner garrisons beside bureaucratic continuity; the suppression of loyalist resistance; the Three Feudatories crisis; the incorporation of Taiwan; fiscal and administrative stabilization; scholarly legitimation; and a durable imperial order under Kangxi. Chapter 45 asked how the Ming crisis became a Qing conquest. This chapter answered how the Qing made conquest last.

The closing question is why Qing rule survived when so many conquest regimes and rebel states of the seventeenth century did not. Students should separate the contributing factors: military capacity (Banners and Green Standard), institutional continuity (counties, ministries, examinations), political adaptation (dual appointments, Confucian language), collaboration (Han officials and generals), coercion (the queue order, censorship, evacuation), fiscal capacity (remissions, registers, customs), and legitimacy (patronage, ritual, results). Then ask which were necessary, which were sufficient, and how they depended on one another.

This section functions as a historical thinking checkpoint. Students should separate necessary causes, without which Qing consolidation could not have occurred, from sufficient causes, which together explain it. Military force was probably necessary but not sufficient, since conquest alone had not produced stable government. Ask also whether durability depended more on continuity with the Ming, such as counties, registers, and examinations, or on institutions that were specifically Qing, such as the Banners and the dual appointments, and what evidence from the chapter supports each view.

Chapter Summary

This chapter examined how the Qing transformed conquest into government. It began with a regime that held Beijing but not the empire, relied on Banner power, and inherited Ming administrative machinery.

The Banners organized households, privilege, and loyalty, while ordinary administration continued through Ming-style counties and ministries. Accommodation and coercion operated together, as the queue order and the retention of Han officials show. Loyalism took many forms, and its scholarly branch influenced later intellectual life.

The Three Feudatories revolt showed that collaborators could become rivals, and its defeat removed a major internal threat. The Zheng regime and the Qing conquest of Taiwan added a maritime and island dimension, on land with Indigenous, Dutch, and settler histories. Kangxi's reign, with its fiscal measures, scholarly patronage, and diplomacy, completed the shift from conquest to durable empire.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that conquest is not the same as rule. The Qing's durability came from adapting existing institutions, building new ones, and managing rivals, not from one decisive victory.

It shaped later Chinese history by creating the state that Chapters 47 and 48 examine: a Confucian bureaucracy and a Manchu-led, multiethnic, Inner Asian empire. It also connects China to wider Asian and global history through maritime Taiwan, Dutch colonial activity, Jesuit science, and the negotiation with Russia.

Understanding the early Qing as contingent and contested helps students see Chinese history as regionally varied, politically changing, and unfinished.
