Part IV — Division, Migration, and Religious Transformation

The Three Kingdoms

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

In 1996, builders excavating in the city of Changsha, in modern Hunan, broke into an old well sealed with tens of thousands of bamboo slips. Most had been written in the 230s CE by clerks of the state of Wu. They record households and their members, grain collected as tax, cloth and labor owed to the government, and seed grain lent to poor families. No general appears in them and no famous battle is described. They show something the later legends rarely mention: a government that had to count its people, feed its soldiers, and keep farmers on the land in order to survive.

This chapter studies the sixty years or so that followed the practical collapse of Han central authority. Historians conventionally date the Three Kingdoms period from 220, when the last Han emperor gave up his throne, to 280, when the Western Jin conquered the state of Wu. Chapter 18 asked how effective Han authority broke apart. Chapter 19 asks a different question: how did three rival regimes, Cao Wei in the north, Shu Han in the southwest, and Eastern Wu in the lower and middle Yangtze, turn military coalitions and regional power bases into functioning states? And why could none of them reunify the former Han realm?

The chapter's central argument is that the Three Kingdoms period was not simply an interval of chaos between unified dynasties. Political fragmentation produced three competing imperial projects. Each adapted Han institutions to different geography, populations, resources, and military circumstances. Each also had to solve the same practical problems of registering households, recruiting officials, extracting taxes, feeding armies, winning over local elites, and justifying its rule. Their rivalry was shaped by unequal resources. Wei held the largest population and the northern plain. Shu held the productive but enclosed Sichuan Basin. Wu held the Yangtze and its waterways. The rivalry was also shaped by the effects of prolonged warfare, which drove migration, displaced elites, and reorganized society.

Students should notice a distinction that runs through the whole chapter: regional power formation is not the same as formal dynastic foundation. The great strategic turning point, the resistance to Cao Cao at Red Cliffs in the winter of 208–209, did not create three kingdoms. Cao Pi founded Wei in 220. Liu Bei claimed the imperial title of Han in 221. Sun Quan, whose regime had long been an independent power, did not become emperor until 229. Cao Cao, the most famous figure of the age, never became emperor and never formally founded a dynasty. Keeping these dates apart is part of reading the period accurately.

The chapter also deals with a problem of evidence that is unusually visible for this period. Most readers meet the Three Kingdoms first through the Romance of the Three Kingdoms, a fourteenth-century novel traditionally attributed to Luo Guanzhong and later revised, one of the most widely read works in the Chinese literary tradition. The novel is valuable evidence for how later centuries remembered the era. It is not evidence for what people said or did in the third century. Historians rely instead on Chen Shou's late-third-century Records of the Three Kingdoms (Sanguozhi), the fifth-century annotations of Pei Songzhi, inscriptions, poems, archaeological finds, and documents such as the Zoumalou slips from Wu. Section 19.9 examines the relationship between these layers of evidence.

Geography shapes the story throughout. The North China Plain gave Wei its population and cavalry but exposed it to steppe frontiers. The Sichuan Basin gave Shu wealth and defensibility but limited its ability to project power. The Yangtze gave Wu its naval strength but also divided its territory into river-oriented zones held together by commanders and local elites. The chapter therefore treats terrain, rivers, grain, and population as causes alongside personality and leadership.

The period also matters for what came after. Later institutions developed from third-century experiments, notably the Nine-Rank Rectifying System of Wei, military-agricultural colonies, and the growth of southern government at Jianye. The migrations of the period prepared the southward shift examined in Chapter 21. The Sima family that finally replaced Wei and briefly reunified the realm is introduced only at the end of this chapter, because Chapter 20 takes up Western Jin and the renewed fragmentation that followed. Chapter 23 will draw together the religious and cultural transformations of the wider Age of Division.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace the process by which Han central authority gave way to regional power blocs between 196 and 220, and explain why 184, 189, and 208 are not the moment the Three Kingdoms began.
  2. Explain how Cao Cao converted military success into a durable northern regime, and distinguish his career from Cao Pi's foundation of the Wei dynasty in 220.
  3. Describe the formation of Shu Han and explain how Liu Bei's claim to the Han inheritance shaped its legitimacy.
  4. Analyze how Sun Quan and the Jiangdong elites built a Yangtze-based state, and why Sun Quan's imperial title came only in 229.
  5. Assess the historical significance of Red Cliffs while distinguishing well-supported evidence from later legend.
  6. Compare the governments, armies, and fiscal systems of Wei, Shu, and Wu.
  7. Evaluate how agriculture, colonies, registration, and regional economies allowed each state to survive prolonged war.
  8. Interpret excavated administrative documents, especially the Zoumalou slips, as evidence for ordinary life and local government.
  9. Describe the effects of warfare, migration, and displacement on families, elites, and frontier communities.
  10. Distinguish Chen Shou's Records of the Three Kingdoms, Pei Songzhi's annotations, and the Romance of the Three Kingdoms as different layers of evidence.
  11. Explain why "the Three Kingdoms period was simply chaos" is an inadequate description of 220–280.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did rulers who began as commanders in a collapsing empire build states with tax systems, offices, and legal codes?
  2. Why did the Han imperial idea survive the loss of Han central power, and how did each regime use it?
  3. How did geography give Wei, Shu, and Wu different strengths and different limits?
  4. Why did Red Cliffs prevent rapid northern conquest of the south, and why did it not by itself produce three kingdoms?
  5. How did states secure grain, labor, and loyalty from populations that war had scattered?
  6. What can excavated administrative documents tell us that court histories cannot?
  7. How did local elites, refugees, and non-Han communities affect the making of each state?
  8. Why did the Three Kingdoms become a central story in later Chinese, East Asian, and world popular culture, and how does that memory differ from third-century evidence?

Historical Context

By the 180s the Han empire, examined in Chapters 14 through 18, was under multiple pressures. Court factions, eunuch power, land concentration, fiscal strain, epidemic, and the Yellow Turban rebellion of 184 had eroded the government's capacity to raise revenue and control armies. Officials appointed to suppress rebellion built up regional forces of their own. In 189 the warlord Dong Zhuo seized the capital at Luoyang and installed the young Emperor Xian. Between 190 and the mid-190s a coalition of regional commanders fought Dong Zhuo and then each other. The emperor was moved from Luoyang to Chang'an, wandered, and returned to the ruined Luoyang before Cao Cao took him under his protection in 196.

Han institutions did not disappear during these decades. The empire's administrative map of provinces (zhou), commanderies (jun), and counties (xian) remained the basic vocabulary of government. Governors and inspectors who had once supervised officials became military rulers, and provincial armies replaced central ones. The ideology of Han rule, the belief that legitimate government required a Son of Heaven, ritual observance, and a moral bureaucracy, remained powerful even among men who had stopped obeying the throne. This helps explain why most warlords, with only a few brief exceptions, claimed to act in the name of the Han rather than to replace it.

The geography of the former empire divided it into zones with different resources. The North China Plain and the middle Yellow River, home to the Han heartland and the largest concentration of households, had suffered the heaviest devastation. The Guanzhong plain and the northwest, connected to the Qiang, Di, and other frontier populations, were long contested. The Sichuan Basin, or Yi Province, was fertile, well watered, and shielded by mountains. Jing Province in the middle Yangtze linked the north to the south and controlled river access to both Sichuan and the lower Yangtze. Jiangdong, the lower Yangtze region south of the river, was more thinly settled and included communities of local peoples often grouped in Chinese sources as Shanyue. Farther south lay Jiaozhi and other regions of the far south, only loosely integrated into Han administration. Chapter 2 introduced these regions, and Chapter 16 showed how Han frontier policy had bound many of them into the empire.

Registered population had fallen sharply, while warfare, famine, epidemic disease, and displacement also caused substantial real demographic loss whose precise scale is difficult to quantify. In the mid-second century Han registration recorded roughly ten million households and more than fifty million people. The war, famine, epidemic, and flight of the following decades reduced the registered population and altered where people lived, and figures reported for the end of the period are not directly comparable with Han totals or with each other, because each state counted and taxed differently, and some populations were held outside the registers by powerful families. Nevertheless, the decline in registered northern population and the movement of people southward, together with the growth of settlement in Jing, Yi, and Jiangdong are among the period's most important trends. Chapters 20 and 21 will show it continuing.

Students should also avoid thinking of "China" as a single fixed political entity in this period. The Han empire had claimed to be the universal government of "all under Heaven" (tianxia), yet its actual territory had shifted repeatedly. The three successor states each claimed to be, or to represent, the legitimate government of the whole, and each also governed a specific region with its own institutions. None was a nation-state, and none had a culturally uniform population. The peoples of Liaodong, the northern steppe frontier, the mountains of the southwest, and the far south were connected to these states in different ways, and their own histories shaped the period.

Section 19.1

The Breakdown of Han Central Authority

Chapter 18 traced the long crisis of the Han. This section is a bridge. It picks up the story in 196, when the decentralization already described became the framework for the political order of the next quarter-century. The Three Kingdoms did not suddenly appear in 184, when the Yellow Turban rebellion began, in 189, when Dong Zhuo entered Luoyang, or even immediately after the battle of Red Cliffs. They emerged gradually as regional commanders consolidated territory, absorbed rivals, and found that the machinery of Han government could be reused for their own purposes.

In 196 Cao Cao, who had by then built a powerful army in the Yellow River plain, intercepted Emperor Xian's court after its flight from Chang'an and brought it to Xu, in what is now central Henan. The move was consequential in two ways. It gave Cao Cao a claim to speak in the emperor's name, and thereby to issue appointments, ranks, and edicts that carried the residual authority of the Han. It also tied him to the court's obligations and to the resentment of rivals, who could accuse him of holding the emperor captive. Cao Cao's opponents called this the manipulation of a puppet. His supporters described it as protecting the dynasty. Emperor Xian retained formal authority but had little independent military or political power; Cao Cao controlled the court environment, while imperial appointments and edicts retained real political value and affected how other rulers behaved. The empire still formally existed, and after 196 it would be inaccurate to call the government of Emperor Xian "Wei."

Other regional powers were consolidating at the same time. In the northeast, Yuan Shao controlled a large area of the plain north of the Yellow River, including the provinces of Ji, Qing, You, and Bing. His cousin Yuan Shu, in the Huai region, went further than most by proclaiming himself emperor in 197; the attempt collapsed by 199, and the fate of his regime showed how dangerous an open imperial claim could be without a strong base. In Jing Province, Liu Biao governed as a relatively stable regional ruler, and his territory attracted refugees and scholars from the war-torn north. In Yi Province, Liu Zhang had inherited power from his father. In the northwest, commanders such as Ma Teng and Han Sui held Liang Province, and further northeast the Gongsun family controlled Liaodong. In the lower Yangtze, Sun Ce, son of the earlier commander Sun Jian, was conquering Jiangdong.

The contest between Cao Cao and Yuan Shao was the great test of the north. In 200, at Guandu near the Yellow River, Cao Cao faced an opponent with a larger army and greater apparent resources. Sources emphasize the role of supply. Cao Cao's raid on Yuan Shao's grain depot at Wuchao is reported as a decisive moment, after which the larger army lost cohesion. As a matter of historical method, students should treat exact numbers reported for the two armies with caution, since they come from later narrative accounts rather than audited records. Yet the outline is clear. Guandu was won by a combination of defensive resilience, logistics, and the defection of some of Yuan Shao's officers, and it made Cao Cao the leading ruler in the north. It has no connection with Red Cliffs eight years later, though the two are often confused in popular memory.

Yuan Shao died in 202, and his sons quarreled. Cao Cao took the northern center of Ye in 204, defeated Yuan Shao's heirs and allies over the following years, and in 207 campaigned against the Wuhuan on the northeastern frontier, removing a source of support for the Yuan family. By 208, when Cao Cao took the title of chancellor and abolished the earlier Han structure of the Three Excellencies, he held most of northern China. That year Liu Biao died, and Cao Cao turned south. The stage was set for the great confrontation examined in Section 19.5.

The result was a three-region pattern: a northern regime centered on the plain, a southwestern power taking shape in Yi, and a lower-Yangtze state in Jiangdong, with Jing Province in the middle as the contested pivot. The sections that follow examine each in turn before returning to the moment when the balance among them was set.

Section 19.2

Cao Cao and the Formation of Wei

Cao Cao's importance lies less in his victories than in what he did with them. Born into a family connected to the Han court through his grandfather, a eunuch official, he began as a local official and cavalry officer, rose during the suppression of the Yellow Turbans, and built his first large army in 192 partly by absorbing surrendered Yellow Turban troops from Qing Province. Chapter 18 introduced the role of such rebel-derived troops. Cao Cao's career shows how the Han state's collapse allowed a talented commander to convert them into a stable force.

His first challenge was food. Armies of this scale needed grain, and the plain had been devastated by war, famine, and flight. In 196, at Xu, Cao Cao began developing agricultural colonies (tuntian), in which land was assigned to registered farmers or soldiers, who owed a large share of the harvest to the government in return for seed, tools, and protection. Colonies were not a Cao Cao invention. The Han had used military colonies on its frontiers, as Chapter 16 showed. Cao Cao's innovation was to expand them across the interior, to use them for civilians as well as soldiers, and to tie them to an administration that could count the harvest. Officials such as Zao Zhi and Han Hao helped organize the system, and later administrators such as Ren Jun extended it. The colonies gave the regime a reliable grain supply, a way to settle refugees, and a means of recruiting soldiers and laborers.

His second challenge was people. Cao Cao's recruitment practices were unusually open to talent. One of Cao Cao's recruitment orders of 210, the Seeking Worthies Order (Qiu xian ling), called for capable men even when their moral reputations were imperfect. This should not be confused with the different 210 political self-defense, Relinquishing My Fiefs and Clarifying My Basic Aims, examined in the Primary Source Window later in this chapter. Both texts should be read as political statements rather than neutral policy descriptions. In practice he relied both on old scholarly families, who provided prestige and administrative skill, and on newcomers whose loyalty was personal. Balancing them was a continuing difficulty and would lead to the institutional solution of the Nine-Rank system under his son.

Cao Cao's authority was expressed through titles rather than a dynastic claim. In 208 he became chancellor. In 213 he was made Duke of Wei, in 216 King of Wei, and he held a large fief in the north with its own administration, while the Han emperor remained at Xu. These steps advanced Cao Cao toward the throne but never crossed the line. Ancient sources record that when advisors urged him to take the imperial title, he declined. Interpretations of this restraint range from genuine reluctance, to prudent avoidance of the fate of Yuan Shu, to a decision to leave the final step to his heir. All are plausible and none can be confirmed.

His government also made choices affecting frontier populations. In the northwest and north, Cao Cao resettled Di groups and other communities into more controlled areas, and in 216 he reorganized the Xiongnu of Bing Province into five divisions under Chinese supervision. These measures gave Wei military manpower and reduced frontier threats, and they also left consequences for the demographic and political map of the north that Chapter 20 will examine. Cao Cao's campaigns in the northwest, including his defeat of Ma Chao and Han Sui in 211 and the acceptance of Zhang Lu's surrender at Hanzhong in 215, extended his reach toward Sichuan and prepared the later contest with Liu Bei.

Cao Cao died in early 220 at Luoyang, and his son Cao Pi succeeded him as King of Wei. Later that year, under pressure that historians regard as more coerced than voluntary, the last Han emperor, Xian, formally abdicated. Cao Pi ascended the throne and founded the Wei dynasty. Cao Cao was posthumously honored as its founding ruler, but the founding was his son's act. Students should keep this in mind whenever popular accounts describe Cao Cao as "the emperor of Wei."

Section 19.3

Liu Bei and the Creation of Shu Han

Liu Bei's career differed sharply from Cao Cao's. He did not begin with an established base and he did not hold a single territory for long. Beginning in the 180s as a commander in the suppression of the Yellow Turbans, he served, and fought against, a succession of patrons, including Gongsun Zan, Tao Qian, Lü Bu, Cao Cao, Yuan Shao, and Liu Biao, gaining a following of capable officers, among them Guan Yu, Zhang Fei, and eventually Zhuge Liang, who joined him around 207. This mobility mattered. Liu Bei survived defeat repeatedly by keeping a loyal core of followers and a reputation for generosity and persistence, but it also meant that he lacked a territorial administration until his forties.

His claim to represent the Han imperial house was central to his political identity. Liu Bei belonged to the Liu surname and described himself as a distant descendant of the Han imperial family. Historians cannot independently verify the genealogy, and one should not treat the claim as either proven or fraudulent. What matters historically is its use. In a world where legitimacy depended on the Han inheritance, a Liu who claimed the dynasty's line held a powerful argument against Cao Cao, who ruled through an emperor he controlled, and against anyone proposing to end the dynasty.

After Cao Cao's occupation of Jing in 208, Liu Bei retreated with a mass of refugees and allied with Sun Quan, a partnership that succeeded at Red Cliffs. In its aftermath he gained control of parts of Jing, including the southern commanderies of the province, and married into the Sun family. Sun Quan's and Liu Bei's regimes disagreed for years about who controlled the central Yangtze stronghold of Jiangling, a dispute later summarized in the phrase "the loan of Jing," though historians question whether it was really a loan or an informal arrangement that each side described differently.

The decisive step came in 211–214, when Liu Bei entered Yi Province at the invitation of its governor Liu Zhang, who wanted help against Zhang Lu's religious regime in Hanzhong. Liu Bei turned on his host, and after fighting and negotiation Liu Zhang surrendered Chengdu in 214. Liu Bei now had the productive Sichuan Basin, an area protected by mountain barriers and connected to Jing by the Yangtze route and to Hanzhong and the Guanzhong plain by mountain passes. The new regime had to reconcile two groups: Liu Zhang's former officials, who were locally rooted, and Liu Bei's own followers, who were outsiders. Zhuge Liang, Fa Zheng, and others created a legal code and administrative system that tried to enforce order while integrating the local elites, and the tensions between the groups remained a feature of Shu politics for years.

In 219 Liu Bei defeated Cao Cao's forces at Hanzhong, killing the Wei commander Xiahou Yuan, and took the title King of Hanzhong. That same year, however, Guan Yu, who held Jing, attacked northward toward Fan, was hit from behind by Wu forces under Lü Meng, and was captured and executed. The loss of Jing cost Shu its middle-Yangtze position and left it confined to the west. After Cao Pi ended the Han in 220, Liu Bei proclaimed himself emperor of Han in 221 at Chengdu, presenting his regime as the continuation of the dynasty rather than a new one. Historians usually call it "Shu Han" to distinguish it from other Han states.

Liu Bei's remaining aim was to recover Jing. In 222 his large expedition against Wu was defeated at Yiling (also called Xiaoting) by Lu Xun, apparently through a combination of Wu's defensive patience and fire attacks on the long Shu camp lines. Liu Bei withdrew to Baidicheng and died in 223, leaving the young Liu Shan on the throne and Zhuge Liang as regent. Shu subsequently reconciled with Wu, pacified the southwestern region of Nanzhong in 225, and mounted a series of expeditions toward the north between 228 and 234. Zhuge Liang died on campaign in 234. Shu's small population and resource base limited its offensive capacity, and its later history is largely a story of defensive survival under officials such as Jiang Wan, Fei Yi, and Jiang Wei until its conquest by Wei in 263.

Section 19.4

Sun Quan and the State of Eastern Wu

The state of Eastern Wu grew out of a family enterprise. Sun Jian, a commander in the campaigns against the Yellow Turbans and Dong Zhuo, was killed in 191 or 192. His son Sun Ce, taking a small force and the backing of allied officers, conquered the lower Yangtze region of Jiangdong between 194 and 200. Sun Ce was assassinated in 200, and his younger brother Sun Quan, then about eighteen, took over, aided by the veteran advisors Zhang Zhao and Zhou Yu.

Jiangdong differed from the northern plain. It was less densely populated and had been less deeply integrated into Han administration, and its society included local Chinese families of long standing, such as the Gu, Lu, Zhu, and Zhang of Wu commandery, as well as communities of mountain peoples that Chinese sources call Shanyue. Sun Quan's regime could not simply impose itself. It negotiated with these local elites, granted them offices and military commands, and gradually turned them into supporters. Northern refugees who had arrived in Jiangdong, such as Lu Su and Zhang Zhao, also served in the government, producing a mixed elite of southern families and northern émigrés. This alliance between local elites and the ruling house, and the tension within it, is a central theme in Wu's political history.

The environment was decisive for Wu's military strength. The Yangtze and its tributaries formed a river network in which ships, not cavalry, decided battles. Wu built and maintained a substantial fleet, and its commanders developed expertise in naval and riverine operations. Sun Quan shifted his headquarters to Jianye, modern Nanjing (formerly Moling), around 211 and fortified it further in 212. He moved to Wuchang in 221, took the imperial title there in 229, and returned to Jianye later that year. Wu used the river as a defensive line and a means of moving troops between the lower and middle Yangtze. Its weakness was that its strength depended on commanders who held large private followings, some of them hereditary, so that the ruler had to reward and manage powerful generals.

Sun Quan's relations with the other powers were pragmatic. In 208–209 he joined Liu Bei against Cao Cao. In 219 his forces under Lü Meng seized Jing and killed Guan Yu, ending the alliance with Liu Bei, and Sun Quan then briefly accepted Wei's investiture as King of Wu in 221 to secure his northern flank while he prepared to face Liu Bei. After defeating Liu Bei at Yiling in 222, Wu resumed friendlier relations with Shu. In 229 Sun Quan took the imperial title at Wuchang and moved back to Jianye later that year. This sequence shows why one should not treat all three imperial states as formally founded in 220. Wu was an autonomous regime long before it became an empire.

Wu's expansion within the south was a state-building project of its own. The regime brought further areas of the Yangtze basin and southeast under administration, fought and negotiated with local populations, and pushed into Jiaozhi, where the local governor Shi Xie had ruled a semi-autonomous region until he acknowledged Sun Quan's authority; Wu formally reorganized the far south into Jiaozhou and Guangzhou in the 220s. Wu also sent expeditions overseas, including a force to a place called Yizhou in 230, whose location has sometimes been identified with Taiwan but remains uncertain, and diplomatic missions to Southeast Asia, which Section 19.7 and the "China and the Wider World" feature will consider. In such campaigns, one aim was manpower. Wu's need for soldiers and laborers drove repeated military operations against mountain communities and often incorporated them into the state's registered population and military households.

Sun Quan ruled until 252, with periodic succession disputes, court factionalism, and disputes over the treatment of great families. His successors were less able. Wu survived until 280, when Sun Hao, its last ruler, surrendered to Jin forces. Wu's late history is often told as decline under a tyrannical ruler, but such accounts derive mostly from Jin-era histories, and their perspective should be treated carefully.

Section 19.5

The Battle of Red Cliffs and the Balance of Power

Red Cliffs was the strategic turning point of the period. In 208, after Liu Biao's death, his son Liu Cong surrendered Jing Province to Cao Cao, who gained the province's fleet and resources and the strategic pivot for an advance downriver on Jiangdong. Liu Bei, who had been sheltering in Jing, fled south with a large following and joined forces with Sun Quan's envoy Lu Su and the Wu commander Zhou Yu. Sun Quan's court debated whether to submit. According to the sources, the decision to fight was strongly influenced by Zhou Yu and Lu Su, who argued that Cao Cao's army was not invincible.

The fighting occurred in the winter of 208–209 along the Yangtze in the region of Jing Province, near a place called Red Cliffs (Chibi). The exact location remains debated, and several candidate sites along the river have been proposed. What sources agree on is broad. Cao Cao's forces, exhausted by a rapid advance, were weakened by disease and unfamiliar riverine conditions. His northern troops had little experience of naval operations. The Wu commander Huang Gai is reported to have staged a fire attack against Cao Cao's ships, which were positioned close together, and the resulting damage forced Cao Cao's army to retreat overland. Cao Cao withdrew northward, leaving forces at Jiangling under Cao Ren; Zhou Yu took the city after a long siege in 209. Liu Bei took several southern commanderies of Jing.

Students should be careful with the numbers. Later accounts describe Cao Cao's army in the hundreds of thousands and the allied forces in the tens of thousands. Historians are skeptical of such figures, which served narrative purposes and cannot be checked against audited records. Many of the most famous episodes, including Zhuge Liang's summoning of a southeast wind, the "borrowing of arrows" with straw-covered boats, and the elaborate personal duels, come from later storytelling, especially the Romance of the Three Kingdoms, and are not established history. Even the sober core of the battle depends on sources compiled later, notably Chen Shou's account and Pei Songzhi's annotations.

The consequences were significant but delayed. Red Cliffs blocked Cao Cao's immediate conquest of the south, kept Sun Quan's regime alive, and gave Liu Bei the room to establish a territorial base. Cao Cao held the north and the Han emperor, but his path to reunifying the empire by rapid advance had failed. It did not, however, create three kingdoms. The outcome in 209 was an uneasy balance: Cao Cao held the north and much of the middle Yangtze's northern bank, Sun Quan held Jiangdong and the lower Yangtze, and Liu Bei held a fragment of Jing without a stable base. What produced the eventual three-state system were events afterward: Liu Bei's acquisition of Yi in 214, the division of Jing in 219, the Han abdication in 220, and the competing imperial claims of 220–229. This is the point of the Historical Debate and Cause and Effect features in Section 19.10.

Red Cliffs also shows how warfare depended on the material environment. Northern armies accustomed to cavalry and land campaigns were vulnerable to disease in the humid south and to naval operations. Wu's advantages lay in the river and its fleet. Once Cao Cao's advance failed, the difficulty of crossing large rivers, sustaining armies far from northern granaries, and governing populations that had not previously supported him became a durable limit on northern power. It would take over seven decades, and a change of dynasty, to overcome it.

Section 19.6

Government and Military Organization

A history that stops with generals cannot explain how these states lasted. All three regimes retained much of the structure of Han government: a ruler at the top, ministers and a secretariat handling documents, provinces, commanderies, and counties administered by appointed officials, and a body of law and ritual expressed in Han vocabulary. All three also adapted this structure to conditions of nearly permanent war. The result was three variations on the same inherited pattern.

Wei. As the state with the largest population and territory, Wei had the most elaborate government. Cao Pi strengthened the secretariat and its director, whose office became the central channel of decision-making. He also restricted the influence of eunuchs and the families of empresses, both major sources of instability in the late Han, and, in a decision with serious later consequences, kept the imperial princes under close supervision, denying them the independent power that earlier dynasties had used to protect the throne. A new legal code was compiled in the 220s and 230s, associated with Chen Qun and other officials. The military was organized around hereditary military households (shibing), whose members served as soldiers in return for land and special status, and around regional commands, the most important along the southern and western frontiers, whose commanders combined civil and military authority. This system produced capable armies. It also placed power in the hands of great regional generals, and Sima Yi and his family would eventually use it to displace the Cao ruling house, as Chapter 20 shows.

Wei's most important institutional innovation was the Nine-Rank Rectifying System (jiupin zhongzheng zhi), introduced in 220 and associated with the official Chen Qun. Officials called Impartial Assessors (zhongzheng) in each commandery and province assessed the character and ability of local candidates for office and placed them in nine grades, and their rankings guided appointment. The system was designed to give the court a means of selecting officials when the Han recommendation system had broken down and when war had scattered families. It is not an examination system. It relied on reputation and the judgment of local assessors, and it therefore favored established families who could command reputation. Over time it hardened into a mechanism for reproducing elite privilege, a development examined in later chapters. Chapter 26 will contrast it with the Tang examination system.

Shu Han. Shu governed a smaller territory with a more concentrated administration. Zhuge Liang, who ran the state for Liu Bei and then for Liu Shan, was known for strict enforcement of law, careful record-keeping, and an emphasis on merit within the regime's own circle. The state operated salt and iron works and a brocade industry, and it depended on maintaining a fragile compromise between the Jing-origin officers who had followed Liu Bei and the Yi Province elites who had served Liu Zhang. The state's capacity for expansion was limited by its population. Its northern expeditions strained supplies, which had to be carried over difficult mountain roads, and Zhuge Liang's successors moved to a more defensive posture until the Wei conquest in 263.

Wu. Wu's government reflected the compromise between the ruling house and regional elites. Sun Quan granted powerful generals hereditary commands over troops and sometimes over households assigned to them, which gave them a personal stake in the regime while limiting the ruler's direct control. The court's key task was managing relations with the great families of Jiangdong and the northern émigrés while creating central offices to check them. Wu also relied on a large fleet and on military settlements and conscripted labor drawn from newly conquered or registered populations. Its capital, Jianye, became the first major imperial center on the lower Yangtze, an important precedent for the Eastern Jin and Southern Dynasties discussed in Chapter 21.

Section 19.7

Agriculture, Population, and Regional Economies

Armies eat, and states that could not feed them did not last. The economic history of the Three Kingdoms is therefore an integral part of its political history. Each state built an economy around its region's resources and its own limits.

The north. Wei's territory had suffered serious depopulation, and the state's first task was to put land back into production. Agricultural colonies were the principal tool. Civilian colonists received oxen and tools, cultivated state land, and gave a large share of the crop, typically a half or more, to the government, while military colonies fed frontier armies. Wei extended the system into the Huai region in the 240s, when the official Deng Ai proposed large-scale colonization to supply campaigns against Wu. Wei also invested in irrigation and canal works in the Huai and Yellow River regions. Currency policy in the north was unstable. Dong Zhuo's debased coinage had damaged confidence in money; in 221 Cao Pi briefly restored Five-Zhu coinage, then abolished it later that year, directing exchange toward grain and silk. Five-Zhu coinage was restored under Cao Rui in 227. The sequence is a reminder that the north's economy in this period leaned heavily on payments in kind.

The southwest. Shu's economy rested on the Sichuan Basin, whose irrigation systems, notably the ancient Dujiangyan works, sustained rice cultivation, and on salt and iron production, which had been important since the Han. Shu brocade was a valuable export and a source of revenue, and Zhuge Liang is reported to have encouraged it. Hanzhong served as a military-agricultural forward base. The basin's productivity supported the state but could not match the north's manpower, and the limits of supply were a central reason for the failure of its northern campaigns. Shu issued its own high-denomination coins.

The Yangtze. Wu's territory was less densely populated but expanding. Rice agriculture on the lower and middle Yangtze depended on managing water, and Wu encouraged settlement and land reclamation, established military colonies, and dug canals in the region around Jianye, including works in the 240s. It also issued large-denomination coins, which were often overvalued and are a topic of scholarly discussion. The state's outward-looking commercial connections included trade along the Yangtze and the southern coast, and contacts with Jiaozhi and Southeast Asia. Population grew as northern refugees arrived and as the state registered previously unregistered communities, but the regime's need for soldiers and laborers meant that it exerted intense pressure on households.

Population. Transmitted administrative figures illustrate the unequal scale of the three states but are not directly comparable censuses. Around 263, Wei was reported to have about 663,000 households and 4.43 million registered persons. At Shu's surrender that year, its records listed about 280,000 households and 940,000 registered civilian men and women, alongside approximately 102,000 soldiers and 40,000 officials. Wu records report about 520,000 households and 2.3 million men and women in 242; at its surrender in 280, roughly 523,000 households and 2.3 million men and women were recorded, with soldiers and officials listed separately. Differences in date, categories, registration, and administrative practice make these figures unsuitable for simple population ratios, and powerful families sheltered dependents outside the registers. They do make Wei's larger registered resource base clear, while the much lower surviving registration figures compared with Han-era totals reflect some combination of demographic loss, migration, underregistration, and differing administrative categories.

The strongest evidence for how ordinary administration worked comes from an archaeological find. In 1996, in Changsha, Hunan, excavators recovered a large cache of bamboo slips and boards from a sealed well at Zoumalou, most dating to the Jiahe era (232–238) of Sun Quan's reign. Numbering well over one hundred thousand slips, tablets, and fragments, they preserve records of household registration, taxes in grain and cloth, labor obligations, and government loans of seed grain to poor families, together with official correspondence. Scholars are still reading and editing them. They document a local government that counted households, assigned obligations, and, at least in some cases, extended state aid to farmers who could not plant their own fields, though the loans also served the state's interest in maintaining taxpayers. The Primary Source Window and Everyday Life features in Section 19.10 examine them.

Section 19.8

Society, Migration, and Cultural Life

War and state-building changed who lived where. Refugees from the north moved into Jing, Yi, and Jiangdong, in flows that began with the wars of the 190s and continued through the third century. Some fled ahead of armies; others were relocated by governments. In the Huai region, for instance, Cao Cao's efforts to move populations inland in the 210s prompted large numbers of residents to flee across the Yangtze to Wu. Refugees often traveled as extended families under the leadership of a lineage head, and elite families brought their followers, retainers, and tenants. Some regimes welcomed them as settlers and soldiers. Others suspected them. The refugees affected the regions they entered, sometimes displacing local families from land, sometimes strengthening local governments.

The states also settled people by design. Military households held hereditary obligations that bound families to service. Colonists were tied to state land. Some captured populations, from campaigns against mountain communities in Wu or against groups in the north and northwest, were resettled as soldiers or laborers. These practices show how the states treated people as a resource to be registered, moved, and put to use, and they help explain why so many people tried to escape registration by taking refuge with powerful families or in frontier areas.

Elite society was also transformed. Great families with a tradition of officeholding and learning survived by moving, by serving new regimes, and by dividing their branches among different states, so that a single lineage could be represented in several courts. Zhuge Liang served in Shu while his brother Zhuge Jin served in Wu, and a cousin served in Wei. This strategy of dispersal protected family interests and reinforced the importance of kinship networks. The Nine-Rank system in Wei gave such families a formal role in the selection of officials, and the Jiangdong families in Wu kept local power by service.

Women's experiences are less visible in the sources, but they are not absent. Chen Shou's history preserves brief biographies of empresses and consorts, including Cao Cao's wife Lady Bian, who became a dowager under her son's rule, and Cao Pi's consort Lady Zhen, whose fall and death in 221 the sources present differently. In Liu Bei's and Sun Quan's households, marriage alliances such as that between Liu Bei and Sun Quan's sister connected the powers. The poet Cai Yan (Cai Wenji), daughter of the scholar Cai Yong, was reportedly captured by Xiongnu raiders in the disorders and later redeemed by Cao Cao; poems attributed to her are the subject of scholarly dispute about authorship. Less prominent women appear indirectly in the Zoumalou household registers, where wives, mothers, daughters, and widows are counted within households, and in the widespread effects of war on family life: separation, widowhood, and remarriage.

Cultural life continued and in some respects flourished. The literature of the Jian'an period (196–220) is among the most celebrated products of the era. Cao Cao, his sons Cao Pi and Cao Zhi, and a circle of writers including Kong Rong, Wang Can, and others, sometimes called the Seven Masters of Jian'an, wrote poetry and prose that drew on the experience of war, displacement, and mortality. Cao Cao's own verse describes fields littered with bones and the silence of ruined villages, and other writers turned to themes of separation and the brevity of life. Cao Pi's essay on literature argued for the importance of writing as an enterprise of lasting value. Cao Zhi, a poet of exceptional gifts, lived under suspicion after the succession dispute. Jian'an writers gave influential new force to poetic developments already under way in the Han, especially five-character verse, more individualized poetic voices, and direct treatments of war, separation, mortality, and feeling. Modern scholars stress that these works were produced within courts and under patronage and represent only a small elite.

Religion also moved with people, though Chapter 23 gives it fuller treatment. The Celestial Masters (Tianshi) movement, a Daoist religious community that had ruled Hanzhong under Zhang Lu, submitted to Cao Cao in 215, and its followers were dispersed across the north, a development with long-term consequences for the spread of organized Daoism. Buddhism was present in several regions but was not yet the mass tradition it would become. Confucian learning continued to matter in court education and in appointments, while intellectual currents that would become important in the following decades were only beginning.

Section 19.9

History, Memory, and the Romance of the Three Kingdoms

Few periods in world history are remembered so differently from the way they can be reconstructed. The first layer of evidence is the Records of the Three Kingdoms (Sanguozhi), compiled by Chen Shou (233–297) in the decades after Wei's fall and completed under the Western Jin. Chen Shou had served the state of Shu as a young man and then served Jin, and he drew on earlier histories of Wei and Wu, on official documents, and on his own inquiries. His work has sixty-five chapters in three parts, on Wei, Shu, and Wu, and it lacks the treatises and tables found in some other official histories. Its structure reflects a political choice. It gives Wei's rulers annals, the format reserved for legitimate emperors, while the rulers of Shu and Wu receive biographies. Because Jin's claim to rule descended from Wei's abdication, Chen Shou's framework favored Wei, though he treated Shu and Wu with respect and often with sympathy.

The second layer is Pei Songzhi's annotation. In 429, in the Liu Song dynasty, Pei Songzhi presented commentary that added a large body of material from other works, many of which are now lost, and that sometimes weighed conflicting accounts. The annotations roughly double the text and preserve anecdotes, letters, edicts, and alternative versions. They are valuable precisely because they are later and eclectic. Students must distinguish between Chen Shou's third-century text and Pei Songzhi's fifth-century additions, and should not merge them silently. Pei Songzhi sometimes preserves colorful stories that Chen Shou omitted, and some of those stories later became material for popular storytelling.

The third layer is the tradition of storytelling, drama, and fiction. From the Tang and Song periods, storytellers performed episodes from the Three Kingdoms, and Yuan-era plays adapted them. In the fourteenth century, the novel now known as the Romance of the Three Kingdoms (Sanguo yanyi), traditionally attributed to Luo Guanzhong, gathered these materials into a long narrative. The version most read today is a later revision associated with Mao Lun and Mao Zonggang in the seventeenth century. The novel follows the historical outline but invents and elaborates freely. It presents Liu Bei as a virtuous ruler, Cao Cao as a cunning villain, Zhuge Liang as a nearly superhuman strategist, and Guan Yu as the embodiment of loyalty. An eighteenth-century scholar, Zhang Xuecheng, is famous for saying that the book was about seven parts fact and three parts fiction, and students should treat that remark as an early critic's judgment, not a measurement.

The difference in portrayal reflects a long argument about legitimacy. Early historians such as Chen Shou treated Wei as the legitimate successor to the Han. The fourth-century writer Xi Zuochi argued that Shu Han was the rightful heir, and later writers debated the question repeatedly. The eleventh-century historian Sima Guang, in his Comprehensive Mirror, used Wei's chronology, while the Neo-Confucian scholar Zhu Xi and his followers in the twelfth century favored Shu. The preference for Liu Bei in later drama and fiction reflects centuries of moral reading of history, especially in periods when northern regimes ruled part of China, and the debate had contemporary political meaning each time it was revisited.

The memory of the period also took religious form. Guan Yu became the object of worship, honored by rulers and later venerated as Guandi, a god associated with loyalty, protection, martial power, and commerce, with temples across China and overseas Chinese communities. The novel and its adaptations traveled beyond China, shaping literature, theater, and popular culture in Korea, Japan, and Vietnam, and, in modern times, comics, television, film, and video games in many languages. This reach makes the story a topic in the history of reception as well as in the history of the third century.

For students, the practical rule is straightforward. The novel is excellent evidence for how later people understood loyalty, statecraft, and moral character, and for the sensibilities of the periods in which it was written and revised. It is not evidence for what Cao Cao or Zhuge Liang said, planned, or felt in the third century. For that, historians turn to Chen Shou, Pei Songzhi, documents and inscriptions, poems, and archaeological finds, and they read even those with caution, noting who compiled them, when, and for whom.

Section 19.10

Chapter Review and Three Kingdoms Checkpoint

This chapter has traced a system rather than a list of battles: Han fragmentation, regional consolidation, Red Cliffs, competing states, institutional adaptation, economic survival, continuing warfare, and later memory. Cao Cao built a northern base on the emperor's authority, agricultural colonies, and skilled recruitment. Liu Bei, after two decades of wandering, built a Sichuan state on the Han inheritance. Sun Quan and Jiangdong's elites built a river state on the Yangtze. Red Cliffs blocked rapid northern conquest but did not create three kingdoms, which took shape through later events: Yi in 214, Jing in 219, the Han abdication in 220, Liu Bei's imperial claim in 221, and Sun Quan's in 229.

Each state adapted Han institutions to its resources. Wei had the largest population and the most elaborate government, and its Nine-Rank system, colonies, and military households shaped later history. Shu was small, defensible, and administratively concentrated. Wu was maritime and riverine, dependent on regional elites and hereditary commanders. All three struggled to feed armies, count households, and keep people from fleeing. Excavated records such as the Zoumalou slips reveal these efforts in a way that court histories rarely do.

In the end, the balance did not hold. Wei conquered Shu in 263. In 265 the Sima family, whose ancestor Sima Yi had risen as a Wei general, replaced the Cao house and founded the Jin dynasty. In 280 Jin conquered Wu. These dates are transition markers only. How the Sima family took power, why reunification proved fragile, and how the north then fragmented again are the subjects of Chapter 20.

The chapter's larger point is that "Three Kingdoms = chaos" is an inadequate summary. The period contained destruction and famine, but it also contained functioning governments, new institutions, economic recovery, migration that reshaped regions, and creative writing. It also generated a body of legend that later readers often mistake for third-century evidence.

Historical Evidence

Reading the Sources for the Three Kingdoms

Official history: Chen Shou's Records of the Three Kingdoms. Compiled by a former Shu official under the Western Jin, it is the foundation of modern knowledge. It is concise, sometimes evasive about events that reflected badly on the Sima family, and it organizes the period around Wei's legitimacy. Compilers of dynastic histories worked from earlier archives and drew on personal inquiry, which means their accounts of battles and speeches were shaped by reports, by the compiler's purposes, and by the political sensitivities of his own time.

Annotation: Pei Songzhi. Pei's fifth-century additions preserve material from lost works, giving multiple versions of episodes. The quality of these excerpts varies. Some are contemporary, others are later anecdote, and some are clearly legendary.

Documents and inscriptions. Edicts, memorials, and stele inscriptions preserved in histories or found in collections provide direct access to how rulers presented themselves, though they represent formal, often self-justifying speech. Cao Cao's order of 210 is one example.

Archaeology and excavated texts. The Zoumalou slips are contemporary administrative documents. Tombs, coins, and material remains illuminate the economy and society of each state. Archaeological finds do not answer questions about high politics, but they can correct impressions produced by elite narratives.

Poetry and prose. The Jian'an writers left works that convey the emotional world of war and displacement. They speak for a small literate elite and must be read as literature as well as testimony.

Later fiction and drama. The Romance of the Three Kingdoms and its predecessors are evidence for later memory. They cannot be used to reconstruct third-century events.

Limits. Few sources survive from ordinary people. Many texts were compiled by winners or by members of winning families. Numbers of soldiers and casualties are especially unreliable. The distribution of evidence favors elites and the north, and southern communities are often seen only through the administrators who governed them.

Historical Vocabulary
Tuntian (屯田), agricultural colonies

Tuntian refers to state-organized agricultural settlements in which soldiers or civilians farmed government land under supervision, with a fixed share of the crop going to the state. The Han had used military colonies on the frontiers, and they were not invented in the Three Kingdoms. What was new in Wei was the expansion of civilian colonies across the interior. Cao Cao developed colonies at Xu beginning in 196, and later Wei administrators extended the system to the Huai region. Colonies mattered because they supplied armies, settled displaced people, and let the state count and control farmers. For colonists, they offered land and protection, but often at high rates of return and with limited freedom to leave. Later governments would adapt the idea.

Historical Vocabulary
Nine-Rank Rectifying System (九品中正制)

Introduced in Wei in 220 and associated with Chen Qun, this system assigned officials called zhongzheng (Impartial Assessors) to evaluate local candidates and place them in nine grades, which guided the appointments the court made. It was created to replace the Han recommendation system at a time when war had scattered communities and the court needed a way to identify capable men. It is not an examination system, since it depended on the judgment of assessors and on a candidate's reputation. It tended to favor families with established standing, and over time, in Western Jin and the Southern Dynasties, it helped consolidate hereditary aristocratic privilege. It is a useful contrast with the examination institutions examined in later chapters.

Historical Vocabulary
Zhengtong (正統), legitimate succession

The term zhengtong refers to the idea that political authority passes in a rightful line from dynasty to dynasty. In the Three Kingdoms period, all three states made claims about the Han inheritance. Wei rested its claim on an abdication ceremony, Shu on the imperial Liu descent, and Wu on effective rule and the failure of the Han. Later historians argued repeatedly about which state was the true heir, and their answers often reflected their own political situations. The concept shows that historical writing was also political argument and that the question "Which was legitimate?" was a matter of interpretation rather than a fact to be looked up.

Primary Source Window

Primary Source Window: Cao Cao's Order of 210, Relinquishing My Fiefs and Clarifying My Basic Aims

Creator: Cao Cao · Date: 210 · Type: Political order (ling) preserved in later historical compilations, including Pei Songzhi's annotations to the Records of the Three Kingdoms

Context: By 210, Cao Cao dominated the north and held the chancellorship, and rivals accused him of aiming to overthrow the Han. The order, often called an apologia, is a public self-defense. He describes his early ambitions as modest, defends his conduct, and explains why he cannot give up command. It is a different document from the Seeking Worthies Order, another text of 210 discussed in Section 19.2.

Paraphrase (not a translated quotation):

Cao Cao recalls that as a young official he first hoped to become a commandery governor and earn a reputation through capable government. Later, once drawn into warfare, he hoped to win merit for the Han and eventually be remembered as a marquis and General Who Conquers the West. He denies intending to seize the throne, argues that surrendering his troops would endanger himself, his family, and the state, and offers to return part of his fief.

Analysis: The document reveals how Cao Cao wished his actions to be understood, and it shows that the language of loyalty to the Han was still politically necessary in 210. It also reveals the constraint he faced: he needed to justify keeping the army that sustained him. It cannot prove his motives or his innocence. It is an argument addressed to an audience of officials and rivals, and later tradition in the Romance turned him into the villain of the story. Reading the text as political self-presentation, rather than confession or propaganda alone, helps students see him as a historical actor rather than a literary type.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: How Decisive Was the Battle of Red Cliffs?

The question: Traditional accounts treat Red Cliffs as the event that created the Three Kingdoms. Historians ask how much weight the battle should bear.

Interpretation A — Red Cliffs as the decisive turning point: By stopping Cao Cao when he held the north and Jing and was poised to conquer the Yangtze, the battle prevented rapid reunification and preserved Sun Quan's regime and Liu Bei's chance to build a base. Without the allied victory, there might have been no southern or southwestern states.
Interpretation B — Red Cliffs as one step in a longer process: The battle produced a temporary balance, not three states. Liu Bei's acquisition of Yi, Sun Quan's seizure of Jing, Guan Yu's defeat, Cao Pi's founding of Wei, and the imperial claims of 221 and 229 all mattered as much. Geography, logistics, and the difficulty of governing a conquered south might have limited northern power even without the battle.

Evidence: Interpretation A emphasizes the strategic situation in 208 and the accounts of the campaign in Chen Shou and Pei Songzhi. Interpretation B emphasizes the later sequence of events and the persistence of the balance for decades, and it notes that our accounts of the battle itself are thin, come from later writers, and are affected by the novelistic tradition.

Points of disagreement: Historians differ on what counts as "decisive": preventing an outcome, or producing one. They differ on the reliability of the battle narratives, since the location, numbers, and details are uncertain. They differ on the relative weight of geography and leadership. And they disagree on whether later outcomes are being read backward into 208, which is the danger of assuming that events had to lead to the state of affairs that followed.

Why it matters: How one weighs a single battle shapes the way one teaches history: as a story of decisive moments or as one of structures, resources, and sequences.

Student task: Using evidence from Sections 19.3 to 19.5, decide which interpretation is better supported, and identify what additional evidence would help settle the question.

Multiple Perspectives

Multiple Perspectives: Living Through Three Competing States

Note: these are reconstructed perspectives based on patterns in the evidence, not quotations from individuals.

A Wei military-agricultural settler in the north: Land and seed were provided, and the colony offered protection and food after years of famine, but a large share of the crop went to the state, and movement was restricted. Family members might be soldiers with hereditary obligations.
A Shu official or soldier in Sichuan or Hanzhong: Service under a regime with a strong ethic of duty and legal order came with the strain of supplying northern expeditions across mountain roads. Officials from Yi Province and those who had arrived with Liu Bei held different positions and expectations.
A household in Wu registration records: The family was listed by member, obligated for grain and cloth, and possibly dependent on a seed-grain loan to plant. The registers show the state's reach, but they do not show how the family felt about it.
A displaced northern family in the south: Refugees might receive land or service, or be viewed with suspicion by local families. Elite refugees brought networks and skills and could rise in government. Poorer refugees had fewer options.
A lower-Yangtze local elite family: Support for the Sun house brought offices, command, and protection of local interests, but it also meant negotiating with a ruler who wanted more control and with northern émigrés who competed for influence.
A frontier or southern community incorporated into a state: Groups such as the Shanyue in Wu's territory, or communities in Nanzhong and on the northern frontier, could be targets of military campaigns, sources of soldiers and labor, or partners in negotiation. Their own accounts are missing from the record, and the sources use terms imposed by outsiders.

These perspectives were not equal in power. Rulers, generals, and great families shaped policy, while colonists, refugees, and captured populations were subject to it.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change Over Time: From Han Imperial Government to Regional Imperial States

What Changed

  • Central authority over the whole Han realm ended; three governments claimed imperial rule over regions.
  • Provincial and military commands gained power at the expense of civil administration.
  • New institutions appeared, notably the Nine-Rank system, expanded colonies, and hereditary military households.
  • Population moved south and west, and the balance of economic and political weight began to shift toward the Yangtze.

What Stayed the Same

  • Han titles, offices, taxation, law, household registration, and ritual vocabulary were adapted by all three states.
  • The idea that a legitimate government required an emperor persisted, and each ruler eventually claimed the title.
  • Agriculture remained the basis of revenue, and rural households the basis of society.
  • Classical learning continued to matter in education and government.

When and how fast: Change was gradual and uneven. Han institutions were adapted between 196 and 229, and the pace differed by region.

Who experienced change most directly: Refugees, colonists, and soldiers' families in war zones, and elite families forced to move or choose sides. Who experienced continuity: Farmers in stable regions, and local officials who continued to use familiar forms.

Why it matters: Han government did not vanish in 220. It was adapted by rival states, which is why later dynasties could draw on Han precedents while also inheriting third-century innovations.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Why Did Red Cliffs Produce a Regional Balance Rather Than Immediate Reunification?

Long-term causes

The weakening of Han central authority, regionalized armies, and the pattern of resources: a populous north, a defensible southwest, and a river-based south whose terrain favored defenders against cavalry armies.

Short-term causes

Cao Cao's conquest of the north by 207 and Liu Biao's death in 208, which delivered Jing Province to Cao Cao and opened the Yangtze route.

Immediate context and trigger

Cao Cao's move south, Liu Cong's surrender, Liu Bei's flight, and the Wu court's decision to resist rather than submit.

The event

The winter campaign of 208–209 along the Yangtze, in which disease, logistics, unfamiliar river warfare, a reported fire attack, and allied cooperation forced Cao Cao to retreat.

Immediate effects

Cao Cao kept the north and part of Jing; Zhou Yu took Jiangling in 209; Liu Bei gained southern Jing commanderies; Sun Quan's regime endured.

Long-term consequences

Space for Liu Bei's acquisition of Yi and for Wu's consolidation, the three imperial claims of 220–229, and decades of contest along the Yangtze, the Huai, and the Qinling mountains.

Historical complexity

Red Cliffs was necessary but not sufficient. Later events, resources, geography, alliance politics, and the choices of Liu Bei, Sun Quan, and Cao Pi all shaped the outcome. Explanations that credit a single battle or a single commander reflect the influence of later storytelling.
Everyday Life and Social History

Everyday Life and Social History: A Household in Sun Wu Changsha

The Zoumalou records do not allow historians to reconstruct a named family, but they permit a cautious composite picture. A household in Changsha commandery in the 230s was registered with the county, with the names, ages, and relations of its members recorded, and sometimes with notations about illness or disability. Its obligations included grain and cloth, and labor service for adult men. The household lived by agriculture, with rice as the principal crop, supplemented by other farming and by fishing and gathering.

A poor household might lack seed grain in the spring. The local government's loan registers show that officials advanced grain to such households to be repaid at harvest. Historians read this as a sign that the state cared about production, since an unplanted field produced no tax, and also that it responded to the practical problem of poverty. Loans created debt, and a household that could not repay faced difficult choices, including flight to the protection of a powerful family, which is one reason states so closely watched registers.

Women appear in the records as wives, mothers, daughters, and widows within households, and their labor in textiles and farming, together with the cloth owed to the state, was central to household economy. Children were counted as they grew. Local officials, clerks, and the writers of these records were members of a literate administrative class that connected the household to the wider state. Students should remember that this is an official's view. Religious life, festivals, kinship obligations, and the emotional life of families are largely invisible, and other evidence is needed to fill them in.

Regional and Frontier Context

Regional and Frontier Context

North China. Wei held the plain and faced the steppe. Its frontier commands relied on Wuhuan, Xianbei, and Xiongnu troops as well as Chinese soldiers, and its northern policy included resettlement and division of groups. The Xiongnu organization of 216 and the movement of Di and Qiang populations left demographic effects examined in Chapter 20.

Northwest. Liang Province and the Guanzhong plain were contested between Wei and Shu, with Qiang and Di communities involved as allies, targets, and settlers.

Sichuan and the southwest. Shu's Nanzhong campaign of 225 secured the region of modern Yunnan and Guizhou, where local leaders and communities negotiated their relations with the state, and Chengdu's government drew soldiers and resources from them. The sources describe the region from the outside.

Jiangdong and the southeast. Wu's expansion involved conflict with Shanyue communities in the mountains, who were sources of manpower and objects of campaigns. It also involved incorporation of local Chinese lineages through office and marriage.

The far south. Jiaozhi, under Shi Xie and then under Wu administration, was a commercial and administrative frontier with links to Southeast Asia.

The northeast. Liaodong's Gongsun family ruled a semi-independent state, dealing with Wei, Wu, Korea, and Japan until Sima Yi destroyed it in 238.

Frontier peoples were not merely recipients of policy. They negotiated, fought, migrated, and often decided the outcome of campaigns. Yet almost all surviving sources were written by officials of the states involved, and the frontier's own voices are difficult to recover.

China and the Wider World

China and the Wider World

The Three Kingdoms were not isolated. Wei's records, preserved in the Records of the Three Kingdoms, describe embassies from a ruler called Himiko of Yamatai in Japan in the late 230s, and Wei's outreach in the northeast connected it to Korean peoples through Liaodong. Wu's rulers sent officials such as Zhu Ying and Kang Tai to Southeast Asia, where they reached the kingdom known in Chinese sources as Funan, and Wu's contacts through Jiaozhi linked the Yangtze economy with maritime networks extending toward the Indian Ocean. Wu's overseas expeditions and its diplomatic interest in the Liaodong region, including contacts with the Gongsun, show that its rulers thought beyond the Yangtze.

Central Asian connections continued to matter to Wei, which maintained a presence in the Western Regions through officials and garrisons and exchanged envoys. The Buddhist tradition was moving along these routes, a development that Chapter 23 explores. The period also matters in later East Asian memory. Korean, Japanese, and Vietnamese audiences read and adapted the Three Kingdoms story, and its popularity in modern global media reflects a long process of reception rather than direct third-century influence.

These interactions show that the states were active participants in regional diplomacy, trade, and cultural exchange, and not only survivors of a domestic collapse.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the collapse of Han central authority produced not simply disorder but three competing regional regimes, each of which built a government, an army, an economy, and a claim to imperial legitimacy. Its central argument is that the Three Kingdoms period was an era of state-building under conditions of war, in which rival governments adapted the institutions of the Han to new regional realities.

The sequence of events matters. From 196, when Cao Cao brought Emperor Xian to Xu, regional commanders consolidated power. Guandu in 200 gave Cao Cao the north. Red Cliffs in the winter of 208–209 blocked his southern advance. Liu Bei's acquisition of Yi in 214, the division of Jing in 219, and the Han abdication in 220 set the map. The formal imperial claims came at different times: Cao Pi in 220, Liu Bei in 221, and Sun Quan in 229. Cao Cao himself never became emperor. The chapter has stressed that regional power formation is not the same as formal dynastic foundation.

Geography shaped each state. Wei held the North China Plain, with the largest population and the most elaborate government, and it faced the steppe and northwestern frontiers. Shu held the Sichuan Basin, a rich and defensible region whose limited population and difficult supply lines constrained its ambitions. Wu held the Yangtze, where a river-based fleet and an alliance between the Sun house and the lower-Yangtze elites created a durable southern state. Jing Province, in the middle Yangtze, was the contested pivot among them.

The governments had features in common and differences. All retained Han provinces, commanderies, counties, titles, taxation, and law, and all adapted them to wartime conditions. Wei developed agricultural colonies, hereditary military households, a strengthened secretariat, and the Nine-Rank Rectifying System, which selected officials through local assessors and would later contribute to aristocratic privilege. Shu relied on a concentrated administration and a fragile balance between Jing-origin and Yi Province elites. Wu depended on hereditary commands, regional families, and its fleet.

Economic survival was a political achievement. The states fed armies through colonies, irrigation, salt and iron production, textiles, and the registration of households, and each used different forms of money. The Zoumalou slips from Wu offer contemporary evidence of local government counting households, collecting grain and cloth, and lending seed grain to poor families, and they show the value of excavated documents for seeing beyond courts and battlefields.

War also transformed society. Migration brought northern refugees south and west, reshaped elite families, and changed relations with local and frontier communities such as the Shanyue, the peoples of Nanzhong, and the Wuhuan, Xianbei, Xiongnu, Qiang, and Di of the north. Women, refugees, colonists, and soldiers' families experienced state-building differently from commanders and great families. The literature of the Jian'an period expressed the disruption of the time, and religious movements such as the Celestial Masters were moved by war, themes that Chapter 23 will develop.

Memory transformed the period again. Chen Shou's Records of the Three Kingdoms, Pei Songzhi's annotations, and the later Romance of the Three Kingdoms are different layers of evidence. The novel, enormously popular, is a source for later moral imagination and not for third-century events. Debates over which state was legitimate continued for centuries, and the figure of Guan Yu became a god.

The period ended with the Sima family's replacement of Wei in 265 and the conquest of Wu in 280, developments treated in Chapter 20. The Three Kingdoms period contributes to the larger history of Chinese civilization by showing that political division could be creative as well as destructive, that institutions of the Han survived by adaptation, and that the southward movement of population and government, later so important, began in these decades.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows what happens to a great empire's institutions when its central power ends. Han government did not disappear. It was adapted by three rival states, each shaped by its own geography and resources. That adaptation is a reminder that Chinese political history is not a simple alternation of unity and chaos, and that division could be a period of institutional experiment.

The period shaped later history in specific ways. The Nine-Rank system, colonies, and hereditary military obligations influenced the institutions of Western Jin and the Northern and Southern Dynasties, which the next chapters examine. The establishment of a capital at Jianye and the growth of settlement along the Yangtze prepared the ground for the southern dynasties of Chapter 21. Migration and frontier settlement anticipate the transformations of Chapters 20 and 23.

The chapter also reveals a theme that recurs throughout this textbook: the relationship between evidence and memory. The Three Kingdoms are both a historical period and a cultural story, and students must learn to tell them apart. That skill applies equally to later periods, from the Tang and Song to modern political memory.

Finally, the states of this chapter belonged to a wider world of steppe, Southeast Asian, Korean, and Japanese connections. Understanding them as diverse, contested, and interconnected, rather than as the beginning of a single national story, helps students see Chinese history as regionally varied, politically changing, and unfinished. In Chapter 20, the Sima family will bring the three states together, and the reunification they achieve will prove fragile.
