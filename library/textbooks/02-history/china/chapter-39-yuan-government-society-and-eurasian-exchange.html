Part VII — Mongol, Yuan, and Ming China

Yuan Government, Society, and Eurasian Exchange

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

Chapter 38 told the story of conquest. This chapter asks what the conquerors built. The Yuan dynasty was a Chinese imperial government and, at the same time, one component of a vast and politically divided Mongol world. Neither description is complete without the other.

In the winter months, the court of Khubilai Khan lived in a newly built capital on the northern edge of the North China Plain, a walled city laid out on a grid with palaces, ancestral temples, granaries, markets, and a lake fed by engineered waterways. In the summer, the same court traveled north to Shangdu, a grassland residence where horses, tents, hunting, and Mongol ceremony shaped daily life. The seasonal movement between these two places captures the central problem of this chapter. The ruling house governed many millions of farmers, artisans, merchants, monks, and officials in agricultural China, yet its legitimacy came from Chinggis Khan's descendants and the steppe political tradition as well as from inherited Chinese ideas of imperial rule.

This chapter studies the Yuan dynasty (1271–1368, with the conquest of the Southern Song completed in 1279) as a system of government, a society, an economy, and a node in Eurasian exchange. Its guiding claim is that the Yuan was neither simply a foreign occupation of China nor simply another Chinese dynasty under a new family. It was a Mongol-ruled imperial state that governed Chinese territories through inherited institutions while remaining embedded in the political, commercial, religious, and cultural networks of the wider Mongol world. Its institutions reflected both adaptation and hierarchy, producing new opportunities for exchange while also generating fiscal, social, and political tensions.

The topic matters because it changes how students understand the relationship between "China" and its neighbors. Under Yuan rule, the Yellow River and Yangtze regions, Mongolia, Tibet, Yunnan, and the Northeast were brought into differentiated relationships with a single imperial court, although they were governed through different institutions and should not be treated as equivalents of later provincial or modern territorial arrangements. The Yuan also moved the political center of the empire north to Dadu, on the site of present-day Beijing, a shift that outlasted the dynasty.

Much changed. The capital moved north; a four-part scheme of status categories privileged Mongols and many Central and West Asians; the civil service examinations were suspended for decades before being revived in a limited form; paper currency became the primary medium of state finance; religious institutions of many traditions received patronage and tax privileges; and merchants, astronomers, physicians, artisans, and envoys moved across Eurasia with unusual frequency. Much also continued. Farming households still paid taxes in grain, cloth, and labor; local government still depended on county magistrates, clerks, and local elites; the Grand Canal still carried the grain that fed the north; and Confucian learning, Buddhist monasteries, and village temple life continued, sometimes under new conditions.

The people affected were enormously varied: Mongol princes holding appanages; Uyghur and Central Asian administrators; northern Chinese households classified under former Jin rule; southern scholars who found their examination careers closed; hereditary military and artisan households; Muslim merchants financed by Mongol elites; Tibetan Buddhist teachers; Daoist priests; Christian communities of the Church of the East; farmers along the Yellow River who suffered flooding and relief shortfalls; and women whose property, family and religious roles varied by region and community.

Historians understand the Yuan through an unusually multilingual body of evidence: Chinese dynastic and local records, legal compendia such as the Yuan dianzhang, Persian histories such as Rashid al-Din's work, Mongolian-language and Phags-pa inscriptions, Tibetan Buddhist sources, Arabic and Syriac accounts, European travel narratives, archaeological finds from Dadu, and artifacts such as porcelain and paper currency. Reading these sources against one another is one of the best illustrations in this textbook of why historians cannot rely on a single national or dynastic record.

Finally, the chapter connects China to a wider story. The Yuan emerged from the Mongol Empire's fragmentation into several khanates, was linked to the Ilkhanate in Iran and the Golden Horde and Chagatayid realms by trade, diplomacy, and kinship, and drew on Indian Ocean commerce that southern Chinese ports had developed since the Song (Chapter 37). The chapter ends as fiscal strain, environmental disaster, and rebellion pushed the dynasty toward terminal crisis. Chapter 40 follows the rebellions and the rise of Zhu Yuanzhang.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  2. Describe the planning, geography, and political functions of Dadu and Shangdu.
  3. Analyze how a small Mongol ruling elite governed a large agricultural population through adapted institutions.
  4. Distinguish the Yuan categories of Mongols, semu, Hanren, and Nanren, and explain why they should not be read as fixed racial castes.
  5. Evaluate the roles of land registration, taxation, paper currency, salt revenue, and grain transport in Yuan state finance.
  6. Assess what political integration across much of Eurasia did and did not change for trade and travel.
  7. Compare Yuan patronage of Tibetan Buddhism, Daoism, Islam, Christianity, and Confucian learning.
  8. Interpret scientific, artistic, and literary developments as products of conquest, exchange, and local continuity.
  9. Evaluate Marco Polo's account alongside Chinese, Persian, and other sources.
  10. Analyze the fiscal, environmental, political, and social causes of Yuan crisis without reducing them to a "dynastic cycle."
  11. Contextualize the Yuan within debates over whether it was a Chinese dynasty, a Mongol empire, or both.

Historical Context

By the 1270s, the lands that Khubilai ruled had been politically divided for roughly a century and a half. North China had been governed by the Khitan Liao and then the Jurchen Jin (Chapters 35–36), the south by the Southern Song (Chapter 36), and Tibet, Yunnan, and the Northeast by other polities. Each region had its own administrative traditions, tax systems, elites, and currencies. The Mongol conquests (Chapter 38) had started in the north in the 1210s and reached the Southern Song only in 1279, so a northern region that had been under Mongol rule for decades and a southern region only recently conquered entered the Yuan state with very different experiences. Northern society had endured heavy destruction and the policies of the early Mongol period; the south was commercially richer, more populous, and less disrupted by the early wars.

The geography of the new state was equally unusual. The Yuan's productive heartland lay in the lower Yangtze and southeast, but its political centre was in the north, near the steppe frontier where the Mongol elite had its roots. Moving grain, silver, cloth, and officials between these zones required canals, sea lanes, relay stations, and warehouses. The Yuan state also claimed direct authority over Mongolia, Tibet, Yunnan, and the Northeast through different administrative arrangements, and maintained relations of kinship and rivalry with the other Chinggisid khanates in Central Asia, Iran, and the Russian steppe. After the succession struggle that followed Möngke's death in 1259, the Mongol Empire was no longer a single political unit in practice, even though ideas of Chinggisid unity persisted.

Socially, the region's people were divided by language, religion, region, occupation, and legal status. Chinese-speaking farmers formed the overwhelming majority. Mongol-speaking pastoralists, Central Asian merchants and administrators (including many Uyghurs, Persians, and Turkic peoples), Khitan and Jurchen communities, Tibetan religious leaders, and Christian and Muslim communities were all present. The Song intellectual world of Neo-Confucian learning, commercial printing, and a large examination-oriented elite (Chapters 31–34) remained alive in the south, but its relationship to political power was now uncertain.

The term "China" in this period requires care. The Yuan court used the Chinese dynastic name Da Yuan, "Great Yuan," while Mongol and Persian sources often used other names for the northern and southern regions it ruled. For students, the safest approach is to treat the Yuan as a state that governed Chinese territories and incorporated many Chinese institutions, without assuming that its rulers or its subjects shared a single idea of "China" or that the Yuan's territory matched either the Song's or the modern People's Republic's.

Section 39.1

Khubilai Khan and the Founding of Yuan

Chapter 38 followed the military campaigns that ended the Jin and Southern Song. This section begins where those campaigns leave off and asks how Khubilai made a conquest into a government. His position was uncertain at the start. After the death of the Great Khan Möngke in 1259 during the campaign against the Southern Song, two brothers claimed supreme authority. Khubilai was proclaimed Great Khan in 1260 at Kaiping, while Ariq Böke was proclaimed at the Mongol heartland by a rival assembly. The conflict was settled in Khubilai's favor when Ariq Böke surrendered in 1264, but other Chinggisid branches, notably Kaidu in Central Asia, resisted for decades. Khubilai therefore ruled as Great Khan with an authority that was only partly recognized beyond his own realm.

In 1271 he proclaimed the dynastic name Da Yuan, "Great Yuan," a term drawn from the Book of Changes and carrying connotations of origin and primacy. Adopting a Chinese dynastic name did not mean abandoning Mongol identity. It meant adding a claim that would be legible to the Chinese-speaking administrators, scholars, and local elites who had to be persuaded to serve. In 1279, with the final Song resistance defeated, the Yuan became the first dynasty ruled by a non-Han house over both northern and southern China. Khubilai held several kinds of legitimacy at once: as a descendant of Chinggis Khan, as Great Khan of the Mongols, as the sovereign of settled agricultural territories who performed ancestral rites in a Chinese-style temple and issued edicts in Chinese form, and, for his Tibetan Buddhist advisers, as a universal ruler in a Buddhist sense.

Not everyone accepted this combination. Nayan's 1287 revolt in Manchuria reflected opposition among eastern Chinggisid princes to Khubilai's increasing centralization and bureaucratic control of their appanages; some accounts and historians have also interpreted the conflict as reflecting resistance to Khubilai's growing use of Chinese-style institutions. Some Chinese scholars refused to serve a conquest dynasty, remaining at home as private teachers or, in some famous cases, as artists and writers expressing quiet loyalty to the fallen Song. Others, including northerners who had long served the Jin or the early Mongol court, served willingly and urged Khubilai to adopt Chinese institutions. The founding of the Yuan therefore combined negotiation, pressure, and opportunism on all sides.

Military and diplomatic expansion continued alongside the conquest of the Song and after 1279. Yuan armies and fleets attempted invasions of Japan in 1274 and 1281, campaigns in Vietnam and Champa in the 1280s, an expedition to Java in 1293, and operations in Burma. Many of these failed or gave mixed results, and they strained the treasury and the shipbuilding economy of the southeast. Their relevance for this chapter is that they show the Yuan acting as a Mongol imperial power as well as a territorial Chinese state, and that they imposed costs on the ordinary households that built ships, supplied grain, and served in the armies.

Khubilai's Road to Power

Khubilai (born 1215) was a grandson of Chinggis Khan and a younger brother of the Great Khan Möngke. In 1251 Möngke placed him in charge of Mongol affairs in North China, and Khubilai built a personal base there, gathering advisers of varied backgrounds: Buddhist and Daoist clergy, Confucian-trained scholars such as Yao Shu, Xu Heng, and Dou Mo, the Buddhist monk Liu Bingzhong, Uyghur and Central Asian administrators, and Tibetan religious figures. He supervised the campaign that brought the Dali kingdom in Yunnan under Mongol control in 1253–1254, and in 1256 Liu Bingzhong laid out the city of Kaiping on the grassland edge, which became Shangdu. These years matter because they show that Khubilai's later policies had a long apprenticeship in governing agricultural populations under Mongol overlordship, years before he held the supreme title.

His accession in 1260 was irregular by Mongol standards. A proper succession required a quriltai, an assembly of princes and commanders, ideally held in the Mongol heartland. Khubilai's assembly at Kaiping was attended mostly by his own supporters, while Ariq Böke, who held Karakorum, was elected by a rival gathering. The war between the brothers ended with Ariq Böke's surrender in 1264, but the cost was lasting. The western khanates, ruled by Chinggisid lines such as Berke of the Golden Horde, Hulegu of Iran, and Kaidu in Central Asia, did not all accept Khubilai's supremacy, and a long conflict with Kaidu continued until Kaidu's death in 1301. When the Yuan court received nominal recognition from the western khans in 1304, the Mongol world had already become a set of connected but separate realms.

The early 1260s also taught Khubilai caution. In 1262 Li Tan, a powerful hereditary military commander in Shandong and son-in-law of the senior Han Chinese official Wang Wentong, rebelled; Wang was accused of complicity and executed. In the aftermath, Khubilai curtailed the autonomy of several Han hereditary military families and strengthened central supervision, although hereditary and appanage power did not disappear. This episode helps explain why the Yuan kept overseers and Mongol or Central Asian officials at many levels, and why the empire's administrators were not simply drawn from the Chinese literati.

What the Dynastic Name Claimed

The 1271 edict that proclaimed the name Da Yuan drew on the Book of Changes and, as drafted by Chinese advisers, presented the new dynasty as continuing the succession of legitimate rulers rather than as a mere conquest. A ruler who took a dynastic name could be addressed through the vocabulary of ritual, calendar, and temple honors familiar to Chinese officials. Khubilai received a temple name, Shizu, and the ruling house adopted ancestral rites in forms that Chinese ritual specialists could recognize, although the Mongol practice of honoring ancestors, including Chinggis Khan, continued in its own ceremonies. The new dynasty also adopted a Chinese calendar system and reign periods (the first, Zhongtong, in 1260, then Zhiyuan from 1264).

The Chinese dynastic claim did not displace the others. For Mongol princes and commanders, Khubilai's authority rested on Chinggisid descent and on the loyalty of the guard and army. For Tibetan Buddhist leaders such as Phags-pa, he could be a universal ruler in the Buddhist sense, a cakravartin, whose reign protected the religion. For Persian-language court historians in Iran, he was the Great Khan, and the realm he ruled was one part of a Mongol family of states. A single ruler could thus be addressed in at least four political languages, and part of the skill of his government lay in keeping those languages from contradicting one another.

Chinese responses varied. Many northern scholars and officials had already served Mongol or Jin governments and saw the dynastic name as a basis for orderly rule. Some southern literati regarded the Song's fall as a catastrophe and refused to serve, while others, such as Zhao Mengfu, accepted office and then faced criticism from some contemporaries. The Song imperial family itself was dispersed: the child emperor Gong, who surrendered in 1276, was later sent to Tibet, where he is said to have become a Buddhist monk, a story preserved in sources that historians treat with caution. Such stories remind students that the new order meant different things to different communities and that loyalty after conquest was negotiated, not automatic.

Section 39.2

Dadu and the Yuan Court

Dadu ("Great Capital," Khanbaliq in Turkic and Mongol usage) was built in stages from 1267 on a plain north of the old Jin capital, with a plan attributed in part to Liu Bingzhong and his collaborators and to the Chinese-Muslim designer Ikhtiyar al-Din (Yeheidie'er). Dadu's gridded plan and ritual geography have often been interpreted as drawing on capital-planning ideals associated with the Rites of Zhou, although historians of architecture debate how directly the ancient prescription determined the actual city. The plan also integrated an imperial lake zone and a palace complex that housed Mongol ceremonial practices. Archaeological work at the site of modern Beijing has recovered street grids, drainage lines, and residential remains, offering evidence for the city's scale and its mixed population.

The choice of the north mattered. Lin'an, the Southern Song capital, lay in the rich lower Yangtze; Dadu lay close to the steppe, the Mongol homeland, and Northeast China, and it controlled the approach from the North China Plain to Mongolia. This reflected the Yuan's identity as a steppe-oriented empire and made strategic sense. It also meant that the capital depended on grain and goods from the south, creating the logistical problems discussed in Section 39.5. Shangdu, in the grasslands to the north, served as a summer residence where the court hunted, held assemblies (quriltai), and entertained envoys. The court's seasonal movement kept Mongol elites in familiar environments and political practices.

Court life combined ceremonies. Chinese-style audiences, ancestral sacrifices, and calendrical rituals coexisted with Mongol enthronement customs, banquets with fermented mare's milk, the wearing of formal robes, and a mounted imperial guard known as the keshig. Imperial consorts and princesses, notably Khubilai's wife Chabi, held real political influence and managed household institutions. The court's size and diversity was striking: Mongol nobles, Uyghur scribes, Persian and Arabic-speaking administrators and astronomers, Tibetan monks, Chinese officials, Christian clergy, and craftsmen from many regions all lived in or near the capital.

Dadu also served as a hub linking North China, Mongolia, Central Asia, and the maritime southeast. Roads and relay stations ran to Shangdu and beyond; the canal system brought grain from the south; and merchants and envoys from across Eurasia passed through. Evidence for the city's character comes from Chinese descriptions, archaeology, Persian accounts, and European travelers such as Marco Polo, which should be compared rather than treated as interchangeable.

From Zhongdu to Dadu: Building a Capital

The Jin capital Zhongdu had been devastated in the Mongol assault of 1215. Khubilai first considered a site at Kaiping, later Shangdu, and then in 1264 designated the old Yanjing region as the Central Capital. Work on a new city to the northeast of the ruined Jin capital began in 1267; the name Dadu was given in 1272, and the principal palace halls were in use by 1274. The walls and the surrounding city continued to be built and completed over the following decade or more. Archaeology has shown a walled enclosure of about 28.6 kilometers in circuit, pierced by eleven gates, and has traced a street grid, drainage channels, and the foundations of residential courtyards. The city's water depended on engineering: Guo Shoujing's work in the 1290s brought spring water to the capital and linked the city to the Grand Canal through the Tonghui canal, so that grain barges could reach the lake basin near the city's center.

The imperial palace occupied the southern-central part of the city, flanked by an artificial lake and an island pleasure park. Ancestral and sacrificial altars lay to the east and west, and commercial districts clustered farther north near the bell and drum towers, though the exact layout of markets and wards remains a subject of archaeological research. Residents included officials, imperial household staff, soldiers, Buddhist and Daoist clergy, merchants, and artisans, many of them relocated by the state, so Dadu was a planned city whose population was assembled through administrative decisions as well as migration.

Shangdu and the Seasonal Court

Shangdu, whose ruins in Inner Mongolia are today a protected archaeological site, was a smaller city of palaces and pavilions set in grassland, with a larger park for hunting and encampment. The court generally spent the summer there, moving north in spring and returning to Dadu in autumn, an arrangement that suited Mongol habits of seasonal movement and provided a setting for assemblies, hunts, and the reception of Mongol nobles. It also reminds us that the Yuan court was not fixed in a single place; important decisions could be made in a tent or pavilion hundreds of kilometers from the capital's offices, and officials followed the court, or maintained separate administrative arrangements, for much of the year. Succession struggles repeatedly turned on which prince could control the capital and which could control Shangdu and the steppe approaches.

The Court as an Institution

The court was organized around the person of the emperor, his consorts, and the imperial household. The keshig guards, descended from Chinggis Khan's personal retainers, were divided into watches that served in rotation, and members of keshig families often held high office; being in the keshig was a path to influence quite apart from examinations. Imperial women controlled households, estates, and patronage. Khubilai's consort Chabi was widely remembered, including in Chinese sources, as a prudent adviser who encouraged restraint and favored Tibetan Buddhist clergy, and later imperial women also exercised influence in succession politics.

Ceremony expressed the dynasty's mixed identity. At great banquets, nobles and officials wore matching colored robes in a Mongol practice often called jisün, while Chinese-style audiences, ancestral rites, and sacrifices to heaven and earth were also performed. Food, music, and entertainment mingled Mongol, Central Asian, and Chinese elements. The court's ability to mobilize resources for display, including gifts of cloth and silver to princes, was also part of the political system: generosity bound followers, but it was costly, as Section 39.9 explains.

Finally, the capital's geography changed the balance of the empire. Lin'an had sat in the lower Yangtze, close to the wealth that supported the Southern Song state. Dadu sat on the northern margin of the agricultural plain, close to steppe routes and princely appanages. This made the emperor more accessible to Mongol nobles and the northern military, but it made the court dependent on an elaborate transport system to bring southern revenue north. When canals, sea routes, or the Yellow River failed, the capital's food supply and political authority were both at risk.

Section 39.3

Government and Administration

The basic puzzle of Yuan government was scale: a relatively small Mongol elite and its allies had to administer tens of millions of mostly Chinese-speaking farmers and townspeople. The Yuan answered this by combining inherited institutions with Mongol imperial practices and by recruiting administrators from many backgrounds.

Central institutions. At the center, the Secretariat (Zhongshu sheng) handled civil administration, the Bureau of Military Affairs (Shumi yuan) oversaw the army, and the Censorate (Yushi tai) monitored officials. These followed forms known from Jin and Song government and earlier dynasties, but their personnel and procedures were shaped by Mongol norms. Mongol nobles and imperial in-laws occupied many top positions, and the emperor's personal household, including the keshig guard, supplied trusted agents who could move into high offices.

Provincial and local government. The Yuan created branch secretariats (xingsheng, "moving secretariats"), the origin of the word sheng ("province") in later Chinese administration. These were large regional offices, not small units, covering areas such as the northwest, Henan, Jiangzhe (the lower Yangtze and southeast), Huguang, Sichuan, and Yunnan. Below them were circuits, routes, prefectures, and counties, with magistrates and clerks carrying out routine tasks. Supervision by darughachi (Mongol or allied overseers placed in offices at many levels) helped the ruling house keep control. As elsewhere in Chinese history, practical governance below the county depended on village heads, clerks, local elites, and community institutions.

Historical Vocabulary
Darughachi

A darughachi (Mongol term; Chinese daluhuachi) was an official appointed to supervise or co-administer an office, city, or region on behalf of the Mongol ruling house. The role grew out of conquest administration in earlier Mongol territories. In Yuan China, a darughachi usually sat alongside Chinese or other local officials, and the post was typically reserved for Mongols or semu. The system reveals a central strategy: use existing officials for expertise but place trusted overseers beside them. It also shows why historians describe Yuan government as layered rather than simply "Chinese" or "Mongol."

Personnel. Yuan officials came from many groups: Mongols, Uyghurs and other Central Asians, Persians, Khitan and Jurchen families with long service records, northern Chinese who had served the Jin or early Mongol warlords, and southern Chinese recruited after 1279. The Uyghur scribal tradition provided literate administrators for the Mongol court, and Mongol policy made use of multilingual officials, translators, and clerks. In practice, much routine government was carried out by clerks, whose career paths were a major channel into office, in some cases competing with the scholar-official route familiar from the Song.

Hereditary households and service obligations. The Yuan classified much of its population into hereditary categories of households (hu) defined by obligation: military households, post-station households, artisan households, salt-producing households, and others. A military household supplied soldiers over generations; an artisan household worked for the state. This allowed the government to organize labor and services efficiently but locked families into duties that could become heavy burdens, and it contributed to the social tensions discussed later.

Communications. The relay system (yam in Mongol usage, zhanchi in Chinese) provided horses, lodging, and supplies for official messengers and approved travelers, using paiza tablets of authority. It supported both administration and diplomacy, but the costs fell on post households and local communities, which frequently complained of abuses.

Examinations. The civil service examinations that had defined the Song official class (Chapters 31 and 34) were not immediately continued. The early Mongol period had no regular system, apart from a one-time northern examination in 1237. They were revived in 1315 under Emperor Ayurbarwada, with provincial and metropolitan examinations beginning in 1315–1316, using the commentaries of Zhu Xi on the Confucian classics, which helped establish Neo-Confucian orthodoxy for later dynasties. Quotas and separate lists for different categories meant that the number of degree holders was small compared to the Song. The examinations were suspended again from 1335 under the chancellor Bayan and restored in 1340. The interrupted examination system shows that Confucian-trained scholars did not dominate Yuan office-holding, though their influence persisted through teaching, local leadership, private writing, and eventual absorption into bureaucracy.

Princes, appanages, and the court. Mongol imperial practice gave princes and nobles shares of the empire's revenue and population in the form of appanages. Appanage holders could claim income from designated households and sometimes appoint officials, creating tension with central tax administrators and local magistrates. Disputes over succession and rights among nobles repeatedly affected the court's stability.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Government from the Song to the Yuan

What changed? The capital moved north; Mongol and Central Asian personnel and the keshig household gained prominence; hereditary service categories organized much of the population; branch secretariats created large provincial units; examinations lost their central role; appanages and overseers (darughachi) reflected steppe practice.

What stayed the same? The core ministries, the county as the basic unit of local government, written documents and law, clerks as administrative workhorses, land and household registration, the Grand Canal, and the dependence on local elites for practical governance.

Who experienced change most directly? Southern scholars whose examination careers closed, military and artisan households bound by hereditary duties, and Mongol and Central Asian officials who gained new routes to office. Who experienced continuity? Farming families who still paid taxes and answered to county offices. Why the pattern matters: the Yuan neither erased nor merely repeated Song government, and later dynasties inherited the provincial and clerical structures it developed.

Section 39.4

Social Categories and Ethnic Hierarchy

The Yuan is often described as dividing society into four ranks: Mongols; semu ("various categories"); Hanren ("Han people," mainly northerners); and Nanren ("southerners"). This summary, drawn from later Chinese writings, is useful but must be handled carefully. It describes broad administrative and legal distinctions that mattered in recruitment, punishment, and some obligations, not a fixed racial caste system or a formal law code with four sealed classes.

Mongols were the ruling house and its core followers, with privileges in office-holding and legal procedures. Semu included Uyghurs, Tanguts, Persians, Arabs, Turks, and many other Central and West Asian groups, among them important administrators and merchants. Hanren mainly meant the people of the former Jin realm, including Chinese-speaking northerners and also Khitan, Jurchen, and Koreans in northern contexts. Nanren meant the people of the former Southern Song, who had been conquered last and were the most numerous and often the most thinly represented in high office.

Historical Vocabulary
Semu (色目)

Semu was a Yuan administrative designation conventionally rendered "various categories." It grouped numerous peoples, especially from Central and western Eurasia, who were classified neither as Mongols nor within the Hanren/Nanren categories. It did not name a single ethnic group, language, or religion. Semu individuals could be Muslim, Buddhist, Nestorian Christian, or followers of other traditions, and they included powerful ministers and humble artisans. Translating the term as one ethnicity hides this diversity. Historians also note that semu status could bring opportunities, such as service as financial administrators and merchants, as well as resentment from local populations.

These categories affected official appointments (for example, top posts were reserved or favored for Mongols and semu), the structure of examination quotas and lists, certain legal procedures (such as punishments for assault between categories), and some service obligations. But the practical effects varied by time and place, and local class, occupation, family connections, patronage, region, and religion mattered at least as much as the broad label. Many Chinese scholars and clerks achieved influence by building ties with Mongol patrons; many Mongols and semu lived modestly. Intermarriage, cultural borrowing, and shifting identity also blurred boundaries over time.

Historians also use the Yuan categories to ask about social experience in the south. After 1279, southern elites lost the examination route and some saw their landholdings disturbed, yet many retained local wealth and influence through teaching, publishing, medicine, commerce, and local administration. Their relationship with the state was often one of negotiated accommodation. Meanwhile, artisans, hereditary service households, and peasants experienced the state primarily through taxation, labor demands, and local officials, regardless of their category.

Where the Four-Category Scheme Comes From

The four-category list is best known from later writing, including a fourteenth-century miscellany by Tao Zongyi, rather than from a single Yuan law that set out four legal classes. Yuan administrative texts instead mention these groups in particular regulations: who could hold certain posts, how members of different groups were to be tried and punished, who could serve as an overseer, or how examination quotas were allocated. The scheme was therefore a way of describing patterns that arose from many separate rules and practices, not a complete legal code of status. Students should also remember that the labels were applied by the government and by observers, not necessarily chosen by the people described, and that they did not match modern ideas of race.

What the Categories Changed

In office-holding, the clearest pattern was that top posts, such as heads of offices and overseers, were reserved for or strongly favored Mongols and, to a degree, semu, while Hanren and Nanren filled lower and more technical posts. Southerners were the least represented at senior levels, partly because the south was conquered last and partly because of suspicion about southern loyalty. In law, some provisions treated offenses between groups differently; for example, Yuan regulations on assault and homicide could impose lighter consequences on Mongols who injured non-Mongols, and certain punishments, such as tattooing for theft, were not applied equally. Restrictions on weapons and horses affected Hanren and Nanren in some periods, as the state worried about rebellion. These differences created grievances, but their enforcement varied, and historians continue to debate how far the rules operated in everyday local practice.

Occupation and household category also shaped experience. A military household, an artisan household, a salt household, or a household serving a monastery or a prince's appanage had different obligations from an ordinary farming household, and these categories crossed ethnic lines. Wealth mattered as well: a rich southern landholder, merchant, or physician could be more secure than a poor Mongol herder or a semu artisan. Some Mongol commoners, indeed, fell into poverty and sought state relief in the fourteenth century, and some people were held as dependent servants or "driven households" (quku), often captives from conquest, whose condition resembled bondage.

Contemporaries also commented on status in ways that reveal attitudes rather than law. A popular saying, associated with the Song loyalist Xie Fangde and later writers, ranked occupations in a way that placed "Confucians" below Buddhist and Daoist clergy and above beggars. It is best read as satire expressing scholars' resentment at their changed position, not as an official ranking. At the same time, many scholars did find roles as teachers, clerks, physicians, academy heads, private tutors, local leaders, or advisers to officials, and some entered office after the revival of examinations.

Interaction also blurred boundaries. Mongol and semu families adopted Chinese names, language, and literary tastes in some cases, and Chinese officials learned Mongolian or worked through interpreters. Mongol, Uyghur, and Persian vocabulary entered official vernacular, and many documents were translated word for word into a stiff written style in Chinese that scholars call "direct translation." Intermarriage occurred, though its extent is hard to measure. Historians therefore see the Yuan categories as a framework of unequal access that shaped incentives and resentments, within a society whose everyday relationships were more varied than the labels suggest.

Section 39.5

Agriculture, Taxation, and State Finance

Ruling China required feeding cities, maintaining armies, moving grain, and collecting taxes. The Yuan's economic foundation was agriculture, not the Silk Roads, and the dynasty's fiscal choices shaped its stability.

Recovery from conquest. North China had suffered heavy population loss and disruption during the long Jin–Mongol wars, and in the early Mongol period some officials proposed converting farmland to pasture, a proposal associated with court debates that Khubilai's advisers opposed. Under Khubilai, agricultural offices promoted resettlement, reclamation, and farming manuals; the Nongsang jiyao (1273), a state-sponsored agricultural handbook, circulated advice on crops and sericulture. The south, conquered with less destruction, remained the empire's richest agricultural and commercial region, with rice cultivation, silk and cotton production, and dense urban markets. Cotton spread significantly in this period, changing textile production and clothing for many households.

Registration and taxation. Yuan registers classified households by hereditary category and occupation, and censuses were conducted in the north and south at different times. The 1290 registers recorded 13,196,206 households and 58,834,711 persons, although neither figure represented a complete census of everyone living under Yuan authority; registration was uneven, and many people were hidden or exempt. The north relied on household levies (silk, silver, and grain) and a poll-type tax, while the south continued a version of the Song's two-tax (summer and autumn) system, based on land. These regional differences meant that taxpayers in the former Jin and former Song territories faced different obligations. Tax exemptions for religious institutions, certain service households, and privileged groups reduced the tax base and shifted burdens to others.

Grain transport and the canal. Dadu needed southern grain. The Yuan rebuilt and extended the Grand Canal so that it ran directly to the capital, with the Huitong and Tonghui sections in the 1280s and 1290s; Guo Shoujing's engineering was central to the Tonghui canal. Because the canal could not always carry enough grain, the state also developed maritime grain transport from about 1282, shipping rice from the Yangtze region up the coast. Sea transport used experienced shipowners and merchants and eventually carried large volumes, but wrecks and delays occurred. The system tied the capital's food supply to the south's agriculture and to coastal shipping skills inherited from the Song.

Paper currency. In 1260 the Yuan introduced Zhongtong paper notes pegged to silver at an official exchange rate. The early monetary reforms also drew on older silk-backed exchange arrangements, but Zhongtong currency itself should be understood primarily through its silver standard. Convertibility weakened after the conquest of the south as expenditures and note issues increased. The state later issued the Zhiyuan notes in 1287 as part of a reform associated with the finance minister Sangha. Paper money was the main medium of state accounts, and the state used it to pay officials and soldiers and to buy goods. Notes worked well when the government limited issues and maintained confidence, but overissue led to inflation. The currency reform begun in 1350 introduced new coinage and prepared a new paper-money regime; the final Yuan paper issue, the Zhizheng notes, entered circulation in 1352 and depreciated rapidly during the rebellion era as the state tried to cover rising expenses. Foreign silver flowed in and out of the empire through trade, and the interplay between silver and paper shaped the monetary economy.

Monopolies and expenditure. Salt was a leading source of revenue through a state monopoly, with merchants purchasing certificates to trade salt. Other monopolies and commercial taxes (on tea, liquor, and goods in transit) added income. Expenditure included the court, imperial household, princes' grants, military campaigns (including overseas), religious patronage, canal and relay maintenance, and relief. As costs outpaced revenue, officials turned to higher issues of currency and heavier levies, creating a cycle that helped erode trust.

Disaster and relief. The state maintained granaries and relief practices inherited from earlier dynasties, and Yuan records show relief grants and tax remissions after floods and droughts. But the quality of relief varied, and the Yellow River's repeated flooding in the fourteenth century strained resources (see 39.9).

Everyday Life and Social History

Households under Yuan Rule

For a farming household in the lower Yangtze, life under the Yuan might look familiar: planting rice, raising silkworms or cotton, paying summer and autumn taxes, attending local temples, and relying on kin and neighbors. Change came through new obligations, such as service in a hereditary household, grain transport duties, or local levies, and through exposure to paper currency and market prices. For a family in a hereditary artisan household near Dadu, a state workshop assignment might bring stable provisioning but little freedom to change occupation. For a Mongol pastoral family, seasonal movement and herd-based wealth remained central, while members of the elite lived partly in cities.

Women's experience varied by group. Mongol women, especially in elite households, held substantial economic and political roles, managing households and property and sometimes advising rulers. In Chinese-speaking communities, family law and property rules, including widows' control of property and the practice of widow chastity, were debated and often reinforced in local ethical writings, with regional variation and the gap between prescription and practice that students should keep in mind. Evidence includes legal compendia, local gazetteers, epitaphs, drama, and archaeological finds, most of them produced by or for elite or official audiences, so ordinary experience must be inferred carefully.

Section 39.6

Trade Across Mongol Eurasia

The Yuan existed within a Mongol world that, by the late thirteenth century, was politically divided. The Ilkhanate ruled Iran and surrounding regions, the Golden Horde the western steppe, and the Chagatayid realm much of Central Asia, while the Yuan ruled East Asia. Wars among these khanates (including Khubilai's long conflict with Kaidu) continued, and the old phrase "Pax Mongolica" should not be taken to mean uninterrupted peace. What changed was that large areas shared overlapping rulers, kinship ties, relay networks, and tax and safe-conduct systems that reduced some barriers for merchants, envoys, and religious figures.

Merchant partnerships and finance. Many commercial ventures were financed through ortoq partnerships.

Historical Vocabulary
Ortoq

An ortoq (Turkic and Mongol term, "partner") was a merchant who received capital, often silver, from Mongol princes, officials, or imperial women to trade on their behalf and share the profits. Many ortoq were Uyghur or Muslim merchants, though not all. The arrangement tied commerce to political power: the merchant gained protection, access to relay stations, and sometimes tax privileges; the elite investor gained income and access to luxury goods and information. Ortoq partnerships helped fund long-distance trade but also attracted complaints about lending at high interest, which could burden peasants and local officials. Understanding the ortoq shows that Eurasian commerce was closely linked to state and household finance, not simply a free-market flow.

Overland routes. Caravans moved between North China, Central Asia, and Iran through oasis towns and relay stations, carrying silk, silver, horses, textiles, spices, medicinal substances, and jewels. Official envoys and merchants used paiza tablets and the yam for safe passage. Overland routes were politically sensitive, since conflict among khanates could close them for periods.

Maritime routes. The Yuan inherited a vibrant maritime economy from the Song (Chapter 37) and extended it. Quanzhou, Guangzhou, Hangzhou, and other ports hosted merchants from South and Southeast Asia and the Islamic world. The state established maritime trade supervisorates, regulated shipping and customs, and at times tried to channel profit through official shipping partnerships. Ceramics (including the developing blue-and-white porcelain of Jingdezhen, which used cobalt associated with Iranian sources), textiles, and metals traveled outward; spices, aromatics, and other goods came in. The Indian Ocean remained a major arena of commerce independent of any single empire. Wang Dayuan, a Chinese merchant-traveler, wrote a description of overseas places (Daoyi zhilüe, compiled in 1349 and circulated in standalone form around 1350) after voyages in the 1330s.

What moved besides goods. People, techniques, crops, medicines, technologies, and information moved with commerce. Persian and Arabic knowledge of astronomy, medicine, and geography entered the Chinese court; Chinese printing, paper money, and ceramics reached Iran, and Persian historians such as Rashid al-Din drew on Chinese materials for their accounts. Plague, discussed below, may also have traveled along such routes, though how and when disease moved between regions is debated by historians and not firmly established for China.

Primary Source Window

Marco Polo's Description of Paper Money in Khubilai's Realm

Creator: Marco Polo, as told to and written down by Rustichello of Pisa · Date: c. 1298–1299 · Type: Travel narrative in Franco-Italian, surviving in many variant manuscripts

Context: Polo, a Venetian merchant, claimed to have served Khubilai for many years. His account was composed in a Genoese prison after his return and circulated in many versions. Among its subjects is the Yuan state's use of paper notes as money.

Paraphrase: Polo describes the Great Khan's notes as made from a prepared material, stamped with official seals, and accepted throughout his dominions, with merchants from distant lands compelled to exchange precious goods for them. He presents this as evidence of the ruler's extraordinary power and wealth.

Analysis: The description is consistent in broad terms with Chinese sources on state-issued notes, and scholars such as Hans Ulrich Vogel have used Polo, alongside Chinese and Persian sources, to study Yuan currency. But Polo's text is not a neutral report. It was composed through a collaborator, exists in many versions, and sometimes exaggerates; scholars have debated how much Polo personally witnessed, focusing on the complicated manuscript tradition, inconsistencies in some episodes, disputed claims about offices he held, and the relationship between his account and other Eurasian geographical traditions. Other specialists have shown that his unusually detailed descriptions of Yuan currency, salt administration, and revenues correspond closely with Chinese evidence. It reveals a foreign merchant's wonder, not the experience of Chinese taxpayers who handled notes, nor the inflationary problems of later decades.

Section 39.7

Religion and Cultural Diversity

Yuan rule brought an unusually visible mix of religious communities into the court and cities. Mongol rulers patronized many traditions, partly from interest and partly for political reasons, seeking blessings, legitimacy, and allies. This was not modern "religious tolerance" in the sense of equal rights. Patronage was selective and political, exemptions from taxes and service created competition and resentment, and rulers sometimes intervened sharply in disputes.

Tibetan Buddhism. Khubilai's relationship with Phags-pa of the Sakya school was central. Phags-pa served as imperial preceptor, performed initiations, and oversaw Buddhist affairs; the Yuan also gave the Sakya leaders authority over Tibet within the imperial framework, administered through a Tibetan office at court. Khubilai commissioned the Phags-pa script (1269) to write Mongolian and other languages, though it never replaced Uyghur-Mongol script for most uses. Tibetan Buddhist ritual and art spread at court and in Dadu, shaping Yuan temple building and sculpture. Within China, the favored status of Tibetan monks sometimes caused friction with other Buddhists and with local communities, and later Yuan administrators faced criticism over their privileges.

Chinese Buddhism and Daoism. Chan, Pure Land, and Huayan communities continued, and the state supported translation and printing of Buddhist works. Daoism, especially the northern Quanzhen order associated with Qiu Chuji, who had met Chinggis Khan in 1222, gained large landholdings and influence in the north. Disputes between Buddhists and Daoists, including the court debates of 1255 and 1258 and the ordered destruction of some Daoist texts, show that Mongol rulers made choices among competing communities.

Confucian traditions. Confucian scholars sought roles as advisers and teachers. Academies and local schools persisted, and the Yuan eventually adopted Zhu Xi's commentaries for the examinations of 1315. Confucian learning thus did not disappear; its institutional place changed.

Islam. Muslim communities, many of them semu, were prominent in trade, finance, astronomy, and administration, with mosques in Dadu, Quanzhou, and other cities. Sayyid Ajall Shams al-Din, a Central Asian Muslim administrator, governed Yunnan and integrated it more closely into the empire. The expansion and settlement of Muslim communities during the Yuan became an important part of the long history from which later Chinese-speaking Muslim communities developed; they should not simply be equated with the modern Hui nationality.

Christianity. Church of the East (often called Nestorian) communities existed among Turkic, Mongol, and other peoples, and some Mongol elite families were Christian. Franciscan missionaries, notably John of Montecorvino, arrived in the 1290s and established a Roman Catholic mission in Dadu. Rabban Sauma and Rabban Marqos were Chinese-region Christians of Turkic or Uyghur origin who traveled west.

Buddhists, Daoists, and the Politics of Debate

The debates at Kaiping in 1255 and 1258 are among the best-known episodes of Mongol religious policy. Quanzhen Daoists, who had gained large holdings in the north after Qiu Chuji's meeting with Chinggis Khan, were accused of seizing Buddhist temples and of circulating texts that portrayed the Buddha as a disciple of Laozi. In 1258 a panel of Buddhist and Daoist representatives debated before Khubilai, and the Buddhists were declared the winners; Daoists were required to return properties, and some texts were ordered destroyed. A further ruling in 1281 ordered the burning of most Daoist scriptures, except for the Daodejing. Enforcement was partial, and Daoism survived and revived; in the south the Zhengyi tradition of the Celestial Masters of Mount Longhu received official recognition. The point is that rulers did not treat all traditions equally but decided among them on political and practical grounds.

Tibetan Buddhism and Chinese Buddhism

The court's Buddhist administration was expanded in the 1260s and renamed in 1288 as the Bureau of Buddhist and Tibetan Affairs (Xuanzheng yuan; formerly the Zongzhi yuan), which supervised monks, monasteries, and Tibet. Tibetan monks served as preceptors and ritual specialists, and some became wealthy and influential. One controversial official was Yang Lianzhenjia, a Tibetan Buddhist monk-official of uncertain background appointed to oversee Buddhism in the Jiangnan region, who oversaw the desecration and opening of Southern Song imperial tombs near Shaoxing. Zhou Mi's near-contemporary account dates the major episode to 1285, although later sources give differing dates; Chinese sources describe it with horror, though they were written from a hostile perspective. Chinese Buddhist communities, especially Chan and Pure Land, continued to thrive, and the court supported the compilation of catalogs that compared the Chinese and Tibetan canons (1285–1287) and sponsored sutra printing. Mongol imperial women also acted as donors to monasteries.

Islam, Christianity, and Other Communities

Muslim communities were visible in trade, finance, and technical offices, and southeastern ports such as Quanzhou had mosques that were repaired and endowed in the Yuan period, as inscriptions and Arabic tombstones there show. Some court decrees regulated Muslim and Jewish ritual slaughter, which caused resentment and was later relaxed, an example of how toleration was limited by administrative preference. Christians of the Church of the East served as officials and soldiers, and some built churches, including in Zhenjiang, where Mar Sargis served as an administrator in the 1280s. The court maintained an office for Christian clergy, and Latin Christian missions, beginning with John of Montecorvino, were small but real. Jewish communities are also mentioned in Yuan sources.

Local Religion and State Honors

The great majority of ordinary people experienced religion through local temples, festivals, ancestors, and healing cults rather than through court-sponsored institutions. The state sometimes recognized these cults, in part to secure their loyalty. The goddess Mazu, protector of seafarers, received official titles from the Yuan court, a decision linked to the importance of sea transport of grain; temples to her spread along the coast. The court also supported the Confucian temple and academies, honoring the Kong family descendants. Religious institutions often held land, ran mills and shops, lent money, and provided charity, so religion intersected with local economy and politics.

Because religious professionals were exempt from many taxes and services, the number of people claiming clerical status rose, and officials complained about false monks and abuse of privileges. These disputes show why religious policy cannot be separated from fiscal policy: each exemption was also a shift of burden onto other households.

Section 39.8

Art, Science, and Foreign Travelers

Astronomy and calendar science. The Yuan court supported astronomers from both Chinese and Islamic traditions. Jamal al-Din introduced Persian instruments and an Islamic astronomical office, while Guo Shoujing, Wang Xun, and colleagues compiled the Shoushi (Season-Granting) calendar in 1280–1281, one of the most accurate calendars of its time, using improved instruments and observations at stations across the empire. The result drew on both exchange and long Chinese astronomical practice.

Medicine and geography. Muslim medical offices (the Huihui pharmacy) introduced Persian and Arabic medicine, and Chinese physicians of the Jin-Yuan period developed distinct schools. Hu Sihui's Yinshan zhengyao (1330), a dietary manual for the court, shows the mix of Mongol, Central Asian, and Chinese foods. Cartographic and geographic knowledge expanded through Mongol imperial surveys and Persian scholarship; Chinese and Persian maps of the period reflect this exchange.

Painting and literati culture. Many southern scholars who did not enter government turned to painting, poetry, and calligraphy. Zhao Mengfu, a Song imperial descendant who served the Yuan, promoted calligraphy and painting that looked to Tang and Northern Song models. Later painters such as Huang Gongwang, Ni Zan, Wu Zhen, and Wang Meng developed personal landscape styles, often read as reflecting the experience of life under foreign rule, though interpretations differ.

Drama and vernacular literature. Urban entertainment flourished. Zaju (variety drama) by writers such as Guan Hanqing and Wang Shifu mixed song, speech, and plot, and the vernacular and colloquial language increasingly appeared in written form. Why drama flourished is debated: reduced examination careers freeing literate men to write, large urban audiences, and the court's tastes are among proposed factors.

Material culture. Jingdezhen blue-and-white porcelain, with cobalt from Iran, shows exchange in materials and decorative taste; textiles with Central Asian gold-thread techniques appear, and metalwork and ceramics were exported widely.

Travelers. Marco Polo's account is one source among many. Rabban Sauma, born near Dadu, traveled west in the 1270s, reached Baghdad and then Europe as an envoy of the Ilkhan, meeting rulers and clergy in 1287–1288, reversing the usual direction of the travel narrative. Ibn Battuta's Rihla also contains a description of China, although modern scholars debate how much of the East Asian itinerary reflects his own travel rather than reports incorporated into the narrative. John of Montecorvino's letters, Persian histories, and Wang Dayuan's maritime writing give further views. Comparing them shows both the reach of Yuan connections and the biases of travelers and compilers.

Exchange in the Mathematical and Technical Sciences

Beyond astronomy, Yuan scholars continued the sophisticated mathematics of the Song and Jin. Zhu Shijie, active around 1300, wrote works on algebraic methods for solving polynomial problems, including the Siyuan yujian (1303); Li Ye and Qin Jiushao, though closer to the conquest era, belong to the same tradition. Many Chinese mathematicians worked outside the court in teaching and private study, and some of their work was lost or preserved only in Korea and Japan, a reminder that survival of texts shapes what historians know. Technical writing also flourished: Wang Zhen's Nong shu (1313) combined agricultural advice with an account of wooden movable type that he had developed for printing, though woodblock printing remained dominant. Persian and Arabic works on astronomy, medicine, and pharmacology were translated or compiled at the court, and Rashid al-Din's Persian writings on Chinese medicine and history show influence in the opposite direction.

Astronomy in Detail

Jamal al-Din presented the court with a set of instruments and a proposed calendar in 1267, and an office for Islamic astronomy was set up alongside the Chinese astronomical bureau. Guo Shoujing's Shoushi calendar, promulgated in 1281, used a tropical-year value of 365.2425 days, numerically identical to the mean year later used by the Gregorian calendar (the same value appears in the earlier Tongtian calendar of 1198), and was based on new instruments, including a simplified armillary sphere, and on observations at a network of stations across the empire. It remained in use, with modifications, for centuries, under the Ming. The calendar demonstrates that exchange built on a strong local tradition: Chinese astronomers adopted ideas selectively and continued their own methods.

Medicine, Geography, and Maps

Chinese medical writing of the Jin and Yuan periods is associated with four influential masters, among them Liu Wansu, Zhang Zihe, Li Gao, and Zhu Zhenheng, whose theories about heat, purgation, the digestive system, and "yin deficiency" shaped later practice. At the same time, Muslim physicians and pharmacists introduced medicines and hospital models, and the court's dietary manual combined foods from several regions. In geography, Zhu Siben's map of the early fourteenth century and Persian maps from the Mongol world show attempts to record a larger Eurasian space. Zhou Daguan, who accompanied a Yuan embassy to Angkor, arriving in 1296 and remaining into 1297, wrote a vivid account of Khmer society, a reminder that Chinese writers observed Southeast Asia in detail.

Literary Life Under Conquest

With the examination system largely closed for decades, literate men followed varied paths: clerical service, teaching, medical practice, writing for the theater, and painting. The great dramas associated with Guan Hanqing, including Injustice to Dou E, and Wang Shifu's Romance of the Western Chamber were performed to urban audiences, often by professional troupes in which women performers had prominent roles. Many works center on questions of injustice, love, and the plight of ordinary people, though whether they express a coded reaction to Mongol rule is contested. Song lyrics (sanqu) and local vernacular literature also flourished. At the court, texts were translated into Mongolian, and the Secret History of the Mongols, composed in the thirteenth century in Mongolian, reflects a different literary tradition within the same empire.

Travelers Compared

Different travelers reported different things. Marco Polo, a merchant, emphasized wealth, cities, and revenue. John of Montecorvino, a missionary, wrote about conversions and difficulties. Rabban Sauma, a Church of the East monk born in the Yuan realm, traveled west in the 1270s with his companion Marqos, who later became the head of his church as Mar Yahballaha III. Sauma was sent by the Ilkhan in 1287 to seek European allies, meeting rulers and clergy, including Philip IV of France, Edward I of England, and Pope Nicholas IV. Wang Dayuan sailed across the Indian Ocean as a merchant, and Zhou Daguan reported on Angkor. Reading these accounts together shows that "foreign travelers in China" is only part of the story; Yuan subjects and officials also traveled abroad as envoys, merchants, and clerics.

China and the Wider World

The Yuan interacted with the wider world as an active power, not only as a recipient. It sent embassies and military expeditions, regulated overseas shipping, supported scholars who exchanged knowledge with Persia and Central Asia, and exported ceramics, textiles, and ideas. Connections included the Ilkhanate, whose ruling house maintained close kinship and scholarly ties with the Yuan; Korea (Goryeo), which was tied to the Yuan by marriage and political subordination; Japan and Southeast Asia, where Yuan campaigns met resistance; and the Indian Ocean world. These links also had costs: wars, taxes for shipbuilding, and movements of disease and people. Seeing the Yuan within Mongol Eurasia helps students avoid imagining China in isolation.

Section 39.9

Crisis, Rebellion, and the Fall of Yuan

Yuan instability did not result from a single cause. This section explains why government became increasingly fragile; Chapter 40 narrates the Red Turban wars, Zhu Yuanzhang's rise, and the founding of the Ming.

Succession and court politics. After Khubilai's death in 1294, the throne passed through a rapid series of rulers: Temür Khan (1294–1307), Khaishan (1307–1311), Ayurbarwada (1311–1320), Shidebala (1320–1323, assassinated), Yesün Temür (1323–1328), and then a civil war between rival courts in 1328. Succession depended on negotiation among princes, imperial in-laws, and powerful ministers rather than on a stable rule of primogeniture, and factions at court shifted policy repeatedly, as in the on-and-off suspension of examinations. Powerful chancellors such as Bayan dominated the court in some periods. Toghon Temür, who became emperor in 1333 as a youth, ruled through a long reign marked by factional struggle.

Fiscal and monetary strain. Heavy spending on court gifts, religious patronage, princely grants, military operations, and construction outran revenue. Governments turned to more paper currency, and notes lost value; the currency reform begun in 1350 and the Zhizheng notes issued in 1352 aggravated the problem. Exemptions narrowed the tax base. Corruption and administrative strain, including abuses by clerks and by agents of ortoq lenders, worsened local grievances, though historians caution against attributing collapse to "corruption" alone without examining the institutional pressures behind it.

Environment and labor. The Yellow River repeatedly flooded and shifted its channel in the 1340s, with a major breach in 1344 affecting the Shandong and Huai region and the Grand Canal. Famine and displacement followed in several regions. In 1351 the chief minister Toghto organized a huge labor mobilization, reportedly of about 150,000 civilians and 20,000 soldiers, to restore the river's course. Contemporary accounts say that wage and conscription burdens helped inflame unrest. Records also mention epidemics and famine in parts of the north in the fourteenth century. Whether and how plague spread in China in this period is debated, and evidence is uneven.

Social unrest and early rebellions. Even before 1351, local uprisings occurred, including that of the salt trader and sea merchant Fang Guozhen on the coast from 1348. Millenarian Buddhist and White Lotus-influenced movements, such as that led by Han Shantong and Liu Futong, found support among laborers and impoverished farmers. When the Red Turban movement broke out in 1351, the government's attempt to restore the Yellow River and its fiscal and military weaknesses left it poorly placed to respond. Local armies and regional commanders, some Mongol and some Chinese, gained autonomy, and central authority weakened further.

By the mid-1350s, the Yuan regime had entered terminal crisis. It still possessed formidable institutions, armies, and resources, and it survived for years more, but it could no longer restore control across its southern and central provinces. The detailed account of how rebellion became a new dynasty belongs to Chapter 40.

Cause and Effect

Why Yuan Government Became Unstable

Causes

Long-term: hereditary service burdens; a narrow tax base shaped by exemptions; reliance on paper money without durable reserves; unstable succession practice; layered and sometimes competing institutions; thin ties with southern elites. Short-term: Yellow River floods and the 1351 labor mobilization; the 1350 currency reform; factional struggle at court; famine in several regions; millenarian preaching.

Event or transformation

Between the 1340s and the mid-1350s, fiscal strain, environmental disaster, and political division converged, and the Red Turban movement and other uprisings spread across the central and southern provinces, so that the court entered terminal crisis.

Effects

Immediate: spreading rebellion; loss of southern revenue; regional commanders acting on their own authority; paralysis at the capital. Long-term: the end of Mongol rule over China and the creation of a new dynasty (Chapter 40), with lasting legacies in capital location, administrative geography, and religious and commercial connections.

Historical complexity

No single factor explains the fall. Fiscal, environmental, political, social, and military causes interacted, and historians disagree about their relative weight. "Corruption" or "decline" alone is not an explanation without the institutional conditions behind it.
Historical Debate

What Kind of State Was the Yuan?

The question: Was the Yuan a Chinese dynasty ruled by Mongols, or a Mongol empire that governed China as one part of its realm?

Interpretation A — A Chinese dynasty under Mongol rule: Khubilai adopted the dynastic name, built a Chinese-style capital and ancestral temple, used Chinese ministries, and inherited Song and Jin administration; later Chinese historiography included the Yuan in the official dynastic sequence.
Interpretation B — A Mongol imperial state that included China: Legitimacy rested on Chinggisid descent, the keshig household and appanages mattered, officials came from across Eurasia, and the ruling house maintained ties to other khanates and to Mongolia; many scholars thus emphasize the Yuan's place in Mongol Eurasian history rather than only Chinese history.

Evidence: Chinese administrative records and ritual institutions support A; Persian and Mongol sources, appanage rules, and the multilingual composition of the elite support B. Many historians now argue the two are not mutually exclusive.

Why it matters: The answer shapes how historians treat conquest dynasties, the continuity of "Chinese" institutions, and modern narratives about the Yuan's place in national history.

Student task: Which kinds of evidence best distinguish the two interpretations, and what would a combined interpretation need to explain?

Regional and Frontier Context

Yuan experience varied by region. North China, long under Mongol and Jin influence, faced reconstruction, appanage claims, and proximity to the capital. The south, especially Jiangzhe, supplied revenue, grain, and manufactures while experiencing the loss of examination careers and Mongol overseers. The southeast coast linked to Indian Ocean commerce. Yunnan, incorporated under Sayyid Ajall and others, became a new frontier province with Muslim and Buddhist influence. Tibet was governed through the Sakya-linked office and religious ties. The Northeast, including Liaoyang, was home to Mongol princes, Jurchen, Korean, and Khitan communities, and was the site of Nayan's 1287 revolt. Mongolia remained a heartland whose governance depended on princely and military arrangements. Frontier peoples were active participants, negotiating tribute, trade, marriage, and service rather than merely objects of central policy.

Section 39.10

Chapter Review and Yuan Dynasty Checkpoint

The Yuan dynasty governed a Chinese empire as a Mongol imperial state. Its institutions combined adaptation and hierarchy: inherited ministries and local government alongside the keshig, darughachi, hereditary households, and appanages; status categories that privileged some groups without forming a rigid racial caste; a fiscal system based on land, households, monopolies, and paper money; and a religious and intellectual world of unusual diversity. Eurasian connections expanded exchange but did not erase warfare or local constraints. By the mid-fourteenth century, fiscal strain, environmental disaster, political division, and widening rebellion undermined the dynasty's ability to rule.

Historical Evidence

The Yuan may be the most multilingual period in the history covered by this textbook. A historian who reads only Chinese bureaucratic records sees a state of ministries and registers; one who reads Persian court histories sees a branch of a Mongol imperial family; one who reads Tibetan monastic sources sees a patron of Buddhism; and one who reads European travel accounts sees a wealthy court visited by merchants and missionaries. None of these views is simply wrong, and none is complete.

Chinese Official and Local Records

The Yuan shi (History of the Yuan) was compiled in 1369–1370 under the Ming, in haste, using Yuan archives and veteran records. Because it was written by the dynasty that replaced the Yuan, it contains errors, duplicate entries, and a Ming-era perspective, yet it preserves treatises on institutions, finance, and the examinations. The Yuan dianzhang, a compendium of Yuan administrative and legal cases compiled in the early fourteenth century, is valuable because it records actual regulations and disputes in a vernacular and "direct translation" style, giving insight into how officials and clerks thought and wrote. Other sources include local gazetteers, which record taxes, temples, and local elites; epitaphs and collected writings of scholars; agricultural manuals such as the Nongsang jiyao; and statutory collections. Most of these were produced by officials or literati, so they say more about administration and elite concerns than about the lives of herders, laborers, or women.

Persian, Mongolian, and Tibetan Sources

Persian histories, notably those produced at the Ilkhanid court such as Rashid al-Din's world history, describe the Mongol conquests and the Yuan court, drawing on Chinese and Mongol informants. They are essential for the transregional picture but they were written for Ilkhanid patrons and may reflect rivalries and the limits of information about distant China. The Secret History of the Mongols offers an insider's view of Chinggisid political culture. Tibetan chronicles and biographies of Sakya leaders illuminate religious ties and the Tibetan administrative arrangements, with the bias of religious institutions. Stone inscriptions, often in Chinese and Mongolian in Phags-pa script, record imperial decrees that grant privileges to temples and monasteries and are among the best evidence for how policy was announced locally.

Travel Accounts and Missionary Letters

Marco Polo, John of Montecorvino, Odoric of Pordenone, Zhou Daguan, and Wang Dayuan offer perspectives from outside the court bureaucracy. They provide details that official sources omit, such as markets and customs, but their reliability varies and each writes for an audience. Ibn Battuta's account of China is especially debated. Historians therefore compare such accounts with Chinese and Persian records and treat uncorroborated details cautiously.

Archaeology and Material Evidence

Excavations at Dadu in Beijing and at Shangdu have revealed street plans, drainage, palace foundations, and household goods. Paper currency and printing plates, coins, and hoards illustrate the monetary system. Ceramics are also vital: the blue-and-white vases known as the "David Vases," bearing an inscription that corresponds to 1351, are among the earliest securely dated examples of Jingdezhen blue-and-white porcelain. The wreck of a merchant ship found off Sinan in Korea, which sank in 1323 after departing Qingyuan (modern Ningbo), carried thousands of Chinese ceramics, metal objects, and coins and provides direct evidence of maritime trade. Multilingual tombstones in Quanzhou, in Arabic, Syriac, and other scripts, record the port's diverse communities. Paintings and calligraphy, such as those of Zhao Mengfu and the later landscape masters, are evidence for elite culture but need interpretation, since they were produced for specific patrons and audiences.

Limits and Questions for Students

Survival bias, language barriers, and political purpose shape every source. Many Mongolian-language materials are lost; many Chinese materials were edited under the Ming; Persian and European accounts reflect distance from events. Numbers such as population totals and revenue figures depend on registers with known gaps. Students should ask who produced a source, in which language, for whom, and what it would not have recorded. They should also ask how combining sources changes the picture: for example, Chinese legal compendia, a Phags-pa stele, and a Persian chronicle may each describe the same religious privilege or merchant partnership very differently.

Chapter Summary

The Yuan dynasty was a Mongol-ruled imperial state that governed Chinese territories through inherited institutions while participating in the wider Mongol world. Chapter 38 explained conquest; this chapter examined governance.

Khubilai Khan emerged from a succession struggle to claim multiple forms of legitimacy: Chinggisid, Great Khan, and emperor of a Chinese dynasty. The dynastic name Yuan (1271) and the completion of the Song conquest (1279) did not mean abandoning Mongol identity.

Dadu, built on the northern plain, and Shangdu, on the grassland, linked agricultural China and the steppe. The move north changed the geography of government and made the capital dependent on southern grain.

Government combined Chinese ministries with Mongol overseers, the keshig, appanages, hereditary households, relay stations, and a multilingual officialdom. Provincial secretariats shaped later provincial administration. Examinations were suspended, revived in 1315, and interrupted again.

The four-part scheme of Mongols, semu, Hanren, and Nanren described real distinctions but should not be read as a rigid caste system; class, region, occupation, and patronage mattered.

The economy rested on agriculture, taxation, canal and sea grain transport, paper currency, and monopolies. Overissue of currency, exemptions, and heavy spending created fiscal strain.

Mongol Eurasia fostered exchange through ortoq partnerships, relay networks, and maritime routes, but it was politically divided and not uniformly peaceful. The Yuan inherited Song maritime institutions rather than inventing them.

Religious patronage of Tibetan Buddhism, Daoism, Islam, Christianity, and Confucian learning was political, produced competition, and varied over time.

Astronomy, medicine, painting, drama, and ceramics show both exchange and local creativity. Marco Polo is one traveler among many, and his account must be compared with other sources.

Succession instability, fiscal strain, floods, labor demands, and rebellion pushed the Yuan toward crisis. Chapter 40 follows the Red Turbans and Zhu Yuanzhang, whose movement ended Yuan rule in China and founded the Ming.

Chapter Reflection

The Yuan dynasty shows that political unity over a vast territory could be achieved through institutions that were neither purely Chinese nor purely Mongol. Its capital, provincial structure, and administrative practices shaped the Ming and Qing, even as later regimes defined themselves partly against it.

The chapter also demonstrates that exchange, hierarchy, and strain can coexist. Eurasian connections brought knowledge, goods, and people, while taxation, hereditary service, and currency problems burdened households. Understanding both sides prevents simple narratives of foreign oppression or cosmopolitan golden age.

Finally, the Yuan connects Chinese history to Asian and global history. It was one component of the Mongol world and part of the Indian Ocean economy, and it affected Korea, Tibet, Yunnan, and the Northeast. Chapter 40 follows the rebellions that ended Yuan rule and the rise of Zhu Yuanzhang, whose movement would found the Ming dynasty.
