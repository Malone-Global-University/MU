Part X — Revolution, Republic, and War
65

Civil War and the Communist Victory

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

In August 1945 almost everyone in China wanted peace. Within four years, one of the largest civil wars of the twentieth century had ended in the collapse of the internationally recognized government on the mainland, the proclamation of a new state in Beijing, and the relocation of the old government to an island that had been under Japanese rule for fifty years.

When news of Japan's surrender spread across China in mid-August 1945, people poured into the streets of Chongqing, Kunming, and Chengdu, and in the countryside villagers who had endured eight years of invasion, requisition, and bombing allowed themselves to hope. The war had killed millions of soldiers and civilians, displaced tens of millions, wrecked railways and farmland, and left the economy dependent on printing money. Yet the victory also left two rival political systems facing one another. The Nationalist government of Chiang Kai-shek was recognized by the Allies as China's government and had a larger army, an air force, and a seat among the great powers. The Chinese Communist Party, led by Mao Zedong, governed base areas containing many millions of people and commanded a battle-hardened army that had grown dramatically during the war. Both claimed to speak for the nation, and each regarded the other as a threat to the nation's future.

This chapter studies how that rivalry turned from negotiation to full-scale war, and how, between 1945 and the end of 1949, the Communists came to control the Chinese mainland. It studies a political settlement that failed despite widespread hopes for peace and major diplomatic efforts to achieve it; a military contest whose balance shifted from Nationalist superiority to Communist ascendancy; and a struggle over land, money, cities, villages, and legitimacy that shaped the capacity of each side to sustain war. It ends in late 1949 with two governments, the People's Republic of China in Beijing and the Republic of China government on Taiwan, each claiming to be China.

The chapter matters because this outcome defined the political map of modern East Asia. The Communist victory established the state that governs the mainland today, set the terms of the Sino-Soviet alliance and the Cold War in Asia, and opened a division across the Taiwan Strait whose status remains contested. It also matters because it is easy to explain badly. A narrative of inevitability, in which a corrupt regime was always doomed, hides the real uncertainty of 1946. A narrative of foreign betrayal or of mere battlefield luck hides the weight of economic collapse, administrative weakness, and rural organization. This chapter therefore asks students to treat the result as the product of interacting causes, and to distinguish immediate causes of military defeat from longer-term political and social conditions.

What changed in these four years was dramatic. The Nationalists went from holding almost all of China's principal cities and railways to holding a few coastal islands and Taiwan. The Communists went from a largely guerrilla force, anchored in rural base areas, to an army capable of conventional campaigns involving a million soldiers and millions of civilian laborers. Land was redistributed in much of the north and northeast through a movement that gave many poor villagers land and status while also involving coercion and violence. A currency lost nearly all its value, wiping out the savings of urban households. A new constitution was promulgated in Nanjing while war raged, and a very different founding document, the Common Program, was adopted in Beijing.

What continued is equally important. Both parties remained heirs to the revolutionary nationalism of Sun Yat-sen and the May Fourth generation, and both promised national strength, an end to semi-colonial humiliation, and social modernization. Both were Leninist in organization to a significant degree, with disciplined parties, party-controlled armies, and doubts about liberal pluralism. Regional commanders, local elites, and ordinary households continued to bargain, hedge, and adapt, as they had throughout the Republic. And the settlements of 1945, including Japan's surrender, the return of Taiwan, and the Soviet presence in Manchuria, left unresolved questions about sovereignty that outlasted the war itself.

The people affected included soldiers conscripted from the countryside, peasants who received land or lost it, landlords and rich peasants classified by new class labels, students and professors protesting rising prices, merchants and clerks whose savings vanished, villagers who pushed wheelbarrows of grain for armies, residents of besieged cities such as Changchun, minorities in the northwest and southwest, and the residents of Taiwan, who experienced the end of Japanese rule and the arrival of Nationalist administration in 1945 and who were drawn into the crisis of 1949 without having been participants in the civil war.

Historians reconstruct these events from a diverse and uneven body of evidence: diplomatic records, notably the American Foreign Relations of the United States volumes and the 1949 China White Paper; party documents, directives, and speeches from both sides; military telegrams and campaign histories (which require careful reading because many were written under partisan or official sponsorship); constitutional texts and legislative records; newspapers, diaries, and memoirs by participants including generals, officials, students, and foreign observers; and economic statistics of uneven reliability. Newer archival openings in China, Taiwan, Russia, and the United States have refined older assumptions. The chapter connects to the larger history of China by closing the Republican era that Chapters 58 to 64 traced and by leading into the founding and consolidation of the People's Republic in Chapter 66. It connects China to Asia and the world through the Cold War, Soviet and American policy, and the decolonizing region around it.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the conditions in August 1945 that left two rival political and military systems claiming authority over postwar China.
  2. Distinguish the 1945 Chongqing negotiations from the 1946–1947 Marshall Mission and analyze why neither produced a lasting settlement.
  3. Trace the course of the military conflict from the Manchurian campaigns of 1946 through the Communist counteroffensive of 1947.
  4. Compare Nationalist and Communist armies in manpower, equipment, command structure, and strategy at the outbreak of war.
  5. Analyze how inflation, currency reform, administrative weakness, and urban protest eroded Nationalist legitimacy.
  6. Evaluate Communist land reform as a strategy of mobilization, recognizing both its redistributive effects and its coercion and violence.
  7. Describe the Liaoshen, Huaihai, and Pingjin campaigns separately and assess why they together destroyed Nationalist field armies.
  8. Assess the roles of the United States and the Soviet Union without treating either as the sole determinant of the outcome.
  9. Interpret primary sources including the Double Tenth Agreement, the 1947 Constitution, the Outline Land Law, and the Common Program.
  10. Connect the history of Taiwan, including the February 28 Incident, to the Nationalist retreat of 1949.
  11. Evaluate competing historical explanations of why the Nationalists lost the mainland and why the outcome was not simply inevitable.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did the Nationalists, recognized internationally as China's government and initially superior in conventional strength, lose the mainland between 1945 and 1949?
  2. Why did political settlement fail in 1945–1946 when neither side could be certain of military victory?
  3. How did geography, railways, and the importance of Manchuria shape the early military contest?
  4. How did economic collapse and administrative weakness affect the Nationalist government's ability to command loyalty and sustain war?
  5. How did land reform help the Communist Party mobilize soldiers and supplies, and what were its costs and variations?
  6. What role did foreign powers play, and why should that role not be mistaken for a single decisive cause?
  7. Why did the decisive campaigns of 1948–1949 produce such a rapid collapse of Nationalist field armies?
  8. Why did the end of the civil war leave two governments claiming to represent China, and what does Taiwan's own history add to that question?

Historical Context

The civil war of 1946–1949 cannot be understood as the simple resumption of the conflict that began in 1927 (Chapter 61), because the Second Sino-Japanese War (Chapter 64) had transformed both parties. The Nationalist government, forced from Nanjing in 1937, had governed from Chongqing in the Sichuan Basin, a region of rich agriculture but limited industry. It depended on taxes in kind, conscription, and inflationary finance, and its armies, though large, suffered from disease, desertion, and poor supply. The wartime government nonetheless bore the principal diplomatic and military burden of resistance in the eyes of its allies, and in 1943 the abolition of unequal treaties and the Cairo Declaration seemed to confirm China's great-power status.

The Communist Party had survived the Long March and the Nanjing Decade (Chapter 62) and had used the war to expand from a few poor northwestern areas into a network of base areas across North China, the Shandong peninsula, the lower Yangtze, and elsewhere. From its Yan'an base it consolidated leadership under Mao Zedong, completed a rectification movement (1942–1944), promoted rent reduction in the countryside, and formally affirmed "Mao Zedong Thought" in 1945. By the war's end its armed forces numbered about a million regular soldiers by most estimates, supported by many more militia. Communist organization penetrated villages that Japanese forces could garrison only in the towns and along the railways, and Communist leaders emphasized mass mobilization, disciplined behavior toward civilians, and local self-government.

The geographic setting shaped the conflict. The Nationalists held the south and southwest, the Yangtze valley, and returned to the great coastal cities, which were the centers of finance, industry, universities, and press. The Communists were strongest in the rural north: the base areas of Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia, Shanxi-Chahar-Hebei, Shandong, and the Taihang mountains. Manchuria, the richest industrial region, with heavy industry and railways built by Russia and Japan, was the great prize. Railways and cities were easier to hold with conventional armies, but the countryside between them was where the Communists were organized. The Yellow River, the Yangtze, and the Huai River defined the main strategic corridors.

International circumstances added complexity. The United States had supported Chiang Kai-shek throughout the war, flown and shipped troops, and wished to see a unified, friendly China, but disliked the prospect of committing American forces to a civil war. The Soviet Union had entered the Pacific war on August 8–9, 1945, occupied Manchuria, and in the Sino-Soviet Treaty of Friendship and Alliance of August 14 recognized the Nationalist government while securing privileges in Manchuria (railways, Port Arthur, Dalian) that the wartime Yalta understanding had promised. Stalin's policy was cautious, and Chinese Communists and Soviet leaders did not always agree.

The geographic and political meaning of "China" was also in flux. Taiwan had been Japanese territory since 1895 and, following allied statements at Cairo and Potsdam, came under Republic of China administration in October 1945. Outer Mongolia had voted for independence in a plebiscite in October 1945 and was recognized by the Nationalist government in January 1946 under Soviet pressure. Hong Kong returned to British administration. Xinjiang, Tibet, Inner Mongolia, and the southwest contained peoples whose political loyalties and local rulers did not map neatly onto the two parties' competition. The two parties and their rivals each had a different image of what China ought to be, in constitutional design, land and class relations, and international alignment, and those competing visions are at the heart of this chapter.

DIVISION I — Victory, Rivalry, and the Failure of Peace, 1945–1946

Division purpose: to explain why political settlement failed even though neither side could be certain of military victory.

Section 65.1

The End of World War II and China's Uncertain Peace

Japan's announcement of surrender on August 15, 1945, was followed by the formal ceremony in Tokyo Bay on September 2. In China the surrender of the Japanese China Expeditionary Army was signed at Nanjing on September 9, when General Okamura Yasuji surrendered to the Nationalist general He Yingqin. More than a million Japanese soldiers on the Chinese mainland and in Taiwan, along with large numbers of civilians, had to be disarmed, concentrated, and eventually repatriated. Who would accept their surrender, and who would take over the territory they held, quickly became the central question of postwar politics.

Chiang Kai-shek ordered Japanese commanders to surrender only to Nationalist authorities and ordered Communist units to remain in place. Zhu De, commanding the Communist armies, issued competing orders instructing Japanese and puppet forces in areas near Communist units to surrender to them. Each side thus claimed the legal mantle of victory. The Nationalists also incorporated many former puppet troops of the Japanese-sponsored regimes into their ranks and used them to hold towns against Communist advance, a practice that outraged many Chinese who saw collaborators rewarded.

The surrender produced real celebration, but also immediate hardship. China had suffered between roughly fifteen and twenty million deaths (estimates vary widely), enormous destruction, and a refugee population that crowded the cities and roads. Returning "downriver" officials from Chongqing took over former occupied cities in a process many residents resented as predatory; the pun between jieshou (reception, takeover) and a homophone meaning plunder became widely repeated. In Shanghai and other cities the conversion rate fixed between the Japanese-sponsored currency and the Nationalist fabi (200 to 1) sharply reduced the savings of ordinary residents and made the early postwar months a source of lasting political resentment.

For historians, the first lesson of this period is to distinguish the state of affairs in August 1945 from later hindsight. The Nationalist government was the recognized government of a victorious ally, enjoyed prestige, and could expect American aid. The Communist Party was a substantial but regionally concentrated force that, in 1945, faced a stronger opponent and had to be wary of Soviet and American intentions. Neither outcome was assured.

Section 65.2

The Race to Control North China and Manchuria

The most urgent postwar contest was a race to occupy territory. The Nationalists' best divisions were in the southwest, far from the north and Northeast. The Communists were already in the countryside of North China, and in the weeks after August 15 they moved to take towns, rail lines, and Japanese weapons stocks. The United States, committed to helping the recognized government take over, used its air and naval transport to move several hundred thousand Nationalist troops north and east, and landed some fifty thousand U.S. Marines at Tianjin, Beiping, and Qinhuangdao to guard ports and railways. Their presence blurred the line between assisting a government and taking sides in a civil conflict.

Manchuria presented the sharpest test. The Soviet Red Army had swept in during August 1945, ending the Japanese puppet state of Manchukuo and occupying the main cities. Under the Sino-Soviet Treaty, the Soviet Union agreed to deal with the Nationalist government as the central authority, and delayed its withdrawal while the Nationalists struggled to arrive; it removed substantial industrial equipment from the region as "war booty," damage later estimated in the hundreds of millions of dollars. Soviet forces did not hand the Communists a formal victory: they obstructed Communist entry to some major cities at first, but allowed Chinese Communist forces to enter the countryside and smaller towns and to acquire stocks of Japanese weapons and supplies in several places.

Tens of thousands of Communist soldiers and cadres from North China and Shandong, many by sea or on foot, were rapidly sent into the Northeast. Led by Lin Biao and Peng Zhen, they built a regional army and administration. Nationalist forces under Du Yuming, supplied in part by American shipping, fought their way up from Shanhaiguan and eventually occupied Shenyang in March 1946, after the Soviet forces announced their departure. By then the Northeast had become the strategic center of gravity of the civil war, and the Nationalist government's commitment to hold its cities and railways would stretch its lines.

Section 65.3

Chiang Kai-shek, Mao Zedong, and the Chongqing Negotiations

Between August 28 and October 10, 1945, Mao Zedong, accompanied by Zhou Enlai and others, travelled to Chongqing to negotiate with Chiang Kai-shek. Chiang had issued several invitations, partly to test Communist intentions and partly to show Chinese and American audiences that he sought peaceful unity; American ambassador Patrick Hurley escorted Mao on the flight. It was the first face-to-face meeting of the two leaders in two decades.

Their aims diverged. The Nationalists wanted the Communist armies placed under government command and the "liberated areas" governments dissolved or integrated into the national administration. The Communists wanted a coalition government, recognition of their local governments, and a reorganization of the military that left them a large share of forces. Behind the table stood facts on the ground: while talks proceeded, forces clashed in the Shangdang campaign in Shanxi in September and October, where Communist troops defeated a Nationalist force under Yan Xishan's command.

The talks ended with the "Summary of Conversations between the Representatives of the Government and the Chinese Communist Party," known as the Double Tenth Agreement because it was announced on October 10. It affirmed a commitment to peaceful reconstruction, agreed to convene a political consultative conference with other parties, and acknowledged the end of political tutelage as a goal. It left the two most important questions, the Communist armies and the status of liberated-area governments, unsettled.

Historical Vocabulary
Political Tutelage (xunzheng, 訓政)

In Sun Yat-sen's three-stage scheme, political tutelage was the period after military unification during which the Kuomintang would train the people in self-government before constitutional rule began. The Nationalist government formally declared tutelage in 1928, and its end had been promised, repeatedly postponed, and criticized by liberals and Communists alike. Whether and when tutelage would end, and who would share power in the interim, is therefore the constitutional core of the 1945–1947 negotiations.

Primary Source Window

"Summary of Conversations between the Representatives of the Kuomintang and the Communist Party" (the Double Tenth Agreement), October 10, 1945

Creators: Negotiators for the Nationalist government (including Wang Shijie, Zhang Qun, Zhang Zhizhong, Shao Lizi) and the Communist Party (Zhou Enlai, Wang Ruofei) · Date: October 10, 1945 · Place: Chongqing · Type: Jointly released political communiqué · Audience: the Chinese public, the Allies, and both parties' followers

Context: Issued at the end of Mao's six-week stay in Chongqing, with American mediation in the background. The text exists in English translation in the American diplomatic record and in Chinese in both sides' document collections.

Paraphrase: The two sides agreed that China must pursue peaceful reconstruction and avoid civil war; that a political consultative conference with representatives of parties and non-party figures would be convened; that political tutelage should end as soon as possible and be replaced by constitutional government; and that basic civil liberties and the legal equality of parties should be assured. They recorded that they had not agreed on the reorganization of the Communist-led armies or on the status of the liberated-area governments.

Analysis: The document shows both parties willing to commit publicly to peace and constitutional government, and it is valuable as evidence of the stated aims of each. It does not show how either side intended to carry them out. Its omissions are the evidence: the unresolved questions of armies and territory later caused the failure of the arrangement. Because the text was written for publication, it also reflects the need of both parties to appear reasonable before a war-weary public and American mediators; it does not tell us about private calculations. The voices of smaller parties, local leaders, and ordinary citizens are absent.

Questions for students:

  1. Which issues did the agreement settle, and which disputes over armies and liberated areas remained unresolved?
  2. Why did both parties have an incentive to publish commitments to peace in October 1945?
  3. What does the agreement reveal about the proposed political consultative conference and the end of tutelage?
  4. What would private negotiating records and military orders reveal that the published agreement cannot?
Section 65.4

The Marshall Mission and the Search for a Coalition

In late 1945 President Truman sent General George C. Marshall to China with instructions to promote a ceasefire, a national conference, and the integration of armies, linking American support to progress toward a representative government. Marshall arrived in December 1945. His mission differs from the Chongqing talks of September–October: it was a mediation by a foreign government with real leverage over the Nationalists' supplies and prestige, conducted in Nanjing, Chongqing, and Beiping over more than a year, until his recall in January 1947.

In January 1946 Marshall helped negotiate a ceasefire, effective January 13, and established a Committee of Three, initially Marshall, Nationalist representative Zhang Qun (Chang Chun), and Communist representative Zhou Enlai, to negotiate military arrangements. A separate tripartite Executive Headquarters in Beiping, with its own commissioners and field teams, was charged with supervising the ceasefire. In February the parties agreed on a plan for reducing and integrating the armies into a single national force under civilian control, with Communist units forming a minority fraction of the whole. The scheme, on paper, was a rare agreement; implementation required both parties to surrender the military power that guaranteed their safety.

The difficulties were concrete. The ceasefire covered China proper, but its application to Manchuria was disputed, and local commanders on both sides interpreted it according to their own advantage. Field teams could investigate but could not enforce. Marshall himself was seen by each side as biased, by Communists because the United States continued to supply and transport Nationalist troops, and by Nationalists because he pressed them to compromise. The mission remains a central subject in the study of American policy; as a historical process, it demonstrates that mediation cannot substitute for an agreed distribution of power.

Section 65.5

Political Legitimacy, Constitutional Reform, and Competing Visions of China

The Political Consultative Conference (PCC), which met in Chongqing from January 10 to 31, 1946, brought together delegates from the Kuomintang, the Communist Party, the China Democratic League, the Youth Party, and independents. It agreed on principles for a constitution including a cabinet system accountable to the legislature, stronger provincial autonomy, and guarantees of civil rights, and on a coalition government in the interim. For a moment it appeared that China might move toward a pluralist constitutional order.

The agreements prompted opposition within the Kuomintang, where conservative leaders objected that they diluted the party's authority and the powers of the presidency. In March 1946 the party's Central Executive Committee modified the principles in ways that the Democratic League and the Communists saw as retreat. Disputes over the number of seats in a coalition cabinet, and whether the Communists could veto major decisions, also deepened mistrust.

The competing visions were real. The Nationalists proposed to move from tutelage to constitutional government through a National Assembly under their own supervision, claiming a mandate from the wartime leadership and from the legacy of Sun Yat-sen. The Communists advocated "New Democracy," a coalition under their leadership of classes opposed to imperialism and feudalism, and presented themselves as defenders of democracy against one-party rule while maintaining their own party-army. The smaller parties and many liberal intellectuals wanted a middle way, but had no army, and the assassinations in Kunming in July 1946 of Democratic League figures Li Gongpu and Wen Yiduo, widely attributed to elements connected to the security services, showed the risk of that position.

Section 65.6

The Breakdown of Negotiations and the Renewal of Civil War

Fighting never fully ceased. In April and May 1946 the contest for Manchuria escalated: Communist forces briefly seized Changchun as Soviet troops left; Nationalists won heavy battles at Siping in May and retook Changchun on May 23. Marshall secured a short truce in Manchuria in June, but the front turned again toward battle, and in the summer Nationalist offensives began in central and northern China. In October 1946 Nationalist forces took the Communist-held city of Zhangjiakou, and Chiang's government convened the National Assembly in November without participation from the Communists or the Democratic League.

The assembly adopted a constitution in December 1946. In January 1947 Marshall left, issuing a public statement criticizing extremists on both sides, and in March 1947 the Communist delegation under Zhou Enlai left Nanjing. The United States had imposed an embargo on arms shipments between July 1946 and May 1947, but supplies continued to flow, and large quantities of surplus equipment were sold on easy terms to the Nationalists. By early 1947 there was no longer a political process, only a nationwide war.

Historical Debate

Could Renewed Civil War Have Been Avoided?

The question: Was the failure of the 1945–1946 negotiations avoidable through different decisions, or did structural conditions make a coalition government impossible?

Interpretation A — Peace was possible but sabotaged by decisions: Some historians stress that the Political Consultative Conference showed that agreement was within reach and that peace failed because of choices: Nationalist hardliners' resistance to power-sharing and Chiang's confidence in military superiority; Communist maintenance of a party army and base areas; and American policy that ostensibly mediated while supporting one party. Avoiding the breakdown in Manchuria in spring 1946 might have given the political process more time.
Interpretation B — The structure made settlement unlikely: Others argue that two parties, each with its own army, administration, and ideology, could not share power without one controlling the army, and neither could rely on the other to honor an agreement. In this view the negotiations were a contest conducted by other means, and the issue of the armies was insoluble. The Soviet position in Manchuria, the leverage American supplies gave the Nationalists, and the fear on both sides of being destroyed after demobilizing all reduced the room for compromise.

Evidence: Diplomatic records (the American Foreign Relations of the United States volumes), party documents, memoirs of negotiators, and military telegrams show real attempts at settlement and also preparation for war on both sides. Interpretations depend on how much weight is given to stated aims versus action on the ground.

Why it matters: The question shapes judgments about responsibility for the war, the significance of constitutionalism and the "Third Force," and what American policy could have achieved. It also tests the habit of avoiding both determinism and the assumption that any outcome was equally likely.

Student task: What additional evidence would help decide between A and B? Consider whether agreements about armies were enforceable.

DIVISION II — Renewed Civil War and the Changing Military Balance, 1946–1947

Division purpose: to explain how the conflict changed from battles over postwar occupation into a nationwide contest for military control.

Section 65.7

Nationalist and Communist Armies at the Outbreak of War

Commonly cited estimates for mid-1946 give the Nationalists about 4.3 million troops, including rear-area and service forces, against about 1.2 million Communist regular troops, with Communist militia adding perhaps two million more. The Nationalists had an air force and navy, tanks and heavy artillery, and about thirty to forty divisions trained and equipped with American assistance. They controlled most railways and large cities and about three quarters of the population (figures are estimates). Such statistics come from partisan sources and should be treated cautiously, but they convey the Nationalists' advantage in conventional capacity.

Communist forces were organized differently. They had little heavy equipment at first, but extensive experience in mobile and guerrilla warfare, close relations with local populations in the base areas, and a unified party-army command in which political commissars worked beside commanders. Field armies, regional forces, and militia formed layers of a system that tied soldiers to villages. Weapons came largely from captured Japanese stocks and, increasingly, from Nationalist forces whose soldiers were captured and often incorporated into the PLA.

Nationalist weaknesses were organizational. Chiang Kai-shek distributed command through factions, and kept close personal control over operations even from a distance, often issuing detailed orders that ignored local conditions. Units loyal to the central government stood alongside regional armies under commanders such as Yan Xishan, Fu Zuoyi, Bai Chongxi, and the Ma family in the northwest, whose interests did not always align with the center. Troops were held in garrisons in cities and along railways, dispersing strength. The Nationalist strategy was to take cities and lines of communication; the Communist strategy was to destroy enemy units, even at the cost of giving up places.

Section 65.8

The Campaigns for Manchuria

After the Siping and Changchun battles of spring 1946, the Nationalists held the main cities and railway lines of southern Manchuria, while the Communists, under Lin Biao and political commissar Luo Ronghuan, controlled much of the countryside and northern areas, including Harbin. In the winter of 1946–1947 Communist forces made four crossings of the Songhua River to strike Nationalist outposts and relieve pressure on their base area in the south, around Linjiang, which they held against several Nationalist drives.

In the summer and winter of 1947, Lin Biao's forces launched large offensives that cut rail lines and isolated garrisons; by early 1948 the Nationalists held only a few cities (Shenyang, Changchun, Jinzhou and corridor towns). Nationalist commanders, including Du Yuming, then Chen Cheng, then Wei Lihuang, struggled with supply by rail and by air and with garrisons that could not be relieved. Meanwhile the Communist administration built a base from land reform, wartime taxation, and the Northeast's industrial and mining resources, using also its connection to the Soviet-occupied area of North Korea and the Soviet-run railways to the north for sanctuary and trade.

Section 65.9

Warfare in North China, Shandong, and the Central Plains

Outside Manchuria, the Nationalists launched offensives in 1946 to open the railways (the Jinpu line between Tianjin and Pukou, the Longhai line east to west, and the Pinghan between Beiping and Hankou), and to take Communist-held towns. In central China, their attempt to crush Communist forces near the Yangtze and the Central Plains in mid-1946 failed to destroy the main units, which dispersed or withdrew. In the north, they captured Zhangjiakou and Chengde in 1946.

The Nationalists in 1947 shifted to "key-point" offensives against Communist areas in Shaanxi and Shandong. In Shandong, Chen Yi and Su Yu's East China Field Army fought a flexible war of concentration and withdrawal, culminating in May 1947 at Menglianggu, where the elite Nationalist Reorganized 74th Division was destroyed and its commander, Zhang Lingfu, was killed. This defeat showed that Nationalist spearhead units could be isolated and lost if they advanced faster than neighbors. The war in these regions also involved local forces, militia, intelligence from villagers, and rail sabotage, aspects emphasized in Communist accounts and in studies of the war's regional history.

Section 65.10

The Capture of Yan'an and Communist Strategic Adaptation

On March 19, 1947, Nationalist forces under Hu Zongnan captured Yan'an, the Communist Party's wartime capital since 1937. The Nationalist press celebrated, and many observers expected the northwest base to collapse. Communist leaders had evacuated, leaving a small garrison and carrying out a plan of defending the area with a small force while the party leadership moved around northern Shaanxi under Mao, Zhou Enlai, and Ren Bishi, avoiding capture. Peng Dehuai's Northwest Field Army, a fraction of Hu's numbers, won tactical victories at Qinghuabian, Yangmahe, and Shajiadian in March–August 1947, destroying individual Nationalist brigades.

Yan'an illustrates the distinction between holding territory and defeating an army. The Nationalists took a symbolic city but dispersed troops to garrison it and to chase the Communist army across difficult terrain, while the Communists preserved their forces and sought opportunities to destroy isolated units. The episode did not decide the war, but it set a pattern: Communist planners were willing to trade territory for time and for the annihilation of enemy units, a logic Mao summarized in principles issued in December 1947 that emphasized destroying enemy forces rather than holding cities or places.

Section 65.11

The Communist Strategic Counteroffensive of 1947

In June 1947, Communist leaders decided to shift from defense to offense. At the end of June, Liu Bocheng and Deng Xiaoping's Central Plains force crossed the Yellow River and in August marched south into the Dabie Mountains, in the interior between the Yangtze and Huai rivers, placing Communist armies in the Nationalist rear and threatening Wuhan and Nanjing, areas that had seemed safe. They suffered heavy losses and hardship in the march, but the move forced the Nationalists to divert units and gave the Communists a base in the central plains. Chen Yi, Su Yu, and Chen Geng's forces launched parallel advances in Shandong and south of the Yellow River.

By the end of 1947 the Communist forces were taking the offensive in most theaters, enlarging their regular army through recruitment and captured Nationalist soldiers, who were retrained and reorganized in "speaking bitterness" meetings. The Communist force had been officially termed the People's Liberation Army, and its transition from primarily guerrilla to conventional warfare, with artillery and engineering units, was underway. The Nationalists still held most large cities, but the strategic initiative had changed.

Section 65.12

Nationalist Government and the Crisis of Political Authority

The Nationalist government in 1946–1948 was a party-state in transition. Chiang Kai-shek held the offices of chairman of the National Government (until 1948), leader of the Kuomintang, and commander-in-chief, and relied on factions: the military men of the Whampoa Academy, the "CC Clique" of party organizers, technocrats and bankers, and regional leaders such as Li Zongren and Bai Chongxi of Guangxi. Rivalries among these groups, and Chiang's habit of balancing them, made administration slow and consistent policy difficult.

The constitution adopted by the National Assembly on December 25, 1946, promulgated on January 1, 1947, and effective on December 25, 1947, provided for a president, five branches of government (the Executive, Legislative, Judicial, Examination, and Control Yuans), a National Assembly, and guarantees of rights. Elections to the assemblies were held in late 1947 and early 1948, under conditions of war and without Communist participation, and several smaller parties took part. In April 1948 the National Assembly elected Chiang president and Li Zongren vice president, in a contest in which Li's candidacy showed party dissent. In May 1948, the Emergency Temporary Provisions for the Period of Communist Rebellion, adopted by the Assembly in April, took effect and allowed the president to exercise emergency powers beyond the constitution's normal limits. Students should keep these dates distinct: the Constitution's democratic provisions and the Emergency Provisions' emergency powers coexisted, and the latter were not repealed until 1991.

Administrative problems compounded the political ones. Corruption in the takeover of former occupied areas, smuggling, official profiteering, and the use of conscription to extract funds or labor alienated many. These accusations should be assessed with evidence, not assumed: contemporary American and Chinese observers documented abuses, but many officials also worked honestly under severe constraints. What historians emphasize is a government that could not credibly claim to be reforming itself while it fought, and whose authority outside the cities was thin.

Section 65.13

Inflation, Currency Reform, and Economic Collapse

The economy inherited from wartime was already under strain. The government had financed deficits by printing money, the Japanese-occupied industrial areas were damaged and disorganized, transport was crippled, and the takeover of industries and the exchange rate for puppet currency created further disruption. Postwar American credits and the arrival of UNRRA relief helped, but the government's budget deficit, of which military expenditure was by far the largest part, remained financed by the note press.

Prices rose rapidly throughout 1946–1948 and accelerated into hyperinflation in 1948. Wholesale price indices in Shanghai rose by many thousands of times between 1945 and 1948, and by mid-1948 prices might change within a day. Salaried employees, teachers, civil servants, pensioners, and small savers saw the real value of their incomes and savings vanish. Landlords, merchants with stocks, and speculators who held goods or foreign currency could do better, and hoarding and black markets expanded. Currency lost its function: people demanded silver dollars, gold, rice, or U.S. dollars, and the government's own paper no longer served as stable money.

On August 19, 1948, the government introduced the gold yuan, to replace the fabi at 3 million to 1, with a nominal peg of four gold yuan to one U.S. dollar. Citizens were required to exchange gold, silver, and foreign currency for the new notes, and prices were frozen at August 19 levels. In Shanghai, Chiang Ching-kuo, Chiang Kai-shek's son, led an enforcement campaign against hoarders. Initially prices stabilized, but the government kept issuing money, shortages grew as producers would not sell at frozen prices, and by late October controls collapsed. The government abandoned the price freeze in early November. The collapse destroyed the savings of many who had trusted the exchange, and with it much of the remaining public confidence among urban middle-class households, whose support the government had needed.

Historical Evidence

Using Inflation and Currency Statistics

Historians of the 1940s economy use price indices compiled by the government, by Shanghai research institutes, by foreign banks and consulates, and in contemporary newspapers, along with the memoirs of financial officials and the records of the Central Bank. These sources differ in method and coverage: Shanghai wholesale prices do not represent villages; official exchange rates were fixed while black-market rates moved quickly; and indices assume the availability of goods at the quoted price. Series are therefore best used to show direction and rough magnitude, not exact figures.

Students should also ask what the numbers cannot show. They do not show who had access to hard goods or foreign exchange, how families coped by barter or by relying on rural kin, or how the Communist-held areas, which had their own currencies and parallel inflation problems, managed price stability through grain-based accounting and local production. Comparing the two systems requires care: Communist base areas were smaller, less urban, and less dependent on imports.

Historical Vocabulary
Fabi (法幣) and Gold Yuan (金圓券)

Fabi, literally "legal tender," was the paper currency introduced by the Nationalist government in 1935 and used until 1948. Its collapse in value during and after the war is a central economic fact of the period. The gold yuan was the replacement note introduced in August 1948, nominally backed by gold and foreign exchange reserves; its rapid failure ended attempts to stabilize the mainland economy.

Section 65.14

Urban Society, Protest, and the Loss of Confidence

The economic crisis fell hardest on the urban salaried classes and on workers whose wages lagged behind prices. University professors and teachers sold books and furniture to buy food; civil servants relied on rice allowances; factory workers faced layoffs as industries struggled with competition from imported American goods and with shortages of raw materials and power. Strikes and labor disputes multiplied in Shanghai and other cities, and many were met with repression.

Students took on a central political role. In December 1946 the alleged rape of a Peking University student, Shen Chong, by an American marine set off nationwide protests against American troops in China. In 1947 the "Anti-Hunger, Anti-Civil War" movement, with demonstrations in Nanjing, Shanghai, and elsewhere, expanded into broader criticism of government policy. The government responded with arrests, bans on demonstrations, and surveillance. In 1947 and 1948 the deaths of student activists, such as Yu Zisan at Zhejiang University in October 1947, further intensified resentment. Communist underground organizations were active among students, but the movement drew on grievances that were not simply manufactured by the party.

Intellectuals and professionals who had admired Chiang during the war or hoped for a liberal alternative in 1946 were increasingly alienated by 1948. Some businessmen kept silent and prepared to move capital to Hong Kong; others stayed in Shanghai hoping that a change of government would bring stability. The loss of confidence among cities' educated and propertied people did not mean enthusiasm for the Communist Party, which they distrusted in many cases; it meant growing willingness to accept or even wait for change. Historians often describe this as a collapse of legitimacy, but it is best understood as the unwillingness of influential groups to bear costs for the government's defense.

Section 65.15

Communist Land Reform and Rural Revolution

Land and rent were central to rural life in North China. During the war against Japan the Communist Party had moderated its policy, emphasizing rent and interest reduction to build a broad coalition. After the surrender, however, party leaders concluded that peasant support required more far-reaching change. On May 4, 1946, the Central Committee issued the "Directive on the Land Question," which called on local party organizations to support peasants in settling accounts with landlords, including by confiscating land, while generally protecting the property of middle peasants and small landlords.

In 1946 and 1947 land reform spread in the north, northeast, and Shandong, accompanied by "settling accounts" meetings and "speaking bitterness" sessions in which villagers recounted grievances. Excesses occurred: landlords and rich peasants were beaten, humiliated, killed, or dispossessed, and sometimes middle peasants were wrongly classified or attacked. The national land conference of September 1947 and the "Outline Land Law" issued on October 10, 1947, called for the abolition of landlord land ownership and the distribution of all village land, including that of landlords, on an equal per-capita basis, administered through poor peasant leagues and village committees.

In late 1947 and early 1948 leaders recognized that radical practices were harming production and the unity needed for war. Mao's report of December 1947 emphasized protecting middle peasants and some commercial and industrial property, and in early 1948 party leaders including Ren Bishi criticized "leftist" deviations and ordered corrections and reclassification. These corrections show that the party responded to local reports, and that the extent of violence varied: it was greater in some counties and under some work teams than in others, and relatively less in areas where reforms were carried out earlier or where landlords were few.

The effects were significant but should be measured carefully. Land reform gave many poor and landless villagers land, animals, and tools, and a new sense of standing; it created village organizations loyal to the party and provided a base for recruitment. It also involved coercion, killings whose numbers cannot be firmly established, property confiscations, and the creation of class labels that shaped lives. Historians debate how far peasant support rested on material benefit, on fear, or on political participation; the evidence suggests a mixture varying by place. Land reform in this chapter refers to the civil-war-era program in Communist-held areas; the nationwide program after 1949 belongs to Chapter 66.

Historical Vocabulary
Fanshen (翻身) and Speaking Bitterness (訴苦)

Fanshen, "to turn the body over," described the transformation of peasants' material and social position through land reform. Suku, "speaking bitterness," denoted public meetings where villagers recounted past injustice to build collective grievances against landlords and, later, against enemies of the revolution. The terms came to stand for the Communist Party's own account of rural revolution; scholars treat them both as real experiences and as organized political practices.

Section 65.16

Communist Rural Government and Mass Mobilization

Communist administration in the liberated areas combined party committees, village governments, peasant associations, women's associations, militia, and army-supported local units. Villages were organized to supply grain, carts, stretchers, shoes, and labor; taxes in grain and money were collected under progressive rules; and local cadres, many of rural origin, were trained in political education. Recruitment of soldiers was linked to land reform: families receiving land were expected to send sons, and families of soldiers received preferential treatment such as help with farm work.

Women took on new roles in some places: they were encouraged to join associations, to spin and weave cloth and make army shoes, to work in the fields as men left, and sometimes to claim a share of land. Marriage reform and campaigns against practices such as child betrothal were part of the program, though the party often subordinated women's issues to military needs and encountered resistance from local patriarchal norms.

The system had coercive elements. Pressure on individuals to enlist, mandatory labor, class labels, and public humiliation were also tools. The party also paid attention to discipline in the army and to avoid harming civilians, and the Three Main Rules of Discipline and Eight Points of Attention were repeatedly taught. Captured Nationalist soldiers were reeducated and often joined PLA units. All this gave the Communist side a logistical and organizational capacity in the countryside that the Nationalist administration, relying more on conscription officers and local elites, lacked.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Village in the Land-Reform Years

For a family in a northern village, the years 1946–1948 could bring land, new obligations, and danger at once. A landless laborer might receive a few mu of land and a share of tools or a draft animal, and find himself elected to a peasant association; his wife might attend a women's meeting and spin cloth for the army. His sons could be urged to join the army, and the household would owe grain tax and labor service. A formerly comfortable landlord family could lose its land and house, be publicly denounced, and perhaps be beaten or killed, while a middle-peasant family could fear being misclassified. A village's experience depended on the work team, the local balance of lineages, the proximity of fighting, and the season.

Historians reconstruct these experiences from party reports, accounts by participant observers such as William Hinton's Fanshen (about a Shanxi village), interviews with survivors, and local gazetteers; each source carries limits, including sympathy, selection, and political constraint. The evidence supports the view that local variation was large.

Section 65.17

Civilian Experience and the Human Cost of Civil War

The civil war's human costs fell heavily on civilians. Armies on both sides requisitioned grain, animals, and carts; conscription removed laborers from farms; fighting disrupted planting and harvests; flooding and the breached dikes of the Yellow River, which had been redirected during the Japanese war and was returned to its former course in 1946–1947, forced villagers to move and rebuild. Millions were displaced, and refugees filled the cities and railway stations.

Sieges brought some of the worst suffering. At Changchun, besieged by Communist forces from mid-1948 until October, residents starved as the Nationalist garrison controlled limited food and the besiegers tightened the cordon; estimates of civilian deaths range from tens of thousands to several hundred thousand and are disputed, reflecting the uncertainty of the evidence and the political sensitivity of the subject. In other places, cities changed hands with less violence, or through negotiated surrender. Both sides also carried out executions of perceived enemies, and Nationalist forces and police, like Communist work teams, killed or imprisoned suspected opponents.

Total casualty figures for the war are not firmly established. Military casualty claims published by each side are partisan, and civilian death estimates are uncertain; scholars treat them as approximate. Understanding the war's human cost requires attention to ordinary experiences: a conscript's march, a villager's decision to hide grain, a student's flight from a city, a family's choice to stay or go.

Section 65.18

The United States, the Soviet Union, and China's Civil War

American support for the Nationalists after 1945 was substantial but limited and conditional. The United States provided transport, surplus equipment, and credits; a total aid figure of roughly two billion dollars is often cited for the postwar years in American official accounts, though the exact figure depends on definitions. In the July 1946–May 1947 embargo, military shipments slowed, and in 1948 a smaller China Aid Act provided further funds. American advisers, including General Albert Wedemeyer on a 1947 mission and General David Barr as military adviser, reported pessimistically on Nationalist leadership and strategy. Washington would not commit American troops to the war, and it increasingly concluded that no amount of assistance could save a government that its own officials thought was losing popular and military support. The August 1949 China White Paper presented this view, and was fiercely criticized in the United States by supporters of Chiang.

Soviet policy was also complicated. The Soviet Union recognized the Nationalist government, signed the 1945 treaty, and was ambivalent about Communist victory; Stalin reportedly doubted the Chinese Communists' prospects at times and favored a coalition. Nonetheless, Soviet occupation of Manchuria helped the Communists to a degree: access to Japanese weapons, sanctuary in North Korea and in areas near the border, training and medical support, and trade. Historians continue to study Russian archives and memoirs to assess how far Soviet help was decisive.

Cause and Effect

Foreign Assistance and Chinese Political Choices

Causes

Cold War strategy; American wartime commitment to Chiang; Soviet security interests in Manchuria; Chinese leaders' requests for aid and their own diplomacy.

Transformation

American transport, surplus supplies, and credits supported Nationalist deployments; Soviet occupation shaped the access of both sides to Manchuria's cities, arms, and railways.

Aid enlarged Nationalist capacity at the start but could not resolve the problems of command, finance, and morale; Soviet assistance helped Communist consolidation in the Northeast without determining campaigns. Foreign involvement also gave each side propaganda material.

DIVISION IV — The Three Decisive Campaigns, 1948–1949

Division purpose: to explain the military decisions that transformed the balance of power. Figures for troop strengths and casualties come largely from official campaign histories and should be treated as approximate.

Section 65.19

The Strategic Balance in 1948

By mid-1948 the military balance had narrowed sharply. Commonly cited estimates place the PLA at about 2.8 million men and the Nationalist armies at about 3.6 million, a much smaller gap than in 1946, with the Communists holding a large part of North and Northeast China, including many villages that provided recruits and supplies. Communist forces had acquired artillery and engineers, often from captured stocks, and had learned to conduct sieges and large-scale encirclements. Their headquarters moved from northern Shaanxi to Xibaipo in Hebei in 1948, nearer to the main theaters.

The Nationalists were stretched. Garrisons were scattered across isolated cities, supply by rail was repeatedly cut, and morale suffered from inflation and long service. Chiang Kai-shek weighed whether to withdraw from Manchuria and consolidate in North China and south of the Yangtze, but concerns about prestige, American opinion, and the loss of the industrial Northeast delayed decisions. His commanders in the field often disagreed with Nanjing's directives, and Chiang's personal interventions, including flights to the front, sometimes complicated command. Mao and his generals decided in the summer of 1948 to mount a series of large campaigns using the more than a million men of the Northeast, Central Plains, East China, and North China forces to destroy the main Nationalist armies in fixed positions.

Section 65.20

The Liaoshen Campaign

The Liaoshen campaign, named for the Liaoxi corridor and Shenyang, lasted from September 12 to November 2, 1948. Lin Biao and Luo Ronghuan's Northeast Field Army, numbering around 700,000 by commonly cited estimates, aimed to cut the Nationalist garrison in Manchuria off from North China by seizing Jinzhou, at the neck of the Liaoxi corridor, rather than first attacking the besieged but formidable positions at Changchun and Shenyang. Mao and Lin Biao held to this plan against some commanders' doubts about the risks.

The Communists attacked Jinzhou on October 14 and took it in about thirty hours, capturing its commander Fan Hanjie. Nationalist relief columns from Shenyang and from the sea at Huludao were stalled. In Changchun, the Nationalist 60th Army, composed of troops from Yunnan under Zeng Zesheng, defected on October 17, and on October 19–20 the remaining Nationalist force surrendered. Liao Yaoxiang's 9th Army Group, moving west from Shenyang to try to retake Jinzhou, was encircled and destroyed near Heishan and Dahushan in late October. Shenyang fell on November 2, and Yingkou shortly after.

The campaign destroyed or captured about 470,000 Nationalist troops by Communist accounts and gave the Communists control of Manchuria's industries, rail network, and captured weapons, including artillery and tanks, and freed Lin Biao's army to move south. The political consequences were also large: the loss of the main Nationalist force in the Northeast, which had seemed capable of holding the cities, signaled collapse to others. The siege of Changchun, however, was also an episode of civilian catastrophe (see Section 65.17), and students should hold the military and human dimensions together.

Section 65.21

The Huaihai Campaign

The Huaihai campaign (November 6, 1948–January 10, 1949) was fought in the region around Xuzhou, the rail junction of the Longhai and Jinpu lines, and the Huai River, a territory strategically close to Nanjing. The Communists committed the Central Plains Field Army (Liu Bocheng, Deng Xiaoping) and the East China Field Army (Chen Yi, Su Yu), under a general front committee chaired by Deng Xiaoping, with the Communists' own estimates indicating about 600,000 troops against some 800,000 Nationalists, though Nationalist strengths were dispersed and uneven.

The campaign unfolded in three stages. In the first, Communist forces trapped and destroyed Huang Baitao's 7th Army Group around Nianzhuang, east of Xuzhou, after the Nationalist 3rd Pacification Zone commanders He Jifeng and Zhang Kexia defected on November 8, opening the way for the attack. In the second stage, Huang Wei's 12th Army Group, advancing to relieve Xuzhou, was encircled at Shuangduiji, southwest of Suxian, and annihilated in mid-December. In the third stage, the Nationalist command under Du Yuming withdrew from Xuzhou at the end of November with three army groups, was caught by Communist forces at Chenguanzhuang, and was destroyed by January 10, 1949; Du Yuming was captured. The Nationalist side lost some 550,000 troops by Communist estimates.

The campaign depended on logistics as well as tactics. Official histories report several million civilian laborers, perhaps more than five million over the campaign, who moved grain, ammunition, and wounded on carts, wheelbarrows, and shoulder poles in conditions of winter cold. Historians treat such figures cautiously, but agree that rural mobilization in the Shandong, Henan, and Jiangsu base areas was essential, and that the Communists' integration of captured Nationalist soldiers, over many thousands, into their ranks accelerated their growth. Nationalist failures included delay and uncertainty in command, poor coordination among army groups, and the defections of local commanders.

Section 65.22

The Pingjin Campaign

The Pingjin campaign (November 29, 1948–January 31, 1949) targeted the Beiping–Tianjin region, held by Fu Zuoyi's North China "Bandit Suppression" Headquarters with perhaps 500,000 troops, including elite units and regional troops. Lin Biao's Northeast forces, arriving earlier than the Nationalists expected after the Liaoshen campaign, joined Nie Rongzhen's North China forces. The Communists cut Fu's forces into separate groups around Zhangjiakou, Xinbaoan, Tianjin, and Beiping, to prevent them from retreating by sea or south, and to avoid destruction of Beiping.

In December, the Communists encircled and destroyed the Nationalist 35th Army at Xinbaoan and took Zhangjiakou; on January 14–15, 1949, they stormed Tianjin in about thirty hours, capturing its commander, Chen Changjie. Fu Zuoyi, isolated in Beiping and negotiating through intermediaries, signed an agreement on January 21–22 providing for the peaceful transfer of the city, and PLA troops entered on January 31. His forces, about 200,000, were reorganized into the PLA. The peaceful handover spared Beiping's historic buildings and population from combat and was a model for later surrenders, though it depended on a combination of military pressure, contacts with the Communist underground, and the commander's own calculations. Fu Zuoyi later served in the People's Republic's government. Nationalist losses in the campaign are given by Communist sources as roughly 520,000.

Section 65.23

Why the Nationalist Armies Collapsed

Comparing the three campaigns reveals common patterns. Each involved Nationalist garrisons or armies spread over a wide area, relying on rail lines and cities, and facing Communist forces able to concentrate superior strength at the point of decision. In each campaign defections or surrenders, whether of regional commanders or whole units, played a major role. Command at the top was slow and sometimes contradictory, while Communist generals operated within a unified plan and with strong coordination, though even they faced debates about timing and risk.

Supply and morale mattered. Isolated garrisons depended on airlift and on a currency that had collapsed; soldiers faced low pay, harsh discipline, and doubts about their cause, while the Communist side emphasized political education and treated captured soldiers leniently, which made surrender less fearful and recruitment easier. Local support was uneven but real: the Communists were better able to obtain intelligence, transport, and food in the rural areas where fighting occurred. At the same time, military attrition mattered: the loss of the veteran, American-equipped divisions in 1948–1949 left no reserve force that could stabilize the line.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Nationalists Lose the Mainland?

The question: How should historians weigh military, political, economic, social, and international causes of the Nationalist defeat?

Interpretation A — Political and social explanations: Many historians emphasize the erosion of Nationalist legitimacy through hyperinflation, corruption, repression, factionalism, and the failure to address rural grievances, and the Communist capacity to organize villages through land reform, tax systems, and army-political work. On this view battlefield defeat expressed deeper weaknesses of the regime.
Interpretation B — Military and contingent explanations: Others stress strategy, command, and decisions: Chiang's overextension in Manchuria, divided command, the speed of Communist conversion to conventional warfare, and specific turning points such as Jinzhou. Some also note that Communist success depended on coercion, discipline, and the Soviet-occupied Northeast, and caution against treating Nationalist collapse as inevitable.

Evidence: Party records, military telegrams, diaries, American assessments, economic series, interviews, and local studies of land reform and conscription, all of uneven reliability. Official campaign histories from the PRC and Taiwan have each shaped narratives, and newly opened archives allow comparison.

Why it matters: The question shapes how the 1949 revolution is understood: as the product of popular choice, organizational strength, coercion, accident, or a combination, and how much weight is given to the war against Japan and to foreign powers.

Section 65.24

The Final Peace Efforts and the Nationalist Crisis of 1949

After the campaigns of winter 1948–1949, Nationalist leaders debated whether to fight on or seek terms. On January 1, 1949, Chiang Kai-shek's New Year message hinted at willingness to negotiate; on January 14 Mao Zedong published eight conditions for peace, including punishment of "war criminals," abolition of the 1947 constitution, reorganization of Nationalist armies, confiscation of "bureaucrat capital," land reform, and a new political consultative conference leading to a coalition government without "reactionaries." On January 21, 1949, Chiang announced he was stepping aside from the presidency, and Vice President Li Zongren became acting president. Chiang retained control over the party, the remaining gold and silver reserves, and key units, and began shifting resources to Taiwan.

Li Zongren, supported by the Guangxi clique, sought a peace settlement and sent a delegation led by Zhang Zhizhong to Beiping in April. The Communist side presented a draft Agreement on Internal Peace on April 15 with a deadline of April 20. Li and the Nanjing government, divided between those who wanted peace and those who wanted to resist along the Yangtze, rejected it, in part because it required essentially accepting Communist terms and in part because acceptance would have split the Nationalist camp. Some members of the delegation, including Zhang Zhizhong, remained in Beiping and joined the new regime.

The failure of the 1949 peace efforts is sometimes treated as proof of obstinacy on one side or the other. A careful reading distinguishes what each side could realistically offer. The Communists, with their military advantage, offered terms that preserved a place for individuals and some forces willing to cooperate but not the Nationalist state as it existed; the Nationalist leadership was divided between Chiang, who preferred to preserve forces for a longer struggle, and Li, who lacked the means to enforce a settlement.

Section 65.25

Crossing the Yangtze and the Fall of Nanjing and Shanghai

Beginning on the night of April 20–21, 1949, a PLA force of about a million men crossed the Yangtze along a front of several hundred miles, from Jiujiang to near Jiangyin, against defenses that had been expected to hold much longer. Nationalist units, with low morale and a broken command, often retreated or surrendered. Britain's HMS Amethyst and other British vessels were fired on in the river on April 20, an incident that damaged British relations with the Communists. Nanjing, the Nationalist capital, was occupied on April 23, a symbolic moment, since the government had already moved its offices to Guangzhou in February. Hangzhou fell in early May, Wuhan in mid-May, and the Communists also took Taiyuan (April 24) and Xi'an (May 20) in the north.

Shanghai, China's largest city, was defended for two weeks by troops under Tang Enbo, who had prepared fortifications; fighting from May 12 to 27 caused damage and casualties on both sides, including many civilians, though the city's infrastructure was largely preserved. The PLA, instructed to maintain discipline, was widely noted for soldiers sleeping on pavements rather than entering homes. The new authorities under Chen Yi as mayor faced inflation, unemployment, hundreds of thousands of refugees, and a blockade of the port by the Nationalist navy. The way Communist authorities handled Shanghai, partly by retaining skilled personnel and cooperating with some industrialists and professionals, and partly by controlling the currency, is a subject for Chapter 66.

Section 65.26

Communist Advances Across Southern and Western China

The extension of Communist authority across the south and west in the second half of 1949 was uneven and often negotiated. In Hunan, Nationalist commanders Cheng Qian and Chen Mingren declared an uprising on August 4 and handed over Changsha; Fuzhou fell in mid-August; in the Northwest, Peng Dehuai's army overcame the forces of Ma Bufang and Ma Hongkui in Gansu, Qinghai, and Ningxia in August and September, taking Lanzhou on August 26. Guangzhou, where the Nationalist government had relocated, was taken on October 14 after a Nationalist withdrawal, and the government fled to Chongqing. Guiyang fell in mid-November, Chongqing on November 30, and Chengdu in late December, after Yunnan's governor Lu Han defected in early December and Sichuan commanders opened talks.

Some border and island areas remained beyond Communist control in 1949, including Tibet, Hainan, the Zhoushan islands, and offshore islands such as Jinmen (Quemoy), where Nationalist forces defeated a Communist landing at Guningtou on October 25–27. These were taken, or left, in 1950 or later, and the chapter's narrative closes before those developments.

Regional and Frontier Context

The Northwest, Xinjiang, and the Southwest

The Northwest of 1949 was governed in part by the Ma family of Hui Muslim commanders, who combined provincial rule with army command in Gansu, Qinghai, and Ningxia, and whose defeat opened the region. In Xinjiang, the garrison commander Tao Zhiyue and the provincial chairman Burhan Shahidi, a Tatar political figure, announced the province's transfer to the Communists on September 25, and PLA units entered in October. In the northern part of the province, the Ili-based "Three Districts" movement, which had begun in 1944 as a revolt with Soviet support and had administered Ili, Tarbagatai, and Altay, negotiated its own accommodation with the Communists in 1949; several of its leaders died in an aircraft crash on the way to Beijing in August, and the region's later incorporation is part of the history of Xinjiang covered in Chapters 66 and 76.

In the Southwest, the political and social landscape was diverse: Yunnan was ruled by Lu Han and local military families, Sichuan by regional commanders with their own interests, and Guizhou, Yunnan, and western Sichuan were home to Yi, Miao, Bai, Tibetan, and other peoples with their own local leaders. Tibet was administered by its own government at Lhasa, which expelled Chinese government representatives in 1949 and whose relationship to the new state was addressed in 1950 and afterward. Frontier regions were not simply followers of the center's change; they involved local rulers, minority communities, and neighboring powers in negotiating how the transition would occur.

Section 65.27

October 1, 1949: Establishment of the People's Republic

In March 1949, at the Second Plenum of the Seventh Central Committee at Xibaipo, the party had announced the shift of its work focus from the countryside to the cities and warned against complacency. From September 21 to 30, the Chinese People's Political Consultative Conference (CPPCC) met in Beiping, with about 660 delegates representing the Communist Party, democratic parties, labor, women, youth, ethnic minorities, overseas Chinese, and independent figures including Song Qingling, Li Jishen, and Zhang Lan. Its delegates adopted the Organic Law of the Central People's Government and the Common Program, chose Beiping as the capital under its old name Beijing, adopted a flag with five stars and a provisional anthem, and elected Mao Zedong chairman of the Central People's Government.

On October 1, 1949, from the rostrum at Tiananmen, Mao proclaimed the establishment of the People's Republic of China (PRC) and the formation of the Central People's Government, with Zhou Enlai as premier and foreign minister. The Soviet Union recognized the new government the next day, followed by several socialist states; Burma and India recognized it in December, and Britain in January 1950; the United States did not.

The founding was a milestone, but it was not the end of the civil war nor of consolidation: fighting continued in the south and west, the Nationalist government still held Taiwan and other places, and the new state had to govern cities, an economy shaken by war, and a vast rural population. Chapter 66 examines these tasks.

China and the Wider World

The proclamation took place in a world dividing into Cold War blocs. In a June 1949 speech Mao said that China would "lean to one side," toward the Soviet Union and the socialist camp, a position that reflected the party's ideology and its expectations of aid, and also its distrust of the United States, which had supported the Nationalists. Mao traveled to Moscow in December 1949 to negotiate what became the Sino-Soviet Treaty of February 1950 (Chapter 66). The new state's diplomacy was active: it sought recognition from neighbors in South and Southeast Asia, where decolonization was underway, and it presented itself as a revolutionary model for Asia. Washington withheld recognition and debated its own responsibility for the "loss of China," a contentious domestic political issue that shaped American policy for decades.

Section 65.28

Taiwan Under Nationalist Rule Before the Retreat

Taiwan had been a Japanese colony since the Treaty of Shimonoseki of 1895. Its population of about six million included Hokkien- and Hakka-speaking Han settlers whose families had come from Fujian and Guangdong over previous centuries, as well as Indigenous peoples of the mountains and east coast. Japanese rule had built railways, schools, public health, and a sugar and rice economy, imposed assimilation policies, and subordinated islanders to colonial authorities. In the Cairo Declaration of 1943, the Allies stated that Taiwan should be restored to the Republic of China, and on October 25, 1945, Chen Yi, Chief Executive of the Taiwan Province and garrison commander, received the Japanese surrender in Taipei, an occasion that many islanders welcomed with hope.

The hope soon faded. The new administration relied heavily on mainland officials who regarded many islanders as tainted by Japanese influence, controlled industries and trade through monopoly bureaus, and presided over inflation, rice shortages, and corruption. Many Taiwanese, who had served as clerks or professionals under the Japanese, found themselves excluded from senior posts. Differences in language, experience, and expectations between the "benshengren" (people of the province) and "waishengren" (people from other provinces) sharpened.

On the evening of February 27, 1947, in Taipei, agents of the Monopoly Bureau struck a widow selling untaxed cigarettes and a bystander was shot. The next day crowds protested, and guards fired on demonstrators at the Executive Administrator's office. Protests spread across the island, and local leaders formed a Settlement Committee that presented demands for political reform. Chen Yi, while negotiating, asked for troops from the mainland; they landed at Keelung from March 8–9, and the army and police suppressed the uprising, killing, arresting, and executing many, including prominent lawyers, professors, and students. Chen Yi was replaced in May 1947, but the incident left deep distrust. Estimates of the death toll vary; a 1992 Executive Yuan report suggested between 18,000 and 28,000 killed, though other estimates differ.

Historical Evidence

Studying the February 28 Incident and Taiwan's Transition

For decades the incident could not be openly discussed in Taiwan, where martial law lasted from 1949 to 1987. After democratization, the government opened archives, commissioned an official report (1992), apologized, and established a memorial day and foundation. Sources now include government and military records, newspapers, diaries and memoirs of officials and residents, Japanese-era and postwar documents, oral history interviews, and records held in Academia Sinica's Institute of Taiwan History and national archives. Each has limits: official reports may omit or minimize; memoirs may be shaped by later politics; oral testimony depends on memory and was collected decades later; and casualty figures remain estimates. Historians therefore compare sources and note disagreements about numbers and responsibility, while agreeing on the core fact of large-scale military repression.

Section 65.29

The Republic of China Government Retreats to Taiwan

The Nationalists had begun preparing Taiwan as a refuge in 1948–1949. Chiang Kai-shek sent his son Chiang Ching-kuo and trusted officials, shipped much of the central bank's gold and silver and cultural treasures, and appointed loyal generals, including Chen Cheng, as provincial chairman. Taiwan's martial law was declared in May 1949 and a currency reform in June introduced the New Taiwan dollar to insulate the island from the mainland inflation. In the second half of 1949 hundreds of thousands of soldiers, officials, and civilians crossed the strait; estimates for the total number of arrivals in 1949 range from about one million to about two million, and uncertainty remains.

The Executive Yuan resolved on December 7 to move to Taipei and began functioning there on December 9; Chiang flew from Chengdu to Taipei on December 10, 1949. Li Zongren left for the United States for medical treatment and did not return; in March 1950 Chiang formally resumed the presidency. The government retained control of Taiwan, the Penghu (Pescadores) islands, Jinmen, Mazu, and some other islands, for a time Hainan and the Zhoushan archipelago, and claimed to be the legitimate government of all China. The People's Republic made the same claim for itself and regarded Taiwan as part of its territory. The American position, in the White Paper period, was to avoid further military commitment, and it changed only after the Korean War broke out in 1950. The legal and political status of Taiwan has since been described differently by the governments concerned and by scholars, and later chapters examine those questions.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The Republic of China Before and After 1949

What Changed

  • The government's effective territory shrank from most of China to Taiwan and some islands; the capital moved from Nanjing to Taipei
  • The government depended on a society, Taiwan, with its own history and population and whose relations with the arrivals were strained by 1947
  • Martial law and emergency provisions suspended much of the constitution's normal operation
  • Its international position changed with the Cold War and with the loss of the mainland

What Stayed the Same

  • The state's name, the 1947 constitution as a formal framework, the five-branch structure, and the leadership of Chiang Kai-shek and the Kuomintang
  • A claim to represent all of China and a commitment to Sun Yat-sen's Three Principles of the People
  • Many of the officials, officers, and institutions of the mainland government

Who experienced change most directly: refugees uprooted from the mainland, and Taiwanese residents who now lived under a government of arrivals. Why it matters: the pattern helps explain why the end of the civil war left two political systems, and why Taiwan's own history cannot be treated as an extension of the mainland story.

DIVISION VI — Final Review
Section 65.30

Chapter Review and Chinese Civil War Checkpoint

The governing question of this chapter is why the Nationalists, internationally recognized as China's government and initially possessing substantial military advantages, lost the mainland to the Communists between 1945 and 1949, and why the result left two rival governments claiming to represent China. The explanation developed here is one of interaction. Postwar negotiations failed because each party held an army and a territorial base and neither could surrender them safely. The military balance shifted as the Communists adapted from guerrilla to conventional warfare and as Nationalist forces were dispersed in garrisons and hampered by divided command. Economic collapse and administrative weakness reduced Nationalist support among urban groups, while Communist land reform and rural organization provided soldiers, supplies, and local administration, at the cost of coercion and violence. Foreign powers mattered but did not decide the result. The decisive campaigns of 1948–1949 destroyed the Nationalists' ability to defend the mainland.

Students should distinguish immediate causes of military defeat (command decisions, defections, supply failures, the loss of veteran formations) from longer-term conditions (inflation, factionalism, rural weakness, legitimacy). They should also avoid two errors: reading inevitability back into 1946 and treating the outcome as merely accident.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the four years between Japan's surrender and the end of 1949, when China's political order was transformed. It began with a country that had won a war but not a peace. Two parties, each with its own army, territory, and conception of China, faced each other with the Soviet Union and the United States as interested outsiders. The Chongqing talks and the Marshall Mission produced agreements on paper, but the unresolved questions of the armies and of liberated-area governments, together with the strategic importance of Manchuria, ensured that the settlements remained fragile.

By 1946 the contest had become a civil war. Nationalist forces enjoyed an advantage in numbers, equipment, and international recognition, and they took many cities. Yet their advance dispersed their strength along railways and in garrisons, while Communist forces concentrated against isolated units, as at Menglianggu, and traded territory for time, as at Yan'an. In 1947 the Communists went on the offensive, moving into the Central Plains and consolidating their position in the Northeast.

The war was also a contest of governance. The Nationalist government's economic management failed under the pressure of deficit financing, and the collapse of the gold-yuan reform in 1948 destroyed urban confidence. Administrative weakness, factionalism, repression of dissent, and corruption alienated students, teachers, workers, and many businesspeople, even those who distrusted the Communists. The 1947 constitution, adopted without major rivals and accompanied by the Emergency Temporary Provisions, showed constitutional aspirations that war constrained.

In the Communist areas, land reform, rural associations, taxation, and recruitment gave the party a means to mobilize villages. It redistributed land and status to many poor villagers, but also involved coercion, killings, class labels, and, in 1947, excesses that leaders corrected in 1948. The system supplied soldiers, laborers, and food, and incorporated captured Nationalist troops. Historians debate the balance of benefit, persuasion, and fear, and evidence shows wide local variation.

Foreign powers affected but did not determine events. American aid helped initial Nationalist deployments, but American officials judged that more assistance could not remedy Nationalist political and military problems. The Soviet occupation of Manchuria and access to Japanese stocks helped the Communists, but Soviet policy was cautious and sometimes at odds with Chinese Communist choices.

The campaigns of Liaoshen, Huaihai, and Pingjin between September 1948 and January 1949 were the culmination. Each had different terrain and commanders, but they shared Nationalist dispersal of forces, defections, command delays, supply failures, and Communist concentration backed by civilian logistics. They destroyed most of the Nationalists' best forces. The final peace efforts of 1949 failed, the PLA crossed the Yangtze, and Nanjing and Shanghai fell.

On October 1, 1949, Mao Zedong proclaimed the People's Republic, whose provisional framework was the Common Program adopted by a CPPCC that included non-Communist delegates. The new state's rise cannot be reduced to a retrospective inevitability: it followed from specific choices, accidents, and structural conditions. The new government faced the tasks of consolidation addressed in Chapter 66.

Taiwan's history was part of the story, not an epilogue. Its transition from Japanese rule to Nationalist administration, and the repression of the February 28 Incident, shaped the conditions in which the retreating government arrived. The movement of the Republic of China government to Taipei in December 1949 produced two governments, each claiming to represent China and neither able to eliminate the other, a situation whose consequences extend beyond this chapter.

For the larger history of Chinese civilization, the chapter shows how a long process of state-building, revolution, war, and social upheaval culminated in a new political order without resolving all claims about sovereignty. It also shows the interplay of military, economic, social, and international factors in explaining political change, an approach that avoids both single-cause explanations and teleology.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 65 matters because it explains how the political map of modern China was drawn. The Communist victory ended the Republican era examined in Chapters 58 to 64 and opened the history of the People's Republic. Understanding it requires holding together war, politics, economy, and society: the failed settlement, the shifting battlefield, the experience of land reform, the collapse of a currency, and the decisions of commanders and ordinary people.

The chapter shaped later Chinese history in many ways. The party that won had built institutions, in the villages and in the army, that became foundations of the new state, and the experience of land reform and mass mobilization influenced policy in the 1950s. The Nationalists' retreat created a government on Taiwan with its own trajectory and a society shaped by both Japanese colonial rule and the arrival of mainlanders. The persistence of two governments made the question of sovereignty a continuing feature of East Asian politics.

It also connects China to wider Asian and global history. The civil war occurred as the Cold War formed and as colonial empires in Asia were challenged; the Soviet and American roles, the recognition of the PRC by neighbors, and the later Korean War placed China at the center of international conflict. These developments were not simply done to China: Chinese leaders made strategic choices and shaped the international response.

Finally, the chapter helps students see Chinese history as contested and unfinished. Different governments, scholars, and communities tell the story of 1945–1949 differently, and the evidence is still being reexamined. The result was a new political order, not a conclusion to Chinese history. Chapter 66 takes up the consolidation of the new state, and the later chapters show how the questions raised here continued to unfold.
