The New Culture and May Fourth Movements

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

On Sunday, May 4, 1919, several thousand students walked out of their colleges in Beijing to protest a diplomatic decision made half a world away. What they carried with them was not a single program but a set of arguments about language, family, authority, science, and nationhood that had been building in Chinese print and classrooms for four years.

The afternoon began quietly. Students from Peking University and a dozen or so other institutions gathered at the Gate of Heavenly Peace (Tiananmen) at the southern edge of the old imperial city. They carried banners and handbills. Their grievance concerned the former German leasehold of Shandong province, which the Paris Peace Conference appeared ready to award to Japan rather than return to China. By the end of the day, some of them had marched through the Legation Quarter, a few had broken into the residence of a government minister, and thirty-two had been arrested. The episode lasted only hours. Its name, "May Fourth," came to stand for a much larger period of Chinese cultural and political life.

This chapter studies two connected but distinct developments. The first is the New Culture Movement, which began around 1915 with the founding of the journal New Youth and which brought together writers, teachers, and students who criticized inherited family and ethical norms, argued for writing in the spoken language, and debated the meaning of science, democracy, and individual freedom. The second is the May Fourth Movement in the narrow sense: the protests, strikes, boycotts, and political organizing of 1919 that grew out of the Shandong crisis. Historians often use "May Fourth" more broadly, to describe the whole era of intellectual ferment from about 1915 to the early 1920s. This textbook uses the terms carefully, because the movements overlapped without being identical, and because their participants did not agree with one another.

The period matters for several reasons. It reshaped how educated Chinese wrote and what they read. It opened public debate about the family, marriage, women's education, and the authority of elders. It showed that students, merchants, and workers could act together in the cities of Republican China, often without the sponsorship of any government or party. And it fed into the later organizing of rival political movements, though not in any predetermined way. Students will see in this chapter that the same intellectual ferment produced liberals, anarchists, socialists, cultural conservatives, and nationalists, and that many participants moved between these positions.

Much changed in these years. The literary language of the examination tradition lost ground in schools and journals to written vernacular. Criticism of the Confucian family ethic moved from the margins to the center of public argument. The idea that "youth" was a distinct social force took hold. Diplomacy, which most urban Chinese had previously treated as the business of ministers, became an object of mass concern. Much also continued. The great majority of Chinese lived in villages where few people could read, where lineage and kin obligations structured daily life, and where the debates of Beijing and Shanghai arrived late, if at all. Warlord governments continued to rule in much of the country (Chapter 59). Many reformers drew on older traditions of reform and moral critique even as they claimed to break with the past.

Those most directly affected were students and teachers in urban colleges, readers of the new journals, young women seeking schooling and a voice in marriage, urban merchants and workers who joined the protests of June 1919, and officials of the Beijing government who faced unprecedented public pressure. Villagers, and many in the cities, were affected indirectly or not at all in the short term. Historians reconstruct these events from journals and newspapers, student manifestos, memoirs and diaries written decades later, government and diplomatic records, photographs, and the literary works of the period. Each of these sources has limits. Memoirs written after the Communist and Nationalist parties had each claimed the legacy of May Fourth, for example, must be read with attention to what later authors wished to remember.

The chapter connects Chinese history to wider currents. The Shandong dispute arose from the First World War, the German and Japanese empires, and the peace settlement at Versailles. Chinese intellectuals read and debated John Dewey, Bertrand Russell, Henrik Ibsen, Nietzsche, Kropotkin, and Marx, often through Japanese translations and through the experiences of Chinese students abroad. The protests echoed, and were contemporary with, other anti-imperial movements in 1919, including the March First Movement in Korea. Chapter 59 describes the political fragmentation in which these events took place; Chapter 61 follows the organizational consequences, including party formation; Chapter 63 treats Republican society and culture more broadly.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how disappointment with early Republican politics and earlier reform efforts contributed to the emergence of the New Culture Movement.
  2. Identify the main figures, institutions, and journals of New Culture and describe how they were connected.
  3. Analyze criticisms of Confucian orthodoxy and family hierarchy, distinguishing them from the broader diversity of Confucian traditions and from contemporary defenses of cultural continuity.
  4. Describe the relationship between literary Chinese and written vernacular, and evaluate the significance of language reform for education and public participation.
  5. Interpret literature, especially the work of Lu Xun, as a form of social criticism and as historical evidence.
  6. Compare differing meanings of "science" and "democracy" among reformers, and assess the movement's treatment of women, marriage, and the family.
  7. Assess the social limits of New Culture, including differences between city and countryside, literate and non-literate communities, and regions.
  8. Reconstruct the international background of the Shandong Question, from the German leasehold through the Twenty-One Demands to the Paris Peace Conference.
  9. Trace the escalation from the May 4 demonstration to the nationwide protests of May and June 1919, distinguishing the roles of students, merchants, workers, and officials.
  10. Distinguish immediate diplomatic outcomes of the movement from unresolved political problems.
  11. Evaluate competing ideological responses (liberalism, anarchism, socialism, Marxism, cultural conservatism) without treating any later political outcome as inevitable.
  12. Contextualize historical interpretations of May Fourth as rupture or continuity, and explain how later political movements used its memory.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did a cultural movement about language, family, and ethics emerge from a political disappointment?
  2. In what sense was the New Culture Movement "new," and what older traditions of reform did it draw upon?
  3. Who was able to participate in the new public debates, and who was left out?
  4. How did the same words, such as "science" or "democracy," mean different things to different reformers?
  5. Why did a diplomatic dispute over a single province mobilize so many different social groups?
  6. What distinguishes the New Culture Movement from the May Fourth protests, and why do historians often blur the two?
  7. What changed in Chinese politics because of the events of 1919, and what remained the same?
  8. How have later political movements and historians shaped what we remember as "May Fourth"?
  9. How does the Chinese experience in 1919 connect to wider anti-imperial and student movements elsewhere?
  10. Why should historians avoid treating the later success of any one political party as the natural outcome of these years?

Historical Context

The story of New Culture begins in the generation before 1915. The Qing dynasty's defeats in the nineteenth century, discussed in Chapters 52 through 56, prompted reformers to ask what Chinese institutions and learning needed to change. Kang Youwei and Liang Qichao proposed constitutional monarchy in the Hundred Days of 1898. Yan Fu translated Western works on evolution, economics, and logic. The government abolished the examination system in 1905 (Chapter 56), cutting the formal link between classical learning and office. Thousands of Chinese studied in Japan, where many encountered translated European thought and, among some, anarchist and socialist writings. By the end of the dynasty a new reading public existed, sustained by newspapers, translation, and new-style schools.

The 1911 Revolution ended the monarchy but did not settle what would replace it (Chapter 57). The early Republic saw a provisional government, an elected parliament, political parties, and then the presidency of Yuan Shikai, who suppressed the Nationalist Party, dissolved parliament, and in 1915 moved toward a restoration of monarchy (Chapter 58). That effort collapsed in 1916 amid provincial opposition, and Yuan died in June of that year. The brief restoration of the deposed emperor Puyi by the general Zhang Xun in July 1917 failed within days. After this, the Beijing government passed among military factions and civilian cabinets with little coherent authority, while rival governments formed elsewhere, such as Sun Yat-sen's southern government in Guangzhou (Chapter 59). Political experience in these years led many educated people to conclude that changes in institutions had not been accompanied by changes in habits, loyalties, and values.

The economy, too, was changing unevenly. The First World War reduced European imports to China and raised demand for Chinese goods, producing a period of rapid growth in Chinese-owned textile, flour, and light industries in Shanghai, Tianjin, and other treaty ports. This growth enlarged both an urban working population and a class of Chinese industrialists and merchants, and it helps explain why, in 1919, shopkeepers and factory workers could join student protests. At the same time, most of the population remained rural, tied to agriculture, and subject to taxation by whichever military authority controlled their county. Modern schools were concentrated in cities and treaty ports. The ability to read classical or even vernacular Chinese was held by only a minority; estimates of literacy in this period vary widely and should be treated with caution.

Geographically, the movements centered on a handful of places: Beijing, where the government, Peking University, and many colleges were located; Shanghai, the commercial and publishing capital and home to the largest foreign settlements; Tianjin, a port and educational center; and Shandong, the province whose fate sparked the protests. Other cities, including Changsha, Wuhan, Guangzhou, Chengdu, and Nanjing, developed their own student and press communities. The word "China" in this chapter refers to the Republic of China as an internationally recognized state whose effective authority was highly divided. Taiwan was then under Japanese colonial rule and Manchuria was a zone of competing Chinese, Japanese, and Russian interests; students will see that the movements' reach in such regions was limited and distinct.

The intellectual background was equally varied. Neo-Confucian and evidential-scholarship traditions, Buddhist revival, late Qing "new fiction," popular religion, Christian missionary schools, and imported European philosophies coexisted. The older slogan of "Chinese learning as substance, Western learning for application" had by the 1910s lost much of its persuasiveness for younger intellectuals, but it had not vanished. The First World War itself shook confidence in Europe as a model. Liang Qichao, traveling in Europe in 1918–1919, wrote of a civilization shaken by war, and his reflections fed a later debate over whether China should look to Europe, to its own traditions, or to some synthesis. These tensions underlie the chapter.

Historical Evidence

The evidentiary base for this chapter differs sharply from that of earlier periods in the textbook. It is dominated by print. Journals such as New Youth, New Tide, and Weekly Critic, daily newspapers, handbills, student declarations, telegrams, and books provide contemporary statements of opinion. Government and diplomatic records, including documents of the Beijing Foreign Ministry, the Chinese delegation at Paris, and foreign ministries in Washington, London, and Tokyo, document the Shandong negotiations. Photographs of the May 4 march and of strikes survive, as do school records, textbooks, and fiction.

Each type has limits. Journals reflect the views of editors and contributors, not of the whole population. Newspapers varied in political alignment and sometimes reported rumor as fact. Diplomatic records show elite decision-making and often silence the experience of ordinary participants. Memoirs and oral recollections, many written after 1949 or after the 1920s political rifts, reflect the authors' later positions; claims about who led, who organized, and who deserves credit were themselves contested. Statistics on participation, arrests, readership, and literacy are estimates and often disputed. Students should therefore treat numbers in this chapter as approximate, and compare accounts rather than relying on any single narrative.

Section 60.1

The Crisis of Culture and Authority in the Early Republic

When the Qing dynasty fell, many reformers expected that a republican constitution, a parliament, and political parties would produce a stronger and more accountable state. In practice, the early Republic brought repeated disillusionment. Parliament was dissolved by Yuan Shikai in 1914, a constitution was manipulated to extend his power, and in 1915 his supporters organized petitions for a monarchy. At the same time Yuan's government promoted Confucian ceremonies, including sacrifices at the Altar of Heaven, and reintroduced classical study into schools. For younger critics, the association of Confucian symbols with attempted autocracy seemed to confirm a link between inherited ethical teachings and authoritarian politics. That link was an interpretation, not a historical necessity; many Confucian scholars rejected both monarchical restoration and Yuan's use of ritual, and some supported the Republic. But the political experience of 1915–1917 made the connection persuasive to many.

A second source of crisis was the repeated failure of institutional reform to produce stable authority. Chinese reformers from Kang Youwei through the republican revolutionaries had treated political change as the central problem. After 1915, a number of intellectuals argued that political forms would remain hollow without a transformation of the "ethical consciousness" of citizens, that is, of the habits of family life, the values taught in schools, and the standards of public conduct. This shift in emphasis from institutions to culture is one of the defining features of New Culture. Its advocates did not abandon politics. Chen Duxiu, for example, remained deeply political. But they came to believe that politics could not be repaired in isolation.

The international setting reinforced the sense of crisis. Japan's Twenty-One Demands of January 1915 sought, among other things, to extend Japanese control over Shandong, South Manchuria, and eastern Inner Mongolia, to secure industrial concessions, and, in a final group of demands, to place Japanese advisers in Chinese government and police. Beijing, lacking means of resistance, accepted a revised set of terms on May 9, 1915, after a Japanese ultimatum. Public anger was substantial; some groups organized boycotts of Japanese goods, and May 9 became a "National Humiliation Day" in school and press calendars. The episode taught a generation of students that national weakness had concrete consequences and that official diplomacy could not be trusted to defend the country's interests.

Finally, ideas of national strength had been shaped by late Qing writers who adapted social Darwinist language: nations, like species, competed for survival. This vocabulary persisted into the New Culture period, but its meaning was contested. Some reformers argued that China's weakness arose from a culture that suppressed individual initiative; others held that only collective discipline could save the nation. The tension between individual liberation and national salvation, a recurring theme in this chapter, was present from the outset. It was not a contradiction that any single thinker resolved, and later political movements would draw on both sides of it.

Section 60.2

The New Culture Movement and the Rise of New Youth

In September 1915 Chen Duxiu, then living in Shanghai after years of political activity and exile in Japan, began publishing Youth Magazine (Qingnian zazhi) through the Qunyi publishing house. In 1916 it was renamed New Youth (Xin qingnian), with the French title La Jeunesse on its cover. The first issue opened with Chen's "Call to Youth," which urged readers to be independent rather than servile, progressive rather than conservative, aggressive rather than withdrawn, cosmopolitan rather than isolationist, practical rather than merely formal, and scientific rather than speculative. These principles show the journal's early emphasis on individual character, outward-looking attitudes, and what the editors called a new ethics for a republic.

The circulation of New Youth was initially modest, reaching a readership counted in the low thousands, and it struggled financially. Its influence grew when Chen was appointed dean of the College of Letters at Peking University in early 1917 and the editorial office moved to Beijing. There the journal gathered a circle of contributors who taught or studied at the university: Hu Shi, Li Dazhao, Qian Xuantong, Liu Bannong, Gao Yihan, Wu Yu, and, from 1918, Lu Xun. Contributors wrote on language, ethics, women's issues, literature, politics, and philosophy. The journal also published translations of Ibsen, Turgenev, Tolstoy, and others, and brought foreign ideas to Chinese readers through accessible essays.

The editorial board was never ideologically uniform. Some contributors favored liberal individualism and gradual reform, others radical criticism of the existing order, and by 1918 a few were turning toward socialism. The editors held an open policy toward debate: letters from critics were printed, and polemical exchanges, sometimes staged by the editors themselves, helped to attract attention. An often-cited example is the 1918 exchange in which Qian Xuantong, writing as a supposed conservative, and Liu Bannong, replying, ridiculed the defenders of classical prose, an episode historians describe as theatrical but effective. Students should note that such rhetorical performances were part of how journals competed for readers.

After the events of 1919, the journal's character changed. Chen Duxiu was arrested in June 1919 for distributing political leaflets and spent about three months in detention. The editorial group dispersed, partly because of disagreements between liberals such as Hu Shi and those moving toward Marxism, and by 1920 the journal had relocated to Shanghai under Chen's direction and gradually took on a more political profile. Its history thus mirrors the larger story of the period: a platform for cultural debate that turned, under pressure of national events, toward political organization.

Section 60.3

Beijing University and the New Intellectual Communities

Peking University traced its origins to the Imperial University of Peking, founded during the reforms of 1898, and became the national university after 1912. In its early years it had a reputation for bureaucratic habits and for students who saw degrees as paths to official posts. In December 1916 the government appointed Cai Yuanpei as chancellor. Cai, who held a classical degree from the Qing system, had studied in Germany and served briefly as the Republic's first minister of education. He brought a conception of the university as a place of scholarship and aesthetic cultivation rather than only vocational preparation. He promoted what is often summarized as "freedom of thought and tolerance of many views," and appointed faculty of widely differing outlooks.

The result was an unusual intellectual mixture. Chen Duxiu, Hu Shi (who returned from the United States in 1917), and Li Dazhao, who headed the library, taught alongside traditional scholars such as Liu Shipei, Huang Kan, and Gu Hongming, the latter famous for defending Chinese culture to Western audiences. Lu Xun lectured on the history of Chinese fiction. The curriculum shifted toward electives and toward the study of Chinese literature, philosophy, and history using critical methods, including new interest in folk songs and popular literature. Student societies flourished: the New Tide Society, which published New Tide (Xinchao); the Citizen's Magazine Society; the Mass Education Lecture Corps; and groups for moral improvement and study of particular ideologies. Cai also supported night schools and public lectures, extending the university's reach beyond its enrolled students.

Peking University was not the only center. Other Beijing institutions, such as the Higher Normal School and various government and private colleges, also produced journals and societies. In Shanghai, Commercial Press and other firms created a publishing infrastructure, while missionary and private universities offered alternative models. Hunan First Normal School in Changsha, Nankai in Tianjin, and teacher-training institutions elsewhere formed their own communities, often connected by correspondence and by the circulation of Beijing and Shanghai journals. Provincial students traveling to Beijing returned with books, ideas, and personal networks, which is one reason ideas spread faster than the limited number of schools might suggest.

Academic freedom had limits. The university depended on government funds that were increasingly unreliable, and Cai faced pressure from officials who disliked criticism of tradition. Conservative critics attacked the faculty in newspapers, and rumors that the university encouraged moral disorder circulated. Students themselves were not uniformly sympathetic to the new ideas; many continued to pursue conventional careers. The university's openness also meant that disagreements flourished within it, as the later splits among its faculty show. Students should see the institution as an enabling environment, not as a single ideological bloc.

Section 60.4

Criticism of Confucian Orthodoxy and Established Institutions

The most provocative element of New Culture was its criticism of what contributors called the old ethics. Chen Duxiu argued that the "Three Bonds," the hierarchical relationships between ruler and subject, father and son, and husband and wife, were incompatible with a republic of equal citizens. He and others claimed that a political system based on loyalty to rulers and obedience to elders trained people to be subjects rather than citizens. Wu Yu, a Sichuan scholar who had studied in Japan, wrote essays attacking the family system and described the ritual code as a mechanism that harmed the weak. His targeting of "the Confucian shop" (Kongjiadian) was popularized by Hu Shi, who called Wu "the old hero who smashed the Confucian shop." Such slogans circulated widely but oversimplified the actual arguments.

Historical Vocabulary
Lijiao (礼教) — "ritual teaching" or ritual orthodoxy

Lijiao refers to the system of ritual norms and moral teaching, especially regarding hierarchy in family and society, that had been promoted by dynastic governments and educated elites as the proper order of social life. It was not identical with the teachings of Confucius or with the full range of Confucian thought. In New Culture writing it often stood for the rigid, state-endorsed side of Confucian practice: obligations of obedience, restrictions on women, and the use of moral language to justify authority.

Students should note that critics of lijiao used it in a polemical sense. Defenders replied that the ethical teachings of Confucian tradition included reciprocal obligations, moral self-cultivation, and criticism of rulers, and that abusive practices were distortions rather than the essence. Understanding this vocabulary helps explain why debates over "Confucianism" in the 1910s were often debates over different things.

Lu Xun's metaphor of a society that "eats people," presented in fiction (Section 60.6), became one of the most memorable expressions of this critique. But it is important not to treat the movement as a rejection of everything in the Chinese past. Hu Shi, while advocating a critical attitude, launched a project to "reorganize the national heritage" through systematic study of classical texts, history, and philosophy using modern scholarly methods. Chen Duxiu, for his part, criticized Confucianism as a basis for state ideology without denying that Confucius was a historical figure of importance. The movement's rhetoric of "overthrowing" tradition coexisted with serious scholarly engagement with it.

There were vigorous defenses. Kang Youwei had campaigned for Confucianism as a state religion and, in 1917, was associated with the Zhang Xun restoration attempt, which discredited that effort for many. Others took more intellectually serious positions. Liang Shuming, a Peking University lecturer, argued in lectures later published in 1921 that Chinese, Indian, and Western civilizations represented different fundamental orientations toward life and that Chinese culture, properly understood, had values the West needed. Scholars such as Du Yaquan wrote in the journal Eastern Miscellany in favor of balancing Western and Chinese learning. The translator Lin Shu, famous for rendering European novels into classical Chinese, publicly opposed the literary revolution. In 1922 a group of scholars at Nanjing launched the journal Critical Review (Xueheng) to defend humanistic traditions. These critics often accepted some of the reformers' premises about modern education while rejecting their conclusions.

Criticism extended beyond Confucian ethics. Reformers attacked arranged marriage, concubinage, footbinding (already under reform campaigns since the late Qing), the authority of lineage elders, popular religious practices that they labeled superstition, and the formal literary style of the examination tradition. They also criticized the political institutions of the Republic, including military dominance and corruption. These attacks reached different audiences in different ways. For students in cities, they could support personal decisions about marriage and schooling; for rural families, the same arguments appeared as threats to family order. Section 60.11 returns to this difference.

Section 60.5

Vernacular Chinese and the Movement for Literary Reform

For centuries, Chinese writing had existed in several registers. Literary Chinese (wenyan) was the written language of the classics, the examination system, official documents, and most scholarly writing. It was compact, allusive, and different from any spoken dialect; one learned it through years of study. Alongside it existed a written vernacular (baihua), used in popular novels such as Dream of the Red Chamber and Water Margin, in Buddhist and Neo-Confucian recorded sayings, in storytellers' texts, and in some official proclamations. Spoken Chinese itself was a family of regional varieties, such as Mandarin, Wu, Cantonese, and Min, that were often mutually unintelligible. A written vernacular based on northern Mandarin thus carried its own implications for regional communities.

Historical Vocabulary
Baihua (白话) and Wenyan (文言)

Wenyan is literary or classical Chinese as a written register; baihua means "plain speech" and refers to written Chinese modeled on spoken usage. Neither term refers to a single fixed standard. The vernacular promoted after 1917 drew on spoken Mandarin, older vernacular fiction, and new grammatical conventions influenced by translation from European languages and Japanese. In 1920 the Ministry of Education ordered that the first two grades of primary school use vernacular readers, and a movement for a "national language" (guoyu) sought to standardize pronunciation and usage.

The significance of the change was both educational and social: a written language closer to speech could, in principle, be learned more quickly and used by more people, though in practice access to schooling remained the main barrier.

Reformers before 1915 had already experimented. Late Qing newspapers and reformist writers, including Liang Qichao, had developed a looser, more accessible prose, and some activists had published vernacular papers for peasants and women. What distinguished the movement after 1917 was its assertion that vernacular should become the standard for all serious writing, including literature, scholarship, and school texts. In January 1917 Hu Shi, then a doctoral student in the United States, published in New Youth an article proposing "eight points" for literary improvement. A month later Chen Duxiu, in "On Literary Revolution," endorsed Hu's ideas and framed them in more combative terms, calling for the overthrow of aristocratic, classical, and hermit literature and the creation of a plain, popular, realistic one. The difference in tone is revealing: Hu was cautious and evolutionary, Chen polemical.

It appeared just before he took up a post at Peking University. Translations render the eight points with varying wording, and their order is sometimes presented differently.

Paraphrase: Hu proposed that writers (1) write about something of substance rather than empty phrases; (2) stop imitating the ancients and speak in the manner of their own time; (3) attend to grammar and the structure of sentences; (4) avoid groaning without illness, that is, the habitual, melancholy sentimentality of conventional verse; (5) remove worn-out stock phrases and invent their own; (6) avoid allusions to classical stories as a substitute for original thought; (7) abandon the requirement of parallel couplets; and (8) not avoid colloquial words and expressions.

Analysis: The text is modest in tone: Hu called his suggestions tentative. It argues in terms of writing practice, not politics. Yet its implications were large. By rejecting allusion, parallelism, and imitation, Hu challenged the stylistic standards that structured elite education. Later in 1917–1918 he expanded the argument, famously proposing a "national language of literature, a literature of national language" and arguing that classical Chinese was a "dead" literary language, a claim that classical scholars disputed. The source shows a reformer addressing fellow educated readers; it was not written for peasants. It also shows the influence of Western debates on language and literature, and of Hu's evolutionary view of literary history.

Opposition was real. Traditionalists such as Lin Shu argued that classical Chinese embodied cultural values that would be lost; others, more moderately, claimed that classical forms and vernacular could coexist, each suited to different purposes. Some supporters of vernacular worried that a uniform standard based on northern speech might marginalize regional languages and literatures, a concern that recurs in later debates over national language. The reform's progress was nonetheless rapid after 1919. The protests and publishing boom of that year created a large market for vernacular journals. Within several years vernacular became common in newspapers and primary textbooks. Classical Chinese persisted in legal documents, much scholarship, and personal writing for decades, and the new written language was itself shaped by translation, producing new vocabulary and syntax. Students should treat the "victory" of vernacular as gradual and incomplete rather than a single event.

Section 60.6

New Literature, Lu Xun, and the Criticism of Society

The new language needed new literature to prove its worth. In May 1918 New Youth published "Diary of a Madman" by Lu Xun, the pen name of Zhou Shuren. Born in Shaoxing, Zhejiang, into a declining gentry family, Lu Xun had studied in Japan, initially in medicine, before turning to writing. His story, written in vernacular after an introductory note in classical Chinese, takes the form of diary entries of a man who becomes convinced that the people around him, including his family, are cannibals and that history itself, read between the lines, records the eating of humans in the name of virtue. The story can be read as a case study in paranoia, as satire, and as an allegory for social oppression. Lu Xun's later comments suggest he intended a criticism of a social and ethical order that consumed its weakest members, but the story's ambiguity was part of its power.

Lu Xun followed with stories such as "Kong Yiji," about a failed examination candidate mocked in a tavern, "Medicine," "A Small Incident," and later "The True Story of Ah Q," which satirized self-deception and a kind of face-saving resignation. These works drew on rural and small-town characters, laborers, impoverished scholars, and women, and portrayed them with a mixture of sympathy and irony. Other writers contributed as well: Ye Shengtao, Yu Dafu, Guo Moruo, Bing Xin, and Zhu Ziqing in the early 1920s, and societies such as the Literary Association (founded 1921), which emphasized realism, and the Creation Society, which favored a more expressive romanticism. Poetry in vernacular, beginning with Hu Shi's experiments, was controversial; early vernacular verse was often judged weak by critics, and more accomplished forms developed later.

The relationship between literature and politics was debated. Some writers viewed literature as a tool for enlightening society, a position associated with Lu Xun's early statements about curing the spirit rather than the body. Others wanted art to be independent of social purpose. Women writers began to publish stories about education and marriage. The new fiction also made use of translation: European, Russian, and Japanese works were introduced in quantity, and their themes of individual conflict with society influenced Chinese writers. Historians use these texts as evidence of changing sensibilities, but they must remember that fiction shows how authors and a particular readership imagined social problems, not a documentary record of average lives. The same caution applies to the later tendency to treat Lu Xun as a symbol of the entire period, a status conferred in part by later political movements.

Section 60.7

Newspapers, Journals, Publishing, and the Expansion of Public Debate

The New Culture Movement depended on commercial and institutional infrastructure that had developed over the previous half-century. Shanghai was the center of modern publishing. Commercial Press, founded in 1897, and Zhonghua Book Company, founded in 1912, produced textbooks, translations, dictionaries, and magazines, using mechanized printing and national distribution networks. Newspapers such as Shenbao in Shanghai and a variety of dailies in Beijing, Tianjin, and other cities reported on political events and carried supplements on literature and ideas. After 1918 several newspapers launched literary supplements that became important platforms for vernacular writing and discussion.

In 1919–1920 the number of new periodicals expanded dramatically, with contemporary and later estimates running into the hundreds. Many were short-lived student or local publications, printed cheaply, circulated among friends, and discontinued when funding lapsed. They covered topics from women's liberation and labor questions to experimental education, anarchism, and local reform. Translation work grew rapidly: Chinese readers could find Dewey, Russell, Kropotkin, Tolstoy, Ibsen, Nietzsche, and, increasingly, writings on socialism and Marxism, though quality and accuracy varied and many translations came by way of Japanese. Publishers and editors also marketed the "new" as a commodity, and some conservative critics argued that fashion rather than conviction drove interest.

Print culture had geographical and social limits. Reading these journals required literacy and, often, money or access to a school library; the rural majority and many urban workers could not easily participate. Circulation was strongest in treaty ports and provincial capitals along rail and river routes and weaker in the interior and in regions where military authorities censored or confiscated publications. The Beijing government attempted to restrict the press at various moments, and local warlords differed widely in their tolerance. The foreign concessions in Shanghai and Tianjin offered a measure of shelter because Chinese authorities had limited jurisdiction there, a fact that connects the history of Chinese expression to the legacy of the treaty ports discussed in Chapter 52. Reading aloud in study groups, lectures, and school assemblies spread ideas to people who could not read the journals themselves, but historians have few records of such oral reception.

Section 60.8

Science, Democracy, and Competing Visions of Modernity

In January 1919, answering critics who accused New Youth of undermining morality and tradition, Chen Duxiu wrote that the journal supported only "Mr. Democracy" (De xiansheng) and "Mr. Science" (Sai xiansheng), and that to defend them it was prepared to oppose Confucianism, the old ritual order, traditional notions of chastity, old ethics, old politics, and old religion. The phrase "Science and Democracy" became shorthand for the movement's ideals. But the terms covered a variety of meanings, and it would be a mistake to treat them as a coherent doctrine.

"Science" (kexue) could mean specific bodies of knowledge, such as physics, biology, and medicine; a method of inquiry based on evidence and skepticism; a worldview opposed to what reformers called superstition; or a tool for national wealth and strength. Hu Shi, influenced by his teacher John Dewey, emphasized scientific method and pragmatic experimentation. Chen Duxiu treated science as a challenge to traditional authority. Some Chinese scientists, many of whom had trained abroad, founded the Science Society of China in 1914–1915 and its journal Science (Kexue), emphasizing research and education. The term could also be used to dismiss practices such as popular religion or traditional medicine, and critics asked whether "science" was being used as a slogan rather than a practice. In 1923 a public debate between Zhang Junmai, who argued that science could not answer questions of the meaning of life, and Ding Wenjiang, who defended the scope of science, showed the persistence of these disagreements.

"Democracy" (minzhu) was equally varied. For some it meant representative government and constitutional rights; for others, equality in social life and rejection of hereditary hierarchy; for others, a participatory politics rooted in local associations; and for some, a goal to be reached through strong leadership. The disappointing record of parliamentary politics in 1912–1917, in which members could be bought and dismissed at will by military men, made liberal democracy seem fragile to many. John Dewey's visit to China from 1919 to 1921, including lectures in a dozen or more provinces, offered one model, an educational and participatory democracy, while the Russian Revolution attracted other readers. Bertrand Russell's visit in 1920–1921 and Margaret Sanger's in 1922 reflected continuing interest in foreign visitors, whose arrival the Chinese press followed closely.

Exchanges with Japan, Europe, and the United States shaped the vocabulary. Many key terms, such as those for "society," "individual," "science," and "philosophy," had been coined or adapted in Japanese translation and entered Chinese in the late Qing and early Republic. Students returning from Japan, the United States, France, and Britain carried back different traditions, so that Chinese reformers could debate "the West" as though it were singular even as they cited rival Western authorities. Chinese intellectuals were thus active participants in a global exchange, adapting and criticizing as well as importing.

Section 60.9

Women, Marriage, Family, and Gender Reform

Debates about women and the family were central to New Culture. They built on earlier efforts: late Qing campaigns against footbinding, the establishment of schools for girls by Chinese reformers and missionaries, and the participation of women in the 1911 Revolution and in the suffrage demands of 1912–1913, which were rebuffed by the new government. After 1915, journals devoted attention to the "woman question," publishing essays, translations, and personal letters. Ibsen's play A Doll's House, translated and discussed in a special 1918 issue of New Youth, made its heroine Nora a symbol of a woman who leaves a marriage to find independence; the play was staged in Chinese in the following years, and "what happens after Nora leaves home?" became a subject of Lu Xun's later lecture, in which he observed that without economic independence a woman's choices were severely limited.

Reform arguments focused on arranged marriage, concubinage, the prohibition on widow remarriage, and the claim that individuals should choose spouses by free choice (ziyou lian'ai). Educational opportunities widened: Beijing Women's Higher Normal School provided higher education for women, and in 1920 Peking University began admitting women students, a step followed by other institutions. Women's student associations formed during the 1919 protests. Some women, including Xiang Jingyu, who was active in Hunan reform circles and later in the work-study movement in France and in labor organizing, became public figures. Women writers and teachers started to publish. Yet women remained a small minority among students, and male reformers often spoke for women rather than listening to them.

The consequences of reform arguments could be tragic as well as liberating. In November 1919 a young woman in Changsha, Zhao Wuzhen, committed suicide in her bridal sedan chair on the way to a marriage her parents had arranged. The episode drew extensive newspaper commentary, including a series of essays by the young Mao Zedong, who denounced the marriage system. Historians read the case as evidence of how the new arguments gave public language to private conflicts, and of the severity of family pressures. They also caution that one incident, heavily publicized, does not show the typical outcome of marriages, most of which were arranged and many of which were stable and affectionate within their cultural frameworks.

Reformers' proposals had limitations. Many men supported women's education as a means of making better mothers or strengthening the nation rather than as an end in itself. Working-class and rural women, who labored in fields, textile mills, and households, were less visible in the debates, and their concerns, such as wages, factory conditions, and land, were different from those of educated urban women. Some women criticized male reformers' inconsistency, since men who demanded free marriage for themselves could be reluctant to dissolve existing arranged marriages. Regional differences were also large: in some southern coastal communities, women had long held economic roles and, in a few areas, practiced delayed-transfer marriage patterns, showing that "tradition" was not uniform.

Section 60.10

Youth, Education, and the Transformation of Social Values

"Youth" itself became a historical actor in these years. The journal's title announced the idea, and student identity acquired a new meaning. In the imperial system, study had been a path to office and household advancement, usually under the direction of lineage and family. By the 1910s modern schools and universities had created a population of students who lived together in dormitories, formed associations, read journals, and developed a sense of generational identity. Reformers urged young people to think independently, to question parental authority, and to take responsibility for the nation. For many students, these ideas offered language for ambitions and frustrations; for some parents, they threatened family cohesion.

Educational reform reinforced the change. After 1912 the government's school regulations had introduced modern subjects, and in 1922 a new school system modeled partly on American practice, with six years of primary school, three of junior and three of senior secondary school, replaced the earlier Japanese-influenced structure. Dewey's lectures, together with efforts by Chinese educators such as Tao Xingzhi and Jiang Menglin, encouraged experiments in child-centered learning. Vocational and mass education movements sought to extend literacy: students taught night classes for workers and peasants, using vernacular textbooks, and James Yen's literacy campaign began in these years. These efforts reached only a fraction of the population but gave participants experience in organizing and in contact with social groups beyond the campus.

The new values carried tensions. Individualism, expressed as freedom to choose one's work, spouse, and ideas, sat uneasily with the demand that youth sacrifice for the nation. Some students saw moral reform as a precondition for national strength; others, especially after 1919, turned to direct action. The work-study movement, in which several hundred Chinese students traveled to France in 1919–1920 to combine labor with study, tested the idea of educated youth engaging in manual work, though many encountered hardship and unemployment. Students also faced questions about their future careers, since a modern education did not guarantee jobs in a weak economy. Historians describe the emergence of a student "stratum" that was educated, politically conscious, and economically uncertain, a social position that helps explain its readiness for political activism.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Student's World, a Villager's World

Imagine a student at Peking University in 1919. He (most students were men) probably lived in a shared dormitory room or lodging house, subscribed to or borrowed several journals, joined a society that met to discuss a topic, and might attend a public lecture on Sundays. He ate in a campus or neighborhood eatery, wore either a long gown or a newly fashionable student uniform, and wrote letters home in a style that could differ from the classical forms his father expected. If his parents had arranged a betrothal before he left home, he might feel torn between obligation and the new ideals.

Now imagine a family in a village in the Yellow River plain or in Sichuan. The household's life revolved around planting, taxes demanded by whichever military authority controlled the county, markets, temple festivals, and lineage ceremonies. Few members could read; if the family had a literate member, he might know classical formulas for contracts or almanacs rather than the vernacular press. News of Beijing might arrive through a returning student, a merchant, or a rumor. A daughter's marriage was arranged by elders through a matchmaker, with an exchange of gifts and considerations of land and labor. For this household, the debate over "Mr. Science" was remote, though disease, flood, and conscription were not. Students should keep both worlds in view and recognize that many people moved between them, as when a rural family sent a son to a modern school at great sacrifice.

Section 60.11

The Social Limits and Contradictions of New Culture

The New Culture Movement was a movement of educated people, mostly young, mostly urban, and mostly male. Its reach into the countryside, where perhaps four-fifths of the population lived, was slight in these years. Peasants experienced the Republic mainly through taxes, military levies, banditry, and the price of grain. Differences among regions were pronounced: coastal and riverine cities with foreign contact and modern schools differed from the interior provinces, and northern, southern, and western regions had distinct linguistic and social worlds. Frontier regions had their own histories. In Taiwan, under Japanese rule, a separate cultural movement took shape in the early 1920s, including the Taiwan Cultural Association founded in 1921, which drew on Chinese vernacular debates and on opposition to colonial policy. In Tibet, Mongolia, and Xinjiang, the debates of Beijing and Shanghai had little immediate effect, and political relationships with the Beijing government were determined by other circumstances (Chapters 48 and 59).

Contradictions existed within the movement. Reformers who attacked elite classical culture were themselves often products of it, and their critique was articulated in elite networks. Advocates of vernacular could publish in a style that remained difficult for the barely literate. Proponents of democracy at times assumed that enlightened intellectuals should lead the masses. Critics of tradition sometimes adopted its forms, as when journals used the traditional prestige of the scholar to claim authority. Those who attacked family hierarchy faced practical dependence on family resources. Foreign ideas were adopted partly to resist foreign domination, a tension that would shape later debates about cultural identity.

Reactions to the movement were also diverse. Many merchants and local elites valued modern education and commerce but distrusted attacks on family and ritual. Religious communities, including Buddhists, Daoists, Muslims, and Christians, were affected differently: reformers criticized "superstition," but Buddhist reformers like Taixu sought to modernize their tradition, and Christian schools were important venues for modern education even as anti-Christian sentiment grew in the 1920s. Rural elites and teachers sometimes adopted parts of the new program, such as vernacular textbooks, while resisting others. Understanding New Culture therefore requires attention to selective adoption and to the ways people combined old and new.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Families, Education, and Cultural Authority, c. 1900–1925

What Changed

  • The examination system, abolished in 1905, no longer defined the path to status; modern schools and universities became new sources of credentials
  • Written vernacular entered newspapers, literature, and primary education
  • Public argument over arranged marriage, women's education, and filial obedience became open and widespread in urban print
  • "Youth" and "student" became recognized social and political categories

What Stayed the Same

  • Most households were rural, agricultural, and organized around kin obligations; most marriages continued to be arranged by families
  • Literacy remained a minority attainment, and classical Chinese retained prestige in many domains
  • Scholarly and moral authority continued to be associated with educated elites, now including intellectuals with modern degrees
  • Reformers still drew on older concerns about moral cultivation, public service, and national strength

Who experienced change most directly? Urban students, teachers, writers, and women with access to schools. Who experienced continuity? Most rural households and many urban workers. Why it matters: This pattern prevents two errors: imagining that a cultural revolution transformed all of Chinese society by 1919, or dismissing the movement because its immediate reach was limited. Its significance lay in the arguments and institutions it created, which later generations extended.

Section 60.12

World War I, Japan, and the Shandong Question

The Shandong Question had its origin in the age of imperialism discussed in Chapter 55. In 1897, after the murder of two German missionaries, Germany occupied Jiaozhou Bay and in 1898 obtained a ninety-nine-year lease of the territory around Qingdao, together with rights to build railways and develop mines in Shandong province. Germany developed Qingdao as a naval base and commercial port, built the Jiaozhou–Jinan railway, and established the Tsingtao brewery and other enterprises. The province, the birthplace of Confucius and home to Mount Tai, was densely populated and symbolically important to many Chinese.

When war broke out in Europe in August 1914, Japan, allied with Britain, declared war on Germany and in the autumn besieged Qingdao with British support, taking it in November 1914. Japanese forces also occupied the railway deep into the interior. China, a neutral state, had asked that hostilities not extend to Chinese territory and was ignored. In January 1915 Japan presented the Twenty-One Demands to the Yuan Shikai government. Group One required Chinese recognition of whatever arrangements Japan made with Germany regarding Shandong. Other groups addressed South Manchuria and Inner Mongolia, the Hanyeping iron company, coastal non-alienation, and, in Group Five, Japanese advisers and other controls. After leaks to the press and foreign pressure, Japan withdrew most of Group Five, and China accepted the rest in May 1915 under ultimatum. Treaties and notes signed in that month confirmed Japan's expanded position.

China entered the war on the Allied side in August 1917, after a political struggle in Beijing between President Li Yuanhong and Premier Duan Qirui. The decision brought some benefits: suspension of Boxer indemnity payments to Germany and Austria and of those to Russia, an end to German extraterritorial privileges, and the hope of a seat among the victors. China also contributed labor: perhaps 140,000 Chinese workers went to Europe under contracts with Britain and France, digging trenches, repairing roads, and working in factories, and some died there. Their experience, including contact with Chinese educators and Y.M.C.A. workers who taught literacy classes, influenced later social movements, though they are often left out of narratives of the war.

Meanwhile Duan Qirui's government borrowed heavily from Japan through the Nishihara loans in 1917–1918 to fund military expansion and to pursue its goal of reuniting the country by force. In September 1918, as the war neared its end, Chinese representatives exchanged notes with Japan agreeing to arrangements concerning the Shandong railways, language that Japan later invoked as evidence that China had consented to Japanese succession to German rights. The Chinese side maintained that the notes did not constitute transfer of sovereignty. The existence of these notes, as well as the wartime secret treaties by which Britain, France, and others had promised Japan support for its claims in Shandong in 1917, set the stage for the diplomatic outcome of 1919. Many Chinese citizens learned of them only after the peace conference revealed them.

Section 60.13

The Paris Peace Conference and the Crisis of National Sovereignty

Chinese expectations of the peace conference were high. In his Fourteen Points and wartime speeches, U.S. President Woodrow Wilson spoke of self-determination and open diplomacy. Chinese newspapers and intellectuals, including many who had read Wilson in translation, interpreted these statements as support for the equality of nations. China sent a delegation under Foreign Minister Lu Zhengxiang that included Wellington Koo, then Chinese minister to the United States, Wang Zhengting (C. T. Wang), Alfred Sao-ke Sze, and Wei Chengxu. The delegation also represented, uneasily, a divided country: southern authorities in Guangzhou sent their own representative.

China's aims at Paris included the return of German leaseholds and rights in Shandong, the abolition of the Twenty-One Demands and related agreements, and the end of foreign extraterritorial privileges and other treaty restrictions. In January 1919, Koo presented a forceful argument, widely admired, that Shandong was the cradle of Chinese civilization and that China's entry into the war had cancelled the German lease. Japan's delegates answered that Japan had conquered the territory, that wartime agreements guaranteed its claims, and that China's 1915 and 1918 commitments had recognized them. Japan also pressed a racial equality clause for the League of Nations Covenant, which was rejected by Britain's dominions and others, and Japanese delegates indicated they might not join the League without satisfaction on Shandong.

The great powers, preoccupied with European questions and conscious of their secret commitments, sided effectively with Japan. In late April 1919 Wilson, Britain's Lloyd George, and France's Clemenceau agreed that German rights in Shandong would pass to Japan, accompanied by Japanese verbal assurances that it would eventually return full sovereignty and keep only economic privileges. Wilson, who had hoped to preserve the League, is often described as accepting the outcome reluctantly as the least bad compromise; scholars continue to debate how much alternatives existed. The decision was embedded in Articles 156–158 of the Treaty of Versailles. The news reached China around May 1 through telegrams from Paris, by way of reports from the Chinese delegation, and from journalists. It struck many Chinese as a betrayal both by the Allied powers and by a government that, in their eyes, had weakened China's position by dealing with Japan.

The diplomatic issue, then, was not a single mistake but a convergence: Japan's military position, secret treaties, Chinese governmental weakness and division, and great-power priorities. Historians treat the outcome as a case study in how sovereignty in the early twentieth century was determined by power as well as principle. The effect in China was to discredit both the government and, for many, the liberal international order that Wilson represented, though others continued to hope for revision through later conferences, as the Washington Conference would show.

Section 60.14

May 4, 1919: Student Protest in Beijing

Reaction in Beijing began before the formal announcement. Student groups at Peking University and other colleges held meetings in the first days of May. On May 3 students and some staff discussed the news at the university, and a meeting that evening, with representatives from several institutions, resolved to hold a demonstration the next day. The planned participants included students from Peking University, the Higher Normal School, the Higher Industrial School, and other colleges; estimates of the number who gathered on May 4 are about three thousand, with variation among sources. Their demands, in summary, were that the government instruct its delegates not to sign the treaty, that the pro-Japanese officials Cao Rulin, Zhang Zongxiang, and Lu Zongyu be dismissed and punished, and that the nation be mobilized in defense of Shandong.

The students assembled at Tiananmen in the early afternoon. They carried banners, some with slogans such as "Return Qingdao" and "Abolish the Twenty-One Demands," and distributed leaflets in Chinese and, for foreigners, in English. They marched toward the Legation Quarter to present their case to the representatives of the great powers. Police and officials prevented them from entering the diplomatic area beyond a point; a delegation was received at the American legation, though the minister was absent, and the group dispersed from there. The crowd then went to the residence of Cao Rulin, the communications minister who had negotiated with Japan, at Zhaojialou. Cao was not at home; Zhang Zongxiang, Chinese minister to Japan, was there and was beaten, and the house was set on fire. Police arrived and arrested thirty-two students. The students had not planned the arson in advance according to most accounts, and later participants differed on who was responsible.

The government response was initially firm; Beijing garrison commanders posted guards and the cabinet debated further measures. Cai Yuanpei sought the students' release and, when this was achieved on May 7, resigned in protest at the situation and left the city, an act that showed the moral position of university leaders though he returned later in the year. Public sympathy, shown in editorials, telegrams from provincial groups, and statements from chambers of commerce and educational associations, was substantial. The protest had been organized by students of varied views; some were influenced by the New Culture reformers, others by nationalist and personal loyalties. This is an important point: the connection between the cultural movement and the demonstration was real, as shown by shared participants and the New Tide Society's roles, but the immediate cause was the Shandong diplomacy, and students with traditional views joined too.

Section 60.15

Repression, Protest, and the Escalation of the Movement

After May 4 the protests spread. Students in Beijing formed a citywide student union and organized strikes by schools, demanding that the arrested students be freed and that the Shandong decision be rejected. Telegrams of support came from other cities, and students in Tianjin, Shanghai, Nanjing, Wuhan, Changsha, Guangzhou, and elsewhere formed their own unions and boycotts. A National Student Union formed in Shanghai in June. Popular boycotts of Japanese goods, which had precedents in 1915, resumed with new energy; merchants in some ports refused to sell them, and groups publicized the names of those who violated the boycott. Lecture groups of students went into streets and market towns to speak to the public.

The government vacillated between concession and coercion. In late May it banned student demonstrations and threatened punishment. In early June, students intensified their speech-making, and on June 3 and 4 police arrested large numbers, with figures of roughly a thousand commonly cited, filling makeshift detention sites, including the Law College building at Peking University. The arrests backfired. Rather than ending the protests, they produced outrage in the press and among merchants, and on June 5 the movement entered its most notable phase in Shanghai, where shops closed and workers walked off their jobs.

Cause and Effect

From Diplomatic Grievance to Mass Protest, 1915–1919

Long-term causes

A history of foreign leaseholds and unequal treaties; the memory of the Twenty-One Demands; weakness and division of the Beijing government; growth of modern schools, student networks, and a vernacular press; expansion of Chinese industry and urban commerce during the war; rising nationalist feeling expressed in boycotts.

Short-term causes

The apparent Allied decision to assign German Shandong rights to Japan; disclosure of the 1918 notes and the role of Beijing officials in loan and railway agreements; the high hopes raised by Wilsonian language; the existence of student organizations able to mobilize quickly.

Event

The May 4 demonstration and arrests; escalation through student strikes, boycotts, mass arrests on June 3–4, and strikes and shop closures in Shanghai and other cities from June 5.

Effects

Dismissal of three officials; the Chinese delegation's refusal to sign at Versailles; growth of student and labor organizations; a new model of public pressure on diplomacy; later ideological realignments. Shandong's actual return took years.

Historical complexity: No single cause explains the movement. The diplomatic decision was the trigger, but its impact depended on prior institutions, networks, and grievances. Government actions, such as the June arrests, also shaped escalation. Students should avoid treating the outcome as inevitable: the protests could have remained a brief Beijing student episode, as many earlier demonstrations had.

In Shanghai the general chamber of commerce and trade guilds, initially hesitant, closed shops in solidarity with the students, and tens of thousands of workers in cotton mills, shipyards, docks, and public utilities, many in Japanese-owned enterprises, went on strike. Estimates of strikers vary widely, from tens of thousands upward. The strike, often called the "three strikes" (sanba), of students, merchants, and workers, was a significant departure from the earlier student-led pattern. Merchants joined for several reasons: patriotic sentiment, resentment of Japanese commercial competition, indignation at the arrest of students, and a desire to influence national policy. Workers' motives included nationalism and also grievances over wages and conditions, which Section 60.18 examines. The strike lasted only days, but it put substantial pressure on Beijing.

On June 6 and in the following days the government released arrested students. On June 10 it dismissed Cao Rulin, Zhang Zongxiang, and Lu Zongyu. The cabinet was in disarray, and Cai Yuanpei, persuaded to return, resumed his post at the university later in the year. Chen Duxiu, arrested on June 11 for distributing a leaflet calling for a "citizens' government," remained in custody for some months. These outcomes show that the movement could obtain concessions, but also that the government retained the capacity to repress individual leaders.

Section 60.16

Students, Workers, Merchants, and the Geography of Protest

The movement's character varied from city to city. In Beijing, the seat of government and of the national university, the protest was largely student-led and directly confronted officials. In Shanghai, a commercial and industrial city with foreign concessions, the participation of merchants and workers made the movement a broader urban coalition; the divided jurisdiction of the International Settlement and French Concession, in which Chinese authorities had limited reach, allowed organizers more freedom. In Tianjin, a port and educational hub, students and women's groups were active, and the movement produced a notable student newspaper and a leader, Zhou Enlai, then a student at Nankai who edited a student publication. In Changsha, Hunan, student and teacher networks, including Mao Zedong, who founded a weekly called the Xiang River Review, linked the movement with local reform. In Guangzhou, under a rival southern authority, support took forms shaped by southern politics. In inland provinces, the movement was weaker and slower to arrive.

Organizational forms were also varied. Student unions coordinated strikes, speech-making, and boycotts and published newspapers. Chambers of commerce and guilds acted according to the interests of their members and differed in enthusiasm. Women's associations, including students from girls' schools, joined demonstrations and formed their own groups. Local movements had different priorities: some emphasized boycotts, some reform of schools, some pressure on provincial authorities.

Multiple Perspectives

The June 1919 Strikes: Who Wanted What?

Note: the viewpoints below are analytical reconstructions synthesizing patterns historians have identified. They are not quotations from individuals.

Students: Most saw themselves as the conscience of the nation, defending sovereignty and exposing officials they considered traitors. Some also wished to reform education and society. Their willingness to be arrested was central to their moral claim, and their movement depended on school networks and press attention.
Merchants: Many shared patriotic goals and welcomed boycotts that favored Chinese products, but also weighed losses from closed shops and risks of disorder. Some leaders hoped to influence national policy through chambers of commerce, a sign of growing commercial political ambition. Smaller shopkeepers, who followed their guilds, acted under mixed pressures of sympathy and community expectation.
Workers: In Shanghai and elsewhere, workers in Japanese-owned and other factories struck for national reasons and, in many cases, for wages and conditions. They were less able to bear lost pay than merchants and had fewer resources if dismissed. Their participation, often unrecorded in their own words, indicates that the movement was not only an elite phenomenon, though workers' leadership was limited.
Government officials: Beijing officials faced pressure from the public, from rival militarists, and from foreign powers. Some sympathized with the students; others saw the protests as disorder instigated by radical teachers. Those accused of pro-Japanese dealings insisted they had acted to secure loans and prevent worse outcomes. Their ability to repress was constrained by the fragmentation of authority and by the risk of broader unrest.
Section 60.17

Diplomatic and Political Consequences of May Fourth

The most immediate consequence was the dismissal of the three officials on June 10. The second was the decision of the Chinese delegation in Paris not to sign the Treaty of Versailles. Delegates had been instructed by Beijing to sign with reservations; when reservations about Shandong were refused, and while protest telegrams and demonstrations of Chinese students and workers in France and elsewhere pressed the delegation, the delegates stayed away from the signing on June 28, 1919. China was thereby the only major participant in the conference not to sign, a gesture that was symbolically significant though it did not alter the treaty's terms. China later made a separate peace with Germany in 1921 and joined the League of Nations as a member.

The Shandong dispute remained unresolved. At the Washington Conference of 1921–1922, convened by the United States, China and Japan, with American and British mediation, negotiated a bilateral treaty signed in February 1922 that provided for Japanese withdrawal from Shandong. Japan returned Qingdao and the leased territory, with China purchasing the Jiaozhou–Jinan railway through payments and retaining some Japanese personnel and commercial rights. The transfer occurred in December 1922. The same conference produced the Nine-Power Treaty affirming principles of Chinese territorial integrity and the open door, and limited Japanese naval power, though it did not abolish the main unequal treaty provisions. Chinese opinion on the settlement was divided: many welcomed the return of Qingdao, others criticized the compromise on the railway.

Domestically, the political consequences were mixed. The Beijing government remained weak and soon fell into fighting among militarist factions, including the Zhili–Anhui War of 1920 (Chapter 59). The protests did not produce parliamentary democracy or a stronger central state. But they changed the political environment. They demonstrated that students and urban associations could mobilize across regions; they encouraged merchants to seek political voice; and they convinced some reformers that cultural change alone was insufficient and that organization, labor, and mass politics were necessary. Others drew the opposite conclusion, that politics had distracted from the slower work of education and scholarship. Hu Shi, for instance, worried that political activism would divert students from study.

Section 60.18

Labor Politics, Social Activism, and Mass Organization

The participation of workers in the June 1919 strikes drew attention to an expanding industrial labor force. By the end of the First World War, China had several hundred thousand modern factory workers, concentrated in Shanghai, Tianjin, Wuhan, Guangzhou, and mining and railway centers, and many more in handicraft and transport trades. Many were migrants from the countryside, including women and children in textile and silk mills, working long hours for low pay under labor contractors and foremen. Workers' organizations took varied forms: craft guilds, native-place associations, secret societies, mutual-aid groups, and, increasingly, trade unions. Strikes over wages and hours occurred before 1919, but the May Fourth period gave them a national political context.

Students and intellectuals began to approach workers more directly. Some set up night schools in factory districts, taught literacy, and published pamphlets. The Mass Education Lecture Corps and other groups carried lectures to workers. Some intellectuals, influenced by anarchist, socialist, and Marxist ideas, argued that labor was a central force in modern society, and journals devoted special issues to "labor" (laogong) with the slogan that "labor is sacred." These efforts were uneven. Educated activists often had little experience of factory life, and workers had their own leaders, loyalties, and priorities; contractors and gang bosses held considerable control. The relationship between students and workers combined solidarity and misunderstanding.

Between 1919 and the early 1920s labor activism increased. Strikes in 1920–1922 included actions by seamen in Hong Kong, miners at Kailuan, and railway workers, and their experiences fed later organizing, treated in Chapter 61. Workers' associations also appeared under non-Marxist auspices, including guild leaders, Christian groups, and the Y.M.C.A. Women's activism extended to cotton mills and to campaigns for female suffrage and legal equality in provinces such as Hunan and Guangdong in the early 1920s, though results were limited. Social activism beyond labor included mass education, anti-footbinding efforts, public health campaigns, anti-opium societies, and cooperatives. Students should treat these as part of a broad civic mobilization, not only a prelude to party politics.

Section 60.19

Liberalism, Anarchism, Socialism, and Marxism

The events of 1919 intensified a debate about what program could save the country and reform society. The range of positions was broad, and the boundaries between them were porous. This section outlines the major currents while leaving the detailed history of party formation to Chapter 61.

Historical Vocabulary
"Isms" (zhuyi, 主义) in the May Fourth Era

Zhuyi, translated as "-ism" or "doctrine," became a key word. Intellectuals debated which zhuyi offered a way forward: liberalism, anarchism, socialism, Marxism, pragmatism, nationalism, and others. In July 1919 Hu Shi published an essay urging that people study "more problems, fewer isms," that is, address concrete questions such as education, labor, and local government rather than adopt grand doctrines. Li Dazhao replied that problems could not be solved without a guiding framework and a commitment to collective action. The exchange is often called the "problems and isms" debate, and it illustrates divergences within the circle of New Culture intellectuals.

Students should be careful not to treat these labels as fixed camps. People moved among them, combined elements, and used the same word in different ways.

Liberalism and pragmatism. Hu Shi, with Dewey's influence, favored gradual reform, scientific method, and individual rights. His circle in Beijing included educators and scholars who emphasized education, constitutionalism, and the independence of intellectual life, and in later years some joined periodicals such as Endeavor and Independent Critic. They faced difficulties because liberal institutions required political conditions that did not exist. Anarchism had already attracted Chinese students in Japan and France before 1915, through figures such as Liu Shipei, Wu Zhihui, and Li Shizeng, and gained followers after 1919, including youths who established communal experiments and published widely. Anarchists criticized the family, the state, and capitalism, supported the work-study movement and labor education, and emphasized mutual aid, drawing on Kropotkin. Their ideas influenced many who later became Communists or Nationalists, and some remained anarchists for life.

Socialism and Marxism. Interest in socialism predated the Russian Revolution, but the October 1917 revolution and the Bolsheviks' announcement in 1919 and 1920 renouncing Russian special privileges in China, known through the Karakhan declaration, made Soviet Russia attractive to some Chinese. Li Dazhao, who published "The Victory of Bolshevism" in November 1918, became one of the first Chinese intellectuals to advocate Marxism, and he organized study groups at Peking University. Chen Duxiu, who had admired French republican thought, moved toward socialism after 1919 and in 1920 began organizing Marxist study circles in Shanghai, in contact with Comintern agents. Other socialists, including those who favored guild socialism or reformist state socialism, rejected Bolshevik methods. Marxist writings were translated, often partially, and interpreted in light of Chinese conditions, including imperialism and a still largely agrarian society. These developments are described fully in Chapter 61. Here it is sufficient to note that Marxism was one option among several in 1919–1920, that its appeal reflected disappointment with the postwar order, and that its eventual organizational success was not predetermined.

Cultural conservatism and other currents. Those who criticized the New Culture, including Liang Shuming and the Critical Review group, remained influential in education and scholarship. Some Buddhists, Christians, and Confucian reformers sought to renew their traditions. Nationalists around Sun Yat-sen, who in 1919 reorganized his party and launched the journal Construction, were attentive to student energies and to the Russian example, and had their own program (Chapter 61). The mix of ideas meant that the same student might read Kropotkin, Dewey, and Marx in a single year.

Section 60.20

Nationalism, Internationalism, and China's Place in the World

May Fourth nationalism was directed against foreign domination, particularly Japanese, and against officials considered complicit. It involved boycotts, public meetings, and symbols such as National Humiliation Day. It also contained internationalist elements. Students read about the Korean March First Movement of 1919, in which Koreans protested Japanese colonial rule and which was brutally suppressed, and some saw parallels. Contacts with Indian, Korean, Vietnamese, and other Asian activists were growing, as were connections with socialist movements. Chinese students and workers in France, Japan, and the United States, such as those in the work-study program and the Chinese Labour Corps veterans, formed networks that exchanged ideas and funds with groups at home.

Attitudes toward the powers were complex. The sense of betrayal by Wilson and the Allies fostered disillusionment with the United States and Britain among many students, though American-educated intellectuals such as Hu Shi and educational institutions with American support remained influential, and American philanthropic and missionary institutions continued to play large roles. Anti-Japanese feeling was intense but coexisted with admiration for Japanese modernization and with continuing contact through Japanese translations and study abroad. The Soviet declarations led some to view Russia as a friend, though others remained skeptical of its aims, especially concerning Mongolia and the Chinese Eastern Railway. Concern about imperialism extended to Britain's position in Shanghai and Hong Kong and would erupt in 1925 (Chapter 61).

The tension between nationalism and universal ideals ran through the period. Many reformers wished to emulate science, democracy, and individual rights, while resisting foreign control. Others argued that only strong national organization could secure these goals. A related tension concerned culture: calls to adopt Western knowledge existed alongside cultural nationalism that valued native traditions, as in debates over "national essence" and in the work of scholars on folk literature and Chinese history. The movement thus produced both cosmopolitan and nationalist currents, which later movements would combine in different ways.

Section 60.21

Interpreting New Culture and May Fourth: Rupture, Continuity, and Historical Memory

The meaning of May Fourth has been contested almost since the day itself. Within a few years, activists and writers had begun to describe it as a turning point. Later, political movements claimed it. The Chinese Communist Party, especially from the 1930s and 1940s, presented May Fourth as the beginning of the "new democratic revolution," an event in which the working class and its leaders began to play a role, and Mao Zedong's 1939 and 1940 writings gave this view authority; May 4 was later designated Youth Day in the People's Republic. The Nationalist government, while associating itself with the revolutionary tradition of Sun Yat-sen, often took a more ambivalent view of the cultural radicalism of the movement and in some periods emphasized Confucian values. Liberal scholars such as Hu Shi described the movement as a "Chinese Renaissance" and criticized the politicization that followed. Cultural conservatives viewed it as a damaging assault on heritage. Scholars in the 1980s, in the People's Republic and abroad, revisited the movement as an "enlightenment," and some critics later reinterpreted it in other ways.

Historical Debate

Was the New Culture and May Fourth Era a Break with the Past or a Continuation of Earlier Reform?

The question: Participants and later writers often described New Culture and May Fourth as a fundamental intellectual revolution. Historians ask whether the era marked a real break in how Chinese thought about language, family, authority, and politics, or whether it extended trends already visible in the late Qing and early Republic.

Interpretation A — A decisive rupture: Proponents emphasize the explicit rejection of Confucian ethical orthodoxy, the adoption of vernacular as a standard written language, the creation of a new literature, the appearance of a mobilized student and urban public, and the introduction of new doctrines such as Marxism. They argue that after 1919 the terms of debate changed: criticism of the family system, the idea of individual autonomy, and mass politics became central, and the reformers' language shaped later movements.
Interpretation B — Continuity with earlier reform and tradition: Others point to precedents: late Qing newspapers and vernacular experiments, the New Policies and educational reform, anti-footbinding campaigns, Liang Qichao's writings, the 1911 revolutionaries' nationalism, and earlier traditions of moral critique and evidential scholarship. They note that many New Culture figures were trained in classical learning, that the movement was limited in social reach, that the idea of "tradition" being rejected was itself a construct, and that conservative and moderate currents persisted. Some stress the continuing influence of Confucian ethical language in reformers' own thinking.

Evidence: Supporters of A cite journals, the speed of change in publishing and school curricula, and the new political practices of 1919. Supporters of B cite earlier texts, biographical continuities, the persistence of classical forms and rural social structures, and evidence from regions less affected by the movement. Memory and later political uses complicate both: much of what is known about "May Fourth" was written after the events by people with stakes in their meaning.

Why it matters: The debate shapes how historians understand modern Chinese intellectual history, how they weigh the role of foreign ideas against domestic traditions, and how they evaluate claims made by later political movements about their origins. It also bears on whether twentieth-century developments can be explained through a single foundational event.

Student task: Which interpretation is better supported by the evidence discussed in this chapter, or is a combination more persuasive? What additional evidence, for instance about rural reception or about earlier vernacular practice, would help resolve the disagreement?

Historians also distinguish among the New Culture Movement of 1915–1919, the May Fourth incident and protests of 1919, and the wider "May Fourth era" of the early 1920s. Using one name for all of them can mislead, for example by attributing the political radicalism of 1920 to the editors of 1915, or by treating the protests as the product of a single ideology. The best approach is to recognize layers: cultural debates, a diplomatic crisis, and an expanding field of political experimentation that interacted without being identical.

Section 60.22

Chapter Review and May Fourth Historical Thinking Checkpoint

This chapter has traced how a movement of intellectual and cultural criticism, centered on New Youth, Peking University, vernacular writing, and debates over family and authority, became connected to a nationwide protest triggered by the Shandong decision in 1919, and how both fed into experiments in labor organization and ideological debate. Students should now be able to describe each of these developments separately and to explain their relationship.

The central insights are these. New Culture and May Fourth were distinct but interacting phenomena, and neither was ideologically uniform. The New Culture Movement emerged from disappointment with early Republican politics and from earlier reform traditions, and was strongest among urban, educated young people. Its criticism of Confucian orthodoxy targeted a particular system of hierarchy and ritual and did not reflect the full diversity of Confucian thought, which had defenders and adaptors. Vernacular reform, new literature, and expanded publishing changed public culture, though social access was limited. The Shandong crisis resulted from imperial rivalry, wartime diplomacy, and Chinese governmental weakness, and the May Fourth protests drew in merchants and workers as well as students. The immediate political results were real but limited, and Shandong was not recovered until 1922. After 1919, competing ideologies multiplied, and none of their later outcomes was inevitable. Finally, the meaning of May Fourth has been shaped by later political and scholarly interpretation.

Chapter Summary

The New Culture and May Fourth movements arose in a political landscape in which the promise of the 1911 Revolution had faded. Yuan Shikai's authoritarianism and monarchical attempt, the Zhang Xun restoration, and the fragmentation of authority among militarist factions led many educated Chinese to conclude that institutional change had not altered the habits and values on which politics rested. Foreign pressure, notably the Twenty-One Demands of 1915, strengthened the sense of national danger. These conditions, together with expanded schooling and publishing, created an audience for a program of cultural criticism.

Chen Duxiu's New Youth, founded in 1915 and strengthened by its association with Peking University under Cai Yuanpei, became the principal forum for this program. Contributors criticized the hierarchical family and ritual orthodoxy, advocated individual independence, and promoted science and democracy, although these terms carried different meanings for different writers. Their criticism targeted lijiao and state-sponsored Confucian orthodoxy and was contested by defenders such as Liang Shuming and the Critical Review group, which argued for the continuing value of Chinese traditions. Reformers also engaged seriously with Chinese heritage through scholarship.

Language reform was among the movement's most durable achievements. Hu Shi's proposals and Chen Duxiu's call for a literary revolution advanced the case for written vernacular, and Lu Xun's fiction demonstrated its literary possibilities and its capacity for social criticism. Within a few years vernacular was adopted in primary textbooks and much periodical writing, though classical forms persisted in various domains. The change had implications for education and access, but the barrier of schooling remained, and the choice of a Mandarin-based standard had consequences for regional languages.

Women, marriage, and family became central issues. Essays, plays, and case reports like that of Zhao Wuzhen's suicide publicized the claims of free choice in marriage and women's education. Women entered universities, joined associations, and began to write for the public, although male-led programs often limited their goals, and working-class and rural women faced different problems. The experience of students, who formed a new social stratum, brought out tensions between individual freedom and national obligation. The movement's reach was socially and regionally limited: most Chinese lived in villages where such debates arrived slowly, and frontier regions and colonial Taiwan had distinct trajectories.

The Shandong Question connected Chinese politics to world war and imperial rivalry. Germany's leasehold in Qingdao was seized by Japan in 1914; the Twenty-One Demands and the 1918 notes strengthened Japan's claims; wartime secret agreements and great-power priorities at Paris produced a decision to transfer German rights to Japan. The news prompted the student demonstration of May 4, 1919. The arrest of thirty-two students and the subsequent mass arrests of early June generated wider outrage, and on June 5 merchants and workers in Shanghai joined in strikes and shop closures. Participation varied by city and group, reflecting different interests and organizational forms.

The immediate results included dismissal of three officials and the refusal of the Chinese delegation to sign the Treaty of Versailles. These were significant but did not resolve Shandong, which was negotiated at the Washington Conference and returned with conditions in 1922. Domestically, the movement did not strengthen the Beijing government, which continued to be dominated by militarists. Its more lasting effects lay in public culture and organization: student unions, labor activism, mass education, and an expanded press.

After 1919, debates about the way forward multiplied. Liberals and pragmatists emphasized education and gradual reform; anarchists promoted mutual aid and voluntary association; socialists and Marxists, influenced by the Russian Revolution, began forming study groups and, within a few years, parties. Cultural conservatives defended heritage. These tendencies interacted and overlapped. The ideological outcomes that followed, taken up in Chapter 61, were products of choices and circumstances, not consequences dictated by 1919.

Nationalism and internationalism coexisted. Chinese activists protested imperialism while drawing on global ideas and building ties with Koreans, Russians, and others, and attitudes toward the powers shifted in response to the events of 1919. The tension between cosmopolitan reform and cultural pride shaped later debates.

Interpretation of the era remains contested. Some historians stress rupture, others continuity with late Qing reform and enduring traditions, and still others emphasize the layered nature of the events. Later Nationalist, Communist, liberal, and conservative narratives used May Fourth for their own purposes, so students must read the sources with attention to memory and politics.

For the larger history of Chinese civilization, this chapter shows how cultural, educational, diplomatic, and social changes combined in a decade of intense experimentation. It also illustrates how China's modern history was shaped by global events, from the First World War to the Russian Revolution, and by the agency of Chinese students, writers, merchants, workers, women, and officials who debated and acted in uncertain conditions.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how, within a few years, debates about language, family, and authority, a diplomatic crisis rooted in global war, and new forms of social mobilization became connected in ways that reshaped Chinese public life. It also shows how fragile that connection was: the cultural movement and the protest movement involved different people and aims, and they drew apart as often as they converged.

The chapter's developments shaped later Chinese history without determining it. Vernacular writing became the basis of modern Chinese public language. The student, the woman reader, and the urban association entered political life. Questions about the family, individual rights, and national strength remained central to later debates. Rival political movements, taking shape in the early 1920s and discussed in Chapter 61, drew on the era's organizations and rhetoric. But the era also produced liberals, anarchists, and cultural conservatives whose paths diverged from those movements, and it left unresolved the tensions between individual freedom and national discipline, between foreign learning and native tradition.

The chapter also connects China to wider histories: the First World War and its peace settlement, Japanese imperial expansion, the Russian Revolution, colonial protest in Korea and elsewhere, and global circulation of ideas by students, laborers, and translators. Chinese actors were participants in these exchanges, not merely recipients. Understanding them helps students see Chinese history as diverse, interconnected, contested, adaptive, regionally varied, and politically changing, and prepares them for the party formation, revolutionary mobilization, and state-building of Chapters 61 and 62, as well as the broader social and cultural history of Chapter 63.
