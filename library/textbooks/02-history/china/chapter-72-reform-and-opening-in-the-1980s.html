Part XII — Reform and Contemporary China
72

Reform and Opening in the 1980s

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

In the early 1980s a Chinese farmer could sign a contract with a village collective, farm a plot of land for his own household, and sell part of what he grew; within a decade a former commune workshop might employ hundreds of people and export to Hong Kong. Neither the Party that approved these changes nor the farmers who made them could have predicted where they would lead.

In 1978, in the village of Xiaogang in Anhui province, a group of farm families reportedly agreed among themselves to divide the collective's fields and take responsibility for their own harvests. The story has been retold many times, and historians caution that details of the episode were shaped by later memory and by official retelling. What is not in doubt is that during 1979 and 1980 many villages in Anhui, Sichuan, Guizhou, and elsewhere experimented with some form of contracting production to groups or households, and that by 1983 the great majority of China's rural households farmed under household contracts. A countryside organized for two decades around people's communes had been reorganized within about five years.

This chapter studies how that and other transformations unfolded during the 1980s. Chapter 71 examined the political transition after Mao's death, the rise of Deng Xiaoping, the Third Plenum of 1978, and the first rural experiments. Chapter 72 asks what happened next. It follows the Household Responsibility System as it spread and matured; the growth of township and village enterprises and of small private businesses; the creation of Special Economic Zones and the wider opening of the coast to foreign trade and investment; the more difficult reform of urban industry, banking, and prices; changes in schools, laboratories, literature, film, and intellectual life; the transformation of consumption, migration, family life, and population policy; and the political debates and campaigns that accompanied all of this. The chapter ends in late 1988, when inflation, public anxiety, and unresolved arguments over political reform set the stage for the crisis examined in Chapter 73.

The decade matters because it altered the material lives of hundreds of millions of people and changed the way the Chinese state related to markets, to the rest of the world, and to its own citizens. It also matters because it shows how a ruling party could introduce far-reaching economic change while insisting on its own political leadership. Reform did not follow a master plan. Policy makers often legalized what local officials and farmers had already begun, tested ideas in selected places, and adjusted course when results were disappointing. Historians call this approach incremental or experimental, and a central task of the chapter is to explain both its achievements and its costs.

What changed was extensive: how farmland was farmed, how factories were managed, how goods were priced, where people could live and work, what books and films reached the public, and what ordinary families owned. What continued was equally important: collective ownership of rural land, state ownership of major industries, the household registration system, the Communist Party's monopoly on political power, and the state's authority over population policy and media. Many reform measures contained both elements at once, and students should resist the temptation to describe the decade as a simple movement from "socialism" to "capitalism," a description that many Chinese leaders of the time rejected and that obscures the hybrid institutions they actually built.

The people affected included farmers and their families; rural officials; village and township industrial managers; private traders; state-enterprise directors and workers; planners and economists; foreign investors and overseas Chinese businesspeople; teachers, students, scientists, and writers; migrants; women and men subject to new marriage and family-planning rules; and Party leaders who disagreed about how far and how fast to go. Their experiences differed sharply by region, class, gender, and place in the household registration system.

Historians draw on a wide range of evidence for this decade: central Party documents and laws; official statistical yearbooks; the press; local gazetteers and village studies; economists' contemporary analyses and later reconstructions; memoirs and interviews with officials and entrepreneurs; fiction, film, and reportage; surveys by Chinese and foreign researchers; and foreign diplomatic and business records. Each has limits, and the chapter's evidence boxes explain several of them. The 1980s also connect China to wider histories of the Cold War's later years, the Asian economic boom, the reform of other socialist economies, and the global movement of capital, goods, and ideas.

  8. Assess how education, science, overseas study, literature, film, and popular culture changed during the decade and how political regulation limited them.

Historical Context

By 1980 the People's Republic had existed for three decades and had passed through land reform, collectivization, the Great Leap Forward and famine, the Cultural Revolution, and the death of Mao Zedong (Chapters 66–70). Its economy had achieved substantial industrial growth and gains in literacy and life expectancy, but it also suffered from low agricultural productivity, shortages of consumer goods, weak incentives, and isolation from most of the world economy. In 1978 the great majority of the population still lived in the countryside and worked in agriculture, and rural per capita incomes were very low. A large share of rural people lived at or near subsistence by official standards used later.

The economic system inherited from the Mao era combined state ownership of industry, collective ownership of rural land, central planning of key goods, administered prices, rationing of grain, cloth, and other necessities, and strict controls on movement through the household registration (hukou) system. Rural people belonged to communes, which were at once units of production, local government, and social provision. Urban workers in state enterprises typically belonged to work units (danwei) that supplied jobs, housing, medical care, and pensions. Foreign trade was handled by a small number of state corporations. Private business had been nearly eliminated, and ideological suspicion of markets was deep.

Chapter 71 described how, after 1976, Hua Guofeng's brief leadership gave way to Deng Xiaoping's, how the Third Plenum of December 1978 shifted the Party's emphasis from class struggle to economic modernization, and how rural experiments in contracting began. Leaders in the early 1980s still disagreed over how far to go. Senior figures such as Chen Yun favored a significant role for planning and a cautious, step-by-step approach; Deng Xiaoping and later Zhao Ziyang were more willing to experiment with markets and foreign capital; Hu Yaobang pressed for rapid rehabilitation of those persecuted in earlier campaigns and for greater openness. Their disagreements shaped the decade, and so did the powerful interests of provincial governments, ministries, state enterprises, and local officials.

Geography also shaped reform. Coastal provinces had ports, access to Hong Kong and Taiwan, overseas Chinese family networks, and old commercial traditions; the lower Yangtze region had dense rural industry and skilled labor; the Pearl River Delta stood next to Hong Kong and Macao. Inland, northwestern, southwestern, and minority regions had poorer infrastructure and thinner markets. The meaning of "China" as an economic space was thus uneven: the reforms did not reach all places at the same time or with the same force.

The international setting mattered. The United States and the PRC had established diplomatic relations in 1979; the Soviet Union was in the last decade before its dissolution; Japan and the newly industrializing economies of Hong Kong, Taiwan, South Korea, and Singapore were exporting manufactured goods at high rates. Chinese leaders watched these experiences, as well as reform experiments in Hungary and Yugoslavia, with interest. Yet the scale of China's population, the peasant base of its economy, and the continued rule of the Communist Party made its path distinctive. Students should keep in mind throughout that the reforms were chosen, contested, and sometimes reversed by identifiable people responding to particular pressures, not the unfolding of a predetermined path.

Division I · Sections 72.1–72.6

Agricultural Reform and Rural Transformation, 1980–1985

Division purpose: to explain how the reorganization of agriculture became the foundation of China's early economic transformation. Students should distinguish collective ownership of rural land from the contractual right of households to cultivate it and keep income after meeting obligations. The Household Responsibility System did not simply privatize farmland, and prosperity did not arrive uniformly.

Section 72.1

The Nationwide Expansion of the Household Responsibility System

Household contracting did not begin as a national program. During 1978 and 1979 some production teams, especially in poor and food-deficit areas of Anhui and Sichuan, divided work or land among groups or households when collective farming had failed to provide enough to eat. Local leaders such as Wan Li in Anhui and Zhao Ziyang in Sichuan tolerated or encouraged such practices. Central policy at first treated them as exceptions. A September 1980 conference of provincial Party secretaries produced a document (usually cited as Central Document No. 75) that said household contracting should not be blocked in remote, poor areas where peasants wanted it, while still favoring collective forms elsewhere. That cautious phrasing is a reminder that the system's later triumph was not planned in 1978.

Two terms from the period are often confused. Baochan daohu meant contracting output quotas to the household: the household farmed the land and delivered an agreed quantity to the collective and the state, keeping a defined share of the rest. Baogan daohu, often called dabaogan ("big contract"), went further: the household took responsibility for all production and, after paying agricultural taxes and meeting delivery quotas to the state and fees to the collective, kept the remainder. By the early 1980s the second form had become the dominant one because it was simple, easy to monitor, and gave families clear control over their income.

The spread was fast. Estimates compiled from official statistics suggest that only a small percentage of production teams used household contracting in 1980, perhaps one in ten, while by the end of 1983 the proportion was in the high nineties. Central Document No. 1 of January 1982 declared that the various responsibility systems, including household contracting, were forms of the socialist collective economy rather than a return to individual farming, and the 1983 document described household contracting as a creation of the peasantry that gave scope to the vitality of collective ownership. A further document in 1984 urged that land contracts run for fifteen years or longer to encourage investment in soil and equipment.

Why did the system spread so quickly? Part of the answer is incentive. Under the commune, work points were distributed according to labor performed in large groups, which made monitoring difficult and effort uneven. Household farming linked effort and reward directly, and families could decide what to plant, how many hours to work, and when to turn to sideline production. Part of the answer is politics: senior leaders such as Deng Xiaoping and Zhao Ziyang had few reasons to oppose a policy that peasants evidently welcomed and that raised output, while opponents of decollectivization had no practical alternative. And part of the answer is local variation, since county and provincial officials chose when to push or hold back, sometimes pressing households to contract even where collective farming still worked well. Reports exist of both enthusiasm and pressure.

Section 72.2

The Dismantling of the People's Commune System

Contracting land to households made the commune's productive role redundant, but the commune was also the lowest level of the rural state. It collected taxes and grain, ran schools and clinics, maintained irrigation, supervised militia, and enforced policy. Dismantling it required a new system of local government. The 1982 Constitution provided for the replacement of people's communes by township (xiang) governments and for village committees as self-governing mass organizations. A Party and State Council notice of October 1983 ordered the separation of government administration from commune economic management, and by the end of 1984 nearly all communes had been replaced, creating tens of thousands of townships and hundreds of thousands of villages.

In practice the change was more gradual and less complete than the formal chronology suggests. Former commune cadres often became township officials. Brigades became villages, and production teams became villagers' groups. Many of the old collective assets, such as tractors, workshops, and irrigation facilities, passed to village or township control or were sold or contracted to individuals, sometimes with disputes over fairness. In some localities the village Party branch and village committee overlapped in personnel and authority. A trial Organic Law of Village Committees was adopted in 1987, after much debate over whether villagers should elect their leaders, and elections were uneven in implementation.

For ordinary villagers the changes were double-edged. Families gained freedom over daily work and over spending of earned income. But the collective no longer guaranteed basic services in the same way, and fees and levies imposed by local governments, which now had to finance schools, roads, and offices, became a source of friction in later years. Women who had held positions in commune-era mass organizations sometimes found fewer formal roles. Local officials, meanwhile, retained substantial authority through control over land allocation, credit, permits, and access to markets and enterprises. This helps explain why township and village governments later took such an active part in industrial development (Division II).

Historical Vocabulary
Collective Ownership, Household Contract, and Production Responsibility

Collective ownership meant that rural land belonged to a village or other rural collective rather than to individuals or the national state; the 1982 Constitution prohibited its sale or lease. A household contract gave a family the right to use a specified plot for a set period and to keep income beyond its obligations. A production responsibility system (shengchan zerenzhi) was the general term for arrangements tying rewards to results. Understanding these distinctions matters because Chinese farmers in this decade neither owned nor merely rented land in the usual senses. Later amendments (1988) permitted the transfer of land-use rights, a change that opened new debates about security of tenure and the rights of villagers.

Section 72.3

Agricultural Production, Procurement, and Rural Income Growth

Official statistics show strong growth in agricultural output in the first half of the decade. Grain production, which had been roughly 305 million tonnes in 1978, reached about 407 million tonnes in 1984, a record at the time. Cotton, oilseeds, sugar, and other crops rose even faster in percentage terms, and pork, poultry, and fish output increased. Scholars estimate that real rural incomes rose at double-digit annual rates between about 1978 and 1984, and nominal per capita net rural income roughly rose from about 134 yuan to about 355 yuan over those years. Nominal figures overstate real gains because prices also rose, a distinction the chapter's evidence box below treats.

The state's procurement system shaped what farmers did. Before the reforms, the state bought grain, cotton, and other products through compulsory deliveries at fixed prices and resold them through rationing. In 1979 the government raised procurement prices for major farm products substantially and paid higher prices for deliveries above quota. After household contracting spread, families met their delivery obligations and could sell any surplus in free or negotiated markets. Many shifted land and labor toward more profitable crops and sidelines. In 1985 the system of unified purchase and sale was replaced by contract purchase for grain and cotton, under which the state signed purchase contracts with farmers at set prices and allowed the remainder to be sold at market. Grain output fell in 1985 to about 379 million tonnes and recovered only slowly, because the procurement price was lower than market prices for some goods, input costs were rising, and farmers allocated less land and effort to grain.

Statistics demand caution. Aggregate output includes different crops measured in different units; per capita figures depend on population estimates; and local officials sometimes inflated reports. Yet independent studies, including household surveys and village case studies by Chinese and foreign researchers, generally confirm substantial gains in productivity and income, while disagreeing on how much came from contracting itself and how much from price increases, inputs, and technology.

Section 72.4

Rural Markets, Sideline Production, and Commercial Agriculture

Reform restored rural markets that had been restricted or closed during the Cultural Revolution. Village and town fairs expanded, and by the early 1980s many places had thousands of free markets where farmers sold vegetables, poultry, handicrafts, and other goods. Households raised pigs and chickens, cultivated fruit and vegetables, grew cash crops such as tobacco and tea, built fishponds, and produced bricks, tiles, and clothing. The state encouraged "specialized households" (zhuanye hu), which concentrated on a particular product or service. Propaganda celebrated "ten-thousand-yuan households," those whose annual income reached that sum, an extraordinary figure compared with average rural incomes. These examples were real but not typical.

Transport and trade changed too. Families bought tractors, trucks, and boats and started long-distance trading, while state commercial departments and supply-and-marketing cooperatives faced competition from private traders. Traders carried vegetables from the countryside to cities and manufactured goods back to villages. In some regions, trade routes expanded across provincial lines despite local protectionism. The relationship between subsistence and commercial farming also shifted: most households continued to grow staple grain for their own needs and for state quotas, but increased shares of land and labor went to higher-value activities, especially near cities and transport lines.

This commercialization had winners and limits. Households near large cities, with access to roads, rail, and credit, could specialize; remote and mountainous villages had fewer opportunities. Market prices for vegetables and meat fluctuated and sometimes collapsed when many farmers made the same choice. Price controls on some products and the state's monopoly on some purchases, such as cotton and tobacco, constrained choices. Even so, the reappearance of markets after decades of restriction was one of the most visible changes in everyday rural life, and it drew many peasants, for the first time in a generation, into regular commercial exchange.

Section 72.5

Rural Families, Land Contracts, and Social Differentiation

Household contracting made the family a unit of production in a way it had not been since the 1950s. Land was usually allocated according to the number of household members or the number of laborers, or a combination, so family size and composition affected the size of plots. Because population differed from village to village and some villages readjusted allocations as families changed, plots were small and scattered; household holdings averaged well under a hectare in many regions and consisted of several non-contiguous strips. Fragmentation limited mechanization but suited intensive family labor.

Division of labor within families changed as well. Men more often took work off the farm, in construction or township enterprises, while women took on a larger share of field work, a pattern later called the "feminization of agriculture," though its extent varied by region. Women also managed sideline production, textile work, and household finances. The Household Responsibility System relied on family labor and thus on family decisions about marriage, children, and the education of sons and daughters, which interacted with family-planning policy (72.32). In some places, families sought larger households to maximize labor, and in others, sons were preferred as providers.

Social differentiation emerged. Households with more laborers, good land, skills, or connections to officials prospered faster; those with illness, few workers, or poor land fell behind. Village leaders who controlled access to credit, contracts, and collective assets sometimes enriched themselves. Regional differences were large: suburban Jiangsu and Guangdong grew far wealthier than interior Gansu or Guizhou. In poor areas, the end of collective provision of welfare left some families more vulnerable. Researchers conducting village studies document both rising prosperity and rising inequality, which official averages obscure.

Section 72.6

The Achievements and Limitations of Agricultural Reform

By the mid-1980s the achievements of rural reform were widely recognized: higher output, rising incomes, better food supplies, a revival of markets, and the release of labor for other work. Chinese and international analysts also credit rural reform with helping to reduce poverty. Estimates of the rural population below China's official poverty line fell substantially between 1978 and 1985, from several hundred million to roughly a hundred and several tens of millions, with the exact figures depending on the poverty definition used.

But the early burst faded. After 1984 the growth rate of agricultural output slowed, and grain output stagnated for several years. Reasons included the one-time nature of some gains, a shift of investment and labor to other sectors, declining collective maintenance of irrigation and drainage, low state procurement prices, rising costs of fertilizer and other inputs, the fragmentation of land, and limited credit. Village and township governments and some farmers complained of receiving "IOUs" instead of cash for deliveries when the state purchasing system lacked funds. The shift to contract procurement in 1985 was intended to address these frustrations but did not fully resolve them. Rural-urban income ratios, which had narrowed in the early 1980s, widened again toward the end of the decade.

Historical Debate

What Caused the Early Agricultural Boom?

The question: Chinese farm output and incomes rose rapidly between about 1978 and 1984. How much of the increase was due to the shift to household contracting, and how much to other factors?

Interpretation A — The responsibility system was the main driver. Econometric studies by some economists attribute a large share of output growth in the early 1980s to the improved incentives created by household farming. They point to the sharp growth in places that adopted contracting earlier and to the timing of the surge.
Interpretation B — Multiple factors mattered and contracting was only one. Other scholars emphasize higher procurement prices, expanded use of chemical fertilizer, improved seed varieties, rural electrification, investment in irrigation made earlier under the commune, and recovery from the disruption of the Cultural Revolution. On this view, contracting was important but cannot be isolated as the single cause, and the declines after 1984 show that the effect of any one factor was limited.

Evidence: Both views use official time series, provincial comparisons, household surveys, and village studies. They differ in how they treat data quality, the timing of adoption across provinces, and the counterfactual of what would have happened without contracting.

Why it matters: The debate affects how later reforms are understood, including whether markets and incentives alone drive growth or whether state investment and prior social development also play roles.

Student task: What kind of evidence would best help to separate the effect of contracting from the effects of price increases and inputs? Why might scholars with different political perspectives still reach similar findings about timing?

Division II · Sections 72.7–72.11

Township and Village Enterprises and Emerging Entrepreneurship, 1980–1988

Division purpose: to explain why China's economic transformation was not simply a transfer of activity from state-owned enterprises to privately owned firms. Township and village enterprises were a distinctive feature of the reform economy: some were collectively owned, others privately run, and many were hard to classify. Their rise deserves treatment separate from urban state-enterprise reform.

Section 72.7

From Commune and Brigade Enterprises to Township and Village Enterprises

Rural industry did not begin with reform. During the Mao era, communes and brigades had established small factories, workshops, and repair stations, especially after the Great Leap Forward and again in the 1970s, when the state promoted the "five small industries" (such as cement, fertilizer, iron, farm machinery, and power) to support agriculture and local self-reliance. By 1978 these commune and brigade enterprises employed roughly 28 million people, a notable figure but a small share of the labor force. They were typically tied to local collectives, produced for local needs and for state contracts, and operated under tight political and planning constraints.

Reform changed their position. Household contracting freed labor and capital in the villages, and rising rural incomes created demand for building materials, clothing, processed food, and consumer goods. At the same time, the state's own industries could not satisfy demand, leaving openings for rural firms. In 1984 the central government formally renamed commune and brigade enterprises "township and village enterprises" (xiangzhen qiye), a category that now also included cooperative enterprises, partnerships, and individually run businesses in rural areas. The renaming was more than semantic. It recognized rural industry as an important part of the national economy rather than as a subordinate adjunct of the collective, and it signaled that firms owned by townships, villages, groups of households, or individuals would be permitted and encouraged.

The ownership of TVEs was often ambiguous. A firm might be formally owned by a village collective, funded by loans guaranteed by the township government, managed by a contracted entrepreneur, and employing villagers as workers. The collective's claim to residual profit and the manager's claims to reward and control were negotiated locally. Economists later debated whether the "collective" label masked effective private control or whether local government ownership was in itself an effective institutional solution in an environment of weak property law and credit markets.

Historical Vocabulary
Township and Village Enterprise (TVE)

A township and village enterprise (xiangzhen qiye) was a rural non-agricultural firm, usually located in a township or village and owned by a local government or collective, though privately owned and jointly owned firms were also counted in the category. The term is a statistical and administrative label that covered very different organizations, from large factories to small workshops. It matters historically because it captured the middle ground in which many reform-era firms operated: neither part of the central plan nor purely private. Students should not assume that "TVE" meant "private business" or that it meant "state enterprise."

Section 72.8

The Expansion of Township and Village Enterprises

The growth of TVEs was rapid. Official statistics show total employment in these enterprises rising from about 28 million in 1978 to about 95 million in 1988, with output growing even faster. By the end of the decade, TVEs accounted for a large and rising share of national industrial output, roughly a fifth by some counts, and for a large share of exports in some regions. They produced textiles, garments, building materials, food products, simple machinery, plastics, and a wide variety of consumer goods, and they provided construction, transport, and repair services.

Why did they grow so quickly? Several conditions combined. Local governments, which retained control over land and labor and could mobilize local savings and bank credit, had strong incentives to promote industry because enterprise revenues funded local budgets, schools, roads, and officials' careers. Reforms in fiscal relations between center and localities, including arrangements by which localities kept a share of revenue, intensified those incentives. Rural enterprises were not bound by the obligations carried by state firms, such as providing lifetime employment, housing, and pensions, and thus had lower labor costs. They could employ surplus rural labor, adjust wages to market conditions, and respond to demand for goods that planners neglected. They also drew on networks of kin, local officials, retired urban technicians, and engineers from state factories who were recruited as consultants, sometimes on weekends.

The risks were real. Many TVEs were small, technologically backward, energy intensive, and polluting; some collapsed when credit tightened or when state firms regained supplies of raw materials. Competition for scarce inputs between rural and state firms led to local protectionism and "wars" over cotton, wool, and other materials. And the entwining of local government and enterprise, while an engine of growth, created opportunities for favoritism, debt, and later difficulties. The success of TVEs in the 1980s also depended on conditions, such as repressed prices for some inputs and unmet consumer demand, that would not last into the 1990s, as Chapter 74 shows.

Section 72.9

Rural Industrial Workers and Changing Labor Opportunities

For rural families, TVEs offered an alternative to farming. Workers moved into local factories, workshops, construction crews, and transport. Many remained in their villages or commuted from them, a pattern captured in the phrase attributed to sociologist Fei Xiaotong, "leaving the land but not the village" (litu bu lixiang). Others traveled further as temporary workers. Wages in TVEs were generally higher than farm income but lower than those in state enterprises, and the jobs lacked the security and benefits of urban state employment. Work discipline in many small firms was strict, hours were long, and safety standards were often weak.

Women formed a large share of the workforce in many labor-intensive sectors such as textiles, garments, and electronics assembly. In some villages young unmarried women went to work in local factories before marriage; elsewhere married women combined factory work with farming and domestic responsibilities. Observers noted that factory work gave many women independent cash income and new social experience, but it also exposed them to wage discrimination, long hours, hazards, and pressure to leave work after marriage or childbirth. Skills were acquired mostly on the job, with apprenticeships and, in some cases, training provided by technicians from nearby cities.

Rural industrialization also interacted with migration. In regions with strong TVE sectors, such as southern Jiangsu, villagers found work locally and in some places hired migrants from poorer provinces. Elsewhere, where TVEs were scarce, workers left home for cities and coastal factories, a pattern examined in 72.31. The relationship between village enterprises and migration thus differed by region: TVEs could retain labor in some areas and, by creating demand for labor and for goods, stimulate movement in others.

Section 72.10

Private Entrepreneurship, Individual Businesses, and the Reemergence of Commerce

During the Mao era, private commerce had been largely abolished. In 1978 the number of individually run urban businesses was only in the low hundreds of thousands. Early in the reform period, the return of educated youth sent to the countryside and the need to provide jobs created pressure to allow self-employment. A 1981 State Council provision legalized individual businesses in cities and towns, and the number of "individual households" (getihu) grew rapidly, eventually reaching well over ten million by the late 1980s. They included shoe repairers, tailors, street vendors, small restaurants, barbers, transport operators, and small shops.

Official attitudes were ambivalent. Leaders sought to expand employment and meet consumer demand but worried about speculation, tax evasion, and the ideology of exploitation. A rule of thumb sometimes cited from Marxist theory held that a business employing more than a handful of hired workers constituted capitalist exploitation; many getihu respected the limit or concealed their hiring. A well-known case, that of an Anhui seller of sunflower seeds who hired dozens of workers, prompted debate in 1984, and Deng Xiaoping reportedly suggested leaving such cases alone rather than deciding them in advance. In 1988, an amendment to the Constitution recognized the private sector as a supplement to the socialist public economy, and provisional regulations on private enterprises followed that summer, officially defining private firms as those with eight or more employees.

Individual and private businesses also had to cope with unpredictable policy. Campaigns against "economic crimes" in the early 1980s targeted some entrepreneurs; official hostility rose and fell; and credit from state banks was hard to obtain. Many entrepreneurs relied on family savings and informal loans. Social attitudes shifted as well. Early getihu were often people with few alternatives, such as former prisoners, returned youth, or unemployed workers, and they had low social status; by the late 1980s some had become wealthy and attracted both envy and admiration. A widely circulated but unreliable slogan attributed to Deng, "to get rich is glorious," is not documented in his speeches, a reminder that the public sometimes remembers reform through phrases that historians cannot verify.

Section 72.11

Regional Models of Rural Enterprise Development

Different regions built rural industry in different ways. In southern Jiangsu (Suzhou, Wuxi, and Changzhou), where commune-era workshops had been strong and ties to Shanghai's technicians and markets were close, rural industry was largely owned and managed by townships and villages. Sociologist Fei Xiaotong's studies of what became known as the "Sunan model" described collective enterprises backed by local governments, using local capital, and often subcontracting for Shanghai factories. Local officials acted as entrepreneurs, drawing on village labor and the support of nearby urban technicians.

Wenzhou in Zhejiang was different. A coastal region with limited arable land, little state investment, and a long tradition of migrant artisans and traders, it developed household-based private workshops and long-distance marketing networks, selling buttons, zippers, shoes, low-voltage electrical goods, and other small commodities across the country. Informal credit associations and kin networks supplied capital. Local authorities were wary at first, and a 1982 campaign targeted prominent private traders, but later local governments came to protect private enterprise, leading observers to speak of the "Wenzhou model." Neither model was purely private or purely collective in practice: Wenzhou firms often registered as collectives to avoid political risk, a practice known as wearing a "red hat."

In the Pearl River Delta, entrepreneurship combined local collective and household enterprise with investment from Hong Kong. Beginning in 1978, factories such as a handbag plant in Dongguan, often identified as the first, accepted materials and designs from Hong Kong firms and assembled goods for export under arrangements known as "three supplies and one compensation" (sanlai yibu). Hong Kong manufacturers, facing rising costs at home, moved labor-intensive operations to Guangdong villages, where land and labor were cheap and local officials were eager for revenue. This model laid the foundation for the export manufacturing boom discussed in Chapters 74 and 75.

Local Government, Credit, and Institutional Difference

Regional differences in rural industry reflected differences in institutions as well as geography. Division III · Sections 72.12–72.17

Special Economic Zones, International Trade, and Foreign Investment, 1980–1988

Division purpose: to explain how China opened selected regions and sectors to foreign economic activity while retaining substantial state control over economic and political institutions. The Special Economic Zones were institutional experiments involving geography, labor, infrastructure, administrative discretion, foreign capital, and new approaches to enterprise management. A critical distinction: the fourteen coastal open cities of 1984 were not all designated Special Economic Zones, and their policies and legal status differed.

Section 72.12

The Establishment of China's First Special Economic Zones

The idea of special zones for foreign trade and investment emerged from discussions in 1979 among Guangdong and Fujian officials and central leaders. Guangdong, adjacent to Hong Kong and Macao and the ancestral home of many overseas Chinese, and Fujian, facing Taiwan and with strong emigrant ties, were granted special policies in 1979 allowing greater autonomy in foreign trade and investment. Guangdong Party leaders, including Xi Zhongxun and Yang Shangkun, argued for zones where regulations could differ from those in the rest of the country. Initially called "export special zones," they were renamed "special economic zones" in 1980. In August 1980 the Standing Committee of the National People's Congress approved regulations for the Special Economic Zones in Guangdong, and in 1980 Shenzhen, Zhuhai, Shantou, and Xiamen were designated as zones.

The purposes were several. Leaders wanted to attract foreign capital, technology, and management skills; earn foreign exchange; create jobs; and learn how a market-oriented economy operated, while confining possible political and ideological risks to limited areas. The zones were intended to be "windows" through which China could observe the global economy and "laboratories" for policies that might later be extended. They were also a way to avoid immediate wider change in a system where many officials were wary of foreign investment.

Their institutional features included preferential tax rates, land-use arrangements, flexibility in labor hiring and wages, simplified customs procedures, and permission for foreign firms to repatriate profits. Strict controls separated the zones from the rest of China: a fenced boundary, the "second line," in Shenzhen regulated the movement of goods and people between the zone and inland areas. Local authorities in the zones had greater autonomy in approving projects and managing budgets. Critics within the leadership questioned whether the zones might create "foreign concessions" or promote smuggling and corruption, and the debate continued through the 1980s.

Historical Vocabulary
Special Economic Zone (SEZ)

A Special Economic Zone (jingji tequ) was a designated area in which different economic rules from those in the rest of China applied, typically offering tax incentives, flexible labor and land arrangements, and greater autonomy to attract foreign investment. China's first four SEZs, established in 1980, were Shenzhen, Zhuhai, Shantou, and Xiamen; Hainan was added in 1988. The term is often used loosely for the later coastal open cities and development zones, but these were different categories with different legal status. SEZs matter historically because they allowed experimentation under controlled conditions before wider policy changes.

Section 72.13

Shenzhen and the Transformation of a Border Economy

Before 1979, Shenzhen was a small town in Bao'an county on the border with Hong Kong, with a population of a few tens of thousands and an economy based on farming, fishing, and border trade. It had been, in the 1960s and 1970s, a point of departure for migrants fleeing to Hong Kong. When it became an SEZ, it was suddenly the site of enormous construction: roads, ports, power, housing, factories, and offices. Early projects were led by Hong Kong and domestic investors. The Shekou Industrial Zone, developed by a Hong Kong–based subsidiary of the China Merchants shipping company from 1979, became known for management practices that included wage incentives and bidding for construction contracts; a slogan displayed there in 1981 about time and efficiency became famous nationally.

Labor came from across China. Construction workers, including soldiers transferred from the army, built the zone; factories recruited young workers, many of them women, from inland provinces; and engineers, managers, and officials arrived from other parts of the country. Wages were higher than elsewhere, and workers' contracts, performance pay, and the possibility of dismissal differed from the "iron rice bowl" of state employment. Dormitory housing, long hours, and the lack of local household registration meant that many workers were temporary residents without full rights to local services.

The results were mixed. Shenzhen's economic growth in the 1980s was fast, and the construction of tall buildings, such as a 53-story trade center completed in the mid-1980s, became symbols of "Shenzhen speed." Deng Xiaoping's visit to the zone in early 1984 and his approval of the experiment strengthened its position. But early investment was concentrated in real estate, trade, and low-technology processing; the zone depended on imports; and domestic sales of imported goods, along with smuggling, drew criticism. A review in 1985–1986 questioned the zone's finances and trade deficit, and local leaders shifted emphasis toward export-oriented manufacturing. The contrast between the original promise of advanced technology transfer and the reality of labor-intensive assembly was a theme of the decade.

Section 72.14

Coastal Opening in 1984 and the Expansion to Hainan

In spring 1984, following Deng Xiaoping's visits to the southern zones and the positive early results there, the central government opened fourteen coastal port cities to foreign investment: Dalian, Qinhuangdao, Tianjin, Yantai, Qingdao, Lianyungang, Nantong, Shanghai, Ningbo, Wenzhou, Fuzhou, Guangzhou, Zhanjiang, and Beihai. These cities, along with the island of Hainan, then administered as part of Guangdong, were given some of the policy incentives available in the SEZs, including greater authority to approve foreign investment projects, preferential taxes for certain firms, and the creation of economic and technological development zones. They were not special economic zones in the legal sense, and their regulatory regimes were narrower. Xiamen's zone was enlarged to cover the entire island in 1984.

In 1985 the government designated the Yangtze River Delta, the Pearl River Delta, and the southern Fujian triangle (Xiamen–Zhangzhou–Quanzhou) as open coastal economic zones, and later additions included the Liaodong and Shandong peninsulas. In 1988 Zhao Ziyang advanced a "coastal development strategy" that urged coastal regions to import raw materials and export manufactured goods, with "both ends outside" (liangtou zai wai), thereby using their abundant labor to compete on world markets rather than competing with inland regions for domestic raw materials.

Hainan's story shows the risks of poorly regulated opening. In 1984–1985, officials on the island imported tens of thousands of vehicles and other goods using foreign exchange and resold them on the mainland at large profits, leading to a major scandal and a central investigation. In April 1988 the National People's Congress approved making Hainan a separate province and the largest of the SEZs. Hainan's later history of real estate speculation and later development is discussed in subsequent chapters.

Section 72.15

Foreign Investment, Joint Ventures, and Technology Transfer

The legal basis for foreign investment began with the 1979 Law on Chinese-Foreign Equity Joint Ventures, with implementing regulations in 1983. A joint venture combined Chinese and foreign partners' capital, with risks and profits shared according to equity. The Law on Wholly Foreign-Owned Enterprises followed in 1986, the same year that the State Council issued a set of provisions to encourage foreign investment with incentives on taxes, land use, and access to foreign exchange. A 1988 law governed contractual (cooperative) joint ventures. These laws gradually clarified a framework, though foreign investors continued to face uncertainty about enforcement.

Foreign investment in the 1980s was limited in scale relative to later decades: actual foreign direct investment amounted to several billion US dollars per year by the end of the decade. Early projects included hotels, such as the Great Wall Hotel in Beijing, and manufacturing joint ventures such as Beijing Jeep (a venture of a Beijing automobile company with American Motors, founded in 1984) and Shanghai Volkswagen. By decade's end, foreign-funded firms in coastal regions produced consumer goods, electronics, garments, and shoes for export. In 1987 an American fast-food chain opened a restaurant near Tiananmen Square, a symbol of change that attracted curious crowds.

Investors faced obstacles. Infrastructure was weak. Chinese partners often had limited understanding of foreign markets and law. Foreign exchange balancing requirements made it difficult for some firms to import parts and repatriate profits. Customs rules were complicated, and local authorities sometimes imposed fees. Labor management differed from foreign expectations because Chinese partners wanted to keep workers' welfare commitments. Foreign managers accused workers of low productivity, while workers complained of harsh discipline. Technology transfer, a central Chinese goal, occurred unevenly: joint ventures taught management and production skills to many workers and managers, but foreign firms often kept key technology and design under their control, and critics later debated whether China traded market access for technology effectively.

Section 72.16

International Trade, Overseas Chinese Capital, and Production Networks

Foreign trade grew rapidly after 1978. Chinese exports rose from roughly 10 billion US dollars in 1978 to nearly 50 billion by 1988, and the composition shifted from agricultural goods, raw materials, and oil toward textiles, garments, and light manufactures. Reforms decentralized the authority to trade from a few national corporations to provincial and local firms and, increasingly, to enterprises themselves. A system of foreign exchange retention allowed exporters to keep a portion of their earnings, and in the late 1980s swap centers allowed firms to trade foreign exchange at rates that departed from the official one. The dual exchange rate system, along with the use of special "foreign exchange certificates" for foreigners, added to arbitrage opportunities. In 1986 China formally applied to resume its status in the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, beginning a long negotiation that would continue into the next century (Chapter 74).

Much of the capital and many of the managers in export manufacturing came from Hong Kong, which accounted for the majority of recorded foreign investment in the 1980s. Overseas Chinese businesspeople from Southeast Asia, such as the Thai-Chinese Charoen Pokphand group in Shenzhen, and later from Taiwan, invested in the mainland for both economic and family reasons. Language, ancestral villages, lineage associations, and shared dialects made it easier for people with ties to Guangdong and Fujian to trust partners and navigate local bureaucracy. Hong Kong also served as an entrepôt for trade with Taiwan and as a source of banking, shipping, and legal services. Production networks emerged in which Hong Kong firms handled design, marketing, and finance while factories in Guangdong did the assembly.

China and the Wider World

Hong Kong, Overseas Chinese Networks, and Foreign Investment

The reforms occurred in a region already integrated by the export-led growth of Japan and the "newly industrializing economies." Hong Kong, Taiwan, South Korea, and Singapore had risen in the 1960s and 1970s by exporting manufactured goods, and rising wages and currency appreciation in the 1980s led many firms to relocate labor-intensive production to lower-cost locations. China's coast offered large pools of labor and proximity. Chinese leaders thus entered a regional process: they were not simply importing foreign capital passively but actively designing zones and rules to attract it, and Chinese local governments competed with each other and with Southeast Asian countries for investment. The overseas Chinese diaspora (Chapter 42 on earlier maritime connections) provided financial and personal links that reemerged after decades of political barriers.

Section 72.17

Hong Kong, Macao, Taiwan, and the International Setting of Opening

The economic opening was tied to political arrangements. Hong Kong island and Kowloon had been ceded to Britain in the nineteenth century (Chapter 52), and the New Territories had been leased in 1898 for ninety-nine years, with the lease due to expire in 1997. After British Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher's visit to Beijing in 1982, the two governments began negotiations. Deng Xiaoping insisted that sovereignty over the whole territory would return to China, while proposing that Hong Kong keep its capitalist system under the formula "one country, two systems," which had first been conceived with Taiwan in mind. The Sino-British Joint Declaration was signed on 19 December 1984 by Zhao Ziyang and Thatcher and took effect in 1985. In 1987 the Sino-Portuguese Joint Declaration on Macao was signed, providing for the return of that territory in 1999. The actual transfers, in 1997 and 1999, belong to Chapter 74 and later treatment.

Taiwan was a different case: the PRC and the government of the Republic of China in Taipei each claimed to be the legitimate government of China, and neither accepted the other's claim. In 1979 the PRC called for contacts and the "three links" (trade, transport, and postal exchange), and in 1981 Ye Jianying, chairman of the National People's Congress Standing Committee, announced a nine-point proposal. The authorities in Taipei at first rejected contact. As martial law ended in Taiwan in 1987, the authorities permitted residents to visit relatives on the mainland, and in the following years hundreds of thousands did so, reuniting families separated since 1949 and creating channels for trade, indirect investment, and information. Taiwanese capital began to flow into the mainland through Hong Kong and other intermediaries, though the political relationship remained unresolved (Chapter 76).

Students should keep careful distinctions. The 1980s events were arrangements about the future status of Hong Kong and Macao, negotiations over their autonomy, and the first steps in cross-Strait personal and commercial contact. The governments involved held different legal positions on historical treaties and on sovereignty over Taiwan; historians distinguish treaties and declarations, which are documented, from political claims, which are positions.

Division IV · Sections 72.18–72.23

Industrial, Financial, and Price Reform, 1984–1988

Division purpose: to explain why reforming the urban industrial economy proved more complicated than introducing household incentives into agriculture. The central difficulty was the coexistence of a planned economy and expanding market activity: institutions still allocated important resources administratively, while firms and individuals increasingly responded to prices, profits, and opportunities. The 1988 price-reform controversy is developed here as an economic and political event of that year; its consequences for 1989 belong to Chapter 73.

Section 72.18

The 1984 Decision on Reform of the Economic Structure

Urban reform began before 1984 but on a limited scale. From 1978 a handful of state enterprises in Sichuan, and then others elsewhere, were allowed to retain a share of profits, make some production decisions, and pay bonuses. Between 1983 and 1984 the government replaced the delivery of profits to the state with enterprise taxes in two stages, an attempt to give firms a clearer claim on what remained. In May 1984 the State Council issued provisions expanding the autonomy of state industrial enterprises in areas such as production planning, sales above plan, pricing of some products, and the use of retained funds. These were small steps, and managers still depended on ministries and local officials for materials, credit, and approvals.

The strong results from rural reform and the early zones encouraged leaders to broaden the effort. On 20 October 1984, the Third Plenum of the Twelfth Central Committee adopted the Decision on Reform of the Economic Structure. The decision described the socialist economy as a "planned commodity economy" on the basis of public ownership, a formula that granted legitimacy to commodity exchange and the market while retaining planning and public ownership as defining features. It argued that ownership and management rights could be separated, so that state enterprises could be managed as relatively independent units responsible for their own profits and losses. It called for reducing the scope of mandatory planning, reforming the irrational price system, shifting from administrative to economic means of regulation, expanding the role of regional and local authorities, and breaking with egalitarian distribution that failed to reward effort. It also called for reform of wage systems and acknowledged that some people and regions might become prosperous earlier than others.

The decision was a major ideological step. Reform supporters regarded it as a theoretical justification for markets within socialism, allowing officials to cite it against conservative critics. But it did not specify how to reform prices, how to limit the power of ministries, or how to handle unprofitable firms. The tension between the language of the document and the practical difficulty of implementation shaped everything that followed. Senior leaders such as Chen Yun continued to emphasize the importance of the plan and warned against allowing market forces to dominate, a concern often expressed through the metaphor of a bird allowed to fly only within a cage.

Section 72.19

State-Owned Enterprise Reform and Managerial Autonomy

State-owned enterprises (SOEs) were the center of the urban economy. They employed most urban workers, produced most industrial goods, and provided housing, schools, clinics, and pensions to their workers. They operated under annual plans and had little control over inputs, output, or prices, and if they lost money, the state covered the loss, an arrangement later economists called a "soft budget constraint." Reform aimed to give managers more freedom and to link rewards to performance without changing ownership.

The principal device after 1986 was the contract responsibility system (chengbao zerenzhi). Under it, the enterprise's manager or management team signed a contract with the supervising agency, committing to deliver a fixed amount of profit or taxes in return for the right to keep above-target earnings and to make decisions in specified areas. By 1987 a large majority of large and medium state enterprises had adopted contracts. Contracts sometimes encouraged managers to improve efficiency and to invest, but they could also encourage short-term thinking, such as underinvestment in equipment and the manipulation of target levels through negotiation. Targets were bargained, and enterprises with better political connections could obtain easier terms. A system of factory director responsibility, introduced experimentally in 1984 and promoted more widely after 1986, aimed to place managerial decisions in the director's hands rather than the enterprise Party committee's, provoking disputes about the Party's role in enterprises.

Reform of labor relations was more limited. In 1986 new workers in state enterprises began to be hired under fixed-term contracts rather than as permanent employees, a break with the "iron rice bowl," but existing workers kept their security. A trial bankruptcy law was adopted in late 1986 and took effect in 1988 after debate; a handful of failing enterprises, including an explosives-equipment factory in Shenyang that was declared bankrupt in 1986, became symbolic early cases. In practice, local governments and banks usually found ways to rescue failing firms, because mass layoffs threatened social stability. The result was that state enterprises gained some autonomy and incentives without facing the full discipline of competition, a situation that left deeper restructuring to the 1990s (Chapter 74). Meanwhile, workers saw bonuses and new consumer goods but also growing concern that managers and officials were gaining more than they were.

Section 72.20

The Dual-Track System and the Changing Relationship Between Plan and Market

Dual-track pricing emerged as a practical way to introduce markets without abolishing the plan. From the early 1980s, state enterprises that fulfilled their plan quotas were allowed to sell above-quota output at negotiated or market prices, a policy gradually extended through the middle of the decade. The result was two prices for the same good: a low state-set price for planned allocation and a higher market price for the rest. A steel mill might deliver part of its output at the plan price to designated users and sell the remainder on the market at a much higher price. Over time the share of output sold at market prices grew, and the plan's share shrank.

The strengths of the arrangement were considerable. It allowed enterprises and consumers to respond to scarcity at the margin, encouraged production above plan, and avoided the immediate disruption of a sudden price change that might have bankrupted firms dependent on cheap inputs or left households unable to afford necessities. It gave existing beneficiaries of the plan time to adjust, which reduced political resistance. Economists later described this as "growing out of the plan." Planners could keep control of essential goods while the market allocated the rest.

The weaknesses were also serious. The gap between planned and market prices created large opportunities for arbitrage: anyone with access to goods at plan prices could resell them at the market price. Officials and enterprises with control over allocations, and traders with connections to them, profited. Popular resentment targeted "official profiteers" (guandao), middlemen with official ties who diverted scarce materials. The system also created incentives for firms to conceal output, for local governments to hoard supplies, and for administrators to demand payments for access. Over time, many economists and officials concluded that the dual track needed to be unified.

Historical Vocabulary
Dual-Track Pricing

Dual-track pricing (jiage shuangguizhi) was the coexistence of administratively set plan prices and market-determined prices for the same goods, especially industrial inputs such as steel, coal, and chemicals. It allowed gradual introduction of market forces but generated arbitrage, because buyers with access to plan-priced supplies could resell at market prices. The term is sometimes extended to the broader coexistence of plan and market in the reform economy. It matters because it explains both the growth of the 1980s and the corruption and inflationary pressure that accompanied it.

Cause and Effect

Dual-Track Pricing, Incentives, and Arbitrage

Causes

Planners feared that sudden price liberalization would bankrupt state firms and cause social unrest. Enterprises needed incentives to produce above quota. Ministries wanted to preserve planned supplies. Reform advocates wanted to introduce markets without a political break.

Transformation

From the early 1980s and especially after 1984, above-plan output could be sold at market-influenced prices, producing a two-price system for many industrial goods and some farm products.

Effects

Output above plan rose, market channels grew, and the share of the plan declined. At the same time, arbitrage, official profiteering, hoarding, and resentment grew, and inflationary pressure built as market prices pulled up planned ones.

Historical complexity

The system cannot be explained by a single cause or judged simply a success or failure. It was both a pragmatic bridge that reduced the cost of reform and a source of rent-seeking that undermined public trust. Its effects depended on enforcement, local conditions, and the particular goods involved.
Section 72.21

Banking, Credit, Fiscal Reform, and Investment Decisions

Before reform, China's financial system was thin. The People's Bank of China combined central and commercial banking and acted largely as a cashier for the plan. In 1983 the State Council designated the People's Bank as a central bank, and in 1984 the Industrial and Commercial Bank of China was established to take over commercial lending. The Agricultural Bank, Bank of China, and People's Construction Bank were restored or expanded as specialized banks, and the Bank of Communications was re-established in 1987. Trust and investment companies, urban and rural credit cooperatives, and, in a few cities, bond and share issues began to appear. In 1986, a few shares began to be traded over the counter in Shanghai, and in 1987 a Shenzhen bank sold shares to the public. These were tiny experiments compared with the later development of stock markets.

Fiscal reform also changed incentives. From 1980, provinces were allowed to retain a larger share of local revenue under "eating in separate kitchens" (fenzao chifan) arrangements, and in 1988 a system of fiscal contracting with provinces was extended. Local governments gained both resources and the incentive to promote local growth, but the central government's share of revenue declined. Government revenue as a proportion of national output fell sharply over the decade, by roughly half from a high level in 1978, limiting the center's ability to invest and stabilize.

Investment decisions were decentralized as local governments, ministries, and enterprises gained access to funds. At the same time, bank credit remained largely directed by plans and political demands, local bank branches were influenced by local governments, and interest rates were fixed at low levels. This produced recurrent bursts of "investment hunger." A surge in credit at the end of 1984 led to overheating, prompting retrenchment in 1985–1986; similar cycles followed. The tools of macroeconomic management, such as interest rates, reserve requirements, and open-market operations, were underdeveloped, so the government relied on administrative credit ceilings and investment approvals. Many officials believed that reform had made coordination harder rather than easier, a concern that fed the debates of 1988.

Section 72.22

Price Reform, Inflation, and the Economic Strains of 1988

Price reform was the hardest part of reform because prices were tied to the entire structure of subsidies and incomes. In 1985 the state decontrolled the prices of many agricultural products, including pork and vegetables in cities, and allowed prices of some manufactured goods to float. These steps improved supplies but caused visible price increases that troubled urban consumers. Official retail price increases were about 9 percent in 1985, 6 percent in 1986, and 7 percent in 1987, high by the standards of the earlier decades of the PRC, in which prices had been tightly controlled, although moderate by later global standards.

In early 1988 inflation accelerated. Credit expansion, wage and bonus increases, speculation in goods, and local investment all pushed up prices. In May, Deng Xiaoping was reported to have said that price reform was risky but unavoidable. At a leadership meeting at Beidaihe in August, the Politburo adopted a preliminary plan to accelerate price and wage reform over the next several years. News of the decision spread quickly, and many people feared a surge in prices. In late August, residents in many cities rushed to withdraw savings from banks and to buy consumer goods, such as televisions, appliances, cigarettes, and food, in what was widely described as panic buying. Official retail prices rose by about 18 percent over 1988, and the government's own figures understated increases in some cities and goods.

Leaders reversed course. In September 1988 the Third Plenum of the Thirteenth Central Committee resolved to "improve the economic environment and rectify the economic order," a program of retrenchment that cut investment, tightened credit, and postponed rapid price reform. Premier Li Peng and Vice Premier Yao Yilin were prominent in implementing it, while Zhao Ziyang, who had championed rapid reform, found his position weakened. The retrenchment slowed growth and caused difficulties for private and township enterprises, which relied on credit and on markets. The episode left a legacy: for officials, a lesson about the risks of price liberalization; for urban consumers, a sense that reform could threaten their livelihoods; and for economists, an argument about whether the speed and sequencing of reform had been mistaken.

Historical Debate

Gradualism versus Faster Market Liberalization

The question: Should China have liberalized prices and restructured enterprises faster, as some economists proposed, or was incremental reform the better choice?

Interpretation A — Gradualism was well suited to China. Economists such as Barry Naughton have argued that the dual-track and incremental approach allowed China to expand output without creating large numbers of losers, preserved political support, and let markets grow alongside the plan. Supporters note that the rapid growth of the decade and the absence of the collapse that affected some other socialist economies suggest a successful strategy.
Interpretation B — Faster, more coordinated reform was needed. Other economists, including Wu Jinglian, argued that partial liberalization without comprehensive reform of prices, ownership, and finance fostered corruption and instability. They favored a coordinated package that would unify prices and strengthen competition. Some reform advocates in 1988 believed a quick "breakthrough" in prices was safer than a long period of distorted incentives.

Evidence: Scholars compare the Chinese experience with Eastern European and Soviet reforms, using data on output, inflation, and employment, and analyses of the dual-track system. They disagree on whether differences were caused by strategy or by initial conditions, such as China's larger rural, labor-abundant economy and the persistence of Party control.

Why it matters: The debate has implications for how historians evaluate policy choices in 1988 and for later arguments over reform, including the role of state ownership and political institutions.

Student task: What kinds of evidence would show whether a faster reform could have worked in China in 1988? How do differences in initial conditions complicate comparisons with other countries?

Section 72.23

Winners, Losers, Corruption, and the Distributional Consequences of Reform

Reform produced gains for many, but not equally. In the countryside, early gains were broadly shared, but later gaps widened between regions and between those engaged in sidelines and enterprises and those relying on farming. In cities, many workers experienced rising wages and new goods but also inflation, uncertainty, and resentment over the gains of private traders and officials. Official surveys and independent studies suggest that income inequality, which had been low by international standards before reform, rose over the decade, especially in the later 1980s, and that the urban-rural gap and coastal-inland gap widened again after the early 1980s.

Corruption became a major public concern. The two-price system made access to goods a source of profit. Officials and relatives with connections to officials were able to obtain scarce supplies, loans, import licenses, or contracts. The Hainan car scandal and the activities of companies run by organs of the Party and government, or by relatives of senior officials, aroused public anger. A popular complaint held that "those who make bombs earn less than those who sell tea eggs" (zao yuanzidan de buru mai chayedan de), a saying that reflected the sense that incomes for intellectuals and skilled workers lagged behind those in trade. Official anti-corruption campaigns in the early 1980s and again later targeted some cases, but critics believed they were limited and selective.

Scholars debate the sources of the problem. Some interpret corruption as a transitional by-product of partial reform that would diminish with price unification and stronger markets. Others argue that its roots lay in the combination of economic liberalization with a political system that lacked independent courts, a free press, or competitive elections, so that officials who controlled resources faced few external checks. Both views draw on evidence from the 1980s and later decades. Students should note that perceptions of unfairness were as politically important as measured inequality, and that these perceptions would shape the grievances of 1989, to be studied in Chapter 73.

Division V · Sections 72.24–72.29

Education, Science, Culture, and Intellectual Change

Division purpose: to demonstrate that reform and opening transformed more than economic administration. Schools, scientific institutions, literature, intellectual discussion, and popular culture became important sites where Chinese citizens reconsidered earlier political experiences and explored possible futures. Disagreements occurred not only between supporters and opponents of reform, but also among reform advocates who differed on how far economic, cultural, and political change should go.

Section 72.24

Educational Expansion and Reform During the 1980s

Education was rebuilt after the Cultural Revolution. The national university entrance examination (gaokao) had been restored in 1977 (Chapter 71), and during the 1980s universities expanded, graduate programs resumed under degree regulations adopted in 1980, and specialized and vocational schools were promoted. Key schools, which concentrated resources on selected primary and secondary institutions, produced strong examination results but widened gaps between better and weaker schools. Radio and television universities and adult education opened paths for older learners. Teachers, who had been denounced in earlier campaigns, were formally honored: a national Teachers' Day was instituted in 1985, though salaries remained low compared with those in many other occupations.

In May 1985 the Party issued a Decision on the Reform of the Education System. It proposed shifting responsibility for basic education to local governments, giving universities more autonomy, expanding vocational education, and moving toward universal nine-year compulsory schooling. The decentralization of responsibility meant that wealthy localities could build better schools while poor townships and villages, which had to raise funds from local taxes and fees, struggled to pay teachers and maintain buildings. Many rural schools relied on community-supported (minban) teachers who were poorly paid. Dropout rates were significant in poor areas, especially among girls and in minority regions, as families faced new fees and the lure of paid work.

Literacy rose substantially. The 1982 census counted a large share of adults who were illiterate or semi-literate, but the proportion fell by the 1990 census, as younger cohorts completed more schooling. At the same time, quality differed widely and access to higher education was unequal. Universities admitted only a small fraction of the age group, and entrance depended on a single examination in which urban and key-school candidates had advantages. Graduates were assigned jobs by the state under a system that began to loosen in the mid-1980s as employers gained more say.

Section 72.25

Scientific Research, Technological Modernization, and the 863 Program

Science and technology were named among the Four Modernizations (Chapter 71), and the 1980s saw efforts to rebuild research and tie it to the economy. In March 1985 the Party issued a decision on the reform of the science and technology system. It sought to reduce reliance on administrative allocation of research funds, introduce contracts and competitive grants, treat technology as something that could be bought and sold, and encourage research institutes to cooperate with enterprises. Technology markets and technology contracts appeared, and researchers took on consulting work. Deng Xiaoping's statement in 1988 that science and technology were the "primary productive force" became a widely quoted slogan.

In March 1986 four senior scientists wrote to Deng, warning that China risked falling behind in new technologies. Deng approved a plan, and the State High-Technology Research and Development Program was launched; because of the month it began, it is known as the 863 Program. It identified priority areas such as biotechnology, space, information technology, lasers, automation, energy, and new materials, and funded projects in universities and institutes. Other initiatives followed, including the Spark Program (1986) to spread practical technology in rural areas and the Torch Program (1988) to foster high-technology industries. In Beijing, scientists and engineers started companies in an area near universities that later became known as Zhongguancun; firms such as Legend (later Lenovo, founded in 1984 by a group from the Chinese Academy of Sciences) began as spin-offs of research institutes. Large projects included the Beijing Electron–Positron Collider, completed in 1988, and a series of satellites and rockets.

There were limits. Many research institutes remained dependent on state funding and poorly connected to industry. Researchers were underpaid, and in the late 1980s, some left for business or for opportunities abroad. Imported technology often lacked support for assimilation. Foreign firms and joint ventures transferred some knowledge but controlled core designs. Basic scientific research made progress, but the economic payoff of many programs came later. Historians assess the decade as one of institutional rebuilding more than of breakthroughs.

Section 72.26

Students and Scholars Abroad and China's Intellectual Reconnection

After 1978, China sent students and scholars abroad on a scale not seen since the early twentieth century (Chapter 60). The first group of visiting scholars went to the United States in late 1978, following Deng Xiaoping's decision to expand study abroad, and exchanges widened after the normalization of relations with the United States in 1979. Government scholarships, university agreements, and programs such as a physics graduate program organized by Chinese and American physicists sent young people to universities in the United States, Japan, Western Europe, Canada, and Australia. Tens of thousands had left by the late 1980s, many of them in science and engineering, and a growing number went at their own expense. Many did not return, which prompted debates about the "brain drain." Official policy encouraged study abroad and welcomed return, though experiences of returnees varied.

Translation and publishing also reconnected China to world scholarship. Book series published translations of Western works in philosophy, economics, sociology, and the natural sciences, as well as Chinese and Japanese works on cultural history. Readers in universities and publishing houses discussed ideas ranging from existentialism to systems theory to Weber's sociology of religion. Foreign experts taught in Chinese institutions, and international conferences brought together Chinese and foreign scholars. In economics, the 1985 Bashan conference, a shipboard meeting on the Yangtze that included Chinese officials and economists and foreign economists such as János Kornai, introduced debates about socialist reform in Hungary and elsewhere.

The new exchanges raised familiar questions. Since the late nineteenth century, Chinese thinkers had argued about how to learn from the West while preserving Chinese identity (Chapter 54). In the 1980s these arguments returned: some advocated wholesale modernization along Western lines; others sought a distinctive Chinese path; and conservative officials warned of "spiritual pollution" and "bourgeois liberalization" (72.37). Chinese students abroad themselves were not uniform. Some were closely linked to their institutions at home and expected to return; others formed communities that debated China's political future.

Section 72.27

Literature, Historical Memory, and New Cultural Expression

Literature became a major medium for reconsidering the recent past. "Scar literature" (shanghen wenxue), which took its name from a 1978 short story, depicted the personal suffering of people during the Cultural Revolution; related "reflection literature" asked how the tragedy had been possible. Reportage by writers such as Liu Binyan exposed corruption and bureaucratic abuses. At a national writers' congress in late 1979, Deng Xiaoping's address promised that the Party would not issue administrative orders on literature and art, and a later congress, in late 1984 and early 1985, again emphasized freedom of creation. Yet the limits were uncertain: the criticism of a film script, Bitter Love, in 1981 and the Anti-Spiritual Pollution Campaign in 1983 showed that works deemed to question Party leadership or socialism could be attacked.

By the mid-1980s, writers were exploring new forms and themes. "Misty" poets such as Bei Dao, Shu Ting, and Gu Cheng wrote in a private, ambiguous idiom that departed from public political verse. "Root-seeking" (xungen) writers of 1985, including Han Shaogong and Ah Cheng, explored rural customs, local cultures, and the legacy of tradition. Mo Yan's novella Red Sorghum (1986) gave a vivid, unconventional account of rural resistance during the war against Japan. Younger experimental writers, including Can Xue, Yu Hua, and Su Tong, adopted fragmented structures and surreal imagery, influenced by translations of Latin American and European modernism. Women writers, such as Zhang Jie and Wang Anyi, wrote about love, work, and the constraints on women's lives. Literary journals and publishing houses became places of debate, and works that sold widely helped create a larger reading public.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Education and Intellectual Life Before and During Reform

What changed? Universities reopened and expanded, entrance examinations returned, foreign books were translated, and thousands went abroad. Writers could address the Cultural Revolution and other taboo subjects, and publications multiplied. Academic and cultural journals aired debates that would have been impossible in the 1970s.

What stayed the same? The Party retained authority over schools, publishing, and media; ideological campaigns recurred; the Four Cardinal Principles, announced in 1979, set limits on public discussion; and the state continued to organize the education system and control access to higher education.

Who experienced change most directly? Urban students, intellectuals, and writers experienced the largest shifts, as did returned scholars. Many rural youth gained access to basic schooling, but the opportunities for higher education remained limited.

Why does this pattern matter? It shows that reform-era openness was real but conditional, and that the boundary between permitted and forbidden discussion was negotiated, shifting with the political climate.

Section 72.28

Film, Television, Music, and the Expansion of Popular Culture

The cultural market expanded rapidly. Television, once rare, became common in urban homes and increasingly in rural ones, and by the mid-1980s provincial, city, and county stations joined the central broadcaster after a policy encouraged local stations. The televised Spring Festival gala, begun in 1983, became a national ritual. Serials such as Journey to the West (1986) and Dream of the Red Chamber (1987) drew vast audiences. Commercial advertising, which had reappeared on Shanghai television in 1979, accompanied the new media. Newspapers and magazines multiplied, and publishers produced martial arts novels, romances, and translated works, much of it for entertainment rather than ideology.

In film, a cohort of directors who had graduated from the Beijing Film Academy in 1982, called the Fifth Generation, produced films with striking visual style and unconventional storytelling. Yellow Earth (1984), directed by Chen Kaige with cinematography by Zhang Yimou, depicted peasant life in the northwest, and Zhang Yimou's Red Sorghum (1987) won the top prize at the Berlin International Film Festival in 1988. Hong Kong and Taiwanese popular music, including songs of the Taiwanese singer Teresa Teng, spread on cassette tapes, while "northwest wind" songs and early rock music emerged domestically. The singer Cui Jian performed "Nothing to My Name" in 1986, and it became associated with young people's feelings of alienation and desire for change. Dance halls, breakdancing, jeans, and long hair drew criticism from conservative officials as signs of foreign influence.

Regulation continued. Film and television were overseen by state bureaus; scripts were reviewed; publishing required licenses; and periodic campaigns targeted works judged politically or morally harmful. Commercial pressures were also growing as state subsidies fell and studios and stations sought revenue through ads and popular programs. Students should note that popular culture did not simply oppose official culture: officials and artists negotiated, and many works combined patriotic or educational themes with entertainment.

Section 72.29

Intellectual Debates over Modernization, Tradition, and Political Reform

Intellectual life in the mid-1980s was marked by what contemporaries called "culture fever" (wenhua re): wide-ranging debate about China's tradition, its backwardness or strengths, and the meaning of modernity. Participants included philosophers, literary scholars, scientists, economists, and journalists, and they debated in journals, translation series, and conferences. Some argued that Confucian tradition and a peasant political culture hindered modernization; others, such as scholars interested in a Chinese cultural renaissance, defended aspects of tradition or asked whether a different path was possible. Philosophers such as Li Zehou and literary scholar Liu Zaifu wrote about human subjectivity, and earlier debates about humanism and alienation, associated with the Party theorist Wang Ruoshui, were criticized in 1983. Historians reinterpreted the Qing and Republican periods in light of modernization theories.

Political thinkers argued over the relationship between economic reform and political change. Fang Lizhi, an astrophysicist, spoke in universities about academic freedom and democracy, which drew student audiences. Reform-minded officials and their advisers, in research institutes tied to Zhao Ziyang, studied political reform proposals such as separating Party and government. In 1988 a group of scholars advanced "neo-authoritarianism," the argument that a strong, modernizing leader might be needed to carry out market reform before democratization, while critics argued that it risked entrenching unchecked power. The six-part television documentary River Elegy, broadcast in 1988, argued that China's inward-looking continental civilization had held back modernization and drew huge audiences and controversy; it was later attacked by official critics.

These debates were not simply for or against reform. Some intellectuals favored market economics but feared political liberalization; others combined admiration for Western democracy with nationalism; others defended socialism but sought a more humane version; and still others focused on culture rather than politics. Their positions changed over the decade. The limits imposed in 1983, 1987, and later shaped how freely they could speak.

Section 72.30

Consumer Goods, Household Incomes, and Changing Everyday Life

In the 1970s, the most prized possessions in many urban households were the "three rounds" or "three big items": a bicycle, a wristwatch, and a sewing machine. During the 1980s the list shifted to television sets, refrigerators, and washing machines, along with radios, cassette players, and electric fans. Color television, almost absent early in the decade, became common in urban homes by the end of it, and ownership of refrigerators and washing machines rose more slowly. Cloth rationing ended in 1983, and shops stocked a wider range of clothing styles, including jeans, suits, and permed hairstyles. A fashion show by a French designer in Beijing in 1979 had been an early sign. Grain and some other rationing continued into the 1990s but mattered less as markets supplied more food.

Rural consumption changed as well. Farm families built new houses with brick and tile, bought bicycles, radios, sewing machines, and eventually televisions, and spent more on weddings and funerals. Diets improved, with more meat, eggs, and vegetables. But gaps persisted: urban per capita consumption was well above rural, and within both groups, richer households consumed far more than poorer ones. Household savings deposits grew rapidly, as families saved for housing, weddings, and emergencies in the absence of strong public welfare for the rural population.

Housing was a pressing problem. In cities, a shortage of dwellings forced multiple generations into small apartments assigned by work units. Experiments in housing reform from the mid-1980s, with limited sales of public housing and rent increases, began to change the system, and a national program was adopted in 1988, but most urban housing remained provided through work units. In the countryside, housing was privately built and funded.

Section 72.31

Migration, the Household Registration System, and the Emerging Floating Population

The household registration system (hukou), established in 1958, tied people to their place of birth and divided them into agricultural and non-agricultural categories, with access to grain rations, housing, and jobs in cities depending on non-agricultural registration. By restricting rural-to-urban migration, it had helped keep the rural population in agriculture. Reform weakened these restrictions without abolishing them. As agricultural labor was freed and markets and factories needed workers, millions of rural people sought work elsewhere.

Policy changed gradually. A 1984 central document and a State Council notice that year permitted peasants who ran businesses or worked in market towns to settle there if they provided their own grain, a step that recognized new possibilities for small towns. In 1985 the government introduced temporary residence permits for people living in cities without local registration and established national identity cards, which made it easier to track residents. Urban governments imposed fees, checks, and restrictions, and migrants without permits risked detention and repatriation. Terms for migrants included the "floating population" (liudong renkou) and, in some official language, "blind flow" (mangliu), implying disorder.

Migrants worked in construction, factories, domestic service, street trade, and sanitation, and many came from inland provinces such as Anhui, Sichuan, and Henan to coastal cities and Guangdong. Young women, called dagongmei ("working girls") in southern factories, formed a large part of the workforce in electronics, garments, and toys. In Beijing, communities from Wenzhou and elsewhere established neighborhoods of tailors and traders. Estimates of the floating population vary widely, but by the end of the decade it numbered in the tens of millions. Migrants lacked access to urban schools, housing, and social services, and the family separation that resulted, with children and elderly left in villages, would become a major issue in later years (Chapter 75).

Section 72.32

Women, Marriage, Family Life, and Population Policy

The Marriage Law of 1980, effective in 1981, replaced the 1950 law. It set minimum marriage ages at 22 for men and 20 for women, affirmed free choice of partner, monogamy, and equality, allowed divorce when mutual affection had broken down and mediation failed, and included a provision on family planning. Divorce, which had been rare, increased gradually over the decade. In the countryside, bride-price payments revived and, in some places, marriage involved large costs. Cases of abduction and trafficking of women, a serious problem in some areas, attracted official attention. Women's employment in the cities, protected in principle by state policy, was complicated by enterprise reforms, as managers focused on profit sometimes preferred male workers, and debates in the late 1980s about whether women should return to the home appeared in newspapers and among scholars. The All-China Women's Federation, an official organization, advocated for women's interests within Party limits.

Population policy was the most direct state intervention in family life. The 1982 census counted more than one billion people, and leaders feared that population growth would undermine economic goals. A stronger one-child policy, announced in 1979, was reinforced by an open letter from the Party Central Committee in September 1980 urging all couples, especially Party and Youth League members, to have only one child. In 1982 a Party document restated the policy but allowed exceptions, such as a second child where the first had a serious disability, and for some minorities. In 1983 a campaign involved a very large number of sterilizations, IUD insertions, and abortions. Rural resistance led to a 1984 adjustment that allowed provinces to make rules for second births in certain rural cases, notably when the first child was a girl, a compromise sometimes described as "opening a small hole." Enforcement relied on local cadres, who had targets to meet and could impose fines, withhold permits, or, in some cases, apply physical coercion. Reports of forced abortions and sterilizations, and of pressure that contributed to female infanticide and abandonment, drew criticism at home and abroad.

The policy's consequences were profound and are still debated. Fertility fell during the decade, though it had already fallen sharply in the 1970s under earlier campaigns promoting later marriage and longer intervals between births. Scholars disagree about how much of the decline can be attributed to the one-child rule rather than to earlier campaigns, urbanization, education, and economic change. The sex ratio at birth moved away from the usual biological range during the 1980s, reflecting a combination of son preference, selective abortion, and under-registration of girls. Many families faced fines, loss of employment, or pressure, while others, especially in cities, accepted one child as the norm. Enforcement and exemptions varied by place and by social group.

Section 72.33

Inequality, Regional Differences, and the Uneven Geography of Reform

Reform did not benefit all places equally. The early policy of allowing some regions to prosper first, which Deng Xiaoping had stated in the late 1970s and reiterated in the 1980s, concentrated opportunities on the coast, where SEZs, open cities, foreign investment, and export industries clustered. The Seventh Five-Year Plan (1986–1990) divided the country into eastern, central, and western belts, with the east expected to lead. By the end of the decade, per capita incomes in coastal provinces and big cities were well above those in interior and western provinces. Rural-urban gaps, which had narrowed during the first half of the decade as farm incomes rose, widened again.

The state responded to poverty with new programs. In 1986 the State Council set up a leading group for economic development in poor areas and designated national poor counties, many in mountainous, arid, and minority regions, for targeted support through loans, work-for-relief, and infrastructure. Results were mixed, with some areas gaining from roads and schools while others saw limited effect.

Minority and frontier regions had particular conditions. The 1984 Law on Regional Ethnic Autonomy set out the legal framework for autonomous regions, prefectures, and counties. In Tibet, central leaders, including Hu Yaobang after a visit in 1980, advocated policies of relaxation and development, and a 1984 central work forum on Tibet approved construction projects financed by the state and other provinces. In Xinjiang, economic expansion, the reconstitution of the Xinjiang Production and Construction Corps in 1981, and cross-border trade grew. These regions had lower incomes and fewer enterprises than the coast, and arguments over language, religion, migration, and political control were part of local life. Political tensions and protests in Lhasa in the late 1980s are addressed in Chapter 76 and Appendix J, where evidence and competing interpretations of these regions' modern history are presented.

Section 72.34

Public Health, Housing, Urbanization, and Environmental Pressures

The collapse of the commune system affected rural health. The cooperative medical system, in which communes and brigades financed clinics and "barefoot doctors," had provided basic care in many villages during the 1970s. After decollectivization, many villages could no longer fund it, and the share of villages covered fell sharply by the mid-1980s. In 1985 barefoot doctors were renamed village doctors and given new certification, and many practiced on a fee-for-service basis. Hospital reforms in the mid-1980s gave hospitals more autonomy and allowed them to earn revenue from services and drugs, while state subsidies declined as a share of costs. Life expectancy and infant survival continued to improve, but costs of care became a burden for poor rural families, and access differed between city and countryside. In early 1988, a hepatitis A epidemic in Shanghai, linked to contaminated shellfish, affected hundreds of thousands of people and illustrated both the strains of urban public health and the capacity of authorities to respond.

Urbanization accelerated. The share of the population living in cities and towns rose from under a fifth in 1978 to over a quarter by 1990, depending on definition. New cities were created, and in 1983 a policy of having cities administer surrounding counties extended urban government into rural areas. Small towns grew with TVEs, and large cities expanded as migrants and suburban industries arrived. Housing, transport, and urban services struggled to keep up.

The environmental costs of growth became visible. Coal supplied most of the nation's energy, causing air pollution in industrial cities. Rural industries, many without pollution controls, polluted rivers and soils. Arable land was lost to construction and brick kilns, prompting a 1986 Land Administration Law. A series of laws and agencies followed: a trial environmental law in 1979, a declaration in 1983 that environmental protection was a basic state policy, water and air pollution laws in 1984 and 1987, and the creation of a national environmental protection agency in 1988. Tree-planting campaigns, including a national voluntary tree-planting program beginning in 1981, tried to counter deforestation and erosion, and a devastating forest fire in the Daxing'anling region in 1987 killed many people and destroyed large areas of forest. Enforcement was weak and growth was often prioritized, an imbalance explored further in Chapter 75.

Section 72.35

Deng Xiaoping, Institutional Reform, and the Reorganization of Leadership

In August 1980 Deng Xiaoping delivered a major speech on reforming the system of Party and state leadership, criticizing the overconcentration of power, bureaucratism, patriarchal methods, and lifelong tenure in office. Over the next two years the top leadership changed. Zhao Ziyang replaced Hua Guofeng as premier in 1980; in June 1981 the Sixth Plenum of the Eleventh Central Committee adopted a resolution on Party history that condemned the Cultural Revolution and criticized Mao's late errors while affirming his role in the revolution and the continuing importance of Mao Zedong Thought; Hua resigned as Party chairman and Hu Yaobang took the post. Deng became chairman of the Party's Central Military Commission. In September 1982 the Twelfth Party Congress abolished the post of chairman, created a General Secretary (Hu Yaobang), and set up a Central Advisory Commission for senior veterans, chaired by Deng. Deng's opening speech introduced the formula of "building socialism with Chinese characteristics."

The Constitution of December 1982 replaced the 1978 text. It restored the office of state president, limited the tenure of top state officials to two consecutive terms, strengthened the National People's Congress and its Standing Committee, created a state Central Military Commission, and provided for township governments and village committees to replace communes. It also omitted the "four great freedoms" (speaking out freely, airing views fully, holding great debates, and writing big-character posters) that the 1975 and 1978 constitutions had included, on the ground that they had been abused. Its preamble reaffirmed Party leadership and the Four Cardinal Principles first set out in 1979.

Leadership renewal also involved a retirement system for veteran cadres, begun in 1982, and efforts to promote younger, better-educated officials; Party rectification from 1983 to 1987 aimed to remove supporters of the Cultural Revolution and to improve discipline. These changes reduced life tenure and brought new people to power, but they did not make the system a collective one: Deng, who left the Party Central Committee in 1987 while retaining the Party Central Military Commission chairmanship, continued to exercise decisive influence through that office and his revolutionary prestige. The relationship between formal offices and informal authority was a feature of reform politics.

Section 72.36

Hu Yaobang, Zhao Ziyang, and Debates over Political Reform

Hu Yaobang led the Party as General Secretary from 1982 to 1987. He pushed for the rehabilitation of people who had been persecuted in earlier campaigns, including many officials and intellectuals, backed younger cadres, spoke for more relaxed cultural policy, and traveled widely, including to Tibet. His informal style and willingness to challenge orthodoxy won admiration among many intellectuals and criticism from senior leaders who felt that he was too lenient toward "bourgeois liberalization." Zhao Ziyang, premier from 1980 to 1987, took the lead on economic reform and later became General Secretary. He supported the coastal strategy, enterprise reform, and a greater role for markets, and sponsored research institutes studying economic and political reform.

Political reform in the 1980s meant different things to different leaders. Deng's emphasis was on improving administrative efficiency, rejuvenating the ranks, and limiting the concentration of power in individuals, not on introducing competitive elections or an independent judiciary. Zhao and his advisers considered more ambitious steps: separating the Party from government administration, setting up a professional civil service, strengthening the legal system and the people's congresses, enhancing transparency and consultation, and allowing some social organizations. Conservative senior leaders, including Chen Yun and Peng Zhen, feared that such changes would weaken the Party.

The Thirteenth Party Congress (October–November 1987) brought these debates into the open. Zhao's report put forward the thesis that China was in the "primary stage of socialism," a long period during which commodity economy and varied forms of ownership were appropriate, and described the economic system as one in which the state regulates the market and the market guides enterprises. It proposed separating Party and government functions, introducing a civil service system, and increasing consultation and public supervision. Deng, who stepped down from the Central Committee, kept the chairmanship of the Central Military Commission, and later accounts, including recollections attributed to Zhao, state that the leadership agreed to consult him on major questions; the evidence for such an informal arrangement is not fully documented in public sources. The new Standing Committee of the Politburo included Zhao, Li Peng, Qiao Shi, Hu Qili, and Yao Yilin.

Section 72.37

Ideological Campaigns and the Limits of Intellectual Liberalization

Reform-era openness was accompanied by recurring campaigns against what the Party called harmful ideological influences. In March 1979 Deng had set out four principles that no reform could challenge: the socialist road, the people's democratic dictatorship, the leadership of the Party, and Marxism–Leninism–Mao Zedong Thought. Campaigns aimed to enforce these limits when critics seemed to cross them.

In October 1983, at a Party plenum, Deng and senior ideologists such as Hu Qiaomu and Deng Liqun denounced "spiritual pollution," a loose term covering Western-influenced writing, art, and behavior regarded as decadent, as well as humanist interpretations of Marxism. Writers and theorists such as Wang Ruoshui were criticized, and some publications were restricted. The campaign soon met resistance. Economic leaders, including Hu Yaobang and Zhao Ziyang, feared that it would frighten scientists, entrepreneurs, and foreign investors and insisted that the countryside and economic policy be left alone. It faded within a few months, but its effects on cultural life lingered.

A second campaign followed the student protests of December 1986. In January 1987, after Hu Yaobang's resignation, the Party launched a campaign against "bourgeois liberalization," a term referring to advocacy of Western-style democracy, rejection of Party leadership, and related ideas. Fang Lizhi, the writer Wang Ruowang, and the journalist Liu Binyan were expelled from the Party, and Fang lost his university post. Articles criticizing liberal ideas appeared widely. Zhao Ziyang, who became acting General Secretary, worked to confine the campaign and, by May, had shifted the emphasis back to economic reform. Intellectuals thus experienced the 1980s as a cycle of relaxation, tightening, and relaxation again, in which the boundaries were uncertain and depended on shifting alignments among top leaders.

Section 72.38

The Student Demonstrations of 1986–1987 and Hu Yaobang's Removal

In late 1986, discussion of political reform was in the air. Officials had debated it in the summer, and Fang Lizhi, then vice president of the University of Science and Technology of China in Hefei, gave speeches advocating academic freedom, criticizing authoritarianism, and arguing that democracy could not be granted from above. In early December, students in Hefei marched over procedures for elections to the local people's congress, and demonstrations spread. By mid-December protests had occurred in Wuhan and Nanjing, and in Shanghai tens of thousands of students and others marched in People's Square after several days of rallies; the city's mayor, Jiang Zemin, addressed students at one university. Protests followed in Beijing in late December, including a gathering at Tiananmen Square on New Year's Day. Participants called for democracy, freedom of the press, rule of law, and an end to bureaucratic privilege, and some complained about conditions on campus.

Local authorities responded with a mixture of dialogue, restrictions, and arrests, and Beijing issued rules requiring permission for demonstrations. Senior leaders, including Deng Xiaoping, Chen Yun, Peng Zhen, and Wang Zhen, blamed the unrest on lax ideological control and "bourgeois liberalization." At the end of December, Deng said that stability must be maintained and that leaders must take a firm line. On 16 January 1987, at an enlarged Politburo meeting, Hu Yaobang was criticized and offered his resignation as General Secretary, which was announced as voluntary. Zhao Ziyang became acting General Secretary, and was confirmed in the post at the Thirteenth Congress. Hu retained a seat on the Politburo until 1989.

The events of 1986–1987 matter for several reasons. They showed that students, intellectuals, and some urban residents were willing to demonstrate for political changes in the context of reform. They revealed that the senior leadership, though divided on economics, would act to protect the Party's authority. They removed a leader widely admired by intellectuals and left him with a symbolic status. And they set precedents for the protests of 1989, though students, causes, and leadership dynamics differed, a point Chapter 73 develops.

Historical Debate

Economic Reform and Political Liberalization

The question: Did economic reform in the 1980s create pressure for political liberalization, and why did the Party resist it?

Interpretation A — Economic change generated political demands. Some scholars, drawing on modernization theory and comparisons with Taiwan and South Korea, where political liberalization came in the late 1980s, argue that growing education, urbanization, and independent economic activity create demand for participation and accountability. They point to the 1986–1987 protests and the debates among intellectuals and officials as evidence.
Interpretation B — The Party could manage reform without political liberalization. Others argue that the Party retained support through rising incomes, preserved control over personnel, media, and security institutions, and used campaigns and selective reforms to limit challenges. They note that many entrepreneurs and officials depended on the state and that many citizens valued stability, and they view the protests as unusual episodes rather than indications of broad pressure.

Evidence: Both draw on party documents, press coverage, surveys, memoirs, and comparisons with other countries. They disagree on how to weigh protest events against evidence of continuing support for the system, and on how much the leaders' own disagreements shaped outcomes.

Why it matters: The interpretation shapes how historians understand the events of 1989 and the later development of Chinese politics.

Student task: What kinds of evidence would indicate whether political demands were widespread or confined to certain groups? Why is the comparison with other East Asian cases both useful and limited?

Section 72.39

Economic and Political Tensions at the End of the 1980s

By 1988 the strains described in earlier sections converged. Inflation approached 20 percent in official statistics and was felt more sharply in cities. People worried about the value of savings, and bank withdrawals and panic buying in the summer revealed distrust in the government's ability to manage prices. Anger at official profiteering and corruption was widespread, and reports of officials' relatives profiting from controlled goods circulated through the press and informal channels. Workers in state enterprises, teachers, and students who had modest incomes felt that others were gaining unfairly. The retrenchment that began in the autumn tightened credit and slowed growth, creating difficulties for enterprises and for workers in some localities.

Within the leadership, the failure of rapid price reform weakened Zhao Ziyang and strengthened those who favored a more cautious and planned approach, including Li Peng, Yao Yilin, and Chen Yun. Deng Xiaoping still supported reform but disagreed with some of Zhao's proposals and continued to emphasize stability. Political reform plans announced in 1987 had advanced only partially, and many intellectuals saw little progress. Debates about neo-authoritarianism and about the lessons of other countries continued. Students and teachers complained about poor campus conditions, low stipends and salaries, and uncertain job prospects, and student-run discussion groups and publications appeared on some campuses. Unrest in Tibet in 1987–1988 added to the leadership's concerns about stability, although the local issues involved should not be collapsed into the national disputes over inflation and urban reform. Chapter 76 revisits Tibet's longer-run political history.

The conditions at the end of 1988, then, were these: significant economic and social achievements; visible inequality and perceived unfairness; inflation and weakened confidence; unresolved disputes about the pace of price reform and the shape of political change; a leadership divided over strategy; and a population whose expectations had been raised by a decade of change. These were conditions, not causes that guaranteed a particular outcome. What happened in 1989 depended on events and decisions that Chapter 73 examines, and students should resist reading backward from the outcome as though it were inevitable.

Section 72.40

Chapter Review and 1980s Reform Checkpoint

The central synthesis question for this chapter is: How did China's reforms of the 1980s produce dramatic economic and social changes, and why did the same policies and institutions that supported modernization also generate inequalities, economic instability, and demands for political reform?

A strong answer connects the several histories the chapter has traced. Rural reform changed incentives and released labor, which fed the growth of township and village enterprises and of private business. Coastal opening and foreign investment linked these developments to international markets and to overseas Chinese capital. Urban reform proved harder because the plan and the market coexisted, creating dual-track pricing, corruption opportunities, and inflationary pressure. Education, science, literature, and popular culture widened what people could learn, say, and buy, but remained subject to Party regulation. Social change, including migration, the one-child policy, and unequal regional development, altered family life and opportunities unevenly. Political leaders disagreed about how far to go, and campaigns and protests showed the limits of change. By the end of 1988, the Party had preserved its monopoly on power while the economic and social system had changed far more than anyone in 1978 had foreseen, leaving tensions unresolved.

Students should compare agricultural, industrial, urban, and coastal development; identify the mechanisms behind economic growth; and assess the consequences of maintaining Communist Party political authority during major economic experimentation. They should also recognize that policy decisions and implementation differed and that experiences varied by region and class.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the 1980s as a decade of transformation in which the Communist Party pursued economic reform and international opening while keeping its monopoly on political authority. Rather than following a master plan, leaders sanctioned local experiments, tested them in selected places, and adjusted them in response to results and political resistance.

The Household Responsibility System spread from local experiments in poor areas to nearly universal practice by 1983. It left land under collective ownership but gave households contractual rights to farm and keep income after meeting obligations. The commune system was dismantled and replaced with township governments and village committees. Agricultural output and rural incomes grew strongly in the early 1980s, though the pace slowed after 1984 and the sources of the boom, including higher procurement prices, inputs, and improved incentives, remain a subject of scholarly debate.

Township and village enterprises, which evolved from commune and brigade enterprises, became a major source of employment and industrial output, followed by the growth of getihu and private businesses. They were not simply a stage toward private ownership: many were collective, tied to local governments, and difficult to classify. Regional models, including southern Jiangsu, Wenzhou, and the Pearl River Delta, show that institutions and geography shaped different paths.

The Special Economic Zones, established in 1980, and the opening of fourteen coastal cities in 1984 created spaces for foreign investment, joint ventures, and export processing. Hong Kong and overseas Chinese networks provided capital and expertise. The results were meaningful but uneven, and early problems of dependence on imports, smuggling, and scandal, as in Hainan, led to adjustments. International agreements on Hong Kong and Macao and the beginnings of cross-Strait contact tied economic opening to questions of sovereignty and identity.

Urban reform was harder. The 1984 Decision on Reform of the Economic Structure embraced a planned commodity economy, and enterprises gained some autonomy through profit retention, contracts, and director responsibility. Dual-track pricing allowed growth beyond the plan but created arbitrage and corruption. Banking and fiscal changes decentralized resources without developing tools for macroeconomic control. In 1988, price reform plans triggered panic buying and a retreat into retrenchment, with consequences for confidence and for debates about gradualism and faster liberalization.

Education, science, and culture experienced revival and reconnection. Schools and universities expanded, a nine-year compulsory education law was passed, high-technology programs were launched, students went abroad, and literature, film, and popular culture explored new themes. Intellectuals debated modernization, tradition, and political reform. At the same time, campaigns in 1983 and 1987 showed that the Party still defined the limits of acceptable expression.

Social life changed profoundly. Families acquired televisions, refrigerators, and washing machines; migrants moved to cities and factories under hukou restrictions; the Marriage Law and the one-child policy reshaped family decisions, with coercion in some places and varied enforcement; regional and rural-urban gaps reappeared; and health, housing, urbanization, and environmental pressures grew. The experience of change differed by gender, region, and household registration status.

Politically, the decade saw leadership renewal, the 1982 Constitution, a retirement system for senior cadres, and proposals for separating Party and government. Hu Yaobang and Zhao Ziyang advanced different parts of the reform agenda, while senior conservatives and Deng Xiaoping set limits. The 1986–1987 student demonstrations and Hu Yaobang's removal showed the boundaries of tolerance for demands for political change.

By the end of 1988 the achievements were significant but the tensions unresolved: inflation, corruption, inequality, leadership divisions, and a gap between rising expectations and political institutions. These conditions, not inevitable causes, formed the background to Chapter 73.

In the larger history of Chinese civilization, the 1980s mark a major reorientation from the planned, inward-looking economy of the Mao era to a hybrid system open to markets and the world. Students should see the decade as part of a long history of Chinese efforts to combine learning from abroad with domestic institutions (Chapters 54 and 60), and as a distinctive period within the history of the People's Republic, separate from the Mao era and from the market transformation of the 1990s.

Review Materials: Key Terms

Household Responsibility System
Arrangements under which rural households contracted to farm collectively owned land, delivered quotas, and kept the remainder.
Baogan daohu (dabaogan)
The "big contract," in which a household took responsibility for all production and kept what remained after taxes and quotas.
People's commune
Rural unit of production and local government from 1958 until its replacement by townships and villages in the early 1980s.
Collective ownership
Ownership of rural land by village or other collectives, not individuals or the state, in the reform era.
Unified purchase and sale
State monopoly on purchase and distribution of key farm goods, replaced by contract purchase in 1985.
Specialized household
A rural family concentrating on one product or service for market.
Township and village enterprise (TVE)
Rural non-agricultural firm owned or controlled by local governments, collectives, or individuals.
Sunan model
Southern Jiangsu pattern of collective, local-government-led rural industry.
Wenzhou model
Zhejiang pattern of household-based private enterprise and long-distance trade networks.
Getihu
Individual household business in a city or town; a legal category after 1981.
Red hat
Practice of registering a private business as collective to reduce political risk.
Special Economic Zone (SEZ)
Area with special regulations to attract foreign investment; first four established in 1980, Hainan added in 1988.
Coastal open city
One of fourteen port cities opened to foreign investment in 1984, with policies short of full SEZ status.
Joint venture
Enterprise combining Chinese and foreign capital under the 1979 law and later regulations.
Three supplies and one compensation
Processing arrangement in which foreign firms supplied materials, designs, and equipment and were compensated in goods.
Planned commodity economy
Formula from the 1984 Decision that accepted commodity exchange within a publicly owned, planned economy.
Contract responsibility system
Arrangement in which enterprise managers contracted to deliver fixed profit or taxes and kept the surplus.
Soft budget constraint
Condition in which an enterprise's losses are covered by the state, weakening discipline.
Dual-track pricing
Coexistence of plan prices and market prices for the same goods.
Guandao
"Official profiteering"; resale of scarce goods by officials or their associates at market prices.
Eating in separate kitchens
Fiscal arrangements from 1980 allowing provinces to retain a share of revenue.
863 Program
State High-Technology R&D Program launched in March 1986.
Compulsory Education Law
1986 law establishing nine years of schooling, implemented in stages.
Scar literature
Writing from 1977–1980s depicting suffering during the Cultural Revolution.
Root-seeking literature
Mid-1980s writing exploring rural and regional culture and tradition.
Culture fever
Mid-1980s wave of debate about tradition, modernity, and China's future.
Neo-authoritarianism
1988 argument that a strong leader might be needed to drive market reform before democratization.
Floating population
Migrants living or working outside their place of household registration.
Hukou
Household registration system, formalized in 1958, tying people to place and status.
One-child policy
Birth-limitation policy announced in 1979 and promoted by the 1980 open letter, with many local variations.
Marriage Law of 1980
Law setting marriage ages and affirming free choice, monogamy, and divorce on grounds of broken affection.
Spiritual pollution
1983 label for Western-influenced writing and ideas regarded as harmful.
Bourgeois liberalization
Label for advocacy of Western-style democracy and rejection of Party leadership, used in 1987.
Four Cardinal Principles
Limits on political debate set out in 1979: socialist road, people's democratic dictatorship, Party leadership, Marxism–Leninism–Mao Zedong Thought.
Primary stage of socialism
Thesis in the 1987 Party Congress report that China would long be in an early stage justifying market elements.

Investigating a Local Case of Reform

Choose a village, township, enterprise, or city that was affected by reform in the 1980s, such as Xiaogang, Shekou, a Wenzhou market, a southern Jiangsu township, or a state enterprise in the north.

  1. Define the case. State the place, period, and question, such as how contracting or enterprise reform affected local people.
  2. Gather primary sources. Use official documents, local reports, interviews or memoirs, newspapers, photographs, or statistics. Note how each was created and by whom.
  3. Gather secondary sources. Read scholarly studies, village or enterprise case studies, and general histories. Compare their arguments.
  4. Distinguish evidence types. Separate official statistics, personal testimony, later memory, and interpretation. Note where stories may have been shaped by later retelling.
  5. Write your findings. In a 4–6 page essay or equivalent presentation, explain what changed, what stayed the same, who gained or lost, and what the evidence cannot show. Use careful language.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has shown how a decade of reform reshaped the lives of hundreds of millions of people without a single blueprint. Farmers, village officials, entrepreneurs, factory managers, planners, investors, students, writers, and migrants all contributed to the changes, often moving faster than formal policy. The decade matters because it created the economic and social foundations for what followed in the 1990s and beyond, while also laying bare the contradictions of combining market change with continued Party control.

The chapter also shows how Chinese history in the reform era connects to wider histories: the export-led growth of East Asia, the reform of socialist economies, the global flows of capital, students, and goods, and the negotiations over Hong Kong, Macao, and cross-Strait contact. China was an active participant in these processes, designing zones, setting rules, and competing for investment, not simply responding to outside forces.

Finally, the chapter invites students to hold together achievement and cost, continuity and change. Rising incomes, new schools, and expanded choices coexisted with inflation, corruption, coercive population policy, and uneven opportunity. Chinese society in the 1980s was diverse, interconnected, creative, contested, and unfinished, and the unresolved tensions of 1988 lead directly into the next chapter, which examines the crisis of 1989 in its context.
