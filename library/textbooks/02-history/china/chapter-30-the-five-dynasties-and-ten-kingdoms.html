Part V — Sui, Tang, and the Post-Tang Transition

The Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

In the mid-tenth century, in the hill-ringed city of Hangzhou, a ruler named Qian Hongchu ordered tens of thousands of copies of a short Buddhist scroll printed from carved wooden blocks and sealed inside pagoda foundations, where they would lie undisturbed for nearly a thousand years. Roughly four hundred kilometers to the north, in the walled canal city of Kaifeng, a very different kind of ruler, an emperor named Chai Rong, was melting down bronze Buddha statues to mint coins, reorganizing his armies, and preparing campaigns to reunite the lands of the former Tang empire. Between these two rulers lay a political map with a dozen or more governments, each claiming authority, each taxing its own farmers, minting or forbidding its own coinage, and fielding its own soldiers. The Tang dynasty, which had ruled for nearly three centuries, was gone. Something new was coming, but no one living in either city could have known what it would be.

This chapter studies the decades between the extinction of the Tang dynasty in 907 and the founding of the Song dynasty in 960, together with the campaigns of reunification that followed. Traditional historians called this age the era of the Wudai shiguo (五代十国), the "Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms." The name preserves a genuine pattern: a rapid succession of five regimes, each claiming to be the legitimate imperial government, in the north, and a shifting collection of regional kingdoms, roughly ten in number by the conventional count, in the south and elsewhere. But the label also hides as much as it reveals. It suggests a tidy list where historians actually see a tangle of overlapping states with different origins, different institutions, and different fortunes; and its traditional framing as an age of chaos placed between two great unified dynasties has shaped, and often distorted, how later generations understood the period.

Why does this period matter? Because it is the place where one of the great transformations of Chinese history can be seen in the making. Chapter 29 showed how the An Lushan Rebellion transformed the Tang without destroying it: military governors, new fiscal methods, and a shifting economic balance from north to south reshaped the state over a century and a half. This chapter shows what happened when the transformed Tang finally failed. Political unity disappeared, but agriculture, commerce, Buddhist institutions, written administration, urban life, and elite culture did not disappear with it. In several regions, especially in the south, they expanded. Many of the institutions, practices, and resources that the Song dynasty would later inherit and reorganize (Chapters 31 through 34) were tested, altered, or created in the tenth century.

What changed? The most visible change was political. A single imperial court in Chang'an, however weakened, gave way to several competing centers of power: Kaifeng and Luoyang in the north, and Nanjing, Hangzhou, Chengdu, Fuzhou, Guangzhou, Changsha, and other courts in the south. The deeper change was in the balance between civilian and military authority, and between the north and the south. In the north, commanders backed by loyal armies repeatedly seized the throne. In the south, regional rulers, many of them former soldiers, built durable governments that combined military strength with civil administration, and presided over some of the most economically vibrant regions in the world at that time.

What continued? A great deal. Tang law codes, fiscal offices, official titles, examination practices in several regimes, tax registers, Buddhist monasteries, textual culture, and above all the idea that legitimate rule meant a single universal empire, all continued. Even rulers who controlled only a few prefectures dressed their courts in imperial ritual and wrote of the day when the empire would be restored. The northern regimes claimed to be the true heirs of the Tang or its predecessors; southern rulers styled themselves kings, and sometimes emperors, and looked toward restoration on their own terms.

Who was affected? Everyone, but not in the same way. Northern peasants endured armies crossing their fields and heavy requisitions to support soldiers. Southern farmers, in many areas, experienced comparatively stable rule and continuing expansion of cultivated land. Merchants in ports such as Guangzhou and Quanzhou found opportunities as well as dangers. Buddhist monks and nuns depended on the goodwill of local rulers, and at times, as under Chai Rong, suffered from state policy. Displaced northern elite families moved southward and found employment at southern courts. Soldiers, from generals to conscripted farmers, staffed the armies on which every regime depended. Non-Han peoples, such as the Shatuo Turks and the Khitans, were not merely outside threats but active participants in the political struggles of the period, and in the case of the Shatuo, founders of three of the five northern dynasties.

What evidence helps historians understand this topic? The sources are unusually varied and unusually contested. Official histories, in particular the Old History of the Five Dynasties compiled in the 970s and Ouyang Xiu's New History of the Five Dynasties written in the eleventh century, were composed after the Song had triumphed and reflect Song moral and political concerns. Sima Guang's great chronicle of the eleventh century preserves an immense narrative. Against these later texts, historians can place contemporary material: printed Buddhist scrolls from the Wuyue kingdom, manuscripts from the Dunhuang caves, tomb inscriptions, coins, temple records, and the surviving works of poets and painters. Learning to weigh these sources against each other is one of the central skills this chapter asks students to practice.

Finally, how does this chapter connect to the larger history of China, Asia, and the world? The tenth century saw simultaneous political transformations across eastern Eurasia: the rise of the Khitan state on the northern frontier, the emergence of independent Vietnamese rule in the south, the consolidation of Goryeo in Korea, and the weakening of the Abbasid caliphate in the west. The states of this chapter were connected to all of these. Their coasts were linked by maritime trade to Southeast Asia, the Indian Ocean, Korea, and Japan; their northern borders were shaped by Khitan power. Understanding the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms means seeing the tenth-century Chinese-speaking world not as a broken empire, but as a set of interacting regional societies within a wider Asian system.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Tang dynasty finally collapsed in 907, distinguishing causes that extended well beyond the An Lushan Rebellion from the immediate events of the Huang Chao rebellion and Zhu Wen's seizure of power.
  2. Identify the Five Dynasties as successive northern regimes and describe the military, political, and geopolitical conditions that made dynastic change so rapid.
  3. Describe the Ten Kingdoms as a conventional retrospective label rather than a simultaneous set of identical states, and explain the different trajectories of the lower Yangtze, Fujian, Sichuan, Hunan, and the far south.
  4. Compare regional political systems, distinguishing regimes that relied heavily on military households from those that developed stronger civil administration.
  5. Analyze why armies remained politically decisive in this period and why some military regimes proved ephemeral while others built durable institutions.
  6. Trace the agricultural, commercial, and demographic foundations of southern economic development, while distinguishing them from the later Song commercial revolution.
  7. Evaluate evidence for cultural creativity in tenth-century cities, courts, and monasteries despite political fragmentation.
  8. Assess the role of printing and administrative continuity in preserving and transmitting knowledge across the Tang–Song transition.
  9. Explain the conditions that made Song reunification possible after more than half a century of division.
  10. Interpret a primary source produced by a successor regime and identify its perspective, purpose, and limits.
  11. Evaluate competing historical interpretations of the period as an age of disorder or of regional transformation.
  12. Distinguish political fragmentation from cultural or economic collapse, and continuity through rupture from mere survival.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did the Tang dynasty fall in 907, and why is it misleading to trace that fall solely to the An Lushan Rebellion of the 750s?
  2. Why did northern dynasties change so rapidly, while some southern regimes lasted for decades?
  3. In what senses was the period an age of fragmentation, and in what senses was it an age of continuity or even growth?
  4. Which Tang institutions survived 907, which were altered, and which disappeared?
  5. How did military commanders come to dominate political life, and what limited their ability to build lasting states?
  6. Why did the south, once regarded as a periphery, become an increasingly central economic and cultural region?
  7. What does the period reveal about the relationship between political unity and cultural or economic vitality?
  8. How did non-Han peoples, especially the Shatuo and Khitans, shape the political history of the tenth century?
  9. What conditions made Song reunification possible, and why was it not inevitable?
  10. How should historians use later Song-era histories of a period that the Song itself had ended?

Historical Context

Chapter 25 traced the founding of the Tang in 618 and its construction of one of the most powerful states of medieval Eurasia. Chapter 26 showed how its government, its equal-field landholding, its examination system, and its cosmopolitan capital at Chang'an supported a large agrarian empire, and Chapter 27 followed its connections across Central Asia, Korea, Japan, Tibet, and the Indian Ocean. Chapter 29 then examined the crisis that began in 755 with An Lushan's rebellion. The Tang survived that upheaval, but it emerged as a different state. Military governors (jiedushi) controlled large regions with their own armies; the equal-field system and the original tax system had been replaced by the Two-Tax reform of 780 and by increasingly important salt and other monopoly revenues; and the great fiscal weight of the empire had shifted toward the lower Yangtze and the Huai valley, whose grain and taxes moved north along the Grand Canal (Chapter 24).

This chapter begins where Chapter 29 stopped. Chapter 29 deliberately did not narrate the last phase of Tang history; it left students with a Tang that was transformed but still functioning. That functioning was more fragile than it looked. The Hebei region north of the Yellow River was governed by semi-autonomous military governors who appointed their own successors and often kept much of the local revenue. The court in Chang'an increasingly relied on a small number of revenue-producing regions, chiefly in the Yangtze and Huai valleys, and on the salt monopoly, which was profitable enough to encourage large-scale smuggling. Palace eunuchs controlled the Army of Divine Strategy and sometimes the choice of emperors. Wars on the southwestern frontier against Nanzhao in the 850s through 870s drained the treasury and strained soldiers who were sent to distant garrisons.

Geography shaped the story. The Central Plain (Zhongyuan), along the middle Yellow River from Luoyang to Kaifeng, was open, flat, densely farmed, and exposed to cavalry, which made it easy to fight over and hard to defend. The northwestern heartland of the Tang, the Guanzhong basin around Chang'an, was more defensible but had lost its economic primacy, since its own harvests could no longer feed the capital without grain shipped from the southeast. The Yangtze valley and the coastal south offered rivers, lakes, and mountains that favored defensive warfare with infantry and naval forces, and their rice-growing economies were productive and growing. Sichuan, ringed by mountains and reached only by a few difficult routes, was a natural refuge and a natural kingdom. Students should compare this chapter's regional map with the environmental regions of Chapter 2.

Politically, the world of 907 was not only a Chinese-speaking one. The Khitan people on the northern steppe frontier were consolidating under a leader named Yelü Abaoji; the Shatuo Turks, who had served the Tang as cavalry and settled in Shanxi, would soon build a major military power; Tibetan and Uyghur states had declined, leaving the Gansu corridor governed by local Chinese and Central Asian communities; and Nanzhao in Yunnan was about to give way to successor states. Students should not read the name "Five Dynasties" as implying that these northern states were purely "Chinese" in the sense of ethnic origin. Ethnic and cultural categories in this period were fluid: military elites in the north included men of Turkic, Sogdian, Han, and mixed ancestry, and political loyalty followed personal ties, patronage, and military success much more than descent.

The historical stakes are considerable. The period is often taught as the "gap" between Tang and Song. But the transition from Tang to Song is one of the most discussed transformations in East Asian history, sometimes referred to by historians as the Tang–Song transition: a shift from a more aristocratic, north-centered, military-influenced society to a more bureaucratic, commercialized, south-centered one. The tenth century is where many of the pieces of that shift were put in place. This chapter asks students to see the age not as a pause, but as a laboratory. It also prepares the ground for Chapters 31 through 34, which will examine the Song state, economy, technology, and intellectual life, while carefully leaving to those chapters the full analysis of the topics they own.

Section 30.1

The Final Collapse of Tang Rule

The Tang did not collapse in 907 because of a single blow. The date marks the moment when the last emperor was deposed, but the underlying authority of the court had been eroded over several generations, and the decisive damage occurred in the last third of the ninth century. Students should therefore treat 907 as the end of a long political collapse, not as the outcome of the An Lushan Rebellion alone. The events of the 750s had created a decentralized structure of military governorships, but that structure had proved workable for a century. What broke it were the pressures of the 860s through the 880s: fiscal stress, regional rivalries, social distress, and above all a rebellion that was national in scale.

Several long-term strains had accumulated. First, the fiscal base was narrow. The court depended heavily on revenue from a handful of regions, especially the Yangtze and Huai valleys, and on monopoly income from salt, which was taxed heavily at the source. High salt prices created a strong incentive for smuggling, and armed salt smugglers, organized in networks with their own boats, guards, and local protection, became a significant social force, especially in the Huai and Shandong regions. Second, the court's control over the provinces was uneven. In the Hebei circuits, military governors passed their positions to sons or chosen successors, kept local revenue, and offered the court only nominal loyalty. Elsewhere governors were appointed by the court but often relied on their own household troops, the yajun (牙军), who could mutiny and dictate a governor's successor. Third, the court itself was divided. Palace eunuchs controlled elite palace armies and sometimes decided who would be emperor, while scholar-officials, drawn largely from the examination system and from aristocratic families, struggled against them and among themselves. The bloody Ganlu Incident of 835, in which an attempt to destroy the eunuchs ended in a massacre of officials, showed how deep those divisions ran.

Frontier wars and military mutinies added to the strain. Campaigns against Nanzhao in the southwest and in Annam (northern Vietnam) in the 850s through 870s required soldiers to be sent to distant, unhealthy garrisons and required heavy supplies. In 868, a group of soldiers from the Xuzhou region who had been held on garrison duty in Guilin beyond their promised term mutinied under a leader named Pang Xun, marched back north, and for over a year defied the government before being crushed. The episode revealed how thinly loyalty was stretched among rank-and-file soldiers, and how easily a local grievance about pay or service could become a regional rebellion.

Environmental and social pressures made things worse. In 873 and 874, floods and drought struck parts of the Yellow River and Huai regions, producing famine and a wave of desperate migration. Tax collection continued, and the court's response was inadequate. In these conditions, in 874, a salt smuggler named Wang Xianzhi launched a rebellion in Henan and Shandong. Huang Chao, who came from a family of salt merchants in Shandong and had failed the civil service examination, joined him in 875. After Wang was killed in 878, Huang Chao took command of the remaining rebels and began a march that would change the course of Chinese history.

The Huang Chao rebellion was unusual in its range. Rather than remaining in one region, Huang's forces moved across much of the empire, crossing the Yangtze, moving through Zhejiang, Fujian, and Jiangxi, and in 879 reaching Guangzhou, the great southern port. The sack of Guangzhou killed many inhabitants and destroyed the foreign merchant community there; medieval Arabic sources record huge death tolls, though historians treat the specific figures with caution, and agree only that the episode was a catastrophe for the port and for maritime trade. Huang Chao's army then turned north, crossing the country a second time, and in late 880 took Luoyang and then Chang'an. Emperor Xizong fled to Chengdu, and Huang Chao proclaimed himself emperor of a new state called Qi. Rebels occupied the capital, and the great city was heavily damaged by fighting, looting, and violence, so much so that the Tang capital never fully recovered its earlier splendor.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Jiedushi (节度使), "military governor"

The jiedushi was originally a Tang frontier commander given authority over troops and supplies across several prefectures. After 755, the office spread to interior regions and became the standard form of regional government: a single man often controlled military, fiscal, and civil administration across a circuit, appointed his own subordinates, and commanded household troops loyal to him personally. Not all military governors were rebels; many governed effectively and remained loyal to the court. But by the late ninth century, many governorships had become hereditary or effectively autonomous, and the term describes a spectrum from loyal appointee to independent warlord. Most of the founders of the Five Dynasties and of the southern kingdoms began their careers as military governors, which is why the office is central to understanding both the Tang collapse and the successor regimes.

The Tang did not survive by its own strength. It survived, in nominal form, because military governors who had stayed loyal, or who saw an opportunity, assembled armies to fight the rebels. Among them were Li Keyong, the Shatuo commander from Shanxi, whose cavalry played a large part in retaking Chang'an in 883, and Zhu Wen, who had begun as a lieutenant of Huang Chao, defected to the Tang in 882, and was appointed military governor of Xuanwu, with his seat at Bianzhou (modern Kaifeng). Huang Chao's forces were driven from Chang'an, harassed, and defeated; Huang Chao died in 884, by tradition a suicide, though accounts differ. The rebellion was over, but the men who had suppressed it were now the true powers in the land. The court returned to a ruined capital and depended on them for survival.

The next two decades were a struggle among these regional strongmen, with a weakened court as the prize. Li Keyong and Zhu Wen became bitter rivals. Others carved out regional bases that would become successor states: Yang Xingmi in the Huai and lower Yangtze region (the origin of the kingdom of Wu), Qian Liu in Zhejiang, Wang Jian in Sichuan, Wang Shenzhi in Fujian, and Liu Yin in Guangdong. The court under Emperor Zhaozong, who reigned from 888, attempted to reassert authority, but each attempt made it more dependent on one warlord or another. By the early 900s, Zhu Wen dominated the Central Plain. In 903 he had eunuchs massacred, ending their control of the court. In 904 he compelled the emperor to move from Chang'an to Luoyang, forcing the population to move with him and having many buildings dismantled, effectively abandoning the old capital, and then had Zhaozong murdered. In 905 he had a group of senior officials, many from prominent families, killed at Baima Station and their bodies thrown into the Yellow River, an act that destroyed a generation of aristocratic officials. Finally, in 907, he forced the boy emperor, Ai, to abdicate, and founded his own dynasty, the Later Liang, with its capital at Kaifeng.

Students should notice what this account does and does not explain. It does not treat Zhu Wen as a lone villain, though later Song historians did depict him as one. It shows a regional commander with a large army, control of the canal junction at Kaifeng, and the will to eliminate rivals, operating in a political system where the throne had become a prize and legitimacy could be conferred by an abdication ritual. It also shows that the collapse of the Tang was a political and military process, involving rebellions, regional rivalries, palace politics, fiscal weakness, and social distress. By 907 the Tang had ceased to be a functioning central government for a generation. The abdication simply confirmed it.

Section 30.2

Northern Military Dynasties

The five northern regimes that succeeded one another between 907 and 960 are the Later Liang (907–923), the Later Tang (923–936), the Later Jin (936–947), the Later Han (947–951), and the Later Zhou (951–960). The prefix "Later" was added by historians to distinguish them from earlier dynasties bearing the same names, and it signals a self-conscious claim: each regime presented itself as continuing or restoring an older legitimate line. The Later Tang claimed the Tang inheritance directly; the Later Jin, Later Han, and Later Zhou reached back to the Jin, Han, and Zhou dynasties of earlier centuries, in some cases through claimed ancestry. These were not simply warlord gangs; they were governments that issued reign titles, held court ceremonies, appointed officials, and wrote themselves into the succession of legitimate dynasties.

The Later Liang, under Zhu Wen and, after 912, his sons, controlled the Central Plain from Kaifeng, and for a time appeared the strongest power in the land. It was undermined by internal conflicts, including the murder of Zhu Wen by his own son, and by the resilience of its main enemy, the Shatuo power in Shanxi. Li Keyong's son Li Cunxu, a talented and ambitious military commander, expanded that power, absorbed rival territories including the Hebei region, and in 923 proclaimed the Later Tang. In the same year his forces captured Kaifeng and destroyed the Later Liang. Li Cunxu moved the capital to Luoyang, restoring in some sense the old eastern capital of the Tang. But his court was troubled by conflicts between his military companions and the civil officials, by financial problems, and by his own neglect of governing, and in 926 he was killed in a mutiny. He was followed by his adoptive brother Li Siyuan (Emperor Mingzong), who reigned effectively from 926 to 933 and is often regarded as one of the more capable rulers of the period, lowering some taxes and appointing capable civil officials. After his death, however, struggles over the succession led to the rise of Shi Jingtang.

Shi Jingtang, a Shatuo military governor of Hedong, rebelled against the last Later Tang ruler in 936 and requested help from the Khitans. Yelü Deguang, the Khitan ruler, aided him, and Shi founded the Later Jin. As payment, Shi ceded to the Khitans the Sixteen Prefectures, a strip of territory on the northern edge of the plain including the area around modern Beijing and Datong, and accepted a subordinate ritual position toward the Khitan ruler, who was styled his "father." This cession was later remembered as a national disaster, since it gave the Khitans control of the passes and the mountain barriers that had protected the plain from steppe cavalry, and it shaped Song strategic anxieties for the next century (Chapters 31 and 35). Students should understand it in its contemporary context, however: Shi Jingtang was a usurper who needed military support, and the loss of territory was the price for survival. Whether contemporaries thought of the cession as a betrayal of a unified "China" is a very different question from how later historians used it.

The relationship broke down under Shi Jingtang's successor, Shi Chonggui, who resisted Khitan demands, and in 946 and 947 Khitan armies invaded, captured Kaifeng, and carried off the last Jin emperor. Khitan ruler Yelü Deguang even briefly proclaimed the establishment of a new dynasty in the city under the name "Great Liao" and in Chinese imperial style, before retreating north in the face of local resistance and supply problems, and dying on the way. The episode reveals something important: the Central Plain was militarily vulnerable, and the border between steppe and plain was a zone of competing state ambitions. The Khitans could take Kaifeng but could not easily hold it, and the northern regimes could not easily hold the frontier.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Zhengtong (正统), "legitimate succession"

Zhengtong refers to the idea that political legitimacy passes in an orderly line from one dynasty to the next, so that at any given time there is one true ruling house. The concept gave rulers strong reasons to claim the imperial title, to build ancestral temples for their dynastic lines, to adopt the dynastic ritual calendar, and to receive formal abdications from predecessors. It also shaped how historians later organized the past: the "Five Dynasties" are the northern regimes because Song historians, who wanted the Song to be the legitimate heir of the Later Zhou, placed them in the main line, while the southern states were classed as regional kingdoms outside it. This is not a neutral list. It is a judgment about legitimacy, made after the fact, which is why modern historians treat both the "five" and the "ten" as conventions rather than as natural categories.

The Later Han, founded by the Shatuo commander Liu Zhiyuan in 947 after the Khitans withdrew, lasted slightly under four years. Its second ruler tried to eliminate powerful generals, including Guo Wei, who instead marched on the capital, killed the emperor's supporters, and in 951 took the throne as the first emperor of the Later Zhou. A member of the Liu family established a rump state, the Northern Han, in Shanxi, which survived until 979 with Khitan protection and is counted conventionally among the Ten Kingdoms. Guo Wei, who came from a poor background and had risen through military service, was an unusually careful ruler: he reduced some of the most burdensome taxes, restrained abuses by officials and soldiers, and encouraged agriculture. On his death in 954, the throne passed to his adopted son, Chai Rong, who is often considered the most capable ruler of the northern sequence. Chai Rong is discussed further in Section 30.9.

Why did dynasties change so quickly? Several reasons recur. First, the regimes rested on personal military coalitions, and loyalty was owed to a commander, not to an institution. When the commander died, or weakened, other commanders with their own troops could and did challenge the succession. Second, the Central Plain was open and difficult to defend, so every ruler needed a large army, and every large army was a potential source of rebellion. Third, the regimes were repeatedly threatened by frontier powers, especially the Khitans, which forced them to keep large forces on the border. Fourth, the fiscal base was narrow: supporting armies drew heavily on the farmers of the Central Plain, and rulers who taxed too little could not pay their troops, while rulers who taxed too much provoked unrest. Fifth, ritual and ideological legitimacy were thin: since every dynasty had itself come to power by force, it was hard to argue that rebellion was illegitimate, and the very ease of dynastic change encouraged more of it.

At the same time, students should be careful about the phrase "military dynasties." The northern regimes were military in origin, but they were also governments. They maintained bureaucracies, held examinations irregularly, compiled laws, collected taxes, minted coins, and celebrated court rituals. Civil officials such as Feng Dao served regime after regime, and their continuity gave the governments a degree of institutional stability that the rapid succession of rulers obscures. The distinction between the ruling household, which changed often, and the administrative machinery, which changed much less, is one of the most important insights for understanding the period.

Section 30.3

The Ten Kingdoms of the South

The conventional list of the Ten Kingdoms names Wu, Southern Tang, Wuyue, Min, Chu, Southern Han, Former Shu, Later Shu, Jingnan (also called Nanping), and the Northern Han in the north. Students should keep in mind that this is a retrospective classification, not a description of ten simultaneous, equivalent states. The kingdoms differed greatly in size, longevity, wealth, and political style. Some, such as Wuyue, lasted seven decades; others, such as Min or Chu, fell within a few decades to civil conflict or invasion. Some overlapped chronologically and some succeeded one another: Southern Tang, for instance, replaced Wu in 937. Other small regimes and autonomous authorities are not included in the conventional count, including the territories around Dunhuang and Dali in the west and southwest, or Khitan-influenced northern zones. The number "ten" is a convenience, not a fact of nature.

The south should not be seen simply as territory waiting to be reconquered, or as a passive backdrop to northern events. Southern regimes built durable governments, patronized learning and religion, supported cities and commerce, and in some cases conducted foreign relations with states across Asia. This is particularly striking because for most of earlier Chinese history the political initiative had come from the north. Chapters 20 and 21 showed the south's development under the Eastern Jin and Southern Dynasties; Chapters 26 and 29 showed its growing fiscal importance under the Tang. In the tenth century, the south became the home of independent states with their own capitals, courts, and policies.

The lower Yangtze region produced two significant states. The kingdom of Wu was established by Yang Xingmi, a soldier of peasant origin who was appointed a military governor in the Huai region and gradually consolidated power; his state, ruling from Yangzhou and later Jinling, controlled the fertile Huai and lower Yangtze plains. In 937, a powerful minister named Xu Zhigao, who took the name Li Bian and claimed descent from the Tang imperial house, removed the Wu ruler and founded the Southern Tang. Southern Tang, with its capital at Jinling (modern Nanjing), became the largest and culturally most ambitious southern state. At its height in the 940s and 950s it controlled much of the Yangtze valley, including the former Min and Chu territories for a period, and it saw itself as the true heir of the Tang, with the resources to eventually reunite the empire. Defeats at the hands of the Later Zhou in 956 through 958, in which it lost the Huai lands north of the Yangtze, reduced it to a defensive kingdom, and its ruler Li Jing relinquished the imperial title. In 961 Li Jing briefly moved his court to Hongzhou (modern Nanchang); after his death that year, his successor Li Yu returned the court to Jinling. Li Yu, remembered as a great poet, ruled until the Song conquered the state in 975.

Wuyue, founded by Qian Liu in 907 and lasting until 978, occupied the coast around Hangzhou Bay, including much of modern Zhejiang and parts of Jiangsu and Fujian. Qian Liu, a former salt smuggler and soldier, was appointed military governor by the Tang, and built his state carefully. Wuyue's rulers gave a great deal of attention to water management: they built sea walls to hold back the tidal bore of the Qiantang River, maintained irrigation channels, and organized labor units for dredging waterways and lakes. They kept peace with more powerful neighbors, including the northern dynasties, whom they recognized as nominal overlords, and they avoided large-scale wars. Their court was a major patron of Buddhism, and the state became a center of religious building and printing. When the Song had conquered nearly all other regimes, Qian Chu, the last ruler, in 978 chose to submit peacefully, sparing the region from a war of conquest, a decision that later Song and Chinese tradition praised.

Fujian, in the southeast coast region, was ruled by the kingdom of Min (909–945), founded by the brothers Wang Chao and Wang Shenzhi, who came from Henan and moved south with a military force. Min's court, based at Fuzhou, welcomed northern refugees, promoted schooling, and supported Buddhism. It also encouraged maritime trade through Fuzhou and other ports. After Wang Shenzhi's death, Min fell into bitter succession struggles, the state divided, and in 945 the Southern Tang conquered its remnants. Yet the region's later history shows the importance of what had been built: parts of southern Fujian, around Quanzhou, remained under local military rulers who submitted to the Song in 978, and Quanzhou's rise as a major port in the Song owed much to the commercial development of this period (Chapter 37).

Sichuan, protected by mountains, was governed by two successive states. The Former Shu (907–925) was founded by Wang Jian, a former soldier who had escorted Emperor Xizong to Chengdu in the 880s and remained to build a power base. It was a wealthy and cultured court that attracted refugees and scholars from the north. After the Later Tang conquered it in 925, its governor Meng Zhixiang re-established independence and founded the Later Shu (934–965), ruled by his son Meng Chang. Both Shu states were known for their court culture, poetry, painting, and use of printing. They were also militarily weak, and the Song conquered the Later Shu in 965, adding Sichuan's revenue and resources to the Song state.

Farther south, Chu (927–951) occupied Hunan, ruled from Changsha by Ma Yin and his sons; it was notable for its tea trade with the north and its use of iron and lead coins, and fell to internal conflict and outside conquest. The Southern Han (917–971), based at Guangzhou, controlled Guangdong and Guangxi, and profited from trade with Southeast Asia and the Indian Ocean; it was ruled by the Liu family and was known for its highly militarized state, its use of palace eunuchs, and its pearl fishing. Jingnan, a small state on the middle Yangtze around Jingzhou, survived by playing larger states against each other until the Song absorbed it in 963.

The result was a set of regional societies that followed distinct paths. Southern Tang looked to the Tang inheritance and to reconquest; Wuyue emphasized stability, water control, and religious patronage; Min and Southern Han emphasized maritime connections; Sichuan combined wealth and isolation; Chu and Jingnan lived by trade and diplomatic maneuver. Students should also remember that these regimes were not permanent or inevitable, and that most of them were weakened by internal struggles for succession, just as the northern dynasties were. But they were also real states with lasting effects: the regional identities, commercial networks, and institutional practices they developed outlived them.

Section 30.4

Regional Political Systems

If the phrase "warlord chaos" were accurate, one would expect to find little variation among the tenth-century regimes: each would be a military camp, and each ruler a commander. What historians find instead is significant variety in how regional states were organized. Some regimes relied heavily on military households and commanders; others built stronger civil bureaucracies, fiscal offices, and court institutions, and developed working relationships with local elites. Comparing them shows both the reach of Tang institutional inheritance and the range of creative adaptations.

Nearly every regime inherited the basic Tang administrative vocabulary. They kept prefectures (zhou) and counties (xian) as units of local government, used Tang official titles and ranks, maintained tax registers, and issued edicts in the familiar forms. The Tang legal code and its commentaries remained the basis for law: successor regimes issued revised statutes and penal compilations, such as the Later Zhou's Da Zhou xingtong of 956, but did not abandon the Tang framework. Many officials moved between regimes, bringing their administrative experience with them. In the south, where the Tang collapse had produced independent states, the continuation of Tang forms was a deliberate assertion of legitimacy: a southern ruler who could show that his administration ran on Tang lines claimed to preserve civilization.

In the ninth and tenth centuries, military leaders often "adopted" promising officers and soldiers, creating a fictive family bond that combined patronage, loyalty, and command. Li Keyong had a number of famous adopted sons who served as commanders, and Li Siyuan, who became the Later Tang emperor Mingzong, was himself an adopted son of Li Keyong; Chai Rong was an adopted heir to Guo Wei. The practice offered a way to bind followers to a leader in a society where formal institutions were weak, but it also created unstable succession, since adopted sons and biological sons could each claim the leader's legacy. Students should read the term as evidence of how personal ties and military ties shaped political structure, and not assume that adoption in this context was primarily a domestic or inheritance arrangement.

Centrally, the northern courts developed some new institutions in response to their circumstances. The Bureau of Military Affairs (Shumiyuan), which had been controlled by eunuchs in the late Tang, was reorganized and staffed by trusted confidants of the ruler, and became a central organ for military planning and secret policy; the Later Liang created a separate office, the Chongzheng yuan, for a similar purpose, and later regimes restored a Shumiyuan under military and civil officials. The Three Finance Bureau (Sansi), which concentrated control of taxation, salt, iron, and grain supply, developed in the Later Tang, and the Song would inherit and expand it. These institutions are important because they concentrated power in the ruler's household and away from the older Tang Chancellery structure. They were not strictly "civil" or "military"; they were the instruments by which a ruler controlled both.

Some northern regimes, especially the Later Tang under Mingzong and the Later Zhou under Guo Wei and Chai Rong, tried to strengthen civil administration and to curb abuses by soldiers and tax officials. The Later Zhou conducted a household and land survey and reformed tax collection, reduced restrictions that burdened peasants, and sought to reduce the corruption of local clerks. Civil officials, drawn from the examination system, which most northern regimes maintained irregularly, played real roles in these efforts. Their continuing presence complicates the image of pure military rule: even in the northern regimes, generals needed accountants.

Southern states show a different pattern. Southern Tang built an elaborate court and a civil service that used examinations regularly, and its ministers were prominent men of letters. Wuyue's government was compact, cautious, and focused on public works and religious patronage, with kings recruiting loyal local elites and family members to key posts. Min and the Shu states received many northern refugees, whose administrative and literary talents they used. The Southern Han relied to an unusual degree on eunuch officials and palace administration, and the Chu regime was more purely military. These differences reflect the varying circumstances of their founders: Wuyue's Qian Liu and Min's Wang brothers were local military men who cultivated local stability, while Southern Tang's rulers, who had inherited the Wu state, had ambitions of reunification and hence needed a large administration.

Regimes also differed in how they dealt with diplomacy and status. Many southern rulers paid nominal tribute to northern courts, receiving titles and recognition in return, while governing independently in practice. Wuyue's rulers were particularly careful in this respect; they sent regular missions to the north and were treated as loyal subordinate kings, which protected them from northern attack. Others, such as Southern Han and Later Shu, claimed the imperial title outright. Choices about status, king or emperor, tributary or independent, were strategic decisions that balanced military reality against legitimacy claims.

The overall picture is one of institutional continuity with regional adaptation. Tang forms survived, but they served different purposes in different places: as instruments of centralization in the Later Zhou, as courtly ritual in Southern Tang, as tools of local stability in Wuyue. The term "warlord" describes the origin of many of these rulers, but not the way they governed. Students should read this section as a corrective to a common assumption: that with the fall of a great dynasty, government simply broke down. In fact, governments multiplied, and each had to solve the problem of how to tax, staff, and defend a state on a smaller scale than the Tang had. Some solved it poorly; others solved it well enough to leave lasting legacies.

Section 30.5

Warfare and Military Government

Armies remained the decisive political fact of the period. Every ruler of every state owed his position to soldiers and could lose it to them. Understanding how these armies were organized, paid, and controlled is essential to understanding why some regimes collapsed quickly and others endured.

The basic building blocks were professional soldiers, not seasonal militias. The Tang had originally used a system of part-time soldier-farmers, but by the late Tang most fighting was done by full-time troops recruited and supported by military governors. In the ninth and tenth centuries, the most important forces were the household troops or "牙军" (yajun) of a commander, and the palace armies of an emperor, elite units stationed in or near the capital. These forces were often quite small by the standards of the Tang or the Song, numbering in the tens of thousands, but they were well equipped and often loyal to their commander in a very personal way. A commander's household troops formed his political base, and their approval could make or unmake a ruler. Zhu Wen, aware of this, is reported to have tattooed his soldiers' faces or arms to discourage desertion, a practice that would recur in later Chinese military history.

Cavalry was crucial in the north. The Shatuo, Khitans, and other steppe-connected forces were superb horsemen, and Central Plain regimes needed horses to compete. Horses had to be acquired from the steppe, from the northwest, or from the southwest, and control of horse-breeding areas mattered. The loss of the Sixteen Prefectures, and access to northern pastureland, made this harder for the plain-based dynasties after 938, and shaped Song military weakness in cavalry for a century afterward. In the south, warfare was different: rivers, lakes, and wetlands favored infantry, boats, and fortified cities, and southern states built naval forces and war fleets. Sieges, in the north and south alike, were common, and city walls were important, which is one reason the walls of Kaifeng were expanded in the 950s.

Military government meant that soldiers were also administrators. Military governors, as Section 30.1 explained, held civil and fiscal powers as well as command, and in a very real sense the regimes of the period were extensions of the governorship. Even after a governor became a king or emperor, he governed through a network of commanders whom he had placed in strategic prefectures. The problem was that these commanders could turn against him. The solutions attempted included adoption of loyal followers, marriage alliances, rotation of commands, and, especially under Chai Rong and later the Song, concentration of the best troops under the direct control of the capital. The Later Zhou's reform, discussed in Section 30.9, is the clearest example: by selecting the most capable soldiers from all units for the palace army, and dismissing the weak, Chai Rong strengthened the center against the provinces.

Succession by mutiny and coup was frequent. Soldiers might proclaim their commander emperor, sometimes reluctantly, sometimes with enthusiasm; the pattern had already occurred before 907, and it recurred in 951, when Guo Wei's troops acclaimed him, and again in 960, when Zhao Kuangyin's troops did the same. In each case, the ritual of acclamation followed by formal abdication of the previous ruler gave the appearance of orderly transition. Historians debate how far these episodes were spontaneous and how far they were planned by the commander in question; the honest answer is that both elements were usually present, and that the sources, written later, had reasons to portray them one way or the other.

The fiscal burden of armies was heavy. Professional soldiers had to be paid in cash, cloth, or grain, and equipped with weapons and horses. In the north, this meant heavy taxation, requisitions, and forced service. Regimes that could not pay their troops risked mutiny; regimes that overtaxed the peasantry risked rebellion. Salt, iron, tea, and wine monopolies, as well as customs on commerce, became important. The Later Tang and Later Zhou both wrestled with this problem, and the creation of the Three Finance Bureau reflects it. In the south, prosperous agricultural regions and maritime trade generated more revenue, which helped explain why some southern states could sustain armies without collapsing.

One useful analytical question is why some military regimes proved ephemeral while others built lasting administrative systems. The northern regimes were repeatedly toppled because they rested on the loyalty of a small number of commanders, faced external threats on an open frontier, and lacked a widely accepted principle of succession. The stronger southern states, such as Wuyue and Southern Tang, had more defensible geography, more productive economies, and rulers who invested in civil institutions and in building networks of local support. These advantages did not make them invulnerable, as the eventual Song conquests showed, but they helped them survive long enough to develop. The Later Zhou is the northern exception that proves the rule: it lasted only nine years, but in that time it built institutions, particularly a reformed army and a stronger central administration, that could survive a change of dynasty and serve the Song.

Students should also remember the human side of warfare. Campaigns devastated the countryside in contested regions, especially the plain between the Yellow River and the Huai, where armies marched repeatedly. Sieges brought starvation and massacre. Soldiers themselves were often conscripted or recruited from among the poor, and desertion and mutiny were common. The costs of these wars fell hardest on the farmers whose fields and stored grain were requisitioned, and it is no accident that the tenth-century regimes that promised relief from military exactions, such as the Later Zhou under Guo Wei, gained a measure of popular support.

Section 30.6

Southern Economic Development

Chapter 29 described the gradual shift of economic weight from north to south during the late Tang, driven by migration, the decline of the northwest, and the increasing productivity of the Yangtze valley. This section continues that argument into the tenth century. It aims to show the foundations that were laid or consolidated in the period, while leaving the full analysis of the Song commercial revolution to Chapter 32. Students should treat the tenth-century south as the seedbed of later growth, not as its full realization.

The agricultural base was the first foundation. Rice cultivation in the lower and middle Yangtze regions had been developing since the Neolithic (Chapter 4) and had expanded during the Six Dynasties and Tang. In the tenth century, southern rulers encouraged land reclamation and water control. In the Taihu basin and the lower Yangtze delta, Wuyue and Southern Tang maintained dikes, canals, and sluices, and organized labor to dredge silted waterways, reclaim low-lying land, and manage the annual flooding. Wuyue's sea walls protected valuable farmland around Hangzhou Bay. In the Huai valley, Yangtze highlands, and Hunan, rulers granted land or tax relief to settlers. These policies did not create the agricultural surplus by themselves, but they helped to secure it, and to make investment in irrigation worthwhile. Historians generally agree that intensified rice cultivation, together with growing use of double-cropping in some areas and of more varied crops including tea and fruit, supported rising southern populations during and after this period.

Migration was the second foundation. The upheavals of the late ninth and early tenth centuries drove many northerners, especially from the Central Plain and the Huai region, to move south. Some were peasants fleeing warfare and famine; others were members of elite families, officials, scholars, monks, and artisans who found employment or refuge at southern courts. Min, Southern Tang, the Shu kingdoms, and Wuyue all welcomed such migrants, and benefited from their skills. Migration also expanded settlement in less densely populated southern highlands, where new fields were opened. Earlier waves of migration, discussed in Chapter 21, had begun this transformation; the tenth century continued it and added new layers, contributing to the demographic shift that would make the south the more populous half of the Song empire.

Transport and trade were the third. Southern rivers and lakes formed a natural network of waterways, and the Grand Canal (Chapter 24) linked the Yangtze to the Huai and to the north, where it remained the essential artery to Kaifeng. Rulers maintained canals, ports, and river ports, and merchants moved grain, salt, tea, textiles, ceramics, and timber along them. Regional specialization was developing: Sichuan produced silk brocades and paper; the lower Yangtze produced rice, silk, and fine ceramics, including Yue ware kilns in the Wuyue region, whose finest pieces were reserved for the court; Hunan exported tea; Fujian produced sugar and tea in later periods and had growing coastal shipping; Guangdong dealt in pearls, spices, and imported goods. The exchange of such goods among regions and with the north encouraged the growth of markets and of commercial towns.

Maritime commerce was the fourth. The southeast coast, from Zhejiang through Fujian to Guangdong, was connected by sea to Korea, Japan, Southeast Asia, and the Indian Ocean. After Guangzhou's sack in 879, trade there recovered under the Southern Han, whose rulers taxed and regulated foreign merchants and profited from the traffic. Fuzhou and, increasingly, Quanzhou grew as ports. Wuyue sent embassies and traded with Korea and Japan, exchanging Chinese silks, ceramics, and Buddhist texts for local goods, and sent gifts to northern courts. Arab and Persian merchants continued to reach southern ports, though the exact volume of their trade is difficult to measure. Maritime commerce was a source of revenue and prestige for the southern regimes and helped make port cities into cosmopolitan centers, a development that would accelerate in the Song (Chapter 37).

Money and taxation followed regional patterns. Coinage was fragmented: rulers minted their own coins, sometimes of iron or lead, as in Chu and Min, because copper was scarce or because they wished to keep copper coins from flowing out. This fragmentation complicated trade but did not stop it. Taxation in southern states built on the Two-Tax structure inherited from the Tang, with summer and autumn levies, and on monopoly revenues. Some rulers, such as those of Southern Tang and Wuyue, are reported to have taxed heavily, and their subjects complained; others were more moderate. The evidence is uneven, and historians must be careful not to romanticize southern prosperity, since tax burdens and forced labor weighed heavily on many ordinary households.

What this section can safely claim is modest but significant. By 960, the south had a more productive agrarian base, a larger population, denser networks of waterborne exchange, more developed ports, and a richer commercial culture than it had had a century earlier. The Song would inherit these, and add its own institutional and technological changes, including the expansion of paper money, the growth of markets and cities, new crop varieties, and the expansion of manufacturing (Chapter 32). But it inherited a south that was already moving in the direction of a commercial economy, and the tenth-century kingdoms deserve credit for part of that inheritance. Just as importantly, when the Song conquered them, it absorbed their resources, ports, and administrative experience, which strengthened the new state considerably.

Section 30.7

Cities, Commerce, and Culture

Political division did not stop cultural production. The capitals and commercial centers of the tenth century were functioning urban environments, not simply battlefields, and many were sites of remarkable creativity. This section examines several, and asks what their example shows about the relationship between political fragmentation and cultural life.

Kaifeng, at the junction of the Grand Canal and the Bian River, grew rapidly after Zhu Wen made it his capital in 907. It was ideally located for receiving grain from the southeast, but was exposed and difficult to defend. Later Zhou emperor Chai Rong ordered a major expansion of the city in 955, adding an outer wall to enclose the swelling suburbs, widening streets, and encouraging construction, reforms that laid the physical basis for the great commercial city of the Northern Song. Luoyang, the Later Tang capital, remained a major political and cultural center, though damaged by the wars. Chang'an, by contrast, never regained its old status; it became a regional administrative center in a much diminished form.

In the south, Jinling (Nanjing) under the Southern Tang was a major court city, with palaces, temples, gardens, and academies. The Southern Tang court was famous for its patronage of poets, painters, and scholars. The ruler Li Yu, who reigned from 961 to 975, wrote lyrics in the ci (词) form that are among the most admired poems of the tenth century. The ci was a song-lyric genre, written to fit existing tunes, often on themes of love, separation, and loss, and in the hands of Li Yu, who was writing as a doomed ruler in a besieged state, it took on a strong emotional depth; his later poems, written after the Song captured him, are especially poignant. An earlier and important collection of ci lyrics, Among the Flowers (Huajian ji), was compiled at the Later Shu court in Chengdu around 940 and shows the vitality of that genre in Sichuan. Court painting also flourished: painters associated with Southern Tang and Shu developed landscape and bird-and-flower traditions that would strongly influence Song painting. The Southern Tang court is associated with a famous painted handscroll depicting a nocturnal banquet of an official, which historians have used as evidence for elite life, furnishings, and dress, though its dating and attribution are debated.

Hangzhou, the Wuyue capital, was a cosmopolitan and commercial city in a rich agricultural region. Its rulers built Buddhist temples, pagodas, and monasteries, including structures whose ruins and foundations have yielded significant finds. The city's wealth, its scenic West Lake, and its role as a port on the Qiantang estuary prepared its later development as the capital of the Southern Song (Chapter 37). Fuzhou, Quanzhou, and Guangzhou were ports through which goods, foreign merchants, and religious communities passed. Chengdu, capital of the Shu states, was one of the great commercial and literary cities of the time, known for silk, paper, and printing. Changsha and Jingzhou were regional trading centers on the Yangtze system.

Religious institutions were prominent in these cities. Buddhist monasteries held land, ran schools and hospitals, and functioned as centers of pilgrimage, learning, and commerce. Chan (Zen) Buddhism, with its emphasis on direct experience and lineages of masters, flourished in the south, especially in Wuyue, Min, and Southern Tang, and several important Chan lineages took shape in the region during this period (Chapter 28 described the Tang background). Daoist temples and popular cults also flourished. Rulers patronized religious institutions for reasons both spiritual and political: temples brought merit, legitimacy, and local support. Not every ruler, however, was generous. In the north, Chai Rong's suppression of Buddhist institutions in 955 shows that religious institutions could be seen as competitors for resources.

Elite culture, too, moved and adapted. Many literati from the north, having lost their families' positions in the political upheavals, took refuge in southern courts, and the courts drew on their talents. Scholarly traditions, library collections, and literary forms passed south with them. The Southern Tang and Shu courts maintained libraries and collected books, which would later contribute to the resources of the Song imperial library after conquest. In the north, scholars such as Feng Dao and other officials continued to preserve the traditions of the Tang bureaucracy. The image of a cultural desert between Tang and Song does not survive contact with the evidence.

Ordinary urban life, though less visible in the sources, was also active. Markets, teahouses, workshops, and entertainment quarters were features of the larger cities; guilds and merchant associations were developing. Historians note that some of the city types and marketplace practices that would blossom in the Song already existed in embryonic form. But this section, like the previous one, should be read cautiously: the surviving sources emphasize courts and religious institutions, and give far less on artisans, day laborers, or women in the marketplace.

Section 30.8

Printing and Institutional Continuity

Two related themes belong together in this section: the growth of printing, and the survival of institutions across the political rupture of 907. Both illustrate a central argument of the chapter: continuity flowed through political fragmentation, and in some ways fragmentation encouraged it, because many small courts needed to invest in shared texts, procedures, and knowledge.

Woodblock printing was not invented in the tenth century. The technique of carving text or images in relief on wooden blocks, inking them, and pressing paper against them was already in use in the Tang. The world's oldest securely dated printed book, a copy of the Diamond Sutra found at Dunhuang, dates to 868, and Tang printers in Sichuan and elsewhere produced calendars, almanacs, dictionaries, and religious texts. What changed in the tenth century was scale, and the range of uses. Printing grew from primarily Buddhist, calendrical, and popular uses to include the Confucian classics and official texts, and was adopted by both courts and monasteries in several regions. The full story of Song printing, movable type, book markets, and expanding literacy belongs to Chapter 33; here the task is to show its foundations.

The most famous state printing project of the period was the printing of the Nine Classics at the state Directorate of Education (Guozijian) in the northern capital. The initiative began under the Later Tang, encouraged by the official Feng Dao and others, around 932, and the work was completed under the Later Zhou in 953. Its significance was that it produced standardized, reproducible editions of the core texts of the scholarly curriculum. Earlier, classics were copied by hand or engraved on stone, as at the Tang Kaicheng stone classics; printing multiplied copies far more cheaply and reduced copying errors. The Later Shu court in Chengdu carried out a similar program, and its editions were also valued. The projects demonstrated that rulers of very different states valued the same texts and used the same technology to spread them, a form of shared culture that political division did not interrupt.

Buddhist printing was also important. In Wuyue, rulers sponsored the printing of large numbers of short Buddhist texts, especially dharani sutras, which were believed to bring merit, and had them deposited in pagodas and stupas. The Primary Source Window in Section 30.10 examines one of these. Many such printed texts survive, having been protected inside sealed foundations, and they are among the earliest datable examples of printing in China. Printing in Sichuan also included Buddhist scriptures, and it would culminate in the great printing of the entire Buddhist canon early in the Song, an undertaking that began in Sichuan in the 970s. This is a good example of how a state, once it absorbed a regional culture, adopted and expanded its technologies.

Printing also began to serve administration. Governments could print calendars, standardized forms, examinations, and official notices, and the ability to reproduce texts reliably supported bureaucratic continuity. But students should not overstate this. Most official documents remained handwritten, and printed books were a small fraction of textual production. The expanded role of printing in government and commerce belongs largely to the Song.

Institutional continuity across 907 extended well beyond printing. Written administration persisted: edicts, memorials, registers of households and land, and legal codes continued to be produced and archived, in forms recognizable from the Tang. The Two-Tax system and monopolies on salt and other goods continued, though with regional variations. The examination system, which had been central to the Tang recruitment of officials though never the sole route, continued in several regimes: the Later Tang, Later Zhou, Southern Tang, and Later Shu held examinations, though irregularly and with varying prestige, and examination-qualified officials served in many courts. Local government stayed built on prefectures and counties. Buddhist monastic organization endured, with its ordination certificates, monastic landholdings, and networks. The idea of imperial legitimacy, with its ritual calendar, ancestral temples, and dynastic titles, was maintained by rulers who otherwise had little in common.

People carried continuity. Officials who served several regimes, of whom Feng Dao is the best-known, kept administrative knowledge alive. Later moralists, notably Ouyang Xiu in the eleventh century, condemned such men for lacking loyalty; modern historians tend to see them as preservers of essential skills in an age when the alternative was administrative breakdown. Skilled clerks, scribes, accountants, and local elites who administered counties and collected taxes, whose names rarely appear in the sources, may have contributed even more. When Song reunification came, it did not have to reinvent government; it took over a working administrative culture that had never entirely stopped operating.

Section 30.9

The Rise of the Song

The question for this section is: why was a new reunification possible after more than half a century of fragmentation? The answer lies in the strengthening of the northern center in the 950s, the exhaustion of the older pattern of military succession, the resources available to a state controlling the Central Plain and the canal system, and the political weaknesses of the southern kingdoms. It also lies in contingency, in the particular decisions of particular men.

The Later Zhou is the immediate background. Guo Wei, who took power in 951, and his successor Chai Rong (r. 954–959) undertook a series of reforms and campaigns. Chai Rong reorganized the army by selecting the strongest soldiers from the provincial and household forces for the palace army, discharging the weak, and making the palace army the core of the state's military power. He reformed administration and taxation, conducted a land and household survey, reduced some of the abuses of local officials, and expanded and rebuilt Kaifeng and dredged the canals that supplied it. In 955 he ordered a reduction of Buddhist monasteries: unauthorized temples were closed, restrictions were placed on ordination, and bronze Buddhist images were melted to make coins, a policy that both addressed a shortage of copper and reflected concern about the large amount of land and labor held by monasteries. It is a sign of how these pressures drove state policy.

Chai Rong also went to war, with remarkable success. In 955 and 956 his forces took several prefectures from the Later Shu. Between 956 and 958 he waged a series of campaigns against the Southern Tang, taking the Huai lands north of the Yangtze and forcing the Southern Tang ruler to abandon the imperial title and acknowledge Later Zhou superiority. In 959 he turned north against the Khitans and recovered a number of frontier districts, but fell ill during the campaign and died shortly afterward at the age of thirty-eight. The Later Zhou then passed to his seven-year-old son, Guo Zongxun. Most northern dynasties had fallen when a child succeeded a strong ruler, and this one would follow the pattern.

Zhao Kuangyin, the man who ended it, had risen through the Later Zhou military. Born in 927 into a military family, he had served Guo Wei and Chai Rong, distinguished himself in the campaigns against the Southern Tang, and by 959 was the commander of the palace army (a post held by only a few) and military governor of Songzhou, the place from which the new dynasty took its name. In early 960, a report reached the court that the Khitans and the Northern Han were invading, and the army was sent north under Zhao's command. At Chenqiao, a short distance from Kaifeng, the soldiers proclaimed Zhao emperor, reportedly draping a yellow robe over him. He returned to the capital, the young emperor abdicated, and Zhao proclaimed the Song dynasty. Later historians disputed whether the acclamation was spontaneous or planned. Whatever the truth, the result was a peaceful transfer, with the former emperor and his family treated with respect, and the Song presented itself as the legitimate successor to the Later Zhou.

Why did the Song succeed where earlier regimes had failed? Several factors mattered. First, Zhao Kuangyin and his advisors deliberately took steps to ensure that the army would not repeat the pattern. In the first years of the dynasty, Zhao is traditionally said to have persuaded senior generals to give up their commands in exchange for wealth and honors, an episode often called the "cup of wine" story, though its details come from later sources and its historicity is debated; the larger point, that the Song sought to bring military power under civilian and imperial control, is developed in Chapter 31 and is not narrated here. Second, the Later Zhou reforms had already produced a stronger, better-organized central army and a more effective administration, which the Song inherited. Third, the Song controlled the Central Plain and the canal junction at Kaifeng, with access to grain from the southeast. Fourth, the southern kingdoms were divided, often weak, and unwilling or unable to combine against a common threat. Fifth, contemporaries were tired: after more than fifty years of coups, campaigns, and requisitions, many officials, merchants, and farmers welcomed a regime that promised stability, which gave the Song a measure of legitimacy that a dynasty founded by a coup might otherwise have lacked.

The reunification proceeded step by step, and students should notice that it was neither inevitable nor rapid. In 963, the Song took Jingnan and Chu with little fighting. In 965 it conquered the Later Shu, gaining Sichuan's resources. In 971 it destroyed the Southern Han, after a hard campaign in which its forces defeated war elephants at Shaozhou. In 975 it captured Jinling and ended the Southern Tang; Li Yu was taken north as a captive. In 978 the last rulers of Wuyue and the Quanzhou–Zhangzhou region submitted peacefully. In 979 the Song conquered the Northern Han, ending the Ten Kingdoms. Yet the Sixteen Prefectures remained in Khitan hands, and Song attempts to recover them in 979 and 986 failed, an outcome that shaped Song foreign policy, as Chapters 31 and 35 explain. The Song, then, was not a restoration of the Tang: it controlled less territory in the north and west than the Tang at its height, and it faced a powerful Khitan state on its frontier.

This section should end where the chapter's argument requires: with the Song's reunification underway and the institutional questions still open. The Song inherited an army and a bureaucracy from the Later Zhou and other regimes, a fiscal system built on late Tang and Five Dynasties practices, and the economic and cultural resources of the conquered south. What it would make of them, how it would reshape the army, the bureaucracy, the examination system, and taxation into a distinctive civil state, is the subject of Chapter 31.

Section 30.10

Chapter Review and Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms Checkpoint

This chapter has examined the political collapse of the Tang, the succession of northern military dynasties, the emergence of southern and other regional kingdoms, the varied systems by which they governed, the economic and cultural development of the period, and the conditions that led to Song reunification. Its central argument is that the collapse of the Tang in 907 did not produce a simple interval of chaos. It produced multiple competing political systems that preserved and modified Tang institutions, developed distinct regional economies and cultures, experimented with military and administrative government, and created the conditions from which the Song emerged.

The core insights are as follows. The Tang fell because of accumulated fiscal, military, political, social, and environmental strains, culminating in the Huang Chao rebellion and Zhu Wen's seizure of the court, not because of the An Lushan Rebellion alone. The Five Dynasties were successive northern regimes based on military coalitions, in which three of the five had founders of Shatuo background, and their rapid turnover reflected the personal nature of military loyalty, the openness of the Central Plain, and pressure from the Khitans. The Ten Kingdoms were a diverse set of regional states, several of which built durable governments and thriving economies. Regional political systems varied widely, but almost all inherited Tang administrative forms. Armies were politically decisive, but the regimes that lasted also built civil institutions. The south developed agriculturally, demographically, and commercially, laying foundations for later Song growth. Cities and courts were centers of literary, artistic, and religious creativity. Printing expanded, and institutions and personnel carried continuity across 907. And the Later Zhou's reforms, the vulnerabilities of the southern states, and the exhaustion of the older pattern of military succession made Song reunification possible.

Historical Evidence

How Do Historians Know? The Sources for the Tenth Century

Official histories. The Old History of the Five Dynasties (Jiu Wudai shi), compiled in 974 under the Song official Xue Juzheng, drew on the veritable records and court archives of the northern regimes and is valuable for its detail. Ouyang Xiu's New History of the Five Dynasties (Xin Wudai shi), written in the eleventh century, is more polished and more moralizing, criticizing officials who served several courts and treating the period as an age of moral collapse. Sima Guang's Comprehensive Mirror for Aid in Government (Zizhi tongjian, completed 1084) weaves these and other sources into a continuous narrative. Limits: The major dynastic histories and Sima Guang's chronicle were Song works and reflect Song-era concerns about legitimacy and political order. Later compilations, including Wu Renchen's Qing-era Spring and Autumn Annals of the Ten Kingdoms (Shiguo chunqiu), preserve additional material on the southern states but stand even farther from the events chronologically.

Contemporary and archaeological evidence. Printed Buddhist scrolls from pagoda deposits in the Wuyue region, tomb inscriptions and epitaphs, coins, temple and stele inscriptions, manuscripts from Dunhuang, surviving paintings and ceramics, and the poems of the period were produced at the time and reflect contemporary concerns. Archaeological excavations of royal tombs, such as those of the Former Shu ruler Wang Jian in Chengdu and the Southern Tang rulers near Nanjing, provide evidence of court culture, burial ritual, and craftsmanship. Limits: These sources are scattered, favor elites and religious institutions, and rarely speak directly about the experience of ordinary farmers or soldiers.

Foreign sources. Arabic accounts of Guangzhou's sack and of maritime trade, Khitan sources, and Korean and Japanese records of missions and merchants provide perspectives from outside. Limits: They are fragmentary and sometimes contain exaggerated figures or secondhand information.

Primary Source Window

Primary Source Window: Printed Baoqieyin Dharani Sutras from Wuyue

Creator: Printed at the order of Wuyue rulers, above all Qian Hongchu (Qian Chu) · Date: Mid- to late tenth century; surviving prints are dated by colophon from the mid-950s to the 970s · Region: Wuyue kingdom (Hangzhou and surrounding region) · Type: Woodblock-printed Buddhist text with frontispiece and colophon, recovered from sealed pagoda deposits

Context: The Baoqieyin (Sanskrit: Sarvatathāgatādhiṣṭhāna-hṛdaya-guhya-dhāraṇī) is a short text believed to bring merit and protection to those who make or venerate copies of it. The rulers of Wuyue, devout Buddhists, ordered the printing of very large numbers of copies and had them placed in pagodas and stupas. Paraphrase of the colophon (not a translated quotation):

The colophon on these prints states, in the standard dedicatory formula, that the king of the Wuyue state had the text printed in a very large number of copies, a figure given as 84,000, to be enshrined in pagodas as a permanent act of devotion.

Analysis: The source shows the technical capacity of a tenth-century regional court to organize carving, printing, and distribution on a large scale, the importance of Buddhism to royal legitimacy and merit-making, and the use of printing for religious rather than only scholarly purposes. The number 84,000 has symbolic resonance in Buddhist tradition (it recalls the stupas attributed to the legendary king Ashoka), so historians read it as a devotional and political statement, not necessarily an exact count. The source reflects the perspective of the ruler and the Buddhist establishment; it says little about the carvers and printers who made the sheets, the cost to the population, the reading of the text by ordinary people (which was not the intention, since the copies were sealed), or the state's political calculations. Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Was the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms Period an Age of Disorder or of Regional Transformation?

The question: How should historians characterize the years between 907 and 960 (and to 979)? Was the period primarily one of political breakdown and violence, or one in which important regional and institutional developments took place?

Interpretation A — An age of disorder: Following the tradition of Ouyang Xiu and other Song historians, this view emphasizes the frequency of coups, the brutality of warfare, the moral compromises of officials who changed loyalties, and the loss of the Sixteen Prefectures. It stresses that, in the north especially, soldiers and rulers destroyed lives and property, and that political instability was real and costly.
Interpretation B — An age of regional transformation: Many modern historians, influenced by the concept of a Tang–Song transition, emphasize the survival and development of institutions, the economic expansion of the south, the creativity of regional courts, the growth of printing, and the strengthening of the central state under the Later Zhou. On this view, the period is a formative phase, and the label "chaos" reflects the perspective of the Song and of earlier moral historians.

Evidence: Interpretation A draws on the narrative sources, which record turmoil, and on evidence of destruction, such as the sack of cities and heavy taxation. Interpretation B draws on contemporary evidence such as printed texts, tombs, coins, and material culture, on the continuity of administration, and on evidence of southern growth. Both use the same period, but they emphasize different regions (north versus south), different social groups (soldiers and officials versus merchants and monks), and different sources (later histories versus contemporary artifacts).

Why it matters: How historians characterize the period affects how they understand the relationship between political unity and cultural or economic vitality, and how they interpret the origins of the Song. A picture of pure chaos makes the Song look like a rescue; a picture of regional development makes it look like a synthesis.

Student task: Using evidence from this chapter, evaluate which interpretation is better supported for the north and for the south separately, or explain why a combined interpretation that varies by region is more accurate. What additional evidence would help?

Multiple Perspectives

Multiple Perspectives: Life After 907

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives based on patterns in the sources, not quotations from specific named individuals.

A northern soldier in a palace army: His livelihood, status, and security depended on the fortunes of his commander. Loyalty was personal, pay could be irregular, and a change of regime could bring reward or execution. He might feel pride in his unit and resentment toward civil officials, and he knew that a successful acclamation of a new emperor could bring promotion.
A southern merchant in a port such as Guangzhou or Fuzhou: Political division meant multiple tolls, different coinages, and diplomatic risk, but stable regional rulers who taxed but protected trade offered predictable conditions. Access to foreign merchants and Indian Ocean or Korean markets could bring wealth; events like the sack of Guangzhou in 879 showed how vulnerable that wealth was.
A regional ruler such as the king of Wuyue: Survival depended on balancing military strength, tribute to stronger neighbors, religious patronage, and public works. Avoiding costly wars and recognizing a northern overlord in name preserved autonomy in practice, though it required diplomatic skill and finally led to submission.
A Buddhist cleric: Monasteries could flourish under generous rulers in the south and lose land and members under state pressure in the north, as in 955. Clerics depended on patronage, yet also preserved texts, ran schools, and offered spiritual continuity.
A displaced northern elite family: Having lost position and property in the upheavals, such a family might migrate south, seeking employment at a court and preserving books and traditions. They could gain a new place but face uncertainty and nostalgia for the lost northern homeland.
A farming household: In contested northern regions, armies meant requisition, destruction, and conscription. In more stable southern regions, tax and labor obligations remained heavy, but there was opportunity to clear new land or benefit from water works. Historians have far less direct evidence of these households' views than of the elites'.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change Over Time: Government, Economy, and Culture Across 907

What Changed

  • A single imperial court gave way to multiple competing regimes, with capitals at Kaifeng, Luoyang, Jinling, Hangzhou, Chengdu, and others
  • The aristocratic officials of the Tang were drastically reduced by violence and displacement, and military men took a greater role in government
  • New central organs, such as the Bureau of Military Affairs and the Three Finance Bureau, concentrated power in the ruler's hands
  • The economic center of gravity moved further south and toward maritime trade
  • Printing expanded from religious and calendrical uses into the state's official culture

What Stayed the Same

  • Prefectures and counties remained the basic units of local government
  • Tang legal codes, titles, and written administrative forms remained the framework
  • Two-Tax and monopoly finance continued, with regional variation
  • Buddhist monasteries, Confucian learning, and the idea of a single legitimate empire persisted
  • Farming households remained the main producers and taxpayers

Who experienced change most directly? Aristocratic officials, soldiers, and residents of contested northern cities. Who experienced continuity? Many farmers, local clerks, monks, and residents of stable southern regions. Why does the pattern matter? It shows that the end of a dynasty is not the end of a social order, and that continuity and rupture coexisted in different degrees for different groups.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: The Collapse of Tang Rule and Regional Fragmentation

Causes

Long-term: regional military governorships created after 755; a narrow fiscal base dependent on a few regions and on salt; semi-independent Hebei circuits; eunuch control of palace armies and court factionalism; costly frontier wars; weak loyalty of garrison soldiers.

Short-term: the Pang Xun mutiny (868); floods and drought in the Yellow and Huai regions (873–874); the rebellions of Wang Xianzhi and Huang Chao (874–884); the fall of the capital in 880; rivalries among the commanders who suppressed the rebels.

Event or transformation

The court was reduced to dependency on regional strongmen; Zhu Wen killed the eunuchs, purged officials, moved the court, and deposed the last emperor in 907; regional regimes such as Former Shu, Wuyue, and Min consolidated autonomous rule under varying claims of kingship, imperial title, and nominal subordination to northern dynasties.

Effects

Immediate: the Later Liang and its successors ruled the north; southern kingdoms consolidated; the Khitans and Shatuo gained influence; aristocratic families were destroyed or displaced; migration to the south increased.

Long-term: a regionalized political order lasted for decades; southern economies and cultures developed; institutions such as the Three Finance Bureau and reformed palace armies emerged; the Sixteen Prefectures were lost; conditions for Song reunification were created.

Historical complexity

Calling the Tang "corrupt" explains nothing. The collapse resulted from the interaction of fiscal, military, political, environmental, social, and regional factors, and the outcome depended on individual choices, such as Zhu Wen's, as well as structural conditions. The same events had different effects in different regions, and the "dynastic cycle" is no substitute for identifying specific mechanisms.
Everyday Life and Social History

Everyday Life: Four Households in the Tenth Century

Evidence for ordinary life in this period is thin, but historians can offer cautious reconstructions. A farming household in the Central Plain lived with the constant possibility of armies: grain might be requisitioned, men conscripted, or animals seized. Taxes in grain and cloth were due each season, and clerks who collected them could add to the burden. Under better rulers, such as Guo Wei, some exactions were reduced, and households could recover.

A farming household in the lower Yangtze or Zhejiang plain faced different conditions. Water control, dikes, and canals meant seasonal labor obligations, but also the protection of fields from flooding. Rice, along with fish, vegetables, and in some places tea, formed the basis of the diet, and silk and hemp were woven by women in the household. Women's textile labor, as in earlier periods, was a significant part of household income and tax payment.

A soldier's family might live near a garrison or in the capital region, dependent on pay, rations, and a commander's favor. Soldiers' wives and children could suffer heavily when a regime fell or a commander was killed. A monastic community in Wuyue or Min lived from land rents, donations, and services, and offered education and charity to lay neighbors. Students should remember that most of what survives comes from courts and monasteries, and that the daily life of women, artisans, and laborers is much harder to see.

Regional and Frontier Context

Regional and Frontier Context

The tenth century shows the regional variety of Chinese-speaking society clearly. The North China Plain and Yellow River basin were the arena of the Five Dynasties, exposed and militarized. Shanxi, a hilly, defensible region, was the base of the Shatuo and later the Northern Han. The northwest, including the Gansu corridor and Dunhuang, was governed by local leaders such as the Cao family at Dunhuang, who maintained ties with Central Asian and steppe peoples and with the Central Plain regimes. Sichuan was an insulated, wealthy region. The lower Yangtze, the southeast coast, and Lingnan developed as commercial and maritime regions. The far southwest saw the fall of Nanzhao in 902 and the later emergence of the Dali kingdom in 937.

Frontiers were zones of interaction, not simple lines. The Khitans under Yelü Abaoji unified their people, expanded into Manchuria and Mongolia, conquered the Bohai state in 926, and dealt with the Central Plain regimes through diplomacy, trade, and war. The Shatuo were both military servants of the Tang and rulers in their own right. From the perspective of frontier peoples, the Central Plain regimes were sources of titles, silk, and trade goods, and sometimes rivals, sometimes allies. Historians increasingly recognize that these frontier polities were active political agents, not merely threats, and that the Khitan state, treated in Chapter 35, was a co-participant in the political world that the Song entered.

China and the Wider World

China and the Wider World

The tenth-century states were embedded in a wider Asian world. In the north and northeast, the Khitan state rose, and in Korea the Goryeo dynasty was founded in 918 and unified the peninsula by 936. In the south, in 938 the Vietnamese leader Ngo Quyen defeated a Southern Han fleet on the Bach Dang River, securing Vietnamese independence after centuries of Chinese rule, a turning point in Vietnamese history. In Japan, the Heian court had ended formal embassies to the Tang in 894, but merchants and monks continued to travel, and Wuyue and Japan exchanged letters, gifts, and Buddhist texts. In the Indian Ocean, Arab and Persian merchants continued to reach southern Chinese ports, though the Abbasid caliphate was declining, and Southeast Asian polities and Indian Ocean networks traded with Guangzhou, Fuzhou, and Quanzhou. Buddhist connections with India, Central Asia, and Korea persisted, though pilgrimage was reduced by warfare in the northwest.

These connections show that Chinese states were not only passive recipients of outside influence. Southern Han and Wuyue conducted active diplomacy and commerce; Chinese Buddhist texts and printed books traveled abroad; and rulers, merchants, and monks were participants in networks that connected East, Southeast, and West Asia. Understanding the period as part of a wider tenth-century transformation of Eurasia avoids the trap of treating it as a purely internal Chinese interval.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the political collapse of the Tang dynasty, the age of the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms, and the beginnings of Song reunification. Its central argument is that the collapse of the Tang in 907 did not simply produce chaos between two unified dynasties. It produced a set of competing political systems whose rulers preserved and modified Tang institutions, developed distinct regional economies and cultures, experimented with military and administrative government, and created conditions from which the Song eventually emerged.

The chapter began by showing that the Tang fell because of accumulated strains that extended well beyond the An Lushan Rebellion: a narrow fiscal base, regional military autonomy, court factionalism, costly frontier wars, and the environmental and social distress of the 870s. The Huang Chao rebellion, which sacked Guangzhou and Chang'an, broke the court's remaining authority, and the regional commanders who suppressed it, above all Zhu Wen, became the real holders of power. Zhu Wen's destruction of the eunuchs and of much of the aristocratic officialdom, and his deposition of the last emperor in 907, formalized what had already occurred.

In the north, the Five Dynasties, the Later Liang, Later Tang, Later Jin, Later Han, and Later Zhou, succeeded one another rapidly because they rested on personal military coalitions, faced an open frontier and Khitan pressure, and lacked a settled principle of succession. Three of the five had founders of Shatuo background, showing that non-Han military elites were central participants in northern politics. The cession of the Sixteen Prefectures in 938 and the Khitan occupation of Kaifeng in 947 show the strategic vulnerability of the Central Plain.

In the south, the Ten Kingdoms were a varied and retrospectively defined set of regional states. Southern Tang, Wuyue, Min, Chu, Southern Han, the Shu kingdoms, and Jingnan followed different trajectories shaped by geography, founders, and external relations. The chapter showed that they were substantive states that governed, taxed, traded, and patronized culture, not merely territories awaiting reconquest.

Regional political systems differed in how they combined military and civil institutions, but nearly all inherited Tang administrative forms. New organs such as the Bureau of Military Affairs and the Three Finance Bureau concentrated power in the ruler's hands, and the rapid turnover of dynasties in the north masked considerable administrative continuity, carried by officials such as Feng Dao and by the anonymous clerks who kept the machinery running.

Armies were politically decisive throughout. The chapter explained the organization of household troops and palace armies, the importance of cavalry in the north and river warfare in the south, the fiscal burdens of paying professional soldiers, and the recurring pattern of succession by mutiny. It also asked why some military regimes proved ephemeral and others durable, and argued that geography, economic resources, and investment in civil institutions helped account for the difference.

The south's agricultural, demographic, and commercial development, including water control, migration, waterborne trade, and maritime connections, laid foundations for the later Song commercial revolution treated in Chapter 32. Cities and courts were centers of poetry, painting, Buddhism, and scholarship, showing that political fragmentation did not mean cultural collapse. Printing expanded from its Tang foundations into Buddhist devotion and state-sponsored editions of the classics, a subject Chapter 33 will develop further, and institutions, texts, and personnel carried continuity across 907.

Finally, the chapter explained the conditions for Song reunification: the Later Zhou's military and administrative reforms, the resources of the Central Plain and the canal system, the weakness and division of the southern kingdoms, and the exhaustion of the older pattern of military succession. Zhao Kuangyin's acclamation at Chenqiao in 960 and the conquests of 963 to 979 ended the Ten Kingdoms, but the Sixteen Prefectures remained in Khitan hands. The Song thus inherited a partly unified realm and a set of institutions, which Chapter 31 will examine as it reshaped them into a distinctive civil state.

The 960 acclamation of Zhao Kuangyin by his troops, leading to the founding of the Song.

A historiographical concept describing long-term shifts from an aristocratic, north-centered society to a bureaucratic, commercialized, south-centered one.

Diplomatic relationships in which lesser rulers accepted nominal subordination in exchange for recognition and protection, varying widely in practice.
Regional kingdom
A state governing a limited region, often with the title of king rather than emperor.
Water management
Construction and maintenance of dikes, canals, sluices, and sea walls, important in Wuyue and Southern Tang.

Investigating a Tenth-Century Regional Kingdom

This project asks you to study one of the Ten Kingdoms, or one northern regime, in depth, and to evaluate how it should be understood within the wider period.

  1. Choose a state. Select Wuyue, Southern Tang, Min, Chu, Southern Han, Former Shu, Later Shu, Jingnan, Northern Han, or one of the Five Dynasties.

Distinguish clearly between primary and secondary sources.
  3. Map the state. Draw or annotate a map showing its capital, main regions, rivers, trade routes, and neighbors, and distinguish historical boundaries from modern provincial boundaries.

  5. Evaluate perspective. Consider how Song-era historians portrayed the state and whether contemporary evidence supports or challenges that portrayal.
  6. Write. In a 4–6 page essay (or equivalent presentation), present your findings and explain how your state contributed to, or was absorbed into, the Song.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a great empire can end without the society it governed ending. The Tang's political structure collapsed, yet farmers planted, monks copied and printed texts, officials kept records, merchants traded along rivers and coasts, and poets wrote. The tenth century reminds students that political history and social history do not always move together, and that the end of a dynasty is not the end of a civilization.

The period also shaped later Chinese history. The Later Zhou's reforms and the institutional experiments of the northern and southern regimes gave the Song tools with which to build a civil state (Chapter 31). Southern agricultural, commercial, and maritime development provided the base for the commercial revolution of Chapter 32, and the spread of printing anticipated the intellectual and educational changes of Chapters 33 and 34. The loss of the Sixteen Prefectures, by contrast, shaped Song strategy and its relations with the Liao (Chapter 35) for generations.

Larger themes emerge as well: the recurring tension between military and civil authority, the relationship between regional diversity and political integration, the role of non-Han peoples in shaping political history, and the way later historians' concerns shape their accounts of the past. The chapter also shows China connected to the wider Asian world, from Khitan power in the north, to Vietnamese independence in the south, to the maritime networks linking southern ports with Korea, Japan, and the Indian Ocean.

As students move on to the Song, they should carry with them a picture of Chinese history as diverse, interconnected, creative, contested, and politically changing: a history not of a single line of dynasties but of many regions, states, and peoples whose interactions produced new possibilities. The Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms were not a gap. They were part of the making of what came next.
