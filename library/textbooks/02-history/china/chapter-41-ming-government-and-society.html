Part VII — Mongol, Yuan, and Ming China
41

Ming Government and Society

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

According to the Ming court's own records, in just eight days in the autumn of 1384 the Hongwu emperor received 1,660 memorials dealing with 3,391 separate matters. An emperor who wished to rule personally had discovered what that wish would cost.

That figure, preserved in the dynasty's Veritable Records and often repeated by historians, captures the central problem of this chapter. Chapter 40 showed how Zhu Yuanzhang, a destitute orphan turned rebel commander, defeated rival regimes and Mongol-Yuan forces and proclaimed the Ming dynasty in 1368. This chapter begins after the Ming had been founded and Zhu had secured the core of his new state, though Yuan power and regional resistance had not vanished. It asks a different question: how did the new state actually govern? How could one emperor, claiming extraordinary personal authority, administer a country with something like ten million registered households, more than a thousand counties, and enormous regional differences in climate, crops, dialect, wealth, and custom?

The argument of this chapter is that Ming government was highly centralized at the top but deeply dependent on intermediaries below. The emperor, the Six Ministries, the censors, and the provincial offices formed an impressive hierarchy on paper. Yet that hierarchy touched most people through a handful of figures: a county magistrate with a very small formal staff; clerks and runners who knew the locality better than he did; village headmen who collected grain; degree-holding scholars who spoke for their neighborhoods; lineage elders who settled quarrels; and household heads who submitted the information on which taxes and labor duties depended. Centralization did not abolish these intermediaries. It tried to organize, record, discipline, and use them.

The chapter is therefore structural rather than chronological. Where Chapter 40 followed one crisis into the next, this chapter moves downward through the layers of the Ming order: from the court and the emperor's desk, through ministries and provincial offices, into the county yamen (the magistrate's office and residence), and finally into the household, the lineage, the workshop, the garrison, and the market town. Along the way it follows people making decisions: a scholar preparing for a provincial examination, a clerk copying a register, a soldier's family sent to a distant garrison, a widow managing her late husband's property.

What changed in this period was the organization of obligation. The Ming state classified households by hereditary occupation, recorded land and population in vast registers, and tied soldiers, artisans, and farmers to duties that were meant to last for generations. What continued was the long-established partnership between the imperial government and local society, which had been shaped by earlier dynasties through schools, examinations, and local elites. The Ming did not invent that partnership, but it reworked it, sometimes harshly, and its choices shaped the rest of the dynasty.

Those affected included almost everyone: officials who risked demotion or execution for misconduct; commoners whose lives were measured in grain and labor days; soldiers' families bound to hereditary service; women whose weaving and household management supported tax payment; and local elites who gained status and protection from their connection to the state. Our evidence includes the Ming Code, edicts and instructions from the Hongwu emperor, Veritable Records, surviving fragments of registers, gazetteers, genealogies, and legal cases, each of which has to be read with attention to who produced it and why.

The chapter also connects to wider history. A bureaucracy staffed through examinations, a registration system that tried to count and classify a population, and a hereditary military supported partly by state-assigned farmland were all attempts to solve problems that other large agrarian states faced: how to extract resources, maintain order, and obtain reliable information across distance. Comparing the Ming solutions with those of the Yuan, Song, and Tang that came before, and with the Inner Asian and Eurasian states around it, helps students see what was distinctive in Ming institutions and what was widely shared.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the imperial court converted ritual status, access to the ruler, and written documents into political authority.
  2. Analyze how concentrating decision-making in the emperor created administrative costs as well as power.
  3. Describe the functions of the Six Ministries, the Censorate, and the military commissions, and what officials actually did.
  4. Trace the territorial hierarchy from province to county and explain why the county magistrate was the critical point of contact with ordinary subjects.
  5. Evaluate the examination system as a route into government, recognizing both its importance and its social limits.
  6. Distinguish serving officials, degree-holders, clerks, and local elites, and assess how each mediated between state and society.
  7. Interpret the Great Ming Code as a functioning institution rather than only a text.
  8. Explain how registration converted households and land into tax and labor obligations, and why records diverged from reality.
  9. Describe how hereditary military households lived within the weisuo system.
  10. Assess the role of family, lineage, and community in organizing property, morality, taxation, and local order.
  11. Compare prescriptive ideals about women with evidence of women's lived and economic experience.
  12. Identify where Ming power operated directly and where it depended on intermediaries.

Historical Context

The Ming inherited a country that had been shaken by more than a century of conquest, division, and rebellion. The Mongol conquest had unified north and south after the long separation of Jin and Southern Song, as described in Chapters 38 and 39, and the Yuan dynasty governed through a mixture of Mongol, Inner Asian, and Chinese practices. By the 1340s and 1350s, floods on the Yellow River, famine, fiscal problems, and the Red Turban uprisings (Chapter 40) broke Yuan authority. Zhu Yuanzhang emerged from the competition among rebel regimes in the Yangtze valley, captured Nanjing in 1356, defeated rivals including Chen Youliang and Zhang Shicheng, and in 1368 proclaimed the Ming. His armies drove Yuan forces from the Central Plains in the same year.

The economic and demographic landscape in which the Ming state began to operate was uneven. North China, devastated by warfare and disease, had lost population and abandoned farmland. The Jiangnan region of the lower Yangtze had wealth, dense populations, and a tradition of landed elites, commercial towns, and scholarly families, but it had also been the stronghold of Zhu's most stubborn rival, which shaped how the new regime later treated its tax burden. Sichuan, the southeast coast, Lingnan, and the southwest were incorporated at different speeds and with differing degrees of control, and many frontier and upland communities continued to be governed through local leaders as much as through county magistrates.

The institutions this chapter describes are best understood as a mixture of inheritance and innovation. The six-ministry structure, the examination system, the county as the lowest regular unit, tax registration, and hereditary military service all had earlier models in the Tang, Song, and Yuan periods. Hongwu's innovations lay in how severely he removed intermediary offices at the top, how comprehensively he tried to register and classify the population, and how he sought to bind communities to their obligations through moral instruction as well as law. Chapter 40 explained why these arrangements were created during the reconstruction of the 1360s–1380s. This chapter explains how they worked after they existed.

Students should be aware of a geographic caution. The Ming state of this chapter, c. 1368–1398, was not the same territory as the modern People's Republic. It did not at first govern Yunnan fully or the Mongolian steppe; its northern border region was a zone of garrisons, trade, and negotiation with Mongol groups; and many local populations in the southwest and elsewhere were administered through native chieftains. The capital in this period was Nanjing, not Beijing. Developments associated with the later Yongle reign (the capital's move to Beijing, the maritime voyages of Chapter 42) lie beyond this chapter's scope and will appear only where needed to show the later significance of an institution.

The intellectual and religious background matters too. The Ming court endorsed Confucian learning, especially the interpretations associated with Zhu Xi (Chapter 34), as the basis of education and examinations, while also regulating Buddhist and Daoist institutions and sponsoring ritual practices at many levels. Yet the dynasty's founder came from a family of peasants and a Buddhist monastery, not from the scholar elite, and he never fully trusted that elite. The tension between an emperor suspicious of educated officials and a state that could not function without them is one of the chapter's recurring threads.

Section 41.1

The Ming Imperial Court

The imperial court was not simply a place where an emperor lived. It was the mechanism through which imperial status became political authority. A ruler's claim to rule depended on ritual, on control of written communication, on the power to appoint and dismiss, and on the public performance of hierarchy. Each of these was part of government.

The Ming palace complex at Nanjing, built in the late 1360s, concentrated these functions. The emperor lived and worked in the inner palace, while ministries and offices occupied separate compounds outside. This division matters because it reflected a basic distinction in Ming government between the emperor's household—empress, consorts, palace women, eunuchs, and servants—and the civil administration, staffed by examination-trained or recommended officials. The two overlapped constantly, since the emperor's decisions passed from the inner palace to the outer offices and back, but the Hongwu emperor insisted that they remain distinct. Sources report that he forbade eunuchs from interfering in government and limited their numbers and ranks. Whatever the exact wording of those prohibitions, later Ming history showed how difficult it was to keep palace staff out of politics, since they stood at the point where documents and information entered the emperor's presence.

Court ritual translated hierarchy into visible practice. Officials attended audiences at fixed hours, stood in assigned positions according to rank, and addressed the throne in prescribed language. Court dress, seating, and the order of entry told everyone who stood where. Such ceremonies were not merely decorative. They controlled who could see the emperor, who could speak, and who might be punished for a lapse in protocol. Access to the ruler was a scarce resource, and officials with reliable access gained influence.

The court also included the imperial family. Hongwu had many sons, and in the 1370s he began to grant them titles as princes, with generous stipends, residences in designated cities, and—in several cases, especially along the northern frontier—authority over troops. The princes were intended as trusted agents of the dynasty: relatives who would defend it without the autonomy of a feudal lord, since they were not given civil administration of their territories. Their status created a distinctive imperial-family structure that later generated serious conflict, but in this chapter's period it illustrates the emperor's preference for governing through kinship and personal loyalty.

Written documents were the court's lifeblood. Officials communicated with the throne through memorials, while the emperor spoke through edicts. Seals authenticated documents, and the movement of paper through designated offices determined what was formally "decided." A proclamation without the right seal had no force; an appointment not entered in the correct record could be questioned. Control over appointments—deciding who held which rank—was perhaps the emperor's most important power, because office meant status, income, and exemption from many burdens.

The designation of an heir was itself a major act of government. Hongwu named his eldest son, Zhu Biao, as heir apparent early in the reign and surrounded him with tutors and officials; when Zhu Biao died in 1392, the emperor designated his grandson instead, a decision whose consequences lie beyond this chapter. The episode shows why historians treat the palace as part of the political system rather than as a private household: succession, marriage alliances, the education of princes, and the status of consorts all affected who held power and how officials positioned themselves.

Historical Vocabulary
Memorial (zouzhang) and Edict (zhao, chi)

A memorial was a written report, request, recommendation, or criticism addressed by an official or subject to the emperor. An edict was a ruler's written pronouncement. Together they formed the basic circuit of imperial communication. In the Ming context, the number of memorials arriving at the palace was a measure of how personally the emperor tried to govern: each memorial required reading, decision, and often a written reply. When historians say that "government worked through documents," they mean literally that authority existed, for practical purposes, only when it was written down, sealed, transmitted, and filed.

The court is also the beginning of the chapter's central problem. If everything that mattered passed through the emperor, the emperor became both the source of power and a bottleneck. The next section examines that contradiction.

Section 41.2

Centralization and Imperial Decision-Making

As Chapter 40 explained, in 1380 Hongwu executed the chief councillor Hu Weiyong on charges of conspiracy, abolished the Secretariat that had coordinated the ministries since the Yuan, and declared that no one should again hold the post of chief councillor. This section does not retell that crisis. It asks what the reorganization changed in the daily working of government.

First, the Six Ministries reported directly to the emperor. Under the earlier system, the Secretariat had stood between the ministries and the throne, receiving their business, organizing it, and giving advice. With that body gone, each ministry had to bring its business to the emperor itself, and the emperor in turn had to decide matters that a chief minister would earlier have settled. Second, the flow of memorials to the throne increased, because there was no longer a senior official to filter them. The figure of 1,660 memorials on 3,391 matters in eight days, noted in the Chapter Opening, suggests how heavy the burden could be. Even if that figure includes routine items, it reveals a ruler overwhelmed by paper he considered his duty to read.

Third, Hongwu turned to assistants and substitutes. He experimented with advisory officials in the early 1380s and used scholars from the Hanlin Academy to help draft and review. These arrangements were informal and did not amount to a new chief ministry; later emperors would develop them into the Grand Secretariat, a story outside this chapter. Fourth, he used princes and trusted palace personnel to carry out tasks that senior ministers would otherwise have handled, such as military command on the northern frontier or inspection. The Imperial Bodyguard established in 1382 gave him a corps of guards who also performed investigative and prison functions, which could bypass regular judicial channels.

The result was a contradiction that gives the section its key insight: personal monarchy had administrative costs. The emperor wanted no powerful intermediary who might rival him, but an empire's business could not be processed by one man. Delegation was unavoidable; what Hongwu could control was the form it took. His solution was to multiply smaller intermediaries—scholars, guards, princes, inspectors, ministry clerks—none of whom held the broad authority that the chief councillor had held. The system could function while an energetic ruler worked long hours, and he did. It was far less certain how it would function under a ruler of different temperament, a question that later Ming history answered in several ways.

The workload also shaped the habits of officials. Because the emperor read memorials personally and could punish a misstep severely, officials had reason to write cautiously, to document their actions, and to pass uncertain decisions upward rather than risk acting on their own. The system therefore tended to generate paper and caution at the same time. Ministries accumulated precedents and procedures to protect themselves, and the clerks who maintained those files became more important as the emperor's direct supervision of detail became more demanding. Personal monarchy did not eliminate bureaucracy; it changed the incentives under which bureaucrats worked.

Process Feature

How a Memorial Reached the Emperor

The steps below are a broad outline of the Hongwu-era pattern, simplified from varied practice; details differed by type of document and by year.

  1. Drafting. An official or office prepares a report—on a flood, a tax shortfall, a disputed appointment, or a frontier incident—often with help from clerks who know the correct formulas.
  2. Transmission. The document travels through official channels, often by the post-station network, from county to prefecture to province, with each level adding its own endorsement.
  3. Reception. In the capital, the Office of Transmission (Tongzhengshi) receives and logs documents before passing them inward.
  4. Reading. The emperor reads selected memorials himself and, in the Hongwu period, often drafts replies; advisers or palace staff help sort and summarize.
  5. Decision. The ruler approves, rejects, or refers the matter to a ministry, sometimes with an instruction to investigate or punish.
  6. Implementation. The responsible ministry issues orders downward; supervising censors can review whether the order was carried out properly.
  7. Archiving. Copies enter ministry and palace records, which later historians use in writing the Veritable Records.

What this shows: every step involved people who could delay, summarize, alter emphasis, or ignore. The emperor's authority was real, but it passed through hands that were not his.

Historical Debate

Should Hongwu Government Be Called "Despotic"?

The question: Hongwu abolished the chief councillorship, purged officials on a large scale, used the Imperial Bodyguard, and issued detailed moral and legal pronouncements. Does "despotism" describe this regime accurately?

Interpretation A — Increased autocracy: Some historians argue that the Ming marked a sharp intensification of imperial autocracy compared with the Song and Tang. They point to the removal of senior ministers, the harsh punishment of officials, mass executions in several purge cases, public humiliation of officials, and the use of security agencies outside normal law.
Interpretation B — Ambitious but limited rule: Other historians emphasize that Hongwu's government was constrained by its dependence on local intermediaries and the practical limits of premodern communication. They stress that he also created detailed law, regulations for village moral instruction, and mechanisms for reviewing officials, and that "despotism" can obscure the ways bureaucrats, clerks, and local elites resisted, negotiated, or adapted orders.

Evidence: Advocates of A cite the Veritable Records, the Great Pronouncements, and accounts of purges; advocates of B draw on local records, evidence of noncompliance, and comparison with other premodern states. Both groups must be careful because Ming and later sources were shaped by dynastic and literati interests; accounts of cruelty may have been exaggerated by later scholars hostile to the founder, while official sources may understate violence.

Why it matters: The question shapes how historians explain later Ming politics and how they compare Chinese states with other early modern empires.

Student task: What kinds of additional evidence—such as records of non-compliance, local resistance, or surviving registers—would help decide how fully Hongwu's orders were carried out? Is "despotic" a description of intent, of practice, or of both?

Section 41.3

The Central Bureaucracy

The central bureaucracy was the machinery that turned an emperor's decisions into orders and information into decisions. A student should come away able to describe what an official in a Nanjing ministry actually did on an ordinary day.

The Ministry of Personnel managed appointments, ranks, evaluations, and the conferral of honors. It maintained the lists of who held which posts and the records of performance used for promotion or demotion. The Ministry of Revenue dealt with household registration, land, grain tax, transport, and state granaries. Consider what this meant in practice: a county's household and land information moved upward through prefecture and province, entered ministry records, shaped assessments of grain and labor, and ultimately mattered to a ministry trying to understand what resources the empire possessed. If a county's register was inaccurate, the error became the ministry's problem. When tax grain failed to arrive, the ministry traced responsibility down the same chain.

The Ministry of Rites handled state ritual, the examination system, foreign envoys' reception, and the regulation of religious institutions. The Ministry of War oversaw military appointments, personnel records, post stations, and the logistics of mobilization, though command over troops was divided among other bodies (see below). The Ministry of Punishments reviewed legal cases, interpreted the Code, and supervised prisons, while the Ministry of Works managed construction, waterways, artisan labor, and state manufacture. Each ministry was headed by a minister and divided into bureaus, with clerks performing the great bulk of paperwork.

Alongside the ministries stood the Censorate, which investigated and impeached officials and could comment on policy, and the Offices of Scrutiny, whose supervising secretaries were attached to the ministries and monitored their documents and implementation. Surveillance was thus built into the structure: those who issued orders were watched by those who checked whether orders were obeyed. Military command was reorganized in 1380 into five Chief Military Commissions, which administered the guard units, while the Ministry of War held appointment powers and troops could be mobilized only on imperial authority. The aim was to prevent any single general or office from controlling an army. The costs were slower coordination and reliance on the emperor for decisions during crises.

Officials were organized into nine ranks, each with upper and lower grades. Rank determined salary, dress, privileges, and place in ceremony. Officials were evaluated at regular intervals, with periodic assessments in the capital and the provinces leading to promotion, retention, demotion, or dismissal. They were commonly posted away from their native places, partly to reduce conflicts of interest, and moved between posts. Paperwork was extensive: appointment certificates, personnel files, reports, registers, copies of edicts, and returns on grain, labor, and prisoners. This reliance on records created a class of clerks whose knowledge of procedure gave them influence beyond their formal status, a theme taken up in 41.6.

Consider how a single problem moved through a ministry. Suppose a prefecture reported that tax grain due to a granary had not arrived. A clerk in the relevant bureau of the Ministry of Revenue would consult the register entries for the prefecture, compare the quota with earlier deliveries, and draft a letter of inquiry. A senior official would review the draft, and a supervising secretary from the Offices of Scrutiny might later check whether the reply had come. The prefect would in turn ask the counties, which would ask the district officers and grain captains. The inquiry thus traveled down the same chain that the grain had been meant to travel up. At each step, someone could explain, delay, or shift blame, and the ministry's picture of what had happened depended on what the lower levels chose to report.

Historical Evidence

How Historians Know How Ming Government Worked

Official histories and records. The Veritable Records (Shilu) of each reign chronicle imperial decisions, appointments, and events, compiled by later court historians. They are indispensable but reflect official perspectives and later editing. Statutes and compilations. The Great Ming Code and later compilations of administrative regulations show intended practice. Imperial writings. Hongwu's own pronouncements, such as the Great Pronouncements, reveal his aims and moral language. Local sources. Gazetteers, genealogies, tax documents, and legal cases show how institutions worked at ground level, though many surviving local gazetteers were compiled after the Hongwu era. Limits. Prescriptive sources tell us what the state wanted; surviving cases and local records tell us more about what happened; but few records survive from the earliest decades, and historians often reconstruct Hongwu-period practice partly from later evidence. Treat claims about "how the system worked" as combining the intended design and the practice that sources allow us to see.

Section 41.4

Provincial, Prefectural, and County Government

From Nanjing, authority descended through a territorial hierarchy. In 1376 the Ming replaced the Yuan's powerful provincial secretariats with a divided structure in which three offices—civil administration, judicial and surveillance, and military—shared authority in each province, so that no single provincial official controlled all functions. Provinces were divided into prefectures, which were divided into counties (and some departments of intermediate status). The area around Nanjing was administered directly from the capital rather than through a province. Well over a thousand counties formed the base of the pyramid.

Above the county, provincial government shows the logic of divided authority. In each province the Administration Commission handled civil government, finance, and population records; the Surveillance Commission oversaw justice and inspected officials; and the Regional Military Commission administered the guard units. The three offices were meant to check one another so that no provincial official could build an independent base, as the Yuan provincial secretariats had at times been able to do. The cost was coordination: when a problem such as a flood, a rebellion, or a tax shortfall required joint action, officials of equal standing had to negotiate or refer matters upward. Later Ming governments would add officials with wider supervisory authority over provinces, but that development belongs to a later period.

The prefecture stood between province and county. A prefect supervised several counties, reviewed the more serious legal cases and appeals from them, forwarded tax and register information, and answered to provincial officials. Departments of intermediate status followed similar patterns. For the magistrate, the prefecture was the next place where a dissatisfied litigant or an ambitious subordinate could carry a complaint, and so prefectural review acted as a check on county decisions, though one that depended heavily on the paperwork the county itself supplied.

The county magistrate was the center of this chapter's argument. He was the lowest regular official and represented imperial government to most ordinary subjects. A magistrate was responsible for tax collection, lawsuits and criminal cases, local infrastructure such as roads and irrigation, famine relief, schools, state rituals, household registration, and public order. A county might contain tens of thousands of people or, in prosperous regions, several hundred thousand. Yet the formal staff was tiny: a magistrate, a few subordinate officials such as an assistant magistrate and registrar, and a handful of others. Magistrates were typically outsiders, assigned away from home, who often did not speak the local dialect well and served limited terms.

Officials were posted away from their native areas and moved at intervals, which reduced the risk that kinship or local loyalty would distort their judgment. The same practice meant that a magistrate often arrived knowing little about local land, families, or customs, and that he might leave just as he began to understand them. Evaluations at intervals rewarded successful tax delivery and public order, which encouraged magistrates to emphasize measurable results. Sub-official staff, by contrast, often stayed for years. The clerks and runners of a county were formally low in status and in many places received little pay for the volume of work they performed, so they commonly relied on customary fees from the people they served. That arrangement kept offices staffed at low cost to the state but gave subordinates strong incentives to extract payment, and it is one reason complaints about clerks and runners recur throughout Ming sources.

These conditions led naturally to reliance on locally rooted subordinates. Clerks handled documents and knew procedures; runners delivered summonses, made arrests, and collected payments; village heads and tax-district officers handled collection in neighborhoods; elders and gentry intermediaries mediated disputes and communicated local views. Many clerks and runners were drawn from local families and held their positions for years, so they often understood a county better than the magistrate who passed through. This knowledge created power: they could speed or slow a case, interpret a document, or demand informal fees. A supposedly centralized government therefore encountered local society through imperfect intermediaries. The principle is essential: a centralized state still relied on local people to function.

The magistrate's duties extended beyond tax and law. He was expected to preside at state sacrifices, including rites to Confucius and local protective deities, to oversee schools, to inspect granaries and organize relief in bad years, to supervise the repair of roads, bridges, and embankments, and to see that the household registers were compiled. Each duty required local knowledge and local labor. The magistrate's formal authority was wide, but his practical capacity depended on how well he could secure the cooperation of those who actually lived in the county.

Section 41.5

Schools, Examinations, and Official Recruitment

How did people enter the governing class? The Ming used several routes, but the one that came to define the elite was the examination system. Its roots lay in the Sui–Tang and Song periods, when examinations expanded as a way to select officials trained in the classics. Under the Yuan, examinations were revived in 1315 but remained small, with places reserved by status group and differentiated requirements that advantaged Mongol and semu candidates in important respects, and only about a hundred successful candidates in each metropolitan session; much recruitment continued through hereditary privilege, clerical service, and recommendation. The Ming made examinations the principal prestige route into office, though it took some time to do so.

Hongwu ordered a network of state schools at prefectural, departmental, and county levels, where selected students received a stipend, instruction, and exemption from some obligations. Above them was the Imperial University in Nanjing. Students who passed qualifying examinations became licentiates (shengyuan), a lower degree that carried status and some privileges but not automatic office. Provincial examinations began in 1370–71 and the first metropolitan and palace examination was held in 1371; after suspension in the 1370s, while the dynasty relied heavily on recommendation, the system was restored in 1384, with the next palace examination in 1385, on a triennial schedule at three tiers: provincial examinations in the autumn, producing provincial graduates (juren); metropolitan examinations the following spring; and a palace examination presided over by the emperor, which ranked the successful candidates as jinshi.

The curriculum centered on the Four Books and Five Classics, studied with the commentaries of Zhu Xi and other approved interpreters, so that examination preparation reinforced a particular reading of Confucian orthodoxy. Candidates wrote essays and answered questions on the classics and on government. Competition was intense: quotas meant that only a small fraction of candidates advanced at each tier. Alternative routes persisted, including recommendation of talented individuals, admission to the Imperial University, and promotion of clerks after long service. In the early Ming, when the state needed administrators quickly, these routes supplied many officials.

It is tempting to describe the examinations as a simple open meritocracy. That description is misleading. The system widened competition beyond a narrow aristocracy and allowed some families from modest backgrounds to rise. But preparing for the examinations required literacy, years of study, a teacher, books, writing materials, and time without full-time farm or craft work. In practice these resources came mainly from landowning, official, merchant, or otherwise comfortable families. A family might spend a generation or two preparing a promising son, pooling resources through a lineage school or charitable fund. Success brought tax and labor privileges, social prestige, and the possibility of office; failure meant years of expense. The system thus opened a path but a narrow one, and the social cost of preparation shaped who could walk it.

An Examination Candidate's Path

Students may compare this system briefly with the smaller Yuan model. The Yuan examination offered only a modest number of degrees and did not dominate recruitment, whereas the Ming system, backed by state schools and regular schedules, became the main route to prestige. Even so, in the Hongwu years many officials still entered by other paths, and the examination order's dominance grew over the fifteenth century.

Section 41.6

Scholar-Officials, Clerks, and Local Elites

The educated elite was much larger than the serving bureaucracy. A county might contain only one magistrate and a few subordinate officials, but dozens or hundreds of men with some claim to learning and status. Students should distinguish several groups: officials currently holding office, who served by assignment, often away from home; examination degree holders, including jinshi, juren, and licentiates, some of whom held posts and many of whom did not; retired officials, who returned home with prestige and connections; local literati who taught, wrote, and advised; wealthy landowners, who might or might not have degrees; clerks, who had practical expertise but lower status; and lineage leaders, who managed kin groups' property and discipline.

Such people did much of the work of local order. Rules gave officials and certain degree-holders exemptions from some labor service, making official status valuable not only for prestige but for fiscal advantage. Elites also acted as informal government: they organized school and temple funding, supervised irrigation and road repair, managed charitable granaries or relief in hard years, mediated disputes before they reached the magistrate, and interpreted official demands to ordinary households. In tax collection, the early Ming made delegation explicit through the grain captains (liangzhang) appointed in 1371, wealthy households charged with collecting and delivering tax grain from their districts. The system was applied chiefly in tax-rich central-eastern regions, including parts of Jiangnan, where dense populations and large landowners made it practical; it was never uniform across the empire. This was a bargain: the state gained a collector who could be held responsible, and the elite household gained status and opportunity, but also the risk of punishment and the temptation to profit.

Clerks occupied a particular and ambiguous position. Officially of low status, they held the knowledge needed to run offices. Hongwu was suspicious of them and regulated their terms and promotions, yet he could not do without them. Many clerks served honestly; others used their positions for informal fees or favoritism. The same pattern appeared among the runners who enforced the magistrate's orders. The result was a layered structure in which formal authority and practical knowledge did not coincide.

The state's relationship with local elites was not uniformly trusting. The regime was wary of wealthy families in Jiangnan, where it had fought its most determined rivals, and it taxed that region heavily and, according to Ming records, relocated some wealthy households to Nanjing and other places. At the same time it granted privileges to office-holders and degree-holders. Some landowners sought to protect land by placing it under the names of privileged relatives or by registering dependents under exempt households, and officials were repeatedly told to prevent such evasion. The elite's relationship with the state was thus both cooperative and competitive: elites wanted the benefits of status, and the state wanted their services without surrendering revenue.

This layer is essential because the empire did not consist simply of an emperor ruling peasants through magistrates. Between formal office and ordinary households stood a substantial world of locally influential people, whose interests sometimes coincided with the state's and sometimes diverged. A local gentleman might help the magistrate keep order and also shelter his tenants from heavy demands.

Section 41.7

Law, Justice, and Government Discipline

Law was how Ming authority was enforced and contested. The Great Ming Code (Da Ming lü), a major version of which was promulgated in 1374, revised in 1389, and finalized in 1397, organized its articles under headings corresponding to general principles and the Six Ministries—personnel, household and marriage, rites, military, punishment, and public works—so that the law mirrored the structure of government. It prescribed five main punishments: light beating with the bamboo, heavy beating, penal servitude, exile, and death, with penalties graded by the offense and the status of those involved. Hongwu also issued supplementary pronouncements, including the Great Pronouncements (Da gao), which set out stricter penalties and exhortations that sometimes went beyond the Code.

In practice, the county magistrate was the first judge. He investigated crimes, heard civil disputes over land, debts, marriage, and inheritance, and decided minor cases himself; more serious cases were reviewed by prefects, provincial officials, and ultimately the Ministry of Punishments and the emperor in capital cases. Magistrates relied on clerks to prepare files and on runners to bring in parties, which meant that procedure could be shaped before the magistrate ever heard a case. Many disputes were settled through mediation by elders or lineage leaders, and officials often encouraged this, both because they were overburdened and because a settled quarrel was less likely to disturb order.

The state also relied on collective responsibility: neighbors in registration units could be held responsible for reporting crime and for each other's obligations. At the same time, Hongwu used the law against officials. The Code and associated pronouncements imposed severe penalties for corruption, including execution for embezzlement above relatively modest sums, and the Censorate, supervising officials, and informers monitored misconduct. Hongwu encouraged ordinary people to report abusive officials and clerks, and in some instances authorized villagers to bring offenders to the capital. Such provisions show the state attempting to use the population as a check on its own agents.

The handling of a serious criminal case illustrates the process. A complaint reached the magistrate, who sent runners to detain suspects and witnesses and, in a homicide, arranged an inquest of the body. Confession carried great weight in Ming legal procedure, and torture within defined limits could be used to obtain it from suspects, which gave runners and clerks further opportunities for abuse. Cases carrying heavier penalties were forwarded with the file to the prefecture and onward for review, and death sentences required the emperor's confirmation. Defendants and families could petition higher officials, and circuit censors inspecting a province might receive complaints. These steps provided some protection against error, but each also depended on clerical files and on the willingness of officials to challenge colleagues.

Students should keep a distinction between statute and enforcement. The Code described harsh penalties, but how often they were applied, and against whom, depended on investigators, clerks, local conditions, and the emperor's attention. Powerful households might avoid punishment through connections; poor litigants might find the process expensive even when the law favored them. Petitions to higher officials were available but costly in time and money. The value of law lay partly in its predictability and partly in its use as a lever: parties used it, delayed it, and negotiated around it.

Section 41.8

Household Registration, Taxation, and Labor Service

The central concept of this section is that registration converted people and land into state obligations. Chapter 40 introduced the Yellow Registers, Fish-Scale Registers, and the lijia system as part of reconstruction. Here we see how they operated as ongoing institutions.

Historical Vocabulary
Lijia (里甲)

The lijia system organized households into units of about 110: ten households whose heads served in annual rotation as the unit's headman, and the others grouped in smaller sub-units. Its duties included helping compile the household register, collecting taxes, and maintaining order and moral instruction. The term matters because it shows how the state gave a mutual-responsibility structure to communities, using neighbors as agents. In practice, village boundaries and actual communities did not always match the neat arithmetic of the system, and units varied by region.

Every ten years, households submitted information that was compiled into Yellow Registers, with copies sent upward and a master set stored in Nanjing. A household's entry recorded its head, members, land, property such as buildings and oxen, and its hereditary occupational classification—civilian, military, artisan, or another category. Land was recorded in the Fish-Scale Registers, which mapped parcels by location and owner, in principle linking each plot to a taxpayer. Together the registers told the state who existed, what they held, and what they owed.

What a Yellow Register Recorded

Reading a Household Entry

  • The household head and members: names, relationships, and ages of males and females counted in the household.
  • Occupational registration: civilian, military, artisan, or other hereditary category, determining the type of service owed.
  • Property: land, houses, and draft animals, with changes since the last register shown in categories such as previously held, newly acquired, sold, and currently held.
  • Obligations: the tax grain and labor assessed on the household.

Using the source: A register is a statement the state wanted to be true. Historians treat entries as evidence of how the state categorized people, not as a direct photograph of how they lived.

The compilation of a register began at the bottom. Household heads supplied information about members, land, and property to the headmen of their lijia units, who assembled it for the county; the county checked and compiled the returns and sent them upward, with copies for different levels of government. A household certificate system introduced earlier, in 1370, had already tried to establish a baseline. Because the entire process was repeated only every ten years, the registers were out of date almost as soon as they were finished, and officials had to rely on supplementary reports and local knowledge between compilations.

The state's demands took several forms. The land tax was collected mostly in grain, in two installments, summer and autumn, with rates varying by land category and by region. Heavy burdens fell on parts of Jiangnan, notably around Suzhou and Songjiang, where large amounts of land had passed into state hands during Hongwu's defeat of rival regimes and where assessments were set high. Labor service required adult males and households to provide work: serving in the lijia system, transporting grain, repairing waterworks and roads, and performing other tasks. Households also owed local responsibilities and, where relevant, special duties tied to craft or salt production. Regional differences were large; households in a fertile, heavily assessed region experienced the state quite differently from those in a poorer upland.

Labor service was assessed according to household resources. Households were graded, in principle, by the number of adult males and the amount of property, and heavier service was meant to fall on higher grades. Service took many forms: headmen's duties within the lijia, transport of tax grain to granaries, work on waterworks and roads, and tasks for local offices. For a poor household, the loss of an adult male's labor for weeks could be as damaging as the grain itself, since it removed hands from the fields at critical seasons.

Tax grain had to be moved, and transport imposed its own burdens. Delivering grain to distant granaries required boats, carts, porters, and time, and sources suggest that in practice collection often involved charges beyond the nominal assessment, including costs of measuring, storing, and transporting grain and fees to clerks. These additional costs tended to fall hardest on those least able to bargain. Officials and degree-holders, in contrast, received exemptions from some service, which meant that the burden of any given quota was spread across fewer households than the register suggested. As Chapter 40 noted, the state also granted tax relief to newly settled and newly cleared land in order to encourage recovery, so the actual pressure on households varied with local conditions and with the stage of reconstruction.

Reality diverged from the registers. Households concealed land or members to reduce obligations; clerks altered entries for a fee or favor; powerful families kept land off the books; and the population changed—through births, deaths, migration, flight, and division of households—faster than ten-year registers could follow. The state's population count of about 60 million in 1393, almost certainly an undercount, reflects both these gaps and the limits of administrative capacity. Registration, in short, was a powerful technique for creating obligations, but it was never a perfect mirror of society.

Cause and Effect

Why Registers Diverged from Reality

Causes

Short-term: incentives to hide land and people to reduce taxes; clerical fees and favors. Long-term: population movement, household division, land sales, and the sheer difficulty of keeping ten-year records current across a vast territory.

The pattern

Households and officials adjusted the information they provided, while the state continued to treat registers as the basis for assessment.

Effects

Obligations fell unevenly: households that could not conceal property bore heavier burdens, and the state's resource picture was incomplete. Later reformers would repeatedly struggle with the gap.

Why not one cause?

The divergence was the combined result of social change, administrative limits, and the interests of people at every level; no single "corruption" explains it.
Section 41.9

Military Households and Hereditary Service

If registration shaped civilian obligation, the military system did the same for soldiers. Chapter 40 explained why it emerged from war and Yuan precedent. Here the question is how a family actually lived inside it.

Historical Vocabulary
Weisuo (衛所) — Guards and Battalions

The weisuo was the Ming garrison system. A wei (guard) was a large unit, nominally around 5,600 men, divided into battalions (qianhusuo) of about 1,120 and companies of about 112. Soldiers came from registered military households that supplied one man at a time, with replacements drawn from the family when he died, fled, or was discharged. The system joined three things: military manpower, hereditary status, and state-supported farming (tuntian). The numbers are standard ones, but actual strength varied and often fell below the paper establishment.

A military household was registered as such in the Yellow Register, and its obligation passed from generation to generation. When the designated soldier died or deserted, the unit sought a replacement from the household or its kin; the state could trace such replacements through the registers. In 1388 Hongwu ordered additional specialized records, a rectification and replacement register and a dedicated military-household register, to track deserters, deaths, household males, and replacement obligations. Soldiers were stationed in garrison communities across the empire—along the northern frontier, near the capital, at coastal and inland strategic points—so many families were relocated from their home districts. A soldier's family might live in or near the garrison, farm the unit's assigned land, and raise children who would eventually enter the same service.

The military colonies (tuntian) were meant to make garrisons largely self-supporting. Soldiers were assigned plots, typically on the order of fifty mu each, and in many units a majority farmed while a smaller portion stood guard, with proportions varying by location—more guarding on the frontier, more farming in the interior. Hongwu reportedly boasted that his army could be fed without burdening the people, and the system achieved real success in the early decades, supplying grain and reducing dependence on civilian taxation. Frontier garrisons also relied on merchants delivering grain in exchange for salt certificates, which connected the military system to commerce.

Garrison units had officers, usually hereditary as well, with ranks and privileges, and their own administrative apparatus parallel to the civil government. The result was a society partly separate from ordinary counties: military households had their own registration, courts for some matters, and obligations. Relations with civilians involved cooperation and friction, since garrisons depended on local markets and land and sometimes came into conflict with neighbors over resources.

Daily life within the system combined civilian and military features. Soldiers received grain rations and, according to varying regulations, some supplies of clothing, and their families relied on the unit's farmland for much of their food. Garrison seats were often walled towns, and some became permanent settlements with markets, temples, and mixed populations. Officers belonged to a separate hierarchy from civil officials, but they dealt with magistrates over land, supplies, and disputes, and the division of authority between the Ministry of War, the military commissions, and the civil administration meant that cooperation could not be taken for granted. The military household thus lived at the intersection of two bureaucratic worlds.

Tensions were visible even in the founding generation. Desertion required constant replacement, and the registers needed constant correction. Officers could exploit soldiers' labor, and soldiers' plots could be taken or sold, creating land disputes. Hereditary service tied family fortunes to a duty that might be dangerous, poorly rewarded, or far from home. These were the seeds of problems that later became severe. This section does not trace that later decline; it notes that the system's logic—stable supply of soldiers through family obligation—was already vulnerable to the gap between registered obligation and real households.

Section 41.10

Family, Lineage, and Community

We now shift deliberately from the state to society. Yet the argument of this chapter is that families and communities were not simply private structures outside government. They helped organize property, morality, taxation, service, education, and local order, and they also pursued their own interests.

The typical Ming household was small by the standards of the ideal. Elite writings praised multi-generational families living together, but most households consisted of a couple, their unmarried children, and perhaps a surviving elder, with larger households more common among the wealthy. The family was patrilineal: descent and inheritance passed through male lines, and a married woman joined her husband's household. Property was usually divided among sons, with daughters receiving a dowry rather than a share of land. Marriage was arranged by families, with exchange of gifts and obligations, and its purposes included alliance, labor, and continuation of the ancestral line. Rites for ancestors—offerings at home altars, grave visits, seasonal ceremonies—tied families to their dead and expressed obligations across generations.

Beyond the household stood the lineage, a group claiming common descent from a known ancestor. In the early Ming, lineage institutions were generally modest, and historians generally see formal lineage organization with ancestral halls, shared estates, and elaborate genealogies as growing more widespread later in the dynasty. Even so, kin groups in the Hongwu years kept genealogies, helped relatives in need, and managed some shared property. In some places they supported schools for promising boys.

The life cycle of a household shaped both property and obligation. When sons married and parents aged or died, households were commonly divided, with each son receiving a roughly equal share of the land and goods. Repeated division could fragment holdings, so families developed strategies: one son might be educated for the examinations, another might trade, and a childless couple might adopt an heir from the male kin. These choices affected registration, because a divided household became several taxpaying households and because a family might report itself in the way that most reduced its burden.

Beyond kin, communities organized cooperation in institutional form. Villages and neighborhoods maintained temples and festivals, shared water for irrigation, lent grain or money in lean seasons, and used elders to settle quarrels.

The state used these structures. The lijia relied on village units, and elders helped resolve small disputes. The Hongwu regime also increasingly added state-sponsored moral instruction to this existing fabric: late in the reign, in 1397, the emperor ordered the Six Maxims, which instructed villagers in filial conduct, respect for elders, neighborhood harmony, family instruction, proper livelihood, and avoidance of wrongdoing, and the 1398 Jiaomin bangwen reiterated the system, with elders expected to proclaim such teachings publicly. Local pavilions served distinct functions: shenming pavilions, established from 1372, handled minor disputes and publicly exposed offenders, while separate “Declaring Goodness” (jingshan) pavilions displayed exemplary local people and conduct. A famous multi-generational kin community, such as the Zheng family of Pujiang, was praised as a model. Villagers received these measures in different ways: some used them to support local authority, while others ignored them or turned them to their own advantage. Families and communities also resisted: they concealed land, resisted unfair assessments, shielded kin from punishment, and bargained with clerks. Their interests overlapped with the state's but were not identical. Seen from the household's vantage, the state was both a protector, in matters of law and relief, and a claimant on grain and labor.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Family and Community from the Yuan to the Early Ming

What changed? The state tied household registration more closely to hereditary occupation and mutual responsibility through the lijia, and promoted Confucian moral instruction at the village level.

What stayed the same? Patrilineal descent, partible inheritance, ancestral rites, and reliance on kin and neighbors in daily life.

Who experienced change most directly? Households newly registered in hereditary categories, especially military and artisan households, whose family strategies were reshaped by state obligations.

Who experienced continuity? Peasant and landowning families whose family structure and rites changed little from earlier centuries.

Why it matters: It shows that state power changed obligations more readily than family life itself.

Section 41.11

Women, Gender, and Household Economy

A central methodological question governs this section: what do prescriptive texts tell us about ideals, and what other evidence tells us about women's lived experience? Much surviving writing about women was composed by men and stressed obedience, chastity, and domestic duty. The classical texts that shaped gender ideals, such as instructions for women and collections of exemplary women's biographies, continued to be read and cited. The state also honored chaste widows and filial women. Such texts reveal what elite men and officials wished to promote. They do not by themselves reveal what women did.

Other evidence shows more variety. Legal provisions protected a woman's dowry as her own property in many circumstances and allowed widows to manage their husband's property until a male heir came of age, though male relatives often pressed their own claims. Widows could remarry in law, but Ming law, retaining a Yuan rule, generally left a remarrying widow's original dowry with her first husband's family, which linked property rules to growing pressure for widow chastity; moral opinion also favored chastity and rewarded long widowhood, and pressure on widows varied with class, region, and family circumstance. Marriage involved gifts and dowry, transferring resources between families, and a woman's dowry might help her new household or provide her with a degree of security. Women also participated in religious life as lay devotees, nuns, and ritual specialists, though some regulations sought to limit women's movement in public religious settings.

Law and custom also shaped women's legal presence. Women could be parties in disputes, though they were often represented by male kin, and widows defending their late husbands' property appear frequently in later case records. Girls might be betrothed young, and in some regions poor families placed a young girl in her future husband's household to be raised there. Women's religious life offered another sphere of activity, from household devotion to temple festivals and, for some, life as a nun. Because few women's own writings survive from this period, historians must combine legal, economic, and local sources to recover their experience, remaining aware that each source type reveals a different side of it.

The household economy depended heavily on women's work. In farming households women tended gardens and animals, processed grain, and often worked in the fields, especially in rice regions. Textile production—spinning, weaving, and in some regions silk work and, as it spread, cotton—was commonly women's labor, and cloth was important both for household use and for sale, and in some cases for tax payment. Hongwu's encouragement of mulberry, hemp, and cotton planting depended on this labor. Midwives, healers, and market sellers added to women's economic roles. Elite women could receive an education, manage large households, and sometimes write poetry, though they had fewer official outlets.

Experience varied by status. Elite women had education and resources but also strict expectations of seclusion and propriety. Peasant women worked outdoors and had less formal protection but often more daily mobility. Artisan and merchant households relied on women's labor in workshops and shops. Military households depended on women to run the household while men served. Poor women faced risks of sale as servants, concubines, or in extreme cases of famine. Practices such as foot-binding were present in some elite and urban settings but unevenly distributed, and historians cannot assume they applied to all women in early Ming society.

Reading Sources on Women

Prescription vs. Practice

Prescriptive sources — instruction texts for women, exemplary-women biographies, honorific edicts for chaste widows — tell us what ideals the state and elite promoted. Other evidence — legal provisions on property and inheritance, tax and textile records, marriage and property contracts, epitaphs and genealogies, gazetteer entries, and surviving writings by women — shows that women managed property, produced goods for exchange, and negotiated within family constraints.

Using both: a source praising a widow's refusal to remarry may also show that she managed a household and its property for decades. Historians read such sources for their ideals and for incidental evidence of what women did.

  2. What kinds of records might show women's economic activity even when texts emphasize domestic virtue?

Section 41.12

Social Hierarchy, Occupation, and Everyday Life

What did the Ming social order look like from below and in everyday life? The traditional ideal ranked scholars first, then farmers, artisans, and merchants. The Ming state added its own formal classification: hereditary occupational registers for civilians, soldiers, artisans, and other service categories. Sumptuary regulations sought to display status through dress and housing, restricting, for instance, the finery that merchants and commoners might wear.

Reality was more fluid. The Ming order included peasants, ranging from smallholders to tenants and laborers; artisans; merchants; soldiers and their families; servants and bonded dependents, with poor households sometimes selling children or members in hard times; wealthy landholders who combined land, trade, and sometimes office; and poorer households that survived through diversified work and kin help. The paragraphs that follow show how these groups experienced the system in different settings.

Rural communities were organized around villages, periodic markets, temples, and festivals. Market towns connected farmers and artisans to wider exchange, and the state itself encouraged farming and cloth-crop planting while restricting some forms of commerce. Housing, diet, and clothing varied by region and status: rice and fish in the south, millet, wheat, and noodles in the north; houses of rammed earth, timber, or brick; and cloth of hemp, cotton, or silk according to means. Everyday religion mixed Buddhist, Daoist, and local cult practices, with officials regulating temples while villagers maintained local gods and ancestral rites.

Rural life varied with environment. In the rice-growing south and the Jiangnan lowlands, intensive cultivation and, where possible, multiple crops supported dense populations, while in the north households grew millet, wheat, and other dry-land crops and faced greater risk from drought and flood. Many peasants owned small plots, others rented land from larger holders and paid rent in kind or cash, and many combined farming with spinning, weaving, or small trade. The state encouraged local granaries to hold reserves against famine, but their success depended on local management and honest accounting.

Artisans and merchants occupied positions that formal ranking only partly captured. Registered artisans owed periodic service in state workshops, while independent craftsmen produced textiles, ceramics, paper, metalwork, and other goods for local and regional markets. Merchants, despite their low formal rank, mattered to the state: those delivering grain to frontier garrisons in exchange for salt certificates, for example, linked commerce to military supply. Sumptuary rules sought, with limited success, to keep dress and display in line with status.

Movement was also a feature of the early Ming social order. The state organized the resettlement of farmers into depopulated parts of North China, relocated some wealthy households from Jiangnan, and assigned military families to distant garrisons. Individuals and families also moved on their own to find land, work, or safety. Such movement meant that registered status and actual residence did not always coincide, which added to the difficulty of keeping records accurate and reinforced the central point of this chapter: that the Ming state attempted to fix people in categories in a society that continually moved across them.

Formal categories never perfectly captured society. A family could include a scholar, a merchant, and a farmer; a military household could farm and trade; and migration, marriage, and the sale of land blurred registered status. Mobility was possible but limited, usually working across generations through education, trade, or good fortune. Regions differed sharply: densely populated, commercial Jiangnan was unlike the war-depleted north or the upland southwest. Students should avoid treating the capital's or an elite household's world as typical.

Regional and Frontier Context

The institutions of this chapter were applied unevenly. In Jiangnan, dense population, state-held lands, and strong scholarly families produced heavy tax assessments and a close relationship between local elites and officials. In North China, depopulated by war and flood, the state encouraged resettlement and farming, and military garrisons were prominent. In Sichuan, the southeast coast, and Lingnan, county government extended more fully into some areas than others. In the southwest, native chieftains remained key intermediaries, confirmed by the court but largely self-governing, and the state's reach was thin. Along the northern frontier, garrisons stood between the Ming and Mongol groups, and trade, negotiation, and conflict shaped local life. Frontier communities experienced the state through soldiers, merchants, and officials very differently from interior villagers. The Ming also maintained relations with the Mongol steppe, the Tibetan plateau, and the Jurchens of the northeast through diplomacy, trade, and military pressure rather than uniform county administration.

Section 41.13

Chapter Review: How the Ming State Governed Society

This section synthesizes rather than introduces new material. The Ming order can be pictured as a chain: emperor → ministries and censors → provinces and prefectures → county magistrate → clerks, runners, and local elites → registered household → family and community. At each link, authority passed downward and information passed upward, and at each link people with interests of their own shaped the result.

Where was Ming power genuinely direct? It was direct in the emperor's control over appointments, punishment of officials, and major decisions; in the ministries' ability to issue orders and monitor compliance through censors; in the law's capacity to impose penalties; and in the registers' power to attach obligations to households. Where did it depend on intermediaries? At the county level, where clerks, runners, headmen, and elites made taxation and justice work; in local order, maintained through mediation and kin; in the military system, where families supplied replacements; and in education, where families paid for preparation.

The final takeaway is that the Ming possessed considerable administrative reach, but not a modern bureaucracy capable of directly managing every subject. It organized, recorded, and disciplined local society more thoroughly than many earlier states, yet it still functioned through partnership—and sometimes conflict—with the people it governed.

The following checkpoint (see the Review materials below) asks students to map each institution in this chapter onto the chain above and identify, for each, whether the state's power was direct or mediated.

China and the Wider World

The Ming's institutions were part of a wider story of how agrarian empires tried to know and control their populations. The Mongol world had practiced registration and hereditary occupational categories, and the Ming adopted and reshaped these. Korea's Joseon dynasty, founded in 1392, drew on Neo-Confucian ideas and examination models that Chinese practice had influenced, and Japan and Vietnam encountered Chinese legal and administrative models in their own ways. The Ming court's reception of foreign envoys through the Ministry of Rites and its regulation of foreign trade connected its internal order to diplomacy, though these topics are developed in Chapter 42. Salt and grain trade connected garrisons to merchant networks across regions. Rather than viewing foreign influences as imposed on China, students should see the Ming state as an active participant in a shared Eurasian repertoire of administration.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the early Ming state governed an empire and organized its society. Its central argument is that Ming government was highly centralized at the top but deeply dependent on intermediaries below. The emperor claimed extraordinary authority, but administering millions of households required clerks, magistrates, village headmen, elites, soldiers, and families.

At the center, the court converted imperial status into political authority through ritual, access, written documents, and control over appointments. The emperor's household and the civil administration were meant to remain separate, though palace staff and imperial relatives were never fully excluded from politics.

The abolition of the Secretariat in 1380 gave the emperor direct relations with the Six Ministries but imposed a heavy workload, illustrated by the eight days of memorials in 1384. Hongwu's solution was to multiply smaller intermediaries rather than create a single chief minister, showing that personal monarchy had administrative costs.

The Six Ministries, the Censorate, and the military commissions formed the machinery of the center. Officials were ranked, evaluated, transferred, and monitored through records, and clerks supplied the practical knowledge on which offices depended.

Below the center, provinces, prefectures, and counties carried authority downward. The county magistrate, with a small staff, depended on clerks, runners, headmen, elders, and local elites. This dependence demonstrated that a centralized state still relied on local people.

Schools and examinations offered a route into office that expanded competition but demanded resources only some families possessed. The larger educated elite—degree-holders, retired officials, landowners, teachers, and lineage leaders—mediated between households and government. The Great Ming Code and related regulations provided a legal framework, while magistrates, mediators, and petitioners made it function.

Registration converted households and land into obligations, and military households supplied hereditary service. Both systems depended on records that could not perfectly match changing realities, and both bound families to the state while giving them reasons to evade or bargain.

Families, lineages, and communities organized property, ritual, education, and local order, while women's work in agriculture, textiles, and household management sustained the household economy. Ming society's formal categories never perfectly captured its mobility and diversity.

The chapter's broader lesson is that the Ming possessed considerable administrative reach but not a modern bureaucracy that could directly manage every subject. Understanding this balance prepares students for Chapter 42, which turns from internal organization to maritime and foreign connections.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a regime that had been built through war learned to rule. The institutions of the early Ming—ministries, registers, schools, the Code, garrisons—provided the framework within which later Ming politics, economy, and society developed. Understanding them helps explain why the dynasty could administer a huge population and why it struggled when the information and obligations on which it relied drifted away from reality.

The chapter also reveals larger themes of Chinese history: the partnership between imperial government and local society, the importance of documents and records, the role of education in forming an elite, and the ways families and communities adapted to state demands. It reminds students that rulers' intentions and lived experience were different things, and that historians must read sources for both.

In a wider frame, the Ming's attempts to register and classify a population belong to a global history of states learning to count and govern. Comparing them with Mongol, Korean, and other Eurasian practices shows both shared techniques and distinct choices.

Chapter 42 turns outward, to the Yongle emperor, Zheng He, and the Ming's maritime and diplomatic connections. Students should carry forward the understanding that Ming power was always exercised through partnerships and negotiation, and that Chinese history is regionally varied, politically changing, creative, and contested rather than a story of a single uninterrupted state.
