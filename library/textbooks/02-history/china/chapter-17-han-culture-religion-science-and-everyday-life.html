Part III — The Han Empire
17

Han Culture, Religion, Science, and Everyday Life

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

In 168 BCE, the family of the Marquis of Dai sealed a tomb near Changsha, in the south. Inside were lacquered dishes and food for the dead, silk garments so fine that one gauze robe weighs less than fifty grams, a painted banner of the cosmos, and, in a neighboring tomb of the same family, rolls of silk inscribed with philosophical texts, medical recipes, diagrams of the body, and maps. More than two thousand years later, archaeologists opened them. What they found was not a single worldview. It was a household's attempt to secure the afterlife, understand the body, preserve knowledge, and display status, all at once.

Chapters 14 through 16 explained how the Han state survived, functioned, and expanded. This chapter asks a different question: how did people in the Han world understand the cosmos, preserve knowledge, educate elites, worship, heal bodies, calculate, communicate, bury the dead, and live ordinary lives? It studies intellectual, cultural, technological, religious, and social life across roughly four centuries, from the early Han to the second century CE.

The chapter's argument is that Han culture emerged from the selective preservation and transformation of earlier traditions. Confucian learning gained increasing institutional importance, but Han intellectual and religious life remained plural: cosmological theories, ritual traditions, ancestor veneration, local cults, technical arts, medicine, mathematics, historical writing, and new religious currents coexisted and interacted. Knowledge was not separated neatly into modern categories such as religion, science, philosophy, and technology, and the everyday lives of most people differed sharply from the literary world preserved by elite writers. A guiding principle follows: the Han should not be made “Confucian China” all at once.

The evidence is unusually rich and unusually uneven. Transmitted works, above all Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian and Ban Gu's Book of Han, preserve the classical, historical, and philosophical traditions that later readers valued. Excavated manuscripts from tombs, such as those at Mawangdui, and from dry frontier sites reveal texts that were not preserved in the later canon, including medical, mathematical, and divinatory works. Tomb furnishings, reliefs, and household objects show material life and belief. Each source type has a geography and a bias: much of what survives comes from wealthy tombs and from arid sites where wood and bamboo endured, while the lives of ordinary farmers left few traces.

What changed across the Han? The court and educated families increasingly invested in classical learning and institutions for teaching it. Historical writing took new forms. Cosmological theories were elaborated and used in political argument. Medicine, mathematics, astronomy, and technology developed as bodies of practical knowledge, and writing media diversified, with paper gradually entering a world of wood, bamboo, and silk. What continued? Ancestor veneration, divination, ritual, household production, and inherited Zhou and Warring States texts remained foundations of daily life and thought.

Who was affected? Court scholars and officials, teachers and students, historians, physicians and healers, artisans, ritual specialists, women in households of varied status, and farming families whose religious and material practices evolved but rarely appear in literary sources. This chapter connects to the larger story of the book: it follows Chapter 15's account of government and society and Chapter 16's account of frontiers, and it prepares for Chapter 18's account of crisis, in which classical learning, religious movements, and factional politics play important roles. It also looks forward: many Han texts, institutions, and ideas were transmitted, transformed, and sometimes only retrospectively associated with the Han by later generations, a process this chapter treats with care.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the growth of classical learning and imperial education in the Han, and distinguish recruiting officials, educating scholars, and the later examination system.
  2. Analyze how Sima Qian constructed history, and evaluate the Records of the Grand Historian as both a source and a work with a viewpoint.
  3. Describe Han cosmological ideas such as yin-yang and the Five Phases, and explain how they connected nature, rulership, and political argument.
  4. Interpret Han religious life, ancestor veneration, popular belief, and burial practices without imposing later categories.
  5. Assess Han medicine as an evolving body of knowledge, using excavated manuscripts and distinguishing them from later transmitted canons.
  6. Evaluate Han mathematics, astronomy, and technology as systems of practical knowledge rather than a list of inventions.
  7. Explain the development of paper and its relation to bamboo, wood, and silk, avoiding the myth of a single inventor.
  8. Describe family and everyday life across social groups, distinguishing prescriptive texts from evidence of practice.
  9. Distinguish Han origins and development from later standardization and retrospective attribution.
  10. Evaluate competing interpretations of how “Confucian” the Han was, using transmitted and excavated evidence.
  11. Connect education, cosmology, medicine, mathematics, writing, and burial as parts of one cultural world.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did classical learning become more important to Han government and elite life, and what limits did that importance have?
  2. How did a historian such as Sima Qian construct an account of the past, and why must historians treat him as a source with a viewpoint?
  3. How did Han thinkers connect Heaven, natural events, and human government?
  4. What did religion look like in Han households, communities, and tombs, and what can burial evidence not tell us?
  5. How do excavated medical manuscripts change our understanding of Han medicine?
  6. How did mathematics, astronomy, and technology serve administration, agriculture, and craft?
  7. What does the history of paper show about how new technologies spread?
  8. Why is it misleading to describe Han culture as simply “Confucian” or as the origin point of all later Chinese civilization?

Historical Context

The Han inherited an extraordinarily rich intellectual landscape. Chapters 11 and 12 described the Warring States thinkers, Confucius and his followers, Daoist writers, Mohists, Legalists, strategists, and others, whose competing ideas shaped the terms of later debate. Chapter 13 described the Qin attempt to control learning, including the burning of certain books in 213 BCE and the punishment of scholars, and it emphasized that texts survived in memory, in private hands, and in the imperial archives. After Qin's collapse, Han rulers and scholars recovered, reconstructed, and transmitted texts, and the recovery itself shaped what later generations knew as the classics.

The early Han court was intellectually plural. Rulers such as Emperors Wen and Jing, and the powerful Empress Dowager Dou, favored approaches associated with Huang-Lao thought, a current linking the legendary Yellow Emperor and Laozi with restraint in government, alongside Legalist expertise in law and administration and various forms of classical scholarship. Kings such as Liu An of Huainan patronized scholars who produced syncretic works, most famously the Huainanzi. Under Emperor Wu, as Chapter 15 noted, the court increasingly patronized classical scholars and founded an Imperial University in 124 BCE. But the Han never abandoned the administrative and legal inheritance of Qin, and it never ceased to draw on many traditions.

Han cultural life was also shaped by material conditions. The agrarian economy of Chapter 15 supported a growing class of literate families with time and resources for study. The empire's communications and record-keeping systems fostered practical arts such as accounting, surveying, and calendrical calculation. Iron production, textile crafts, lacquerware, and building technology expanded. Burial customs involved considerable investment, and a wide range of objects, texts, and images were placed in tombs. Regions differed: the old cultural centers of Qi and Lu in the east were known for classical learning, Chu and the south for distinctive religious and artistic traditions, Guanzhong and the capitals for court culture, and the frontiers for a mixture of soldiers, settlers, and local peoples.

Students should be cautious about categories. The words “Confucianism,” “Daoism,” “religion,” and “science” carry modern assumptions. Han writers used other categories: “classical scholars” (ru), specialists in techniques and arts (fangshu), masters of recipes and methods, and many others. Knowledge about the body, the heavens, and the afterlife was often interconnected. The chapter uses modern terms as convenient labels while trying to explain what Han people themselves distinguished and combined.

Section 17.1

Confucian Learning and Imperial Education

Chapter 15 explained how men entered Han government: by recommendation, summons, clerical advancement, privilege, and study at the Imperial University. This section asks a different question, about the intellectual formation increasingly associated with educated service. Three things must be kept apart: recruiting officials, educating scholars, and the later civil-service examination system of Chapters 26 and 31. Han institutions connected the first two in growing degree, but they did not yet amount to the third.

Historical Vocabulary
Classics (jing 經), Erudite (boshi 博士), and Imperial University (Taixue 太學)

The classics are the canonical texts that educated Han people studied and cited. In the Han, the Five Classics were the Odes, the Documents, the Changes, the Rites, and the Spring and Autumn Annals, each transmitted through teacher-student lineages with distinct interpretations. An erudite was a court-appointed specialist, and by the reign of Emperor Wu positions were associated with particular classics. The Imperial University (Taixue), founded in 124 BCE, taught students under the erudites; by the Eastern Han the transmitted histories report thousands of students, though the figures are uncertain.

These institutions should not be confused with a modern university or with the later examination system. They trained and connected men, but did not by themselves determine entry into office.

The early Han court was not committed to one doctrine. Under the first emperors and their successors, Huang-Lao ideas favoring restraint, Legalist administrative expertise, and various strands of classical learning coexisted. Court erudites included specialists in many traditions. Under Emperor Wu, patronage shifted more toward classical specialists. Traditional accounts date the establishment of erudites for the Five Classics to 136 BCE, and the founding of the Imperial University to 124 BCE at the suggestion of the official Gongsun Hong. The Book of Han summarizes the period by saying that the emperor dismissed the hundred schools and honored only the classical scholars. Students should read that formula as a later, simplified summary. The historical process was gradual, contested, and cumulative: the court increased its support for classical specialists, and it also continued to rely on legal officials, technical experts, and men with many kinds of learning. It is inaccurate to say that Emperor Wu made Confucianism the official religion in 136 BCE, or that the Han became “Confucian” at once.

Dong Zhongshu belongs in this account, but as one important figure among several. A scholar of the Spring and Autumn Annals in the Gongyang tradition, he articulated a view in which Heaven, moral government, and imperial responsibility were closely linked, and works attributed to him discuss these themes. Some of the texts and dates associated with him, including the famous memorials traditionally placed in the 130s BCE, are debated by scholars, and the collection called the Luxuriant Dew of the Spring and Autumn Annals is a composite work whose formation is complicated. His importance lies in a larger transformation that he helped shape: the linking of classical learning, moral government, Heaven, political responsibility, and imperial institutions.

Classical learning in the Han was a matter of transmission and interpretation. After the disruptions of Qin, texts were reconstructed from memory, recovered from private hiding places, and copied. Different lineages of teachers passed on different readings, and by the late Western Han a long-running dispute developed between “New Text” traditions, written in the script in use at the time, and “Old Text” traditions, said to derive from manuscripts recovered in older script. The disputes had scholarly, political, and institutional dimensions, including rival claims to authority and imperial patronage. Court conferences sought to reconcile interpretations, including a discussion at the Shiqu Pavilion in 51 BCE and one at the White Tiger Hall in 79 CE. In 175 CE, the court began having versions of the classics carved into stone tablets at Luoyang, an effort to fix the text in a form that copyists could consult.

Education extended beyond the capital. Private teachers gathered students, and prominent Eastern Han masters, including the great commentator Zheng Xuan (127–200 CE), were reported to have hundreds or thousands of disciples. Local schools existed in some commanderies and counties, encouraged by officials. Families invested in teaching sons the classics and moral texts, including the Classic of Filial Piety. Learning gave status, connections, and, through recommendation, a route to office, which is why wealthy and locally influential families, discussed in Chapter 15, were often prominent in it. Educated officials formed networks of teacher, student, and patron. But it also mattered that learning was not confined to the wealthy: some talented men from modest backgrounds, including those who began as clerks, advanced through study.

Classical learning could serve the state, but it could also be turned against it. Because the classics offered models of ideal rulership, educated officials used them to remonstrate with emperors, criticize policies, and judge officials. The debate on salt and iron in Chapter 15, in which scholars invoked classical ideals against state monopolies, is one example. Education strengthened the imperial state while also providing a language for criticizing imperial behavior, and in the second century CE, as Chapter 18 will show, students and officials at the capital became involved in bitter political struggles with eunuchs and imperial relatives.

Section 17.2

Sima Qian and Historical Writing

This book has used Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian as evidence throughout. This section explains how he constructed history. The project began with his father, Sima Tan, who held the office of Grand Historian at the court of Emperor Wu, a post associated with astronomy, calendrical work, and the keeping of records. According to Sima Qian's own account, his father, on his deathbed in about 110 BCE, charged him to continue the work of recording the past. Sima Qian, who succeeded to the office, carried the project forward.

The Records is organized in five parts: twelve “basic annals” dealing principally with rulers and dynasties; ten chronological tables; eight treatises on topics such as ritual, music, the calendar, astronomy, and the economy; thirty “hereditary houses” dealing with feudal lords and other major families; and seventy biographies. This structure allowed Sima Qian to tell the past through many kinds of account: political sequence, chronological comparison, institutional history, and individual lives. History for him was not merely chronology but explanation, an inquiry into how and why things changed, and he expressed judgments, often in concluding comments, on rulers, ministers, and events.

His biographies extend well beyond imperial elites. Alongside ministers and generals appear philosophers, assassins, wandering knights, merchants and wealth-makers (used in Chapter 15), physicians, diviners, harsh officials, and the peoples beyond the frontier, including the Xiongnu and the states of the Western Regions (used in Chapter 16). This breadth reflects a conviction that the past included many kinds of people and that moral and political lessons could be drawn from all of them. It also makes the Records a rich source for social and cultural history.

Sima Qian's life shaped his work. In 99 BCE he defended the commander Li Ling, who had surrendered to the Xiongnu (Chapter 16), before the emperor, who was angered. Sima Qian was convicted and, in place of a death sentence, subjected to castration, a punishment that carried deep humiliation. In a letter to a friend, preserved in the Book of Han, he explains that he chose to endure it in order to complete his history. He then continued to write. The story is often told to illustrate the historian's integrity, and it helps explain the emotional and moral force of parts of the work. It should also remind students that Sima Qian was a court official whose position, patronage, and relations with the emperor shaped what he could write and how.

Students should avoid the picture of the Records as a pristine book written entirely in one act by one author. Sima Tan began the project and Sima Qian carried it forward, drawing on earlier texts, archives, and travel and inquiry. The transmitted text has its own complicated later history: already in antiquity, notes indicate that some chapters were lost and others supplemented by later hands, and the completion date and textual history of particular chapters are subjects of scholarly discussion. Ban Gu's Book of Han, begun by his father Ban Biao and completed with the help of his sister Ban Zhao, took a different approach, covering a single dynasty rather than a universal history, and it became the model for the official dynastic histories introduced in Chapter 1. A great historian is still a source with a viewpoint. Sima Qian's selection, arrangement, and judgments reflect his purposes, and historians use the Records critically, comparing it with excavated documents and archaeology.

Section 17.3

Cosmology and Political Thought

Han thinkers lived in an intellectual world in which Heaven, Earth, human society, rulership, and natural phenomena were interconnected. This section explains that world without treating it as mere superstition. Han cosmological ideas were systems for explaining order, change, causation, political responsibility, time, and the relationship between human government and the cosmos.

Historical Vocabulary
Yin and yang (陰陽) and the Five Phases (五行, wuxing)

Yin and yang describe complementary, interdependent modes of change, associated with pairs such as dark and light, cold and warm, rest and activity, and female and male. They are not opposing forces of good and evil. The Five Phases (wood, fire, earth, metal, and water) are processes or phases that follow one another in patterned sequences, in some schemes generating and in others overcoming one another. Han writers used these ideas to correlate seasons, directions, colors, bodily systems, and dynastic fortunes.

No single doctrine governed how they were used. Texts and thinkers offered different schemes, and their meaning shifted with context.

Yin-yang and Five Phases thinking developed in the late Warring States and became widespread in the Han. It informed the calendar, the arrangement of ritual and administrative activity according to season, the theory of medicine (Section 17.5), and political thought. One influential kind of correlation was between Heaven's regularities and the conduct of the ruler. Thinkers such as Dong Zhongshu argued that Heaven responded to human government, sending portents in response to rulers' virtue or failings. Others developed different systems. The Huainanzi presents an elaborate cosmology in which the sage ruler brings human affairs into accord with the patterns of the cosmos. A collection of prophetic and interpretive texts, sometimes called the weft texts, gained prominence in the late Western and Eastern Han and was used to legitimize rulers, including the first Eastern Han emperor.

Cosmology also helped structure the Han's understanding of its own place in time. Han officials debated which phase or “virtue” the dynasty embodied. The Han initially identified with water, then, in a calendar reform of 104 BCE, with earth, and in the Eastern Han with fire, each choice carrying implications for ritual, dress, and the relation of the Han to earlier dynasties. The Taichu calendar reform of 104 BCE combined astronomical calculation with political and ritual purposes.

One of the most consequential uses of cosmology was political. Natural anomalies such as eclipses, earthquakes, floods, droughts, and unusual animals were interpreted as omens. The mechanism can be summarized as follows: a disaster or anomaly occurred; scholars and officials interpreted it as a cosmic warning; they submitted remonstrance to the throne; and pressure was put on the ruler to correct government. Han emperors sometimes issued edicts acknowledging responsibility after disasters, and officials could use omen reports to criticize policies or rivals. This political use gave educated officials a way to speak about the ruler's responsibilities, though it could also be manipulated to attack opponents or to legitimize a favorite policy.

Han cosmology was never uncontested. Wang Chong, in the first century CE, wrote a work of critical essays arguing against many popular beliefs about omens, ghosts, and the responsiveness of Heaven to human affairs, drawing on observation and argument. His skepticism shows that Han thinkers debated these ideas, and that intellectual life included critique as well as elaboration. There was not a single universally accepted Han cosmology; competing schemes and interpretations existed, and historians of Han thought emphasize how models changed and differed even among important texts and thinkers.

Section 17.4

Religion, Ancestors, and Popular Belief

Students should resist imposing the modern picture of three neatly separated religions: Confucianism, Daoism, and Buddhism. For most Han people, religious practice involved overlapping forms: offerings to ancestors, funerary rituals, household cults, local spirits and deities, imperial sacrifices, sacred mountains and places, divination, the interpretation of omens, protective practices, healing rituals, and ideas about souls, spirits, and the afterlife. These were not distinct “religions” but parts of a shared world of ritual and belief.

Ancestor veneration was central. Families made offerings to deceased kin at home shrines and tombs, on seasonal occasions, and at funerals, expressing filial duty and maintaining relationships with the dead. The state performed sacrifices at the highest level: the emperor made offerings to Heaven and Earth, and Emperor Wu and, later, the first Eastern Han emperor performed the great sacrifices at Mount Tai. Communities held sacrifices to local altars and gods, often with communal feasting. Divination, through methods such as the Book of Changes, turtle shell and yarrow, hemerological (day-selection) manuals, and other techniques, guided decisions about marriage, travel, building, and burial. Tombs have yielded manuscripts and instruments used for such practices.

Popular movements of devotion appear in the sources. In 3 BCE, according to the Book of Han, a widespread movement across many regions involved people carrying tokens and gathering to worship the Queen Mother of the West, a goddess later prominent in Han art. Emperor Huan in 165 CE ordered sacrifices to Laozi, showing a Han court devotion to Laozi as a divine figure, distinct from the philosophical texts associated with his name. Late Han religious movements that became politically explosive, notably those connected with the Yellow Turban rebellion, are treated in Chapter 18.

Buddhism appears only faintly in Han sources. The transmitted histories mention that Liu Ying, a prince of Chu, made offerings to the Buddha alongside Huang-Lao practices in the mid-first century CE, and later legends about the emperor's dream and a temple at Luoyang are not reliable as they stand. In the second century CE, foreign monks, including An Shigao, from Parthia, and Lokaksema, associated with the Kushan world, worked in Luoyang on translating Buddhist texts. These developments show an emerging Buddhist presence, still small and largely confined to foreign communities and a few Chinese followers, not yet a mature Chinese religious tradition. Its major expansion belongs to Chapter 23.

Historical Vocabulary
Mingqi (明器) and tomb furnishings

Mingqi (“spirit objects” or “bright objects”) are items made specifically for burial, including models of houses, granaries, wells, stoves, livestock, servants, and vehicles, as well as miniature or specially made vessels. They stand alongside ordinary goods placed in tombs. Archaeologists use them as evidence for material life and for beliefs about the afterlife, but the interpretation is cautious: they show what a family chose to provide, not necessarily what everyone believed.

Burial traditions are among the richest sources for Han religious life. Elite tombs were large, often with chambers arranged like a house, and furnished with food, lacquerware, bronze vessels, textiles, musical instruments, weapons, and sometimes manuscripts. The tomb of Lady Dai (Xin Zhui) at Mawangdui, closed about 168 BCE, contained silk garments, lacquer, food remains, and a T-shaped painted silk banner depicting layers of the cosmos, the deceased, and other scenes. The nearby Tomb 3, of a younger man of the family, held the manuscripts mentioned in the chapter opening. At the highest level, princes of the imperial family were buried in jade suits, as at Mancheng in Hebei, an expression of belief in preservation of the body. In the Eastern Han, wealthy families built tombs and shrines with carved reliefs depicting historical stories, mythological beings, offerings, and daily scenes, and some tombs included documents addressed to the officials of the underworld, presenting the afterlife as a bureaucracy.

Students should be cautious in interpreting such evidence. Tombs tell us about the practices and beliefs of specific communities, often relatively wealthy ones, not what every Han person believed. Images and objects can be read in different ways: a scene of a banquet may show an ideal of continued feasting, a record of status, or a ritual offering. No single tomb proves a universal Han doctrine of the soul or the afterlife. Han sources speak of a soul that ascends and a soul that stays with the body, and of the afterlife in various forms, but these ideas varied, and thinkers such as Wang Chong argued against them. Burial practices were also debated: critics complained of lavish burial, and some families were buried simply. The many farmers and laborers whose graves were modest or unmarked leave far less evidence than those who could afford elaborate tombs.

Section 17.5

Medicine and the Human Body

Han medicine should be taught as an evolving body of knowledge, not as a finished system. It included diagnosis, recipes and materia medica (knowledge of drugs and their uses), therapeutic techniques, diet, exercise and practices for “nurturing life,” moxibustion, emerging theories of vessels or channels through which vital substances moved, and ideas about relationships among body, environment, season, and cosmos. Healers included physicians of various training, recipe specialists, ritual healers, and family members who treated one another.

Excavated manuscripts have transformed the field. The Mawangdui Tomb 3, closed in 168 BCE, contained numerous medical manuscripts, including collections of recipes for treating ailments, texts describing vessels of the body and the use of moxibustion, and works on nurturing life through diet, breathing, and gymnastic exercise. Comparable finds from Zhangjiashan, from a tomb at Wuwei in Gansu, from a Western Han tomb at Laoguanshan near Chengdu, and from other sites demonstrate a much more varied medical world than later canonical texts alone suggest. The Mawangdui vessel texts describe eleven vessels, for example, whereas later systems describe twelve principal channels, showing that theory changed over time.

The transmitted tradition preserves other evidence. Sima Qian's biography of Chunyu Yi, a physician of the early Han, records case histories that discuss diagnoses, treatments, and outcomes, and reveals debates over method. Later works, such as the Divine Husbandman's Classic of Materia Medica, which developed in the Han and later, and the treatise on cold-damage disorders associated with Zhang Zhongjing around the end of the Han, became foundations of later medical tradition. The Yellow Emperor's Inner Classic is a composite collection whose layers developed over time; it was not written by the legendary Yellow Emperor or by a single Han author at a single date, and scholars continue to debate the dating of its parts. The surgeon Hua Tuo, who died in 208 CE, appears in accounts at the end of the Han, though the historical reliability of the stories about him is uneven.

This gives students an important source-critical distinction: an excavated medical manuscript is not the same as a later transmitted medical canon. The later canon reflects choices about which texts were copied and revered, and it presents a systematized theory that had developed over centuries. Historians should also not assume that acupuncture existed in precisely its later standardized form at the beginning of the Han. The history of needling, moxibustion, therapeutic recipes, and theories of the body is developmental, with techniques and theories changing and combining. Medicine also interacted with religion and cosmology: some treatments involved ritual and incantation, and the correlation of the body with the seasons and the Five Phases informed diagnosis. Healing was a social activity as well: households, midwives, traveling practitioners, and officials with medical responsibilities all contributed, and access to skilled care depended heavily on wealth and location.

Section 17.6

Science, Mathematics, and Technology

This section explains systems of practical knowledge, not a list of “Chinese inventions.” Han technical knowledge was embedded in administration, agriculture, astronomy, craft production, medicine, and everyday problems. Categories now separated as science, technology, and divination were closely related in Han practice: astronomers were also calendar-makers and interpreters of omens, and mathematicians were often officials or clerks solving problems of taxation and construction.

Mathematics survives in remarkable texts. A book on calculation, the Writings on Reckoning, was found in the Zhangjiashan tomb sealed about 186 BCE, and it contains problems on fractions, areas, volumes, and proportions. The best-known text is the Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art, which presents 246 problems in nine sections on subjects including field measurement, grain exchange, taxation, construction, surveying, and equations. It should not be called a book written by one Han mathematician. Its authorship and formation are uncertain, and it accumulated material over time, drawing on the practical needs of administration. The famous commentary by Liu Hui dates to 263 CE, well after the Han, and later commentators added further layers. The work reflects a culture of calculation using counting rods on a decimal place-value system, and it shows how mathematics served the state and everyday tasks.

Astronomy and calendrical science were linked to the court. The state's calendar had ritual and administrative importance, and reforms such as the Taichu calendar of 104 BCE and later calendars such as the one adopted in 85 CE involved observation, calculation, and debate. Astronomers debated models of the heavens, and instruments such as the armillary sphere were used. Zhang Heng (78–139 CE), an Eastern Han official who served as a court astronomer and was also a distinguished writer, is associated with an improved armillary sphere and, according to the transmitted history, an instrument in 132 CE that indicated the direction of a distant earthquake. Modern scholars debate exactly how the device worked and how well it performed, and modern reconstructions vary. Students should treat him as one of several Han figures in a wider tradition, not as a solitary inventor.

Technological change was often driven by craft and agricultural needs. Iron production expanded, with cast-iron techniques and, in some workshops, methods for producing steel. After his appointment as Administrator of Nanyang in 31 CE, the official Du Shi is reported to have used water-powered bellows to blow furnaces, an application of hydraulic power. Agricultural tools such as the iron plow, seeders, and winnowing devices spread in various regions; the drilling of deep brine wells in Sichuan, depicted in tomb reliefs, supported salt production. Lacquer, silk, bronze, ceramics, and building technologies developed, and surveying and water-control engineering supported irrigation and canals. Measurement mattered: standardized weights, measures, and coinage supported administration and trade, and instruments for measurement appear in state contexts.

The conclusion is that Han technical knowledge was diverse, practical, and institutional. It grew from the needs of state, farm, and workshop, and was transmitted through officials, clerks, artisans, and families. The word “science” must be used with caution: it was not an autonomous modern profession, and mathematics, astronomy, medicine, calendrics, divination, and technical arts occupied different but overlapping categories.

Section 17.7

Paper and Communication

The Han is famous for paper, and this section corrects the familiar story that Cai Lun invented paper in 105 CE. The transmitted history of the Eastern Han credits Cai Lun, a court eunuch who directed imperial workshops, with presenting to the throne in 105 CE a process for making paper from materials such as bark, hemp, rags, and old fishing nets. But archaeological evidence indicates that forms of paper existed significantly earlier, including before his lifetime. Fragments from Western Han contexts, from sites such as Baqiao near Xi'an, Fangmatan in Gansu, and the Dunhuang region, have been identified as early paper, and some bear writing or drawing, though the dating and identification of individual finds are debated. The historically defensible account is a sequence: early paper, followed by experimentation and development, then Cai Lun's reported improvement or presentation, then the gradual expansion of paper as a writing medium.

Paper did not replace other media at once. Bamboo and wooden slips remained major administrative writing media throughout the Han, as the frontier records of Chapter 16 show. Silk was used for valuable texts, maps, and paintings, as at Mawangdui. Each medium had costs and advantages. Slips were durable and standardized, with documents often written on slips about a Han foot in length, but they were heavy and bulky: a long text required many slips bound into rolls. Silk was light and could be painted or written on but was expensive. Paper could be made from relatively inexpensive plant and recycled fibers and was light, but early paper was rough and its quality varied, and it was not yet widely used for official documents. Only in the following centuries did paper become the principal writing medium, a change discussed in later chapters.

Why does writing technology matter? Lighter and cheaper media make copying, storage, and transport easier. That affects administration, since correspondence and records can be moved and kept more readily, and it eventually transforms literary culture, allowing more people to own books and more texts to circulate. In the Han, communication also depended on the postal relay system, seals, and couriers, and on the human networks of teachers and students who copied and passed on texts. The stone classics of 175 CE reflected a different strategy for stable transmission. Students should ask of each medium: what did it cost, weigh, preserve, and allow?

Section 17.8

Family and Everyday Life

Chapter 15 examined the household as an administrative and economic unit. This section examines it as a lived cultural and social world. Evidence comes from tombs, tomb models and reliefs, excavated household objects, administrative documents, and writings by educated authors. Students should distinguish prescriptive texts, which show what an author believed people should do, from administrative documents, tomb objects, and archaeological household evidence, which show what people may actually have done.

Food varied by region and class. In the north, millet and increasingly wheat, prepared as noodles, cakes, and porridge with the spread of rotary querns for milling, formed the staples; in the Huai region and south, rice was more common. Soybeans, vegetables, and fruits were widely eaten, and meat, including pork, chicken, fish, and, for wealthier households, mutton and beef, appeared with different frequency. Fermented sauces and alcoholic drinks made from grain were widespread. Tomb evidence, including the food remains from Mawangdui, shows what elite households considered proper to serve. Clothing reflected status: farming families wore garments of hemp and ramie, and wealthy households wore silk. Robes, headgear, and ornament signaled rank, and the extraordinary lightness of silk gauze at Mawangdui shows the technical skill of the weavers.

Houses ranged from small rammed-earth or timber dwellings with a stove, a well, and storage, to courtyard compounds and, for the wealthy, multi-story towers, as tomb models show. Models of granaries, pigsties with attached latrines, wells, and watchtowers reveal the layout of farmsteads and estates. People sat on mats and low platforms rather than chairs. Lamps, ceramic and lacquer vessels, bronze mirrors, and tools furnished the household. Daily rhythms followed agricultural seasons and the demands of textile work, described in Chapter 15.

Children were raised within families that expected them to contribute labor as they grew and, for boys in families with resources, to receive education. Marriage was a family arrangement. Han legal and prescriptive norms could treat the mid-teens as marriageable age for girls, but actual marriage age varied by household and circumstance. Elderly parents were supported by their children, and filial piety was a central moral ideal, expressed in care for the living and in ritual for the dead. Communal life included seasonal festivals, the New Year, sacrifices at village altars with shared meals, markets, and, in towns, entertainments such as music, dance, acrobatics, wrestling, board games, and hunting, all depicted in reliefs and tomb objects. Funerals were major social occasions, and families sometimes spent heavily on them, a practice that critics condemned.

Women's lives varied enormously. Weaving, agricultural work, food preparation, and the management of the household occupied most women, as Chapter 15 explained. Elite women could hold property, direct households, sponsor religious and cultural activities, and, as empresses and empress dowagers, exercise political power. Some women were educated. Liu Xiang, a scholar of the late Western Han, compiled Biographies of Exemplary Women, offering models of virtuous and wise women. Ban Zhao (c. 45–c. 116 CE), a historian and scholar who completed parts of the Book of Han and served as a teacher at court, wrote Lessons for Women, urging women to cultivate humility, diligence, and proper conduct. That work is evidence for elite prescriptive thought, not a transparent description of how every Han woman lived. Ban Zhao herself also argued that girls should be educated, a reminder that even prescriptive texts contain arguments. A poor tenant's wife, an enslaved woman, a merchant's wife, and a palace consort lived under different constraints.

Social variation was essential. The emperor and his court, an official and his family, a wealthy estate household, a smallholding farming family, a resident of the capital, a village household, a frontier family, free and dependent or enslaved people, and women of different status all lived different daily lives. Elite literary culture, preserved in the transmitted texts, should not be taken to represent the everyday lives of most people.

Section 17.9

Han Cultural Legacies

This section must not become a claim that everything Han was permanently Chinese. The better question is: what was transmitted, transformed, forgotten, rediscovered, or retrospectively associated with the Han? Students should distinguish Han origins and development from later standardization.

Several legacies stand out. Historical writing: the Records of the Grand Historian and the Book of Han gave later historians models of organization and the tradition of official dynastic histories. Classical scholarship: the Han texts, commentaries, and teaching lineages shaped how later generations read the classics, though later scholars reinterpreted them in different ways, as Chapter 34 will show for Neo-Confucianism. Imperial educational traditions: the Imperial University and the link between classical study and office were models later dynasties revived and changed. Cosmological and political vocabulary: ideas of Heaven, omens, and the correlation of order in nature and government persisted, though they were used in different ways. Mathematical and medical traditions: Han texts became foundations for later work. Writing technologies: paper. Burial and artistic traditions: tomb art and furnishing influenced later practice. And “Han” itself became a later cultural and historical identifier, used, as Chapter 1 discussed, in ways that changed over time and that should not be projected back onto everyone who lived under the Han dynasty.

The distinctions must be kept clear. The Nine Chapters reflects Han-era formation, with Liu Hui's commentary of 263 CE. Paper was developed in the Han, and its mass importance came later. Confucian learning gained institutional expansion in the Han, while the examination culture developed much later. Buddhism arrived faintly in the Han and was transformed on a far larger scale later. Recognizing these differences prevents the teleological assumption that later achievements were simply the fulfillment of the Han.

The final conceptual question is: why is it misleading to describe Han culture as either simply “Confucian” or simply the origin point of later Chinese civilization? Han culture was plural, changing, unevenly distributed across social groups, and partly reinterpreted by later generations. It was a process, not a fixed package appearing in 206 BCE.

Historical Evidence

Four kinds of evidence dominate the study of Han culture, each with its own bias.

Transmitted classics and histories. The Records of the Grand Historian, the Book of Han, the Book of the Later Han, and the classics and philosophical works were preserved by later scholars who copied and valued them. They reflect elite interests and later selection, and some transmitted works are composite. Excavated manuscripts. Tombs such as Mawangdui and Zhangjiashan yielded texts on silk and bamboo, including medical, mathematical, philosophical, and divinatory works unknown from the later canon. They survived by chance in sealed tombs, and reflect what a family chose to bury. Tomb archaeology. Structures, furnishings, models, reliefs, and banners show material life, status, and belief, but mainly of families wealthy enough to build them. Administrative documents. Frontier and other records show writing, calculation, and daily practice outside literary settings, but come from particular places.

Prescriptive texts, such as Ban Zhao's Lessons for Women, must be distinguished from evidence of practice. Students should ask of every source: who preserved it, why, and whose lives are missing? Much of what we know about Han intellectual life depends on where texts survived, and the archaeological record itself has a geography.

Primary Source Window

Primary Source Window: Sima Qian on the Purpose and Cost of Writing History

Creator: Sima Qian (c. 145–86 BCE) · Date: Late 2nd–early 1st century BCE · Type: Historian's self-representation, in the postface to the Records of the Grand Historian and in a letter to a friend preserved in the Book of Han

Context: In these passages, Sima Qian describes his father's dying charge, his own aims, and the punishment he endured after defending Li Ling. They are his own account and should be read as a historian's self-presentation, not a neutral biography.

Paraphrase (not a translated quotation):

Sima Qian says his father urged him to continue the record of the past, that he compares his project with earlier efforts to preserve the deeds of worthy men, and that he aims to investigate the relation between Heaven and human affairs and to trace change from the past to his own time. After his punishment, he writes that he might have chosen death but endured humiliation so that his work would be completed and passed on to those who would understand it.

Analysis: The passages reveal how Sima Qian understood the historian's task: to explain change and to link human events with larger patterns, with a sense of moral purpose and of duty to his father. They also show a man reflecting on suffering and meaning. But they are self-presentation, shaped by the emotional and rhetorical aims of the writer, and they cannot by themselves establish the facts of his life or the completion of the book. They give the perspective of a court official and not that of others affected by the events he describes.

Questions for students:

  1. What does this source say about Sima Qian's purposes in writing history?
  2. Who created it, and under what circumstances?
  3. What purposes may have shaped how he presented his own experience?
  4. What can historians learn from it about Han ideas of history and of the historian's role?
  5. What can historians not learn from it alone?
Historical Debate

Historical Debate: How Confucian Was Han China?

The question: Traditional narratives say that the Han, especially under Emperor Wu, adopted Confucianism as its state ideology. Historians ask how far that description holds.

Interpretation A — Increasing institutionalization of Confucian learning: Han rulers increasingly patronized classical scholars, built imperial educational institutions, and used Confucian moral-political language in edicts, debates, and ritual, and later generations saw the Han as a turning point.
Interpretation B — A more plural intellectual world: Calling the Han simply “Confucian” obscures continuing Legalist administrative practices, Huang-Lao traditions, cosmological systems, technical arts, local religious practices, and the wide range of ideas found in Han texts and archaeology.

Points of disagreement: The debate concerns the definition of “Confucian” (moral teaching, classical scholarship, or state doctrine), the degree of institutional dominance, the difference between court ideology and popular practice, and how much weight to give prescriptive texts versus excavated evidence.

Evidence: Educational institutions, canonical scholarship, court memorials, transmitted philosophical texts, tomb manuscripts, legal documents, and ritual evidence.

Why it matters: The debate challenges the tendency to project later imperial orthodoxy backward onto the early empire. The two interpretations need not be mutually exclusive: classical learning gained exceptional institutional authority while remaining part of a more plural Han intellectual and religious environment.

Student task: Using evidence from this chapter and Chapter 15, evaluate which interpretation is better supported, or explain what additional evidence would help.

Multiple Perspectives

Multiple Perspectives: Knowledge and Practice

Note: these are reconstructed analytical perspectives, not quotations from named individuals.

A court scholar: His standing depends on mastery of a classical lineage, on teachers and patrons, and on the court's interest in his learning. He may use the classics to advise or to criticize, at some risk.
A physician or healer: Trained through family or teacher, he uses recipes, moxibustion, and diet, and may combine them with ritual. His reputation rests on results. Access to skilled treatment varies with wealth, location, and social connections.
An artisan or technical specialist: Working in an iron workshop, a lacquer shop, or a state office, he holds practical knowledge passed by apprenticeship, seldom recorded in literary texts.
An elite woman: She may manage a large household, hold property, and direct ritual and textile work. Some are educated; prescriptive texts define what she is told to be, though her actual life varies.
A member of a farming household: Religion means offerings to ancestors and local spirits, seasonal sacrifices, and divination for practical decisions. Literary culture reaches him mostly through officials and occasional teachers.

These perspectives do not have equal access to writing, resources, or recognition; the surviving record is weighted toward the first and the fourth.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change Over Time: From Warring States Intellectual Pluralism to Han Imperial Knowledge Culture

What Changed

  • Institutional support for classical learning grew.
  • Imperial historiography developed new forms.
  • Cosmological syntheses changed and were used in politics.
  • Medicine, mathematics, and writing media evolved.

What Stayed the Same

  • Inherited Zhou and Warring States texts remained foundational.
  • Ancestor practice, divination, and ritual continued.
  • Household production and older writing materials persisted.

When Did Change Occur, and What Was Its Pace?

Unevenly across four centuries. Some changes concentrated around Emperor Wu's reign, such as the erudites, the university, and calendar reform; others, such as the classical disputes and standardization, belong especially to the Eastern Han; paper and Buddhism illustrate gradual change. Some change was contested, as the Old Text and New Text disputes show.

Who experienced change most directly? Educated elites, court scholars, officials, healers, scribes, artisans, and wealthy families participating in new cultural forms. Who experienced greater continuity? Many agricultural households and local communities, even while their material and religious practices evolved. Why it matters: “Han culture” was a process, not a fixed package.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: Why Did Classical Learning Gain Greater Institutional Importance Under the Han?

Long-term causes

Warring States textual traditions; the prestige of antiquity; and the need for models of moral rulership.

Short-term causes

Consolidation of empire; the court's search for ideological frameworks; and the growth of an educated official culture.

Immediate trigger and context

Emperor Wu-era patronage and institutional changes, including the erudites and the university.

Event or transformation

Greater support for classical specialists and imperial education.

Immediate effects

Growing prestige of canonical learning and of educated advisers; disputes over interpretation.

Long-term consequences

Classical learning became deeply associated with government and elite education, though the examination culture developed much later.

Historical complexity

Han institutions continued practices inherited from Qin and other traditions, and the “Confucianization” of government was partial, contested, and cumulative.
Everyday Life and Social History

Everyday Life and Social History: A Han Household Through Objects

Objects show how a household lived. Food vessels of pottery and, for the well-off, lacquer and bronze held millet, wheat cakes, vegetables, and meat. Textiles, from coarse hemp to fine silk, marked status. Iron tools and a rotary quern reveal the labor of farming and milling. Lamps extended the working day, and wooden slips and a writing brush show that someone in the household could write. Tomb models of a granary, a well, and a pigsty with latrine show what a family thought necessary in the afterlife, and what its farmstead looked like.

Read carefully, such objects also suggest limits. A model granary may show an aspiration as much as a fact. A lacquer cup may come from a wealthy tomb and not from a typical home. Most farming households owned few of these things, and their houses of earth and wood have largely vanished. Objects therefore tell about the people who could afford durable goods and to whom archaeologists have had access.

Regional and Frontier Context

Han culture was regionally varied. Qi and Lu, in the east, were known for classical learning and teaching. The Chu south, including the Changsha region, had distinctive religious and artistic traditions visible in the Mawangdui banner and lacquer. Sichuan developed a rich tradition of tomb reliefs and brine-well technology. The capitals at Chang'an and Luoyang hosted court scholarship. On the northwestern frontier, documents show soldiers and officials engaged in writing, calculation, and a mix of practices, and northeastern commanderies such as Lelang in Korea yielded lacquerware and other goods showing Han material culture in a region with its own communities. Students should not treat capital culture as representative of all regions, or assume that classical learning reached all localities equally.

China and the Wider World

Han culture was connected to wider worlds, though this chapter's focus is internal. Chapter 16 described the networks that carried goods and people across Eurasia. Along them came early Buddhist ideas, foreign monks such as An Shigao and Lokaksema, and artistic motifs and techniques. Han lacquer and silks reached Xiongnu tombs in Mongolia and Han commanderies in Korea. Comparisons are illuminating: Sima Qian's project of a universal history has been compared with Greek historical writing, and Han astronomy and medicine developed in a world where other traditions pursued related questions, though direct influence is hard to demonstrate. Chinese practitioners were active participants, adapting and transforming what they received.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how people in the Han world understood the cosmos, preserved knowledge, educated elites, worshiped, healed, calculated, communicated, buried the dead, and lived. Its central argument is that Han culture emerged from the selective preservation and transformation of earlier traditions, and that it remained plural even as Confucian learning gained institutional importance.

Classical learning, supported by erudites, the Imperial University, and networks of teachers, gained importance across the Han. The process was gradual, contested, and cumulative, and it should not be reduced to Emperor Wu making Confucianism the state religion. Dong Zhongshu was one influential figure among many. Learning strengthened the state and gave officials a language for criticizing it.

Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian created a new model of historical writing with annals, tables, treatises, hereditary houses, and biographies, extending to many kinds of people. Its author was a court official whose experience shaped his work, and the text has a complicated history. A great historian is still a source with a viewpoint.

Han cosmology linked Heaven, Earth, and human government through ideas such as yin-yang and the Five Phases. It was used in politics through omens and remonstrance, and it was contested, as Wang Chong's criticisms show. There was no single Han cosmology.

Religious life involved ancestor veneration, local cults, imperial sacrifices, divination, and ideas of the afterlife, and it cannot be divided into three neat religions. Tombs provide rich but selective evidence. Buddhism appeared only faintly, and politically mobilized religious movements belong to Chapter 18.

Excavated manuscripts show that Han medicine was varied and developing, and they must be distinguished from later transmitted canons. Han mathematics, astronomy, and technology were practical systems embedded in administration, agriculture, and craft, and they should not be reduced to a list of inventions.

Paper developed gradually. Cai Lun is credited with presenting an improved process in 105 CE, but paper existed earlier, and bamboo, wood, and silk remained important. Writing media affected the cost and circulation of texts.

Family and everyday life varied by class, region, and gender. Prescriptive texts such as Ban Zhao's must be distinguished from evidence of practice. Objects and tombs illuminate material life but are weighted toward the wealthy.

Han cultural legacies were transmitted, transformed, and retrospectively associated with the dynasty. Han origins should be distinguished from later standardization. The chapter prepares for Chapter 18, in which the institutions, learned networks, and religious currents examined here become part of the empire's crisis.

  2. Explain the political mechanism linking omens to remonstrance.
  3. Compare a transmitted medical canon with excavated medical manuscripts as evidence.
  4. Explain how the geography of preservation shapes what historians know about the Han.
  5. Explain how family life differed among elite, farming, and frontier households.

Discussion Questions

  1. What are the risks of applying the terms “religion” and “science” to Han knowledge?
  2. How should historians use a source, like Ban Zhao's, that prescribes behavior?
  3. Why might later generations have wanted to credit the Han with foundational achievements?
  4. What does the survival of some texts and not others imply about the history of ideas?
  5. How did classical learning relate to the social structure described in Chapter 15?
  6. What would a history of Han culture look like if it were written from farming households' perspectives?

Writing Prompts

  • Prompt 1 (Explanatory): Explain how classical learning gained institutional importance in the Han and what its limits were.
  • Prompt 2 (Comparative): Compare excavated medical manuscripts and later transmitted medical canons as evidence for Han medicine.
  • Prompt 3 (Historical argument using evidence): Using the Historical Debate feature, argue how Confucian Han China was, or explain why a combined description is more accurate.
  • Prompt 4 (Connecting past and present): Explain, without overstating the connection, why later generations associated the name “Han” with a foundational period of Chinese culture, and what that association leaves out.

Research or Library Extension

MGU Library Research Project

Reading a Han Tomb

This project asks you to investigate one excavated Han tomb or manuscript find and evaluate what it does and does not show.

  1. Choose a site. Select a find discussed in this chapter, such as Mawangdui, Zhangjiashan, Wuwei, or Mancheng.
  2. Gather sources. Use library resources to find excavation reports or scholarly studies (secondary) and translations of any manuscripts (primary editions), labeling each as primary or secondary.
  3. Analyze the evidence. Identify what the objects and texts show about belief, knowledge, or status, and note what cannot be concluded.
  4. Compare with transmitted sources. Explain how the find agrees or conflicts with what transmitted texts say.
  5. Write your findings. Produce a 4–6 page essay or presentation connecting the site to the chapter's argument.

Library resources to use: Reference works on Han archaeology, translations of excavated texts, and the MGU History Library's materials on historical methods (see Appendix M).

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 17 matters because it shows how Han people lived and thought, beyond the institutions of state. It shows a culture built from inherited traditions, institutions, experimentation, family practices, religious activity, and technical knowledge, and it warns against treating that culture as uniform.

The chapter shaped later Chinese history through texts, institutions, and ideas that later generations transmitted and reinterpreted, though often they were reshaped in the process. Students should carry forward the distinction between Han origins and later standardization.

It also reveals larger themes of this textbook: the plurality of intellectual life, the importance of evidence and its biases, the difference between prescription and practice, and the connection between ordinary life and elite culture. Chinese civilization appears as creative, contested, and regionally varied.

As students turn to Chapter 18, they should remember that the learned networks, cosmological ideas, and religious currents of this chapter would become part of the political crisis that ended the Han. The Han world was not a fixed package but a changing culture, unfinished when the dynasty fell.
