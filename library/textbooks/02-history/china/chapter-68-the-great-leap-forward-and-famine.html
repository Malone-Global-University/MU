The Great Leap Forward and Famine

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

Between 1958 and 1962, a campaign meant to carry China rapidly toward industrial strength and socialist abundance instead produced one of the deadliest famines of the twentieth century. The scale of the catastrophe is debated; its occurrence is not.

In the autumn of 1958, a visitor traveling through much of rural China would have seen an extraordinary sight. Fires burned day and night on village outskirts, where makeshift clay furnaces were being fed with charcoal, timber, and scrap metal in an effort to produce steel. Villagers who had spent their lives on family plots now marched to work in groups under banners, often at night under lamps, digging canals and building reservoirs. Wall slogans promised that China would overtake Britain’s industrial output within fifteen years. Newspapers reported harvests so large that they seemed to defy farming experience. Households that had recently joined agricultural cooperatives found themselves absorbed into much larger units called people’s communes, where meals were served in shared dining halls. This chapter studies how that moment of mobilization and optimism turned, within two years, into mass hunger.

The chapter examines the Great Leap Forward (1958–1962 in its broadest usage, with its most intense phase falling between 1958 and 1960) and the Great Famine that accompanied and followed it. It matters because it is a central event in the history of the People’s Republic, of twentieth-century famine, and of the relationship between state power and human welfare. Understanding it requires attention to three connected histories: the political and economic ambitions of the Chinese Communist Party leadership; the institutional mechanisms, including communes, production targets, reporting systems, and grain procurement, that converted those ambitions into disaster; and the human experience of the famine, which varied enormously from province to province, county to county, and household to household.

What changed in these years was dramatic. Rural China, organized only a few years earlier into agricultural cooperatives (Chapter 67), was reorganized into roughly twenty-six thousand people’s communes. Household cooking, private plots, and local markets were curtailed. Labor was redirected from farming into steelmaking, irrigation, and construction. The state’s knowledge of the countryside was distorted by inflated reports. When harvests fell, the state continued to extract grain on the basis of those inflated figures. Tens of millions of people died of starvation and associated disease, and millions of births did not occur. By 1961 and 1962 the leadership had reversed many of the policies, restoring a measure of household and team-level autonomy.

What continued is equally important. The Communist Party retained its monopoly on political authority, and Mao Zedong remained its chairman. The household registration system limited the movement of rural people. The ration system continued to give urban residents priority over rural ones. The reforms of 1960–1962 were retreats from specific policies, not a rejection of socialism, and the political conflicts left by the Leap, especially after the Lushan Conference of 1959, shaped the leadership tensions examined in Chapter 69.

The people affected were, above all, China’s rural population of roughly 550 million, but the Leap also reshaped the lives of urban workers, cadres, women who bore much of the new labor, children, the elderly, and minority communities in frontier provinces. Their experiences were not uniform. Some counties suffered mortality rates many times higher than others; some officials resisted extreme demands while others enforced them harshly; some provinces, notably the major cities, were shielded by state priorities that moved food toward urban consumers.

Historians reconstruct this history from a wide range of evidence: Party and government directives and meeting records, provincial and county archives that have become partly accessible since the 1980s, official statistical yearbooks, population censuses of 1953, 1964, and 1982, local gazetteers, internal investigation reports, memoirs, oral histories, and, for the international dimension, Soviet and diplomatic records and trade statistics. Each source has distinctive limits. Wartime-style secrecy, falsified statistics, and later political restrictions on research mean that precise totals remain uncertain. Section 68.26 compares published estimates of deaths attributable to the crisis, which range broadly from roughly 15 million to 45 million but differ in definitions, source bases, and time windows.

The chapter connects to wider histories as well. The Great Leap drew on Soviet models and Soviet rivalry, on a global Cold War competition over development, and on a worldwide twentieth-century faith in planning and mobilization. It also belongs to the comparative history of famine, alongside the Soviet famines of the early 1930s and the Bengal famine of 1943, and to the history of how information flows, or fails to flow, in political systems. Studying it carefully is not an exercise in assigning a single cause or a simple moral; it is a lesson in how multiple failures interact.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why China’s leadership adopted the Great Leap Forward in 1957–1958, distinguishing its stated goals from the methods chosen to pursue them.
  2. Trace the transformation of agricultural cooperatives into people’s communes and describe how communes organized labor, property, and household life.
  3. Analyze the backyard steel campaign, irrigation drives, agricultural experiments, and pest campaigns as distinct policies with distinct consequences.
  4. Distinguish among grain actually harvested, grain reported, grain procured by the state, and grain retained by rural households.
  5. Evaluate how production targets, political pressure, and false reporting damaged the state’s information about the countryside.
  6. Compare the course of the famine in different provinces, including Henan, Anhui, Sichuan, Gansu, Guizhou, and Qinghai, and between rural and urban populations.
  7. Interpret competing estimates of excess deaths and distinguish excess mortality from missing births and from other measures of population loss.
  8. Assess the significance of the Lushan Conference of 1959 and the campaign against “right opportunism” for the prolongation of the crisis.
  9. Describe the retreat from the Great Leap in 1960–1962, including the Twelve-Point Directive, the Sixty Articles, and the Seven Thousand Cadres Conference.
  10. Construct a multicausal explanation of the famine that weighs policy, institutions, weather, and international factors without reducing it to one cause.
  11. Contextualize the Great Leap within Soviet, Cold War, and comparative famine history.

Historical Context

The Great Leap Forward grew from developments traced in earlier chapters. After the Communist victory of 1949 (Chapter 65), the new government completed land reform (Chapter 66), restored the economy, fought the Korean War, and in 1953 began the First Five-Year Plan, which emphasized heavy industry with Soviet assistance (Chapter 67). By 1956 most rural households had been organized into advanced agricultural cooperatives and most private industry and commerce had been nationalized. Industrial output grew rapidly from a low base, but the plan relied on extracting an agricultural surplus from a rural economy that remained small-scale, labor-intensive, and short of capital, fertilizer, and machinery. Grain was purchased from villages at state-set prices under the unified purchase and supply system introduced in 1953, and a household registration system introduced in 1958 sharply restricted movement from countryside to city.

The agricultural base mattered because China in the 1950s was overwhelmingly rural and poor. Roughly four in five people lived in the countryside; most farming was done by hand and animal labor on small plots; and yields, though improving in some regions, were constrained by limited irrigation, fertilizer, and improved seed. The country’s regions differed sharply. The North China Plain and Yellow River basin grew wheat, millet, and sorghum and were vulnerable to drought and flood. The Yangtze basin and the south grew irrigated rice, often with double cropping. Sichuan, the most populous province, was a self-contained basin with a dense rural population. The northwest, southwest, and Qinghai-Tibetan plateau regions had sparse populations, difficult transport, thinner food reserves, and large minority communities. These conditions would matter when procurement and mobilization policies were applied uniformly across very different places.

Politically, 1956–1957 produced tension and doubt. Nikita Khrushchev’s 1956 criticism of Stalin unsettled Mao Zedong’s confidence in the Soviet model. Mao’s April 1956 speech on the “Ten Great Relationships” argued for a more distinctly Chinese path. In 1956 Premier Zhou Enlai and economic planner Chen Yun tried to slow an overheated investment drive, a moderating course later labeled “opposing rash advance.” The Hundred Flowers campaign of 1956–1957 invited criticism of the Party, and when criticism proved sharper than expected the Anti-Rightist Campaign of 1957 punished intellectuals and officials; official reassessments decades later acknowledged more than half a million people had been so labeled (Chapter 67). The campaign taught officials that open criticism carried severe penalties. This is essential context for understanding why so few voices challenged the Leap’s assumptions in 1958.

Geographically, “China” in this chapter means the territory administered by the People’s Republic from 1949 to 1950 onward, including provinces, autonomous regions, and the incorporated frontier areas of Xinjiang, Inner Mongolia, Guangxi, Ningxia, and, after 1950–1951, Tibet. Taiwan was governed by the Republic of China and is not part of the story told here. The famine was not evenly distributed across this space, and the textbook’s regional sections attempt to avoid treating the national picture as uniform.

The chapter’s boundaries are drawn carefully. Chapter 67 explained cooperatives, the planned economy, and the Anti-Rightist Campaign. Chapter 68 begins with the decision to accelerate development in 1957–1958 and covers communes, the famine, the Lushan Conference, and the first policy retreat through early 1962. Chapter 69 covers sustained recovery, leadership debates involving Liu Shaoqi and Deng Xiaoping, the Socialist Education Movement, and the Sino-Soviet split. Throughout, students should keep in mind that the textbook treats the Great Leap Forward and its famine as historically established events, while acknowledging real scholarly disagreement about their mortality totals, mechanisms, and the distribution of responsibility.

Division I

Origins of the Great Leap Forward, 1957–1958

Why did China’s leadership adopt the Great Leap rather than disaster being the intended or inevitable outcome? The central distinction is between the stated goal of rapid modernization and the political and administrative methods chosen to reach it.

Section 68.1

The End of the First Five-Year Plan and the Search for Faster Development

The First Five-Year Plan (1953–1957) was, by most measures, a success in industrial terms. Output of steel, coal, electricity, and machinery rose substantially, and thousands of new factories and projects, many with Soviet technical help, laid the foundation of a modern industrial sector. Yet by 1956–1957 planners faced real problems. Heavy industry absorbed scarce capital while agriculture grew slowly; grain output rose only modestly while the population grew by roughly two percent a year. The state depended on agricultural procurement to feed cities and finance industry, and a rise in urban population increased demand. Investment drives in 1956 had produced shortages of materials and funds, leading Zhou Enlai and Chen Yun to rein in spending.

These imbalances raised a basic question: how could a poor, capital-scarce country accelerate growth? The Soviet model prioritized heavy industry with centralized planning and capital-intensive technology. Chinese leaders increasingly doubted that such a model suited a country with abundant labor and scarce capital, and some feared it created bureaucratic rigidity and a wide gap between industry and countryside. Mao’s answer was to substitute labor for capital: mobilize China’s enormous rural workforce to build irrigation, small-scale industry, and agricultural improvement simultaneously. This is the intellectual root of the “walking on two legs” idea, modern and indigenous, large and small, developed together.

The international setting reinforced the urgency. In November 1957, at celebrations of the Bolshevik Revolution in Moscow, Khrushchev spoke of the Soviet Union overtaking the United States in major products within fifteen years, and Mao responded that China could overtake Britain in a similar time. Cold War competition, the Soviet launch of Sputnik in October 1957, and China’s sense of vulnerability after the Korean War created a climate in which rapid development seemed both possible and necessary. Students should note that these were widely shared aspirations; the dispute was over methods and pace.

Section 68.2

Mao Zedong and the Ideology of the Great Leap

Mao Zedong’s outlook was shaped by the experience of the revolution. The Communist movement had survived and won by mobilizing peasants, improvising in adverse conditions, and overcoming superior material force with determination and organization. From this history Mao drew a confidence, which historians call revolutionary voluntarism, that human will and political commitment could overcome material constraints. In his view, the energy released by land reform and cooperativization could be channeled into economic construction, and the masses, properly led, would exceed the expectations of cautious experts and planners.

This outlook had intellectual components. Mao’s writings of the 1930s and 1940s stressed contradiction and the transformability of conditions. His 1957 speeches emphasized that China’s “poor and blank” condition, as he sometimes expressed it, was an advantage: a blank sheet could receive the most beautiful writing. He also drew on suspicion of professional elites and bureaucratic planning, and on a belief that socialist relations of production, by liberating labor, could raise productivity faster than technical change alone.

Mao’s role requires precision. He was not the only advocate of acceleration; many provincial leaders, ministers, and local cadres embraced it with enthusiasm and competed to exceed targets. But Mao’s authority was exceptional. His prestige as the leader of the revolution, and his willingness to criticize opponents as conservative, meant that his preferences carried extraordinary weight. Understanding the Leap requires holding both facts together: the policy depended on Mao’s leadership, and its implementation depended on thousands of officials making choices in response to the incentives they faced.

Section 68.3

The Political Climate After the Anti-Rightist Campaign

The Anti-Rightist Campaign of 1957 changed what it was safe to say. Intellectuals, students, and some Party members who had accepted the invitation to criticize the Party during the Hundred Flowers period were labeled “rightists” and sent to labor reform, demoted, or otherwise punished. The lesson spread beyond those targeted: criticism of Party policy could be treated as hostility to socialism itself. Local officials learned that caution was dangerous, and that enthusiasm was a political asset.

Party discipline in this period also reinforced a hierarchy in which officials were accountable upward, to superiors, rather than downward, to the people they governed. Promotion and protection depended on meeting targets and showing correct attitudes. Leaders who had expressed reservations about speed in 1956–1957 faced explicit criticism in early 1958. At the Nanning Conference in January 1958, Mao criticized the moderating line associated with Zhou Enlai and Chen Yun, and Zhou made a self-criticism. At Chengdu in March, further discussion supported accelerated plans. The effect was to narrow the range of acceptable opinion among the very people best positioned to understand economic constraints.

Historians do not claim that everyone was merely coerced. Many officials and ordinary Party members believed in the socialist project, were impressed by the early achievements of the 1950s, and sincerely hoped to accelerate prosperity. But political risk and political conviction worked together, and this combination helps explain why enthusiastic compliance spread rapidly while informal skepticism was rarely heard in meetings.

Section 68.4

The Great Leap Takes Shape in 1958

During the first half of 1958 the policy took definite form. In May, the second session of the Eighth Party Congress endorsed the General Line for Socialist Construction, a slogan-based program calling for building socialism by going all out, aiming high, and achieving greater, faster, better, and more economical results. The wording is revealing: it stressed speed and quantity and implied that cautious calculation was a form of timidity. Planning targets were revised upward several times, and the Second Five-Year Plan (1958–1962) was transformed from a technical document into an instrument of acceleration.

The campaign also changed the method of administration. Rather than relying primarily on centralized planning through ministries, the Leap decentralized many decisions to provinces, counties, and communes, which were encouraged to set ambitious local goals and mobilize labor and materials locally. Decentralization sounds like liberalization, but in a system without independent verification or local accountability it meant that officials competed to promise more. Rural mobilization campaigns multiplied: irrigation drives in the winter of 1957–1958, campaigns for fertilizer, seed, and close planting, and pest eradication. Planning gave way to mass movements with quotas.

The move toward decentralization should not be confused with a transfer of authority to farmers. Provincial and county leaders acquired greater latitude to organize labor and pursue local industrial schemes, but villagers rarely gained the freedom to refuse assignments or to challenge the figures being reported upward. This combination—devolved operational responsibility and concentrated political authority—made the policy unusually difficult to correct. Officials could compete to exceed the center’s slogans while claiming that they were carrying out national decisions. Failure could be blamed on local weakness rather than on the assumptions built into the targets.

Section 68.5

Competing Development Strategies and the Decision to Accelerate

The leadership was not unanimous about how to develop. The balanced-growth approach associated with Chen Yun and Zhou Enlai stressed proportion between sectors, careful finance, and attention to consumer supply. The Maoist approach stressed simultaneous expansion, labor mobilization, and decentralization. Within the second camp, differences also existed: some wanted to prioritize steel and heavy industry as the “key link,” others emphasized agriculture and water control, and many provincial leaders saw the Leap as an opportunity to build local industry and show political zeal.

Because labor was abundant and capital scarce, mobilization seemed to offer an obvious advantage. Villages could build earth dams, small reservoirs, and local iron works with labor that, in the slack winter season, was otherwise underused. Early results of the 1957–1958 winter irrigation campaign, which involved tens of millions of peasants, seemed to confirm the approach. Good weather in 1958 produced a substantial harvest in many regions, which further encouraged optimism.

The mistake was in extrapolation. Slack-season labor is not unlimited labor; by autumn 1958, when the harvest required the full workforce, villagers were simultaneously expected to bring in the crop, make steel, and build infrastructure. Sound arguments for labor-intensive development became, under political pressure and without feedback, a plan that assumed labor could be reallocated without losing food production. Leaders who doubted this had few channels through which to say so.

Division II

People’s Communes, Mass Mobilization, and Disrupted Production, 1958–1959

How commune organization and mass-production campaigns altered agriculture, industry, labor, and household life. The communes, dining halls, steel drive, and scientific experiments were distinct policies; their effects are traced separately before the chapter analyzes how they interacted.

Section 68.6

The Establishment of the People’s Communes

The commune movement grew out of local experiments in the spring and summer of 1958. Some counties merged cooperatives to carry out large water-control projects; in Henan’s Suiping county, a unit known as the Weixing (Sputnik) commune was publicized as a pioneer. Mao’s inspection tours in August 1958 and enthusiastic press attention accelerated the trend. At the Beidaihe meeting that month the leadership adopted a resolution endorsing communes, and within weeks rural China was reorganized. By the end of 1958 roughly 26,000 communes had been established, enrolling almost all of the rural population, with an average of several thousand households each.

The speed matters. Cooperatives had been formed over years of preparation; communes were created in weeks, often by administrative order, sometimes with little explanation to villagers. Some local cadres were eager, others obedient, and many unsure what was expected. Communes typically combined several former townships, merged villages’ land and animals, and took over functions including schools, clinics, small factories, militia, and local government. They were intended to be units combining agriculture, industry, trade, education, and military affairs.

A commune was both a promise and a new authority. Supporters argued that a much larger unit could pool scarce draft animals and machinery, build schools and clinics, provide child care, and organize irrigation beyond the resources of a single village. Its political leaders could also requisition food, tools, and labor on a scale previously unavailable to local government. The difference between these capacities mattered. A structure capable of coordinating a useful canal could also deprive small communities of their ability to adjust production to local conditions. The balance depended on whether village-level knowledge and household needs could constrain orders from above.

Section 68.7

Commune Government and the Militarization of Labor

Communes were organized in three tiers: the commune itself, the production brigade (often corresponding to an earlier cooperative or large village), and the production team (a smaller group of households). In the early phase, many accounting and decision-making functions were pushed upward to the commune or brigade, taking them away from the villagers who knew local conditions. Labor was frequently organized along military lines, with brigades and companies, commanders, and “shock troops,” and workers marched to fields in formation. This vocabulary reflected the revolutionary heritage of mobilization but also removed flexibility.

Because commune officials could move workers across villages, large tasks could be performed rapidly. They could also be assigned arbitrarily. People were drawn from farming to steelmaking or construction without regard to harvest timing. Work points, the system of recording labor for later distribution, were sometimes replaced or supplemented by free supply of food, reducing the connection between effort and reward. Women, who in many regions had already done much of the field labor, were drawn into heavy mobilization while still responsible for children and housework; nurseries and dining halls were promoted partly as a way to “liberate” them, though their effects were uneven.

Historical Vocabulary
People’s Commune, Production Brigade, and Production Team

The people’s commune (renmin gongshe, 人民公社) was the large unit created in 1958 that combined government and economic functions in the countryside. The production brigade (shengchan dadui) was a middle tier, often a former cooperative or large village. The production team (shengchan dui) was the lowest tier, generally a group of households working together. Which tier was the “basic accounting unit,” meaning the unit that owned land and distributed income, changed over time: initially higher, then lowered to the team in the early 1960s.

Understanding this vocabulary matters because policy debates often concerned moving ownership up or down these tiers, and because famine outcomes were affected by which tier controlled grain and labor.

Section 68.8

Communal Dining Halls and the Reorganization of Household Life

Communal dining halls, set up in 1958 across much of the countryside, were among the most consequential features of the communes. The idea was to free labor, especially women’s labor, from cooking, to reduce waste through collective provisioning, and to demonstrate the abundance of socialism. In the euphoria of the autumn 1958 harvest, many halls served generous meals, sometimes advertised as free, and villagers were encouraged to eat heartily. Private kitchens were closed; household pots and woks were in many places collected, and some were melted for steel.

The halls changed the structure of food security. When families cooked at home, grain was stored by households, who could make their own decisions during shortage, whether stretching rations, hiding reserves, or gathering wild foods. Under communal dining, grain was held by the collective and often by cadres controlling the kitchens. Households lost control over food storage and cooking, and when supplies shrank, ration quantities were decided by officials. Access could be made conditional on labor or political attitude. In some places dining halls became instruments of discipline: the ill, the elderly, and those who had offended local cadres could be given less.

Dining halls began to collapse in many places in 1959–1960 as grain ran short, and by 1961 central policy permitted or required their dissolution. The experience left lasting memories in villages, remembered by some as an initial period of plenty and by many as the start of hunger.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Village Household Under Communal Dining (an illustrative composite)

Imagine a household of five in a northern village in late 1958: a husband, a wife, two children, and an elderly grandmother. Before the commune, the wife cooked in their own kitchen from grain stored in a jar, supplemented by vegetables from a small plot. Now the husband is assigned to a night-time steelmaking crew, the wife to a field brigade, and the children’s meals are eaten at the dining hall, where food is plentiful at first. The family’s pots have been surrendered. In the second year, rations shrink. The grandmother cannot work and receives less; the wife, who has no kitchen, cannot supplement the family’s meals unless she can find food outside the hall. When a cadre judges the husband insufficiently energetic, his family’s share is reduced.

What the scenario shows is not simply poverty but a changed structure of power over food: decisions that households once made were made by cadres.

Section 68.9

Backyard Furnaces and the Campaign for Steel

At the Beidaihe meeting in August 1958 the leadership set a goal of doubling steel output in 1958 to about 10.7 million tons, compared to roughly 5.35 million tons in 1957. Because the existing mills could not meet this increase, the Party called on the population to produce steel in small “native” furnaces. By autumn, tens of millions of people, by some accounts approaching ninety million at the peak, were involved in making iron and steel, including peasants, urban residents, students, and office workers. Trees were felled for fuel, buildings were stripped for wood, and household metal, including pots, tools, and sometimes farm implements, was fed into the furnaces.

The campaign produced metal, but much of it was of such low quality that it could not be used. Furnaces lacked temperature control and proper ore, and unskilled workers produced lumps of pig iron with high impurities. Official figures for 1958 reported about 11 million tons of steel, a total that met the target, but a considerable portion came from native furnaces and was unusable. The state later abandoned most small furnaces. The costs included lost labor, wasted fuel, environmental damage through deforestation, and, critically, the diversion of workers from harvest at the time when the large autumn crop needed gathering.

Historical Evidence

Steel Targets and Usable Production

Historians use official production statistics, technical inspections of metal quality, factory records, and memoirs to evaluate the steel campaign. Official figures show headline totals, but later Chinese assessments acknowledged that native-furnace output was largely substandard. The evidence limits: the statistical series blur what was produced with what was usable; memoirs may exaggerate or minimize; and no single source measures total labor diverted.

Students should therefore ask of any steel figure: Was it reported or verified? Does it count usable steel or all metal output? Which furnaces produced it?

Section 68.10

Irrigation Works, Construction, and Mass Labor Campaigns

Water control had been central to Chinese rural life for millennia, and the Great Leap added to it on an unprecedented scale. During the winter of 1957–1958 and again in later seasons, tens of millions of villagers were mobilized to build reservoirs, canals, embankments, and dams, mostly with hand tools, baskets, and carrying poles. Leaders joined symbolic labor projects, such as the Ming Tombs reservoir near Beijing in 1958, and the Miyun Reservoir, completed in 1960, became a major source of water for the capital. On the Yellow River, the Sanmenxia Dam, begun with Soviet design assistance, was completed in 1960 but soon suffered severe siltation, forcing costly modification.

Some of these projects had lasting value. But many were hastily designed with insufficient surveys or engineering; some dams failed, some canals caused waterlogging and salinization of fields, and some reservoirs flooded farmland and displaced villagers. The labor burden was heavy and often coerced; accidents and exhaustion were common. Because the projects were built during seasons when people would otherwise have farmed, tended livestock, or handled sideline production, the opportunity costs were high, and when work continued through the busy season, farm work suffered.

Water-control work illustrates the mixture of potential benefit and immediate danger. Irrigation systems can increase yields when designed well, maintained, and coordinated with seasonal farm labor. In the Leap, projects were frequently authorized without adequate surveys or equipment, and their size became a sign of political enthusiasm. Villagers dug earthworks with hand tools, sometimes during periods when they needed to prepare fields, harvest grain, or care for livestock. Poorly designed embankments could waste labor or create new hazards, while some completed works later remained useful. Historians must therefore distinguish the long-term utility of a particular project from the short-term opportunity cost of mobilizing labor to build it.

Section 68.11

Agricultural Experiments and the Misapplication of Science

The Leap promoted a set of cultivation techniques said to promise vast yields: close planting of seedlings far denser than normal, deep plowing to depths of several feet, heavy seeding and fertilizing, and campaigns to use new methods nationwide. These ideas drew partly on the influence of Soviet agronomist Trofim Lysenko, whose theories about plant growth were politically favored in the Soviet Union though rejected by most scientists, and partly on local experiments whose results were reported enthusiastically.

Many trained agronomists were cautious or opposed, but their voices were weakened by the political climate. Close planting, in practice, often caused crops to compete for light and nutrients, and deep plowing could bring infertile subsoil to the surface and use up labor. The newspapers carried reports of “satellite” fields, named after Sputnik, with yields claimed to be many times greater than known farming could achieve; some of these were produced by transplanting crops from several fields into one display plot. These reports shaped official expectations and set targets that no real farming could meet.

It would be a mistake to reduce the agricultural program to foolishness. Peasants and technicians had long experimented, and some practices, such as water conservation and improved seed selection, had value. The central problem was the political context: untested practices were mandated nationwide, with no space for local adaptation, and failed experiments could not be admitted without political risk.

Section 68.12

Environmental Campaigns, Labor Exhaustion, and Agricultural Disruption

Alongside production campaigns, the Leap included environmental campaigns. The Four Pests campaign, which targeted rats, flies, mosquitoes, and sparrows, mobilized millions of people, including children, to kill pests, scare sparrows from landing until they fell from exhaustion, and destroy nests. Sparrows were targeted because they were believed to eat grain. Ornithologists argued that they also ate insects, and by 1960 sparrows were removed from the list and replaced by bedbugs. Historians debate how much sparrow eradication contributed to crop losses; what is clear is that it was another claim on labor, and that it was driven by political mobilization rather than ecological assessment.

More important in aggregate was the cumulative strain on rural labor. In 1958, villagers worked long hours at irrigation, steelmaking, construction, and pest eradication, while the autumn harvest was rushed and in some places left unharvested. In 1959 and 1960, workers weakened by hunger had less capacity to produce, draft animals died or were slaughtered, tools were lost or destroyed, and cultivated area declined. The effect was a spiral in which food shortages reduced labor capacity, which reduced production, which deepened shortages.

Division III

Production Targets, False Reporting, and the Procurement Crisis

How falling agricultural production combined with policies that reduced rural access to food. Four quantities must be kept distinct: grain actually harvested, grain reported, grain procured by the state, and grain retained for rural consumption.

Section 68.13

Radical Production Targets and the Politics of Achievement

In 1958 national targets escalated quickly. Early planning figures for grain were revised upward again and again as provinces announced their own, often higher, goals. Provincial leaders competed to be recognized as models, county officials competed with neighboring counties, and communes with each other. Visits by senior leaders, publicity for “red banner” units, and promotions rewarded those who promised and reported the most. Failure to meet a target could be interpreted as a lack of commitment, or as “right-leaning conservatism,” a label with serious consequences after 1957.

This system produced a structure of incentives in which overstatement was rational for each individual official even if disastrous in aggregate. A county secretary who reported a modest harvest risked criticism; one who reported an exceptional harvest gained praise. Targets passed downward as quotas and reports passed upward as achievements, each level adding an increment to look good to the level above. Historians describe this as a collective escalation, driven neither by a single liar nor by simple ignorance but by a structure in which accurate reporting was penalized.

These incentives operated through several administrative stages. Central ambitions were translated into provincial goals; provincial leaders publicized model counties; county cadres submitted claims about individual communes; and the apparent success of one locality was used to increase expectations elsewhere. Newspapers amplified extreme claims, creating examples that were difficult to challenge without appearing politically backward. An official who privately doubted a figure might nevertheless repeat it because a superior had already endorsed it. The resulting data problem cannot be understood simply by counting deliberate lies: political selection, sincere belief, fear of punishment, and weak verification reinforced one another.

Section 68.14

Exaggerated Harvest Reports and the Breakdown of Information

The most important consequence of escalating targets was falsified reporting. Local cadres inflated yields; higher levels, aware that figures were optimistic, sometimes adjusted them downward only partially; and the national statistical system, which had been developing professional standards in the early 1950s, was politically undermined, with statisticians criticized for “conservatism.” At the national level, the 1958 grain harvest was initially announced at levels far above the level later accepted, with later revisions reducing the figure drastically. Official statistics later put 1958 output at approximately 200 million tons, close to the 1957 figure, rather than the multiples first claimed.

The consequence was not simply wrong numbers but wrong decisions. Leaders who believed that grain was abundant concluded that more labor could be freed from farming, that dining halls could serve generous meals, that grain could be exported, and that procurement could be raised. When the real harvest in 1959 and 1960 fell, the reporting system continued to supply optimistic numbers, and corrections could be treated as political disloyalty.

The changing estimates of the 1958 harvest provide a particularly clear case. Contemporary announcements included a figure of about 375 million tonnes, a number subsequently cut in revisions before the much lower retrospective estimate of approximately 200 million tonnes became standard. These figures do not describe a sequence of harvests; they describe changing claims about substantially the same harvest. Scholars use this contrast to explain why excessive procurement looked feasible to central officials while rural communities knew their granaries were far emptier. The relevant historical question is not only who possessed accurate local knowledge, but why that knowledge failed to alter policy at higher levels.

Historical Evidence

Reported versus Actual Grain Production

Historians compare contemporary announcements with later revised official series, provincial archives, internal reports, and demographic evidence. They also use independent indicators such as rural populations, area sown, and consumption to cross-check statistics. Revised official figures are not perfect either; they were produced by the same state and have been criticized and refined by scholars. Students should treat any single number as an estimate whose source must be named.

The key skill is distinguishing four numbers: the harvest the farmers actually brought in; the figure the cadres reported; the amount the state procured; and the grain left in rural households and collectives.

Section 68.15

State Grain Procurement and the Loss of Rural Food Supplies

Since 1953, the state had bought grain from the countryside under the unified purchase and supply system, setting quotas and prices and supplying cities, industry, the army, and deficit regions. Procurement quotas were calculated as shares of reported output. When reports were inflated, the quotas were inflated too. In 1959, the state procured about 64 million metric tons of grain, approximately 38 percent of the subsequently revised harvest of 170 million tons; the comparable figures for 1957 were about 46 million tons procured from 195 million tons of output (approximately 24 percent). These values follow the retrospective series used by Wei Li and Dennis Tao Yang (2005); other compilations report somewhat different procurement totals. The share left for rural consumption shrank drastically.

Procurement was enforced through political pressure. Cadres who failed to meet quotas were criticized, and villagers who were suspected of hiding grain could be searched, punished, or detained. In several places “anti-concealment” campaigns tore apart households searching for hidden food. Because dining halls and collective storage had replaced household kitchens, the grain that once might have remained as a household buffer was already under collective control. The system therefore transmitted the effect of false reports into real shortage: the quota was set for a harvest that did not exist, and was extracted from the harvest that did.

Procurement figures require careful interpretation. Gross collection was not the same as net extraction because the state sometimes returned grain to rural deficit areas or supplied grain through official channels. Nevertheless, the timing and rigidity of quotas mattered greatly. A delivery demanded immediately after harvest could remove the food a village needed for the lean season, while supplies promised later might fail to arrive. Local administrations also differed in how they calculated targets and reacted when a commune reported a shortfall. Some reduced demands or sought relief; others treated a shortfall as evidence that grain had been concealed. These differences help explain why comparable harvests could produce sharply different mortality outcomes.

Section 68.16

Food Distribution, Urban Priorities, and Unequal Access

China’s food system was not uniform. Residents of cities and many industrial towns held ration entitlements for grain and other staples, funded by state procurement, and the state prioritized urban supply to maintain industrial production and political stability. Large cities, especially Beijing, Shanghai, and Tianjin, were largely protected from the worst of the famine, although their rations too were reduced and quality declined. In the countryside, entitlements depended on what the collective produced and what the state took. The household registration system, tightened in 1958, restricted rural residents from moving to cities to find food, though some tried.

Access also varied within villages. Cadres in many places had privileged access to food, and kinship, labor status, and political standing affected who ate. The weakest, including the elderly, young children, the sick, and those labeled as class enemies, were most vulnerable. Economists have argued that the unequal distribution of available food across regions and groups was a major source of mortality variance; an equal distribution of the shortage would have caused hunger but far fewer deaths. Transport difficulties and rigid administrative channels also hindered relief in remote areas.

Food entitlements were tied to political and administrative status rather than to physiological need. A city worker with a ration card and access to a state shop could experience hunger and reduced variety yet retain a minimum supply, while a village household dependent on collective distribution might receive nothing once procurement and local stocks had exhausted the grain. Regional grain transfers were also shaped by railways, industrial priorities, and official classifications of surplus and deficit areas. A place that had previously supplied other regions could continue to be treated as a supplier after its output had collapsed. Aggregate national availability therefore conceals the mechanisms by which some people were protected and others exposed.

Section 68.17

Agricultural Decline, Weather, and Economic Disruption

Official accounts long described the years 1959–1961 as the “Three Years of Natural Disasters,” attributing the crisis to flood and drought. Later usage shifted to the neutral “Three Years of Difficulty.” Weather was in fact poor in parts of the country: there were droughts and floods in 1959–1961, and 1960 was a particularly bad year in several regions. But scholars who have compared weather records with output find that weather alone cannot explain either the scale of the decline or its geography. Output fell sharply in provinces and counties with ordinary weather, and mortality was highest where policy pressure was most intense.

Other factors compounded the decline. The sown area shrank; draft animals and tools were lost; fertilizer supplies were disrupted; and farmers’ incentives weakened as the connection between effort and reward was cut by collective accounting and free supply. Some scholars stress incentive effects, emphasizing that the loss of the right to leave the commune and reduced rewards for effort lowered productivity. Others stress labor diversion and the destruction of capital. The consensus is that policy factors were dominant and weather secondary, though weather mattered in particular places and years.

Grain Output, Procurement, and Rural Food Availability

Causes

Labor diversion, communal organization, weakened incentives, regional weather shocks, and inflated reports that produced high procurement quotas.

Transformation

Output fell while the state’s share rose; rural households and collectives were left with much less grain than in 1957.

Effects

Immediate: hunger, edema, and death in the countryside. Long-term: lost population, damaged rural trust in the state, and a rebuilt agricultural policy.

Historical complexity

The outcome depended on the interaction of lower production and higher extraction. Neither a poor harvest nor high procurement, taken alone, fully explains the geography of mortality.
Section 68.18

Grain Exports, International Obligations, and Policy Choices

The state continued to export grain during the early years of the famine. Net exports reached roughly four million metric tons in 1959 in one frequently used research series, used to earn foreign exchange, repay Soviet loans and pay for imports of industrial equipment, and to supply allied states. Researchers, including economists Hiroyuki Kasahara and Bingjing Li, have argued that exports, agricultural decline, and rigid procurement together contributed to excess mortality, and that no single factor is sufficient to explain the catastrophe. Exports were a smaller quantity than the shortfall, but in a crisis even a relatively small volume might have fed many people, and the decision to continue them reflects priorities set by leadership assumptions that grain was abundant, by obligations, and by reluctance to reveal weakness.

From late 1960 and early 1961, policy changed. China began to import substantial quantities of grain, chiefly from Canada and Australia, contracting several million tons annually for 1961 and later years, and exports were sharply reduced. Imports helped urban supply and some provinces, though they did not reach most famine regions in time to prevent the worst mortality there.

Grain trade was important both quantitatively and politically, but its contribution should be assessed with the correct denominator. Several million tonnes of net exports were small compared with total national production, yet potentially consequential when rural households faced acute shortages. Imports contracted in 1961 were not simply the mirror image of exports in 1959: transactions occurred in different years, were directed to particular ports and administrative consumers, and did not necessarily reach affected villages. The causal contribution of trade depends on the timing, geographic destination, and counterfactual availability of grain, not on the existence of exports alone.

China and the Wider World

The Leap was an active Chinese project, but it was shaped by international conditions: Cold War competition, Soviet models and loans, and a global faith in planned development. Foreign trade decisions, whether to export or import grain, show that China’s leaders made choices in a world economy, not isolated ones. Relations with the Soviet Union deteriorated from 1958 to 1960; the withdrawal of Soviet advisers in 1960 and debates over debt are discussed in Chapter 69, and historians disagree about how much they affected the famine, with most emphasizing domestic policy as the principal cause.

Division IV

The Great Famine: Regional Variation and Human Experience, 1958–1961

The famine was a human catastrophe whose severity varied substantially by locality, policy, social position, and access to food. Its regional differences are fundamental to understanding both causation and lived experience.

Section 68.19

The Emergence and Spread of Famine

The famine did not arrive everywhere at once. Hunger appeared in some localities as early as late 1958, when unharvested crops and diverted labor reduced supplies, though generous dining halls masked early shortage. In 1959, as procurement rose and the spring grain ran out, shortages and edema (swelling caused by malnutrition) were reported in many provinces. In 1960 the famine reached its worst level across a wide area: official national death rates rose from about 12 per thousand in 1958 to about 25 per thousand in 1960. In 1961 conditions improved unevenly as policies changed, though some regions remained critical, and by 1962 recovery was underway.

Geography mattered. Several provinces, among them Anhui, Sichuan, Guizhou, Henan, Gansu, and Qinghai, experienced exceptionally high mortality in most reconstructions, while others, including Guangdong, Yunnan, and parts of the northeast and the major cities, had lower rates, though none was untouched. Within provinces, differences between counties were extreme; some counties lost a large share of their population while neighboring counties fared much better.

The annual sequence also matters because death rates responded to cumulative deprivation rather than only to the harvest recorded in the same calendar year. Food consumed in the spring might come from grain retained after the previous autumn’s harvest. Poor nutrition weakened resistance to disease and the ability to work, further reducing agricultural output. Administrative data show a national mortality peak in 1960, but local crises began earlier and ended at different times. A national curve therefore summarizes many overlapping local disasters. The official national crude death rate rose from about 10.8 per thousand in 1957 to 25.4 in 1960, a severe increase that itself may understate the true excess mortality because registration was incomplete.

Section 68.20

Everyday Life During Famine

Famine transformed daily life. Households ate less and less: first reduced grain rations, then substitutes such as wild vegetables, leaves, bark, chaff, sweet potato vines, and “substitute foods” made by processing straw or husks, some of which were ineffective or harmful. Chronic hunger caused edema, wasting, and disease; people weakened by malnutrition died of infections, dysentery, and exposure. Families sold or bartered possessions where markets remained, and some sold or gave away children. Marriage and births declined: women stopped menstruating, couples were separated by labor assignments, and fertility fell sharply, with the national birth rate dropping from about 29 per thousand in 1958 to about 18 in 1961.

Community life frayed. Villagers who had once shared were pushed into competition; some neighbors reported each other for hiding food, while others shared at risk to themselves. Funerals were curtailed, graves unmarked, and entire households vanished. In some places, officials prevented people from leaving; some villagers fled to cities or other provinces, where the household registration system made survival harder. Children and the elderly were especially vulnerable, and in some places many orphans were left.

Section 68.21

Henan and the Xinyang Catastrophe

Henan, a populous central province on the North China Plain, had been an early center of commune experiments and enthusiastic reporting. Its leadership under Wu Zhipu promoted radical policies. The worst events occurred in Xinyang prefecture in the south of the province, where, in the autumn of 1959 and winter of 1960, mass deaths occurred. Chinese scholars and later investigations suggest that on the order of a million people died in the prefecture, a figure that should be treated as an estimate. Local officials continued to claim large harvests, pressed on with procurement, and launched campaigns against peasants accused of hiding grain, which involved beatings, confinement, and the seizure of food. Roads were reportedly watched to stop people leaving, and news was suppressed.

Reports and investigations gradually reached higher authorities during 1960; substantial official investigation and corrective action followed later that year. The timing and extent of outside intervention varied, and relief did not undo the mass mortality already recorded. The incident remained largely hidden from public view for decades. Xinyang demonstrates how local political pressure, procurement, coercion, and suppressed information could combine within a particular place, and it also shows that central and provincial authorities could act once they were informed. Historians, including Gao Hua and Yang Jisheng, have drawn on internal investigations, interviews, and archives to reconstruct events, and Western scholars have also studied Henan villages in depth.

Section 68.22

Anhui, Sichuan, and the Variation in Provincial Experience

Anhui and Sichuan illustrate how provincial leadership shaped outcomes. In Anhui, First Secretary Zeng Xisheng was among the Leap’s strongest supporters and promoted aggressive policies, including a program of exchanging household labor and implementing communal organization quickly. The province recorded one of the highest death rates in the country. In Sichuan, the most populous province, First Secretary Li Jingquan continued to send grain to other regions during the crisis, and Sichuan’s absolute mortality was among the highest in many estimates. Some provincial leaders, in contrast, adopted more cautious practices, reported more honestly, or adjusted earlier, and some provinces experienced more limited mortality.

These cases show that national policy was filtered through provincial choices, but also that structures of incentive and the speed of correction mattered. Comparisons between provinces are difficult: provincial statistics vary in reliability, populations have been recorded differently, and migration complicates counts.

Comparisons across provinces should test several mechanisms independently. A leadership committed to extremely high quotas could worsen food scarcity even where agricultural conditions were relatively favorable; an administration that acknowledged shortages and arranged return deliveries might reduce the danger. The political standing of a provincial first secretary affected whether local reports were challenged, while transport networks determined whether grain moved toward or away from distressed districts. Differences within Sichuan or Anhui could be as important as differences between the provinces. The resulting history is not a contest to find one uniquely culpable region but an inquiry into how a common national policy was implemented under markedly different conditions.

Multiple Perspectives

Villagers, Cadres, and Provincial Officials

Villagers: Peasants experienced the Leap as the loss of control over land, labor, and food. They faced pressure to work, to surrender grain, and to remain silent, and many saw family members die. Some resisted by hiding food or fleeing; most had few options.

Not equal positions: These groups did not hold equal power. Villagers bore the heaviest costs and had the least influence, while officials with authority over procurement and distribution shaped who had access to food.

Section 68.23

Famine in Gansu, Guizhou, Qinghai, and Frontier Regions

In the northwest and southwest, famine intersected with remoteness and poverty. Gansu, a dry province with limited farmland and a mixed Han, Hui, Tibetan, and other population, recorded very high mortality in some counties, and Guizhou, a mountainous southwestern province with many minority communities, had death rates among the highest in official statistics. Qinghai, on the Tibetan plateau, with Tibetan, Hui, Mongol, Salar, Tu, and Han communities, had exceptionally high rates in many estimates. Thin food reserves, difficult transport, and rigid procurement worsened conditions.

In several frontier areas, famine was compounded by political events. In Qinghai and parts of Gansu and Sichuan with Tibetan populations, collectivization of pastoral economies, restrictions on religion, and the violent suppression of unrest in 1958–1959 disrupted livelihoods. Documentation for minority and frontier regions is thinner and more politically sensitive, and often comes from outside administrators’ viewpoints. Historians must therefore be cautious about generalizing, and about assuming that a province’s experience represents all its communities. Local evidence, such as county gazetteers, oral accounts, and demographic data, is essential.

Frontier comparisons require more than adding ethnic labels to a provincial mortality table. Pastoral livelihoods depended on herds and seasonal movement rather than grain production alone; different regulations governing livestock, market exchange, and mobility could therefore have severe consequences even when grain statistics appeared less dramatic. In Tibetan areas, military repression and religious restrictions overlapped with economic campaigns, complicating the effort to isolate causes of hardship. Researchers must also specify which administrative territory a source describes, since provincial boundaries and the status of Tibetan regions changed. Where evidence does not permit a reliable community-level estimate, the text should acknowledge that limit rather than infer experience from provincial averages.

Regional and Frontier Context

Differential famine experiences. The north-central plain faced procurement-driven shortages; the Yangtze provinces combined high population density and intense mobilization; the southwest and northwest had limited margins and transport difficulties; frontier pastoral communities faced collectivization of herds and political campaigns; and coastal and urban areas benefited from state priorities. Understanding the famine means comparing these contexts instead of treating any one as typical.

Section 68.24

Rural and Urban Experiences of Food Shortage

Urban residents experienced shortage differently. In cities, grain rations were reduced, meat, oil, and vegetables became scarce, and work was affected by weakness; many urban residents suffered from edema and malnutrition. But the state sustained a minimum supply to maintain industry and order, and mortality in the major cities was far below that in the countryside. Occupation, work unit, and status affected supply: factories and government offices had special provisions, and cadres and some professionals received extra rations, in contrast to their rural counterparts.

Rural residents had no such guarantees. Their food came from what their collective produced minus what the state procured, and when those fell short, they could not claim state rations. Policy in 1960–1961 even sent millions of recent rural migrants back to the countryside, reducing the urban population by roughly twenty million over the readjustment years, depending on the definition and series, which eased urban supply but shifted the burden to villages. The contrast between town and country illustrates how the allocation of food by political priority, not only total supply, shaped who suffered.

Urban protection was relative, not absolute. City residents faced reduced rations, shortages of cooking oil and meat, overcrowded housing, and pressure to maintain production despite hunger. Yet the political priority assigned to industrial labor meant that urban grain supply often took precedence over rural need. The movement of workers back to the countryside during retrenchment further illustrates the hierarchy: reducing the number of urban consumers helped government ration systems but transferred people to villages already facing scarcity. Historical comparisons must distinguish the death rate of a city’s registered population from that of its surrounding rural county and account for changes in residence and registration.

Section 68.25

Coercion, Violence, Resistance, and Survival

Famine was accompanied by coercion. In places, cadres used beatings, confinement, public humiliation, withholding of food, and other punishments to extract grain or enforce work. The accounts of such violence, drawn from internal investigations and oral histories, vary in frequency; some localities were relatively free of it, while others, like parts of Xinyang, experienced severe abuse. Control over movement, through registration and local watch, left people with few ways to escape.

People nevertheless acted to survive. Some hid grain, stole from collective stores, ate in secret, foraged, or fled; some officials under-reported, distributed food or let villagers cultivate hidden plots, sometimes saving lives. Resistance took forms from quiet evasion to occasional protest and, rarely, armed incidents. Acts of assistance between kin and neighbors were also widely recorded. These varied actions show that people were not passive, but that their options depended heavily on local power relations.

Coercive extraction and violence were not incidental to the process of obtaining food. Where quotas could not be met, some local officials assumed that villagers were hiding grain; searches and punishments followed, and accusations could become a means of settling local disputes. At the same time, surviving records reveal officials who petitioned for reduced quotas, warned higher authorities, or quietly permitted private cultivation. These acts of discretion had consequences because villagers’ ability to secure food was constrained by the very institutions that assigned their labor and controlled storage. Oral histories can document suffering and resistance that official reports omit, but historians should test individual recollections against dates, records, and independent testimony when possible.

Historical Debate

What Caused the Famine, and How Important Was Procurement?

The question: Historians agree that policies, not weather alone, produced the famine. They disagree about the relative weight of the mechanisms: lower production caused by labor diversion and weakened incentives; excessive and rigid state procurement; unequal distribution of food; and exports.

Interpretation A — Procurement and distribution were decisive: Economists such as Xin Meng, Nancy Qian, and Pierre Yared find that inflexible procurement, which did not adjust to falling output, was a major driver of mortality, particularly in regions with high output potential where the state extracted more. Justin Yifu Lin and Dennis Yang (2000) stress that unequal access to food, rather than total supply alone, determined who died.
Interpretation B — Production collapse and incentives were fundamental: Justin Yifu Lin’s 1990 study emphasized that the loss of the right to withdraw from collectives reduced work incentives and output, while Dali Yang and others describe a broader institutional failure. Some analyses assign larger roles to weather or to the production shortfall as such, and others to a combination, as in Kasahara and Li’s finding that exports, agricultural decline, and procurement together mattered.

Evidence: Researchers use provincial and county statistics, weather records, procurement data, and demographic reconstructions. The evidence is imperfect and the models depend on assumptions.

Why it matters: The debate shapes understanding of how a policy failure became mass starvation, and whether earlier correction or different distribution could have saved lives.

Section 68.26

Famine Mortality, Missing Births, and Demographic Evidence

Estimating the number of deaths is one of the most difficult tasks in this history. The state conducted censuses in 1953, 1964, and 1982, and published annual population figures, but during the famine record-keeping was unreliable, deaths went unregistered, and officials had reasons to under-report. Demographers therefore reconstruct mortality by comparing census age structures, using life tables, and estimating what the population would have been without famine. Different assumptions produce different results, and published estimates of deaths attributable to the crisis span roughly 15 million to 45 million, but they differ in baseline, time window, and whether they count only demographic excess deaths or also violent deaths identified in archives.

A demographic estimate begins with a counterfactual: how many people would have died if pre-famine mortality patterns had continued? The answer depends on the assumed baseline, the number of people exposed, the period used, and adjustments for deaths that were never registered. Researchers also reconstruct the population by following the ages of cohorts from one census to the next and allowing for migration, changes in registration, and undercounting. Estimates that use the years 1958–1961 do not automatically match estimates limited to 1959–1961. Local gazetteers can reveal very high mortality within a county while remaining unsuitable for straightforward extrapolation to the entire country. The resulting range is evidence of difficult reconstruction, not a reason to avoid quantification.

The difference between death rates and excess deaths is equally important. An annual crude death rate counts deaths per thousand residents during the year; excess mortality asks how far observed deaths exceeded a plausible normal level. A famine also causes births to be postponed or forgone, and many children who were never conceived or born cannot be counted as deaths. Some public accounts combine excess deaths with missing births to describe a broader demographic shortfall, producing a larger figure. The chapter should identify exactly which quantity a cited scholar estimated and resist treating the largest available estimate as a settled minimum. Even conservative demographic reconstructions establish an extraordinary human catastrophe.

Uncertainty about totals does not create uncertainty about the occurrence of the catastrophe. All serious estimates describe a mortality crisis of enormous scale.

Division V

Leadership, the Lushan Conference, and the Policy Retreat, 1958–1962

Why warnings and corrective measures failed to prevent the continuation of disastrous policies, and how the state eventually retreated from the Great Leap.

Section 68.27

Early Warnings and Attempts to Correct the Great Leap

By late 1958 leaders recognized problems. Reports of exhaustion, food shortages, and the confusion of ownership reached Beijing. At meetings in Zhengzhou (November 1958 and again in early 1959), Mao criticized what was called the “communist wind” (gongchan feng): the arbitrary transfer of resources, labor, and property from households and lower units to higher levels without compensation, along with exaggeration and coercion. At the Wuchang meetings in November–December 1958 the Party adopted a resolution on communes that moderated some claims, and Mao announced he would not seek another term as state chairman; Liu Shaoqi took the post in April 1959, while Mao remained Party chairman.

These early corrections were limited. They aimed to fix excesses within the Leap, not to abandon it. Targets for 1959 were reduced but remained high; the steel campaign continued in modified form; and communes were retained. The reports of food shortage were often treated as problems of local management or the work of “bad elements.” The limits of early correction help explain why the crisis deepened in 1959.

The chronology complicates accounts that place all awareness after the famine had become catastrophic. Senior leaders publicly acknowledged inflated claims and excessive communal practices in late 1958; meetings at Zhengzhou and Wuchang discussed the limits of free supply and overly rapid movement toward communism. Yet these corrections did not resolve the underlying reporting system or the political penalties for delivering unwelcome news. Some localities continued the most extreme practices while others scaled them back. The key question is therefore why recognition of error did not lead to sustained, verifiable reductions in targets and procurement: information remained contested, and the authority to reverse the movement was closely tied to the leadership that had promoted it.

Section 68.28

The Lushan Conference and Peng Dehuai’s Criticism

In July 1959 the leadership met at Lushan, a mountain resort in Jiangxi, to review the Leap. The initial atmosphere permitted frank discussion of shortcomings. Defense Minister Peng Dehuai, who had visited rural areas including his home region in Hunan and had seen distress, wrote a private letter to Mao on 14 July. Mao had the letter circulated on 16 July. Mao’s speech of 23 July reacted angrily, and the conference turned from reviewing problems to condemning criticism. A subsequent plenum (2–16 August) denounced Peng and associates, including Huang Kecheng, Zhang Wentian, and Zhou Xiaozhou, as an “anti-Party clique”; Peng was removed as defense minister and replaced by Lin Biao.

Peng’s letter was significant for both its substance and its political setting. He wrote as a senior Communist official who supported socialist construction and recognized achievements, not as an advocate of restoring private ownership. His criticisms concerned unrealistic accomplishments, hasty decisions, and the habit of treating enthusiasm as proof of feasibility. Mao’s response changed the character of the meeting. Instead of weighing the complaints primarily as evidence about rural production, participants were pressured to take sides in a political judgment about Peng. The subsequent August plenum condemned an alleged anti-Party group. This made the practical question—whether targets and methods were harming rural production—harder to discuss openly. Peng’s removal also signaled to officials elsewhere that criticism could be reinterpreted as an attack on leadership authority.

Section 68.29

The Anti-Right-Opportunist Campaign and Renewed Radicalism

After Lushan, the Party launched a campaign against “right opportunism” in Party organizations nationwide. Chinese sources report that several million Party members and cadres were criticized or disciplined. The effect was to punish those who reported shortage, questioned targets, or resisted procurement, and to reward those who affirmed the Leap. Mao and others viewed criticism as evidence of wavering in the face of obstacles.

The consequences for the famine were grave. A second Leap was proclaimed for 1960, with high targets; procurement continued at high levels in 1959–1960; and officials who might have reported the crisis hesitated. Because the main period of rising mortality (1959–1960) coincided with this campaign, historians identify Lushan as a decisive point at which a chance of earlier correction was lost. Mao’s knowledge of conditions is debated, but the available evidence suggests that he had received warning signs and that political considerations led him to prioritize defending the Leap.

The effects went beyond the removal of a few senior officials. County and provincial cadres who had expressed caution were investigated or pressed to retract objections, while renewed emphasis on ideological commitment encouraged the continuation of unrealistic plans. Enforcement was uneven: some administrators continued to make quiet adjustments, but others regarded renewed procurement and mobilization as proof of loyalty. The anti-right-opportunist campaign therefore intensified a structural problem already visible in 1958: the information most needed for correction was also the information politically most dangerous to report. This helps explain why the famine worsened in many localities after the leadership had ample indications that the Leap was not meeting its objectives.

Section 68.30

Recognition of Crisis and the Beginning of Retrenchment

By mid-1960 evidence of mass starvation accumulated: reports from provinces, investigations such as that in Xinyang, and the testimony of officials visiting villages. Zhou Enlai, Liu Shaoqi, Chen Yun, and others became increasingly alarmed. In November 1960 the Central Committee issued the Twelve-Point Urgent Directive on Rural Work, which curtailed the “communist wind,” restored some private plots and sideline production, reduced procurement burdens, and made clear that dining halls should not be forced. The directive was a first official retreat, and in the winter of 1960–1961 relief and some imports began.

The deterioration of relations with the Soviet Union in 1960, including the withdrawal of advisers and demands for debt repayment, made the economic position more difficult, and these developments are treated in Chapter 69. The decision to begin retrenchment reflects a combination of mounting evidence, leaders’ alarm, and economic necessity.

Evidence of crisis reached the center through a combination of routine statistics, provincial inspections, reports by officials, and testimony about particular disaster areas. Each route had limits. Reports could be diluted as they moved through hierarchies, and local officers could conceal deaths that might expose their own misconduct. The emergence of serious discussion within the leadership did not immediately produce food for villages, because reopening transport and adjusting quotas required administrative decisions and because the preceding harvests had already been damaged. Retrenchment should therefore be treated as a process rather than a moment of sudden discovery or benevolent rescue. Its first measures were important but late, and many households had already lost members and productive assets.

Section 68.31

The Retreat from the Great Leap and the Turning Point of 1961–1962

In January 1961, the Ninth Plenum adopted a policy summarized in four characters: adjustment, consolidation, filling out, and raising standards. Heavy industrial investment was cut, many factories closed, and urban workers were sent back to the countryside. Mao encouraged investigation of rural conditions, and leaders such as Liu Shaoqi and Zhou Enlai went to villages. In March 1961 the Party issued draft regulations on rural people’s commune work, the “Sixty Articles,” revised in 1962, which shifted the basic accounting unit downward and by 1962 settled it at the production team, abolished the free supply system and compulsory dining halls, and restored private plots and sideline production.

The Seven Thousand Cadres Conference of January–February 1962 brought together cadres from the center down to the county level to review the experience. Liu Shaoqi’s report acknowledged serious errors and used the formula, in a speech reported in sources, that the difficulties resulted partly from natural disasters but more from human error. Mao made a limited self-criticism and defended democratic centralism, and Lin Biao praised Mao’s leadership. The conference served as a reassessment, but it left tensions that shaped Chapter 69.

The retreat also exposed the difference between ending a destructive campaign and creating a viable new system. Moving accounting and distribution toward smaller production teams gave villagers a somewhat closer relationship between work and reward; reducing communal dining restored space for household cooking and private activity. These steps took time to implement and were unevenly enforced. Restoring agricultural output required seed, draft animals, labor, and confidence that increased production would not simply be taken away. The readjustment began while famine conditions persisted in some regions. The longer economic recovery, the changing balance of authority among senior leaders, and the political disputes generated by these policies are properly the subject of Chapter 69.

Division VI

Historical Responsibility, Evidence, and Memory

Bringing the explanatory arguments together without flattening a complex disaster into a single cause. The famine is historically established; scholars disagree about the mortality total, the relative importance of mechanisms, and the distribution of responsibility.

Section 68.32

Why Did the Great Leap Forward Become a Catastrophe?

The explanations examined in this chapter operate at different levels. At the level of leadership decisions, a voluntarist outlook, an aversion to criticism after 1957, and the defense of the Leap after Lushan set the policy direction and delayed correction. At the level of institutions, the commune and dining hall removed household control over food, the reporting system rewarded exaggeration, and weak accountability prevented the system from learning. At the level of economic mechanisms, labor diversion, weakened incentives, high procurement, exports, and unequal distribution turned reduced output into mass mortality. Environmental conditions, including droughts and floods in some regions, and international circumstances, including Soviet relations, played secondary roles. Coercion determined how the burden fell on particular communities.

These factors interacted. Inflated targets produced inflated reports; inflated reports produced high procurement; procurement drew down food that the commune system had moved out of households’ hands; coercion and household registration prevented flight; and political pressure delayed correction. Removing any one link might have reduced mortality; none alone explains the whole. The relative weight of each remains the subject of research.

An explanation of this kind should keep distinct the causes of reduced production and the causes of death after production fell. Labor diversion, poor agronomic techniques, and weakened incentives affected the harvest; procurement rules, rationing hierarchies, and restrictions on movement affected who could obtain the remaining grain. Political repression and false reporting connected the two by delaying corrective decisions. Weather and international trade added pressures whose scale varied by year and region. Researchers disagree about the weight to assign each mechanism, but the distinction between output, entitlement, and mortality prevents a vague conclusion that the famine occurred simply because the harvest was poor.

Section 68.33

Historical Interpretation, Responsibility, and the Memory of Famine

Responsibility operated at many levels. Mao Zedong, as the leader who most strongly promoted the Leap and whose decisions at Lushan prolonged it, bears major responsibility in most scholarly assessments. But responsibility also lay with other central leaders who supported or implemented policies, provincial leaders who pushed or failed to correct them, and county and commune officials who enforced quotas, though they acted within a system that punished dissent. Distinguishing levels of responsibility is not the same as excusing any.

Official assessments have evolved. For years the crisis was attributed to natural disasters and Soviet pressure. The Party’s 1981 Resolution on certain questions in its history acknowledged that the Great Leap and the commune movement were serious errors caused by leftist mistakes, with Mao bearing responsibility, while also stressing his overall achievements. Public discussion, publications, and research on the famine have been limited and sometimes restricted, but scholars in China and abroad have used newly available local archives, oral history, and demographic work. Memory persists in families and villages.

The historical record is affected by the politics of preservation and release. Official statistical revisions provide national series but cannot on their own explain how villagers died; local investigations expose particular forms of coercion but may reflect the questions authorities chose to ask. Memoirs and oral testimony recover individual experiences while requiring attention to memory, translation, and circumstances of publication. Scholars sometimes reach different mortality totals or assign different emphasis to central and local decisions because they use different sources and definitions. A serious textbook should describe these differences without suggesting that the existence of scholarly debate makes the famine itself doubtful. The question of who knew what, at what time, remains central to assigning responsibility.

Historical Debate

Responsibility, Evidence, and Competing Interpretations

The question: How should historians assign responsibility for the famine among the central leadership, provincial and local officials, and structural features of the system, and how should they use incomplete and contested evidence?

Interpretation A — Leadership-centered: Scholars such as Roderick MacFarquhar and Frank Dikötter emphasize Mao’s decisions and the political dynamics of the leadership, especially at Lushan, as the decisive causes of the scale and duration of the disaster.
Interpretation B — System-centered: Scholars such as Dali Yang, Yang Jisheng, and many economists emphasize institutional features, including the structure of incentives, information, and accountability, and the role of local officials, arguing that similar outcomes could result from a system that suppressed accurate reporting, even if individual choices varied.

Evidence: Leadership documents and speeches, provincial archives, procurement and statistical data, demographic reconstructions, and oral testimony. Each interpretation draws on all of these but weights them differently, and the two are partly complementary.

Why it matters: The question concerns how political systems fail and how history is remembered and discussed publicly.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Great Leap Forward, launched in 1958, was an ambitious attempt by the Chinese Communist Party to accelerate industrial and agricultural development through mass mobilization, people’s communes, and revolutionary enthusiasm, and that it produced one of the most devastating famines of the twentieth century. Its leaders hoped that collective organization of labor would allow China to overcome economic backwardness in a dramatically shortened period. Disaster was neither intended nor inevitable; it resulted from the interaction of specific choices and institutions.

The origins lay in the imbalances of the First Five-Year Plan, doubts about the Soviet model, Mao Zedong’s voluntarist confidence, Cold War competition, and the political climate created by the Anti-Rightist Campaign, which discouraged dissent. The General Line of 1958 and the conferences at Nanning, Chengdu, and Beidaihe turned planning into mass campaigns with escalating targets.

Within months rural China was reorganized into about 26,000 people’s communes. Labor was commanded in military style; communal dining halls removed control over cooking and storage from households; backyard furnaces and irrigation drives pulled workers from farming; agricultural experiments imposed untested methods nationwide; and campaigns such as the Four Pests drive added further claims on labor. Each was a distinct policy with its own effects, but together they disrupted agriculture.

The central mechanism was informational and fiscal. Escalating targets rewarded exaggeration, and falsified reports led to inflated procurement quotas. In 1959 the state took roughly two-fifths of a harvest that had in fact fallen, leaving villages with far less grain than in 1957, while exports continued into the early crisis. Food distribution favored cities, cadres, and some groups, and the household registration system limited escape. Weather mattered in some places, but it does not explain either the scale or the geography of the famine.

The famine varied widely. Henan’s Xinyang prefecture, Anhui, Sichuan, Guizhou, Gansu, and Qinghai suffered exceptionally, while major cities and some provinces were less affected. Within regions, differences between counties and between households were profound. Minority and frontier communities faced the additional effects of collectivization and political campaigns, though evidence about them is thinner. Daily life was transformed by hunger, disease, family separation, and the breakdown of village solidarity, and by coercion and, in some places, violence. People also resisted, hid food, fled, and helped one another.

Estimating mortality is a methodological challenge. Published estimates of deaths attributed to the crisis extend broadly from roughly 15 million to 45 million, but the upper figures are not strictly comparable with narrower demographic reconstructions of excess mortality; all must be distinguished from missing births and broader population loss. Uncertainty about totals does not undermine the historical fact of mass death.

Leadership politics prolonged the crisis. Early recognition of excesses in late 1958 produced limited corrections, but Peng Dehuai’s letter and the Lushan Conference of 1959 turned criticism into a political offense. The campaign against right opportunism renewed radical policies at the moment mortality was rising.

Retreat began in late 1960 with the Twelve-Point Directive and developed in 1961–1962 through readjustment, the dissolution of dining halls, grain imports, urban-to-rural relocation, the Sixty Articles that shifted the accounting base to the production team, and the Seven Thousand Cadres Conference. The retreat modified the Leap without abandoning collective agriculture or Party monopoly on power.

Historians continue to debate the relative weight of procurement, production decline, distribution, exports, and weather, and the distribution of responsibility among Mao, other central leaders, provincial leaders, and local cadres. Most scholars find that no single factor suffices, and that leadership decisions and institutional design both mattered.

The chapter contributes to the larger history of Chinese civilization by showing how state capacity, ambitious policy, distorted information, and weak accountability can combine with human consequences, and by setting the stage for the leadership conflicts and recovery examined in Chapter 69. It should not be read as a story in which the People’s Republic was inevitably bound for catastrophe, nor as a summary of all Mao-era history; it is a study of a particular set of decisions and mechanisms.

Chapter Reflection

The Great Leap Forward and its famine matter because they show how an ambitious program, backed by real commitment to national development, could be turned into catastrophe by the way institutions handled information, incentives, and power. They also show how many people were affected differently: villagers who lost control over food, cadres squeezed between demands and neighbors, urban residents shielded by priorities, and frontier communities with limited evidence of their experiences.

The events shaped later Chinese history. They produced leadership divisions that surfaced again in the early 1960s and in the Cultural Revolution (Chapters 69–70), led to a restructuring of rural organization that lasted until reform, and left a legacy in family and village memory. They informed later debates in China about planning, markets, and the limits of mobilization, which feature in Chapters 71 and 72.

The chapter connects China to wider histories of twentieth-century development, Cold War competition, and famine. At the same time, it is a reminder that Chinese history is not a straight line toward any single outcome. It includes achievement and catastrophe, mobilization and resistance, reform and retreat. Understanding it with careful evidence, distinguishing what is established from what is estimated or contested, helps students see Chinese history as diverse, contested, adaptive, and unfinished.
