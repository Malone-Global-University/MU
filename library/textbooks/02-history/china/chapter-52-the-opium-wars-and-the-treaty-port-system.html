Part IX — Nineteenth-Century Crisis and the End of the Qing
52

The Opium Wars and the Treaty-Port System

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

In the spring of 1839 an imperial commissioner entered Guangzhou with a simple mandate and an unsimple problem: end the opium trade. Twenty-one years later, an Anglo-French army burned the emperor's favorite pleasure garden outside Beijing and the court signed a convention that rewrote how the Qing empire dealt with the world. Between those two moments lay two wars, a series of treaties, and the creation of an entirely new legal and commercial order.

When Lin Zexu reached Guangzhou in March 1839, he was not an ignorant or isolated official improvising a response to a danger he did not understand. He was one of the most experienced administrators of the Daoguang reign, a scholar-official who had managed river works, grain supply, and provincial finance, and who had already pursued opium suppression in the central Yangtze provinces. He had been summoned to Beijing for repeated audiences with the emperor and sent south with extraordinary authority. What he found at the Pearl River estuary was a trade far larger, more profitable, and more deeply interwoven with Chinese officials, Chinese merchants, British India, and the finances of the British Empire than any of the earlier prohibition campaigns had confronted. This chapter begins with that collision.

This chapter studies the First Opium War (1839–1842) and the Second Opium War (1856–1860), and, more importantly, the institutional order that grew from them. Its central argument is that these were not simply wars "about opium." They grew from the commercial, diplomatic, and legal conflicts established in Chapter 51: disputes over who could trade, under what regulations, through which ports, with what protection, and under whose law. Opium was central to the first crisis because it was the commodity that financed British purchases of tea and silk, drained Chinese silver, and prompted the Qing to destroy British-owned property. But the war was also about diplomatic equality, jurisdiction over foreigners, market access, and the right of the British government to protect a trade it regarded as legitimate even when the Qing government had declared parts of it illegal.

What changed was profound. Before 1839, the Qing regulated maritime trade with Europeans through the Canton System, a framework under which foreign trade was concentrated at one port, conducted through licensed Chinese merchants, and governed by Qing law on Qing terms. By 1860, military defeat had forced the Qing to accept a treaty framework that opened a growing list of ports, fixed tariffs by agreement, established foreign consuls with legal authority over their own nationals, permitted foreign residence, protected missionaries, placed resident ministers in Beijing, and ceded territory at Hong Kong Island and southern Kowloon. These provisions limited the exercise of Qing sovereignty in ways that the Qing had not freely chosen and could not unilaterally reverse. Historians call the resulting structure the treaty system.

What continued is equally important. The Qing remained a functioning imperial government that ruled the overwhelming majority of its territory and some four hundred million people. The treaty ports were small enclaves, in a handful of coastal and riverine cities; the great interior of the empire, with its villages, lineages, granaries, and local magistrates, lived with the consequences indirectly. Qing officials negotiated, resisted, delayed, reinterpreted, and sometimes accommodated treaty provisions. Chinese merchants, boatmen, compradors, migrants, and scholars responded to the new order in ways that ranged from collaboration to militia mobilization to intellectual reassessment of China's place in the world. The chapter therefore resists two simple stories: one of a passive China overwhelmed by Western power, and another of a trade dispute whose consequences were minor.

The people affected were many. Chinese opium smokers, dealers, and officials who profited from or struggled against the trade; Hong merchants whose monopoly was abolished; coastal fishing and boat communities caught in the fighting; Manchu bannermen and Chinese soldiers who defended walls at Zhenjiang and Dinghai; gentry-led militias around Guangzhou; British and American merchants; Indian peasants who cultivated poppy under British-Indian regulation and Indian soldiers who fought in the British expeditions; French and British missionaries; and the emperors, ministers, and diplomats who made policy under severe pressure. These groups did not experience the wars in the same way, and this chapter tries to keep their different experiences visible.

Historians reconstruct these events from an unusually rich set of sources: Qing memorials and imperial edicts, Lin Zexu's own writings, British parliamentary papers and diplomatic correspondence, merchants' letters, missionary journals and interpreters' memoirs, the texts of the treaties in Chinese and English, local gazetteers, and later Chinese reflections such as Wei Yuan's geography of maritime nations. Each source reflects a vantage point: an official's, a merchant's, a diplomat's, a missionary's. None is neutral, and the treaty texts themselves exist in multiple language versions whose translation sometimes mattered. Part of the work of this chapter is learning to read these sources against one another.

Finally, the chapter connects China to wider histories: the political economy of the British Empire in India, the industrializing naval technology of the mid-nineteenth century, the global movement of silver, the European expansion of Christian missions, the Crimean War and the Indian Rebellion of 1857, and Russian pressure along the Amur. It also looks ahead. The treaty system created the institutional backdrop for the Taiping Rebellion (Chapter 53), the Self-Strengthening Movement (Chapter 54), and the intensified imperialism of the 1890s (Chapter 55). But it should not be read teleologically, as though Qing decline or later revolution were fixed in 1842 or 1860. This chapter ends with the treaty order as it stood in 1860 and the first Chinese responses to it.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain Qing motives for opium suppression in 1838–1839 and the measures taken by Lin Zexu at Guangzhou.
  2. Distinguish between the immediate triggers and the deeper commercial, diplomatic, and legal causes of the First and Second Opium Wars.
  3. Analyze why British officials chose military action in 1840, and how domestic British debate, Indian opium revenue, and merchant lobbying shaped that choice.
  4. Trace the major campaigns of 1840–1842 and 1856–1860, and explain how control of coasts, rivers, and the approaches to Beijing shaped Qing negotiating positions.
  5. Evaluate Qing military and political responses without reducing the wars to a contest between a "modern" West and a "backward" China.
  6. Interpret the provisions of the Treaty of Nanjing, and identify what it did and did not establish.
  7. Describe how supplementary treaties, most-favored-nation clauses, and the Treaties of Tianjin and Convention of Beijing built a broader treaty system.
  8. Distinguish among treaty ports, concessions, colonies, extraterritoriality, and leased territories as legal and political categories.
  9. Assess the social and economic consequences of the treaty-port order, including the rise of Shanghai, for different groups of Chinese and foreign residents.
  10. Compare competing historical interpretations of the causes of the First Opium War and of the "unequal treaties."
  11. Contextualize the wars within British imperial, Indian, Russian, and global developments, and within Qing domestic crises including the Taiping Rebellion.
  12. Describe early Chinese responses to the treaty system, including resistance, accommodation, and intellectual reassessment.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did a dispute over illegal opium, silver, and merchant security in Guangzhou become a war between the Qing empire and the British Empire?
  2. Why is it historically misleading to describe the Opium Wars as only wars "about opium," and why is it equally misleading to ignore opium?
  3. What did the Qing government hope to achieve through suppression, negotiation, and military resistance, and how did those aims change as the wars progressed?
  4. Why did Qing forces sometimes resist effectively at the local level yet fail to prevent British success across the coast and the Yangtze?
  5. What made the treaties negotiated between 1842 and 1860 "unequal," and how did that inequality operate in law, tariffs, jurisdiction, and diplomacy?
  6. How did the treaty system differ from the old Canton System, and what stayed the same?
  7. How did treaty ports differ from colonies, and why did Shanghai grow so rapidly?
  8. How did Chinese officials, merchants, gentry, and ordinary people respond to the new order?
  9. How do historians use sources written by participants with strongly opposed interests to reconstruct events such as the destruction of opium at Humen or the burning of the Summer Palace?

Historical Context

By the 1830s the Qing was an empire of roughly four hundred million people, governed by the Manchu Aisin Gioro dynasty and extending from the South China Sea to the Tianshan, from Manchuria to the Tibetan plateau (Chapters 47–49). It had expanded across Inner Asia in the eighteenth century and still devoted much of its strategic attention to the northwest, to Mongolia, Tibet, and the Ili valley. Its Han Chinese heartland had experienced extraordinary population growth, putting pressure on land, local government, and granary systems. The court of the Daoguang emperor (r. 1820–1850) faced fiscal strain, uneven provincial administration, and growing concern about corruption, popular unrest, and the reliability of the silver-based tax and currency system. Coastal maritime trade, though economically important to Guangdong and Fujian, was not the center of the Qing's strategic imagination.

Maritime trade with Europe had been organized since the mid-eighteenth century around Guangzhou. The Qing court limited European commerce to that port and relied on the Hoppo (the imperial customs superintendent), the Liangguang governor-general, and a group of licensed merchants known as the Hong merchants, organized as the Cohong, to regulate foreign traders (Chapter 51). Foreign merchants lived seasonally in factories along the riverfront, could not bring wives, and were largely excluded from the walled city. This was not an arbitrary system. It drew on long Qing practice of governing foreign groups through intermediaries and local officials, and it had provided the Qing with revenue, security, and control. But it was also a system in which Europeans, especially the British, felt increasingly constrained: confined to one port, dependent on monopoly merchants, subject to unpredictable fees, and unable to communicate with Qing officials as diplomatic equals.

Britain's position changed markedly in the 1830s. In 1834, Parliament ended the East India Company's monopoly on the China trade, opening the field to private firms such as Jardine, Matheson and Company and putting a government-appointed Superintendent of Trade, Lord Napier, in Guangzhou. Napier's mission, which ran headlong into Qing procedures for correspondence and residence, ended in humiliation and his death at Macao in 1834. British merchants and officials drew from it the conclusion that the old arrangements were incompatible with Britain's growing power and commercial ambitions. Qing officials drew a different conclusion: that they needed to maintain firm control over foreign contact. The two sides were reading the same events through different institutional and cultural frameworks.

Underlying the entire relationship was the flow of goods and silver. British consumers demanded tea, which could be bought almost entirely in China; Chinese consumers wanted relatively little that Britain could sell. British-Indian opium, grown under the East India Company's monopoly in Bengal and by independent producers in Malwa, was sold at auction in Calcutta to private traders who carried it to China, where Chinese dealers distributed it illegally. The proceeds paid for tea, contributed substantially to British-Indian government revenue, and, by the late 1830s, were widely believed by Qing officials to be reversing the flow of silver that had long moved into China. Whatever the exact mechanisms (and historians still debate them), many officials believed that the drain of silver, combined with opium addiction among officials, soldiers, and commoners, endangered fiscal stability and military readiness. The Daoguang court debated legalization, regulated tolerance, and strict suppression before choosing the last.

The geography of the Pearl River delta shaped what followed. Foreign ships anchored at Lintin Island and Whampoa, at some distance from Guangzhou, and opium ships long operated from offshore anchorages beyond direct Qing supervision. The river approaches to Guangzhou passed the Humen narrows (Bocca Tigris, or the Bogue), guarded by forts. Macao, a Portuguese-administered settlement under Qing sovereignty with long-standing negotiated privileges and ground-rent arrangements, provided a retreat. To the east lay the island of Hong Kong, a sparsely settled place used as a sheltered anchorage, inhabited by fishing and boat communities and stone-cutting laborers. North of Guangdong lay Fujian, Zhejiang, and the Yangtze delta, with ports and canals through which grain, salt, silk, and tea moved to the empire's economic heartland. It was along that coast and into the Yangtze that British naval power would later exert its greatest pressure.

Students should keep in mind that "China" in this chapter means the Qing empire, a multiethnic Inner Asian dynasty, not a modern nation-state with fixed borders, and that the Qing court dealt with Britain through institutions designed for a very different set of relationships, including those with Mongols, Tibetans, Central Asian rulers, Russia, Korea, and Southeast Asian states. Terms such as "tribute" and "barbarian" (yi) had specific meanings in Qing diplomatic language and do not translate into a single simple concept (Chapter 51). The Qing had signed treaties before, notably with Russia at Nerchinsk (1689) and Kiakhta (1727), so the idea of a negotiated agreement with a foreign power was not alien. What was new after 1842 was the combination of military coercion, privileged legal status for foreigners, and the absence of reciprocal rights.

Historical Evidence

The Opium Wars and the treaty system are unusually well documented, but every body of evidence has a perspective, a purpose, and limits. Students should learn the kinds of sources before entering the sections that rely on them.

Qing memorials and imperial edicts, preserved in the archives of the Grand Council and in published compilations, show how the court received information and issued instructions, but memorials were written to satisfy the emperor and to protect their authors, and reports of battles were sometimes inaccurate. Lin Zexu's diaries, letters, and official papers reveal his reasoning and his gathering of foreign information, but they are also the testimony of a man defending his policy after dismissal. Local gazetteers, gentry writings, and later Chinese reflections preserve the viewpoint of coastal communities and militias, though many were compiled after the events and shaped by later memory.

British parliamentary papers, diplomatic correspondence, and military and naval records document British aims, decisions, and campaigns, but the published "Blue Books" were selected and edited for political effect. Merchants' letters, and the testimony of interpreters and missionaries such as Karl Gützlaff and the Morrison family, provide direct observation of Guangzhou, the coast, and the treaty ports, from foreign commercial or religious standpoints. Naval and military memoirs record campaigns vividly but tend to emphasize heroic action and technological contrast. Indian sources on opium production and on the soldiers who served in the expeditions are less abundant and require separate attention.

Treaty texts exist in multiple language versions, English, Chinese, French, and Russian, and translation sometimes mattered: the French text of 1860 on missionary land rights is a notorious case. Treaties record what the parties agreed on paper, not how provisions worked in practice. Customs, tariff, and consular records show trade and legal practice in the ports, and maps, prints, and illustrations from both sides show how each visualized the other, while also reflecting artistic convention and propaganda.

Casualty and economic estimates are uncertain: Qing military and civilian deaths were not reliably counted, British combat deaths were few relative to deaths from disease, and opium import figures vary by source. Finally, the wars have been remembered and used politically by later nationalist, imperial, and revolutionary narratives, in China and in Britain, so students must separate contemporary evidence from later memory. Whenever possible, compare at least two sources from opposite sides on a single event, such as the destruction of opium at Humen or the fighting at Zhenjiang, and ask what each knows, what it cannot know, and what it wants its readers to believe.

Section 52.1

Lin Zexu and the Opium Crisis

The Qing prohibition of opium was far older than Lin Zexu. The 1729 statute targeted trafficking in madak (opium mixed with tobacco) and the dens associated with it; later Qing measures progressively widened prohibition, with consumption criminalized more explicitly in 1813, and the court repeatedly condemned smoking and the importation of the drug. What changed in the 1830s was scale. By the end of the decade, imports into China had reached tens of thousands of chests a year, and the trade reached deep into officialdom, the military, and the commercial world of the southeast coast. Provincial authorities had arrested and executed Chinese traffickers before Lin's arrival, but local enforcement was uneven: some officials profited from the trade, some tolerated it, and some pursued it zealously.

The court debate was real. In 1836 a Qing official, Xu Naiji, had argued for legalization and taxation of opium on the grounds that prohibition had failed and that legal import would at least allow the state to regulate and tax it. Other officials answered that legalization would legitimize a drug that was damaging health and morale and that continued drainage of silver would be ruinous. By 1838, the case for suppression had gained strength: memorialists warned of silver outflow, the effects of addiction on soldiers and officials, and the moral danger of tolerating a harmful commerce. A memorial urging severe penalties, including capital punishment for habitual smokers, circulated widely. The Daoguang emperor solicited opinions from provincial governors, and Lin, then governor-general of Huguang, replied with an account of suppression in Hubei and Hunan and a warning about the threat opium posed to the state's capacity to pay and field soldiers.

Lin was therefore appointed imperial commissioner (qinchai dachen, 钦差大臣), a special envoy of the emperor who could override ordinary provincial hierarchies for a defined task. He was summoned to Beijing in late 1838, received repeated audiences, and traveled south with authority over the Guangdong naval forces and with instructions to end the trade at its source. He arrived at Guangzhou on 10 March 1839. Lin should be understood as a serious statesman operating inside a long tradition of Confucian moral statecraft and practical administration, not as a naive moralist. He was known for competent management of river works and public finance and had a record of attention to local conditions. He also came with a belief, shared by many Qing officials, that foreign merchants could be regulated by clear rules and firm incentives.

His first measures targeted both Chinese and foreign participants. He ordered Chinese dealers and officials to cooperate, arrested traffickers, and publicized the penalties for smoking and selling. He then demanded that foreign merchants surrender all opium in their possession and sign a bond promising never again to bring opium to China, on pain of forfeiting cargo and, under Chinese law as written, the lives of offenders. When foreign merchants were slow to comply, Lin sealed off the foreign factories at Guangzhou, withdrew Chinese servants, and prevented foreigners from leaving. He also demanded that the British merchant Lancelot Dent come to the city for questioning. Several hundred foreigners were confined in the factory area for six weeks.

Lin's goals were broader than seizing opium. He sought to restore the credibility of imperial law at a port where law had long been bent, to protect silver reserves, to discipline subordinate officials, and to create enforceable guarantees that foreign merchants would not reintroduce the trade. He also composed letters addressed to the British monarch appealing to moral reasoning and explaining Qing law; these were translated and published, though historians remain uncertain about their exact delivery. His efforts show a Qing official attempting to apply existing Qing categories (regulation, moral instruction, bond, penalty) to a problem that had outgrown them.

Lin also engaged with the world beyond the factories. He assembled translators and had foreign periodicals and geographic and legal works rendered into Chinese, including portions of an international-law text and a European geography. This intelligence-gathering later fed into Wei Yuan's major work. Students should therefore avoid the older image of Qing officials as uninterested in foreign knowledge. The problem was not curiosity but the speed and scale at which Qing institutions could absorb information and convert it into policy.

Section 52.2

Humen, the Destruction of Opium, and the Breakdown at Canton

On 24 March 1839, Charles Elliot, the British Chief Superintendent of Trade, entered the factory area and, facing the confinement of British subjects, ordered all British-owned opium to be surrendered to him for delivery to the Qing. He did so by pledging the British government's responsibility to compensate the owners. This was a crucial move. It transformed what had been private merchants' contraband into property surrendered under the authority of the British Crown, and it laid the foundation for a later British claim that the Qing owed compensation for the loss. Over the following weeks, more than 20,000 chests (each weighing roughly 60 kilograms) were handed over, from British and some other foreign merchants.

Lin chose to destroy the opium publicly and in a way that would be seen as lawful, not merely profitable. At Humen (虎门), in June 1839, laborers dissolved the drug in trenches mixed with lime and salt water and then flushed it out to sea over a period of about three weeks. Lin reportedly composed a text addressed to the sea god apologizing for the pollution. Chinese and foreign observers, including some British witnesses who were present, noted the orderly character of the destruction. Qing officials did not seize the drug for resale or for official use, which would have confirmed suspicions about official profiteering. The act was both a demonstration of principle and a message to the Qing's own officialdom.

The destruction did not end the crisis. Lin demanded that foreign merchants sign a bond binding themselves to Chinese law and the death penalty for future opium smuggling. Elliot refused, partly because the penalty and the procedures of Qing justice, as interpreted by the British, were unacceptable, and ordered the British community to leave Guangzhou. Over the summer, British residents withdrew to Macao and then to ships off Hong Kong. A violent incident in July 1839, in which British and American sailors killed a Chinese villager named Lin Weixi in Kowloon, deepened the dispute. Lin demanded the surrender of the perpetrator for trial under Qing law; Elliot refused and instead held a trial aboard a British ship under British law, imposing penalties on a handful of sailors. The Qing authorities regarded this as evading territorial jurisdiction. The British regarded handing a subject to a legal process they viewed as arbitrary as unacceptable.

The jurisdiction dispute was not new. It echoed earlier cases, such as the 1784 Lady Hughes affair in which a British gunner's accidental killing of Chinese bystanders led the Qing to demand the gunner and, after pressure, to execute him (Chapter 51). Qing officials believed that crimes committed on Qing soil, against Qing subjects, should be tried under Qing law. British merchants and officials increasingly argued that Qing law was harsh, procedurally opaque, and prone to collective responsibility. Each side regarded its own principle as obvious and the other's as unreasonable.

Through the autumn of 1839, the Guangzhou trade arrangements disintegrated. Lin forbade provisioning of British ships, and the British responded in September by blockading and attacking Qing junks at Kowloon. In November, naval clashes at Chuanbi occurred between Qing war junks and British warships. Some foreign merchants, including some Americans and a few British ships whose captains signed the bond, continued legitimate trade. The British community as a whole, however, looked to its government. Elliot dispatched reports to London, and British merchants, especially those linked to Jardine, Matheson, lobbied in London for a forceful response.

It is important not to blur two issues. The Qing seizure and destruction of privately owned opium was a legal act under Qing law against goods that were contraband in Qing territory. The later British claim that the Qing owed compensation was a political claim by a foreign government on behalf of its subjects, based on the argument that British subjects had been coerced into surrender by confinement and threats. Historians still argue about the legitimacy and weight of that claim, and the wider question of whether a government may defend the commerce of its citizens when that commerce is illegal in the territory where it operates.

Multiple Perspectives

Multiple Perspectives: The Opium Crisis of 1839

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns that historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor, and they do not carry equal weight of power or equal ethical standing.

Lin Zexu and Qing suppression officials: Opium was a contraband commodity whose consumption damaged health, drained silver, and undermined official integrity. The destruction at Humen was a lawful act, carefully conducted, and the demand for bonds was a reasonable attempt to prevent recurrence. Lin also understood that suppression had to be credible to Chinese officials who had tolerated the trade.
British opium merchants and traders: The merchants' wealth depended on a trade that had operated for years with the knowledge of local officials. They regarded the confinement and seizure as arbitrary and coercive, and sought compensation and guarantees of safe trade. Some lobbied London for military force; others quietly withdrew from opium. They were not unanimous, and some Protestant missionaries and British critics condemned the trade.
Lord Palmerston and the British government: The Foreign Secretary treated the confinement of the Superintendent and British subjects as an affront to the Crown, and the demand for compensation as a matter of state responsibility. His framing emphasized diplomatic insult, jurisdiction, and commercial security more than the drug itself, an emphasis that critics in Britain saw as evasion of the moral question.
A Qing coastal official in Guangdong: Local officials faced a delicate position: some had benefited from the opium economy, others feared the consequences of confrontation with an unfamiliar naval power. Many hedged, complying publicly with the imperial commissioner while worrying about the practical consequences for revenue, coastal order, and their careers.
Chinese merchants and Hong merchants: Licensed Hong merchants were liable for foreign merchants' conduct and for debts. They had profited from the trade, but they bore the risk of punishment if the system broke down. Other Chinese merchants, boatmen, and dealers who supplied or distributed opium faced arrest and ruin, while others in the legal tea and silk trades found their livelihoods threatened by the disruption.
Indian opium cultivators and Indian merchants: Peasants in the Ganges plain grew poppy under a system of government monopoly and advance payments that gave them little bargaining power. Merchants in western India, including Parsi traders, handled Malwa opium and shipping. Indian soldiers, who would soon serve in British expeditions, had little say in either the commerce or the war. The empire's opium revenue was a significant part of British-Indian finance, tying the China trade to the governance of India.
Section 52.3

From Commercial Crisis to War

Why did Britain choose war? The question is more difficult than it appears, because opium was both central and insufficient as an explanation. Opium financed the tea purchases on which British consumption and British government revenue depended, and the Indian government drew a substantial share of its income from the drug. Prominent merchants, such as William Jardine, who traveled to London, pressed the Foreign Secretary, Lord Palmerston, for a decisive response. Yet Palmerston's instructions to the naval and diplomatic officers sent to China did not emphasize opium legalization. They stressed compensation for the destroyed opium, redress for the insult to British officials, guarantees for the security of British subjects and their trade, the cession of an island or islands for a secure base, and diplomatic communication on terms of equality.

The British position drew on several distinct strands. The first was commercial: merchants wanted wider access, lower and more predictable charges, and the end of the monopoly system. The second was legal: British officials objected to Qing jurisdiction over British subjects and to collective responsibility, and insisted on protection for British lives and property. The third was diplomatic: the Qing court's refusal to treat the British Crown as an equal, evident in the Napier affair and in earlier missions by Macartney (1793) and Amherst (1816), was regarded as an affront. The fourth was imperial and strategic: the Government of India, under Governor-General Lord Auckland, had a financial stake in opium, and India provided the troops and ships for the expedition. Finally, there was the ideology of free trade, held by many British politicians and merchants, which treated Qing restrictions as illegitimate barriers to be removed by pressure if necessary.

British domestic opinion was divided. In Parliament in April 1840, the Whig government narrowly survived a motion criticizing its China policy. Critics, including William Gladstone, condemned the war as unjust in its origin and a source of national disgrace because it defended a trade in a harmful drug. Defenders, including the Secretary at War, Thomas Babington Macaulay, argued that Britain was responding to the insult and imprisonment of its representatives and to the failure of the Qing to maintain a regime of law. Some missionaries and members of the press supported the war in the hope that it would open China to the gospel and to commerce, while others deplored it. The debate shows that the war was never a simple consensus of British interests.

Students should avoid two oversimplifications. One is to describe the war as merely "Britain wanted to sell drugs." Opium was central to the origin of the crisis and the commerce that Britain defended, and it would be inaccurate to sanitize the fact that the British government protected a trade largely based on drugs illegal under Chinese law. But British war aims, as articulated by the government, also included diplomatic, legal, and commercial objectives that went beyond opium and persisted after opium was no longer the immediate issue. The other oversimplification is to treat the Qing side as simply upholding morality against greed. Qing policy involved fiscal and political motivations, the interests of different officials and merchants, and the limits of the court's own information.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: What Principally Caused the First Opium War?

The question: Historians agree that opium, silver, jurisdiction, diplomatic status, and commercial access were all involved in the outbreak of war in 1839–1840. They disagree about which causes were primary and how they relate: was the war essentially a conflict over the drug trade, the product of structural tension between British commercial power and the Canton System, a failure rooted in Qing domestic politics, or a failure of Qing military, institutional, and informational capacity?

Interpretation A — Opium as the root cause: Some accounts treat the British import of opium into China as the root of the conflict. Peter Ward Fay's The Opium War, 1840–1842 (1975), a detailed narrative of the war, has been read in this way: without the opium trade there would have been no confrontation in 1839, and British commercial and diplomatic grievances became war aims because of the crisis the drug had produced. Chinese historiography has often emphasized the same centrality.
Interpretation B — Multiple, interlocking causes: Julia Lovell's The Opium War (2011) is explicitly multicausal. It stresses diplomatic inequality, the restrictions of the Canton System, British attitudes toward China, and opium's role in financing the tea purchases on which British trade depended, and it gives all of these weight together. Scholars who use the idea of the "imperialism of free trade" similarly emphasize British commercial and strategic expansion alongside the drug trade.
Interpretation C — Qing domestic politics and policy-making: James Polachek's The Inner Opium War (1992) examines the literati and official networks, factional debates, and moral-political concerns that shaped Qing foreign-policy decisions, arguing that the Qing side cannot be understood as a simple reaction to British pressure.
Interpretation D — Qing military, institutional, and informational limits: Mao Haijian's study of the Qing empire and the war emphasizes court decision-making, military and administrative structures, the quality of information reaching Beijing, and the worldview in which Qing officials interpreted the British, as explanations of how the crisis became a defeat.

Evidence: The interpretations draw on overlapping sources: Palmerston's instructions and parliamentary papers, merchants' correspondence, Lin Zexu's memorials, imperial edicts, and records of court debate. They differ in emphasis and in how they weigh stated war aims against underlying motives. Note that these interpretations are not always mutually exclusive: A and B concern the origins of the conflict, while C and D concern Qing decisions and outcomes.

Why it matters: The question affects how later history is told. If opium was the principal cause, the war is chiefly a conflict over a harmful trade. If causation was multiple, the treaty system appears as the product of several interacting pressures. If Qing politics and capacity are central, then Qing agency and decision-making are central to the explanation.

Student task: Which interpretation, or combination, do you find best supported by the evidence discussed in this chapter, and what additional documents would help you test it? Which of the four address origins, and which address outcomes?

Section 52.4

The First Opium War Begins, 1840

In June 1840 a British expeditionary force arrived off the Pearl River, composed of warships, armed steamers, troop transports, and several thousand soldiers, many of them recruited in British India. Its commanders were Rear-Admiral George Elliot and the Superintendent, Charles Elliot, who were jointly empowered as plenipotentiaries. The force declared a blockade of Guangzhou but did not attack the city. Instead, it moved north along the coast, a decision that reflected a strategic view: Guangzhou was well fortified and a prolonged fight there would be difficult, whereas pressure on more vulnerable coastal points, and ultimately on Beijing, would force the court to negotiate.

The British captured Dinghai, on the island of Zhoushan off Zhejiang, on 5 July 1840 after a naval bombardment. Zhoushan controlled access to the Yangtze delta and Hangzhou Bay and could serve as a base. The island's population was caught between the invading force and the Qing garrison. The occupation also exposed British troops to disease: dysentery and fever killed many soldiers, a pattern that would recur, so that disease would eventually cause more British deaths than combat. The expedition then proceeded to the Gulf of Bohai and anchored off the Dagu forts near Tianjin in August, close to the capital.

The effect on the court was immediate. Palmerston's letter to the emperor's ministers was received, and the Daoguang emperor, alarmed by the proximity of foreign ships to Beijing, shifted toward negotiation. He blamed Lin Zexu and the Guangdong officials for provoking the crisis and appointed a new negotiator, the Manchu grand secretary Qishan. By September, Lin had been dismissed, and negotiations moved south to Guangzhou. This shift illustrates a central dynamic: British naval mobility allowed pressure at points the Qing had not prepared to defend, and the Qing political system reacted by seeking someone to blame and by trying to relocate the negotiation to a place where it could be controlled.

The technological asymmetry deserves care. The Royal Navy possessed steam-powered shallow-draft vessels such as the East India Company's iron steamer Nemesis, which could navigate rivers and shallow coastal waters against wind and tide, tow sailing vessels, and bring heavy guns to bear on forts from unexpected directions. British forces increasingly employed percussion arms while flintlock muskets remained common; their greater advantages lay in naval artillery, steam mobility, logistics, training, and command. Many Qing soldiers used matchlocks and jingals. But the contrast should not be turned into a story of a "modern" West and a "backward" China. Qing forts mounted many cannon, some of recent manufacture, and Lin had purchased foreign guns and ordered new fortifications. The decisive disadvantage was not simply the quality of weapons but the combination of naval mobility, logistics, unified command, and information.

That combination exposed structural limits in Qing defense. Qing military forces were dispersed: Banner garrisons, Green Standard troops, and local militias were stationed according to a strategy designed chiefly for internal security and Inner Asian campaigns rather than for a war against a naval power operating along thousands of kilometers of coast. Provinces paid for their own defense, and coordination among provincial governors depended on slow memorial communication to Beijing. The Qing did not lack courage or capable commanders; it lacked a coastal defense system that could concentrate force quickly at the points the British chose.

Qing leaders also had incomplete information about their adversary. Early reports sometimes minimized British strength or misunderstood British organization, and officials sometimes reported victories that had not occurred, which Lin and others later struggled to correct. The court's changing instructions, from firmness to conciliation and back again, were responses to unreliable reports as much as to shifting principle. This is a good illustration of how political systems that depend on written memorials and hierarchical reporting can struggle in a rapidly evolving crisis.

Section 52.5

Qing Defense and the War in Guangdong, 1841

Qishan negotiated with Elliot at Guangzhou in late 1840 and produced what is known as the Chuanbi Convention in January 1841. Its terms, never formally ratified, included the cession of Hong Kong, a payment, direct official correspondence between the two sides, and the resumption of trade at Guangzhou. Elliot occupied Hong Kong on 26 January 1841 and proclaimed it British. But both governments repudiated the draft. The Daoguang emperor, enraged by the territorial concession, dismissed and punished Qishan and ordered renewed war. Palmerston, for his part, judged that Elliot had accepted too little and in the following months replaced him with Sir Henry Pottinger. Both capitals, it seems, were dissatisfied with the men on the spot.

Fighting resumed in Guangdong in February 1841. British forces stormed forts at the Humen narrows, including the Anunghoy and Wangtong forts, and killed or captured defenders. Admiral Guan Tianpei, a respected Qing naval commander, died defending the Humen forts. British steamers, notably Nemesis, then moved upriver, destroyed war junks, and advanced toward Guangzhou. In May, a new Qing commander, Yishan, launched a counterattack that failed. The British occupied heights above the city, and the Qing agreed to a ransom of roughly six million silver dollars to spare Guangzhou from attack.

What followed shows that the Qing response was not purely military. Around Guangzhou, villagers and gentry organized local militias. At Sanyuanli, north of the city, in late May 1841, thousands of villagers, drawing on lineage and village networks, converged on a small British force in a confused and stormy engagement, and the British withdrew. Later Chinese accounts treated Sanyuanli as an emblem of popular resistance, and historians have since explored its social background: it reflected local gentry leadership, community defense traditions, resentment of looting and of the foreign presence, and a degree of distrust between local elites and the Qing government. It also shows that Qing authority was not the only locus of resistance, and that gentry-led mobilization could act independently of, and sometimes in tension with, official command.

Qing defenders also encountered a problem that would recur in later wars: local resistance could be effective at specific sites, but the empire's response depended on armies that were not designed for coastal war. Commanders sometimes used traditional defenses such as sunken barriers, fire rafts, and stationary batteries, which were vulnerable to flanking by steam vessels. Funding was scarce and the Qing treasury, already strained, had to find resources for defense while continuing to administer vast territories. Meanwhile, Qing officials such as Lin Zexu, who spent 1841 working on Yellow River dike repair at Kaifeng before being sent into exile at Ili in the far northwest, were reassigned or punished in ways that disrupted continuity of command.

The Guangdong campaign of 1841 illustrates the paradox of the war. British forces repeatedly won engagements at forts, ships, and positions, yet they could not conquer a large, populous province or compel loyalty. Qing authorities negotiated a ransom for Guangzhou but did not accept British dominance. Each side believed that continued pressure, or patience, would improve its position. That belief helps explain why the war continued after the first months, even as Qing casualties and British disease mounted.

Section 52.6

The Coastal and Yangtze Campaigns, 1841–1842

In August 1841, Pottinger and the military commander, Sir Hugh Gough, led a larger force north. They captured Xiamen (Amoy) on 26 August, retook Dinghai on Zhoushan in October after fierce resistance in which the Qing commander Ge Yunfei and other officers died, and took Zhenhai and Ningbo in October. Ningbo, a major commercial city, was occupied through the winter. Qing authorities regrouped, and in March 1842 a Qing counteroffensive intended to recapture Ningbo and Zhenhai failed. The Qing had mobilized troops from multiple provinces but could not coordinate them effectively against British firepower and ship-borne support.

In the spring of 1842, the British adopted a still more ambitious strategy: moving into the Yangtze, the economic artery of the empire. They evacuated Ningbo in May and captured Zhapu, then, in June, took the fortifications at Wusong at the mouth of the Huangpu River, where the Qing admiral Chen Huacheng was killed on 16 June. Shanghai fell three days later, on 19 June with little fighting. Then the fleet entered the Yangtze, moved upriver, and captured Zhenjiang on 21 July. Zhenjiang was strategically critical: it sat at the junction of the Yangtze and the Grand Canal, a node for grain, salt, and merchant traffic. The Manchu banner garrison fought with great determination, and its commander, Hailing, died as the city fell; accounts describe heavy civilian suffering, including suicides of families before the capture.

Why did control of waterways matter so much? The Yangtze and the Grand Canal linked the productive Jiangnan region, with its rice, silk, cotton, and salt, to the capital and northern frontier. The empire's tax-grain system and elite consumption relied on them. A foreign fleet at Zhenjiang threatened to interrupt that traffic and, with it, the revenues and food supply of Beijing. The British did not need to conquer the interior. They needed to demonstrate that they could paralyze the arteries of the Qing economy and approach Nanjing, a former capital. Faced with this threat, the Daoguang court, which had earlier swung between negotiation and resistance, empowered Qiying and Yilibu to negotiate and ordered Niu Jian, the governor-general of Liangjiang, to assist.

The war also had human costs that are difficult to measure. Qing military deaths are not reliably counted, and civilian deaths from bombardment, fire, and the destruction of towns were significant at specific places. British casualties in combat were relatively low, while disease killed many more soldiers. Looting and the occupation of towns damaged local economies, and refugees fled from coastal communities. Hong merchants and local gentry sometimes organized supplies for the British or the Qing as circumstances demanded, and Qing officials accused some Chinese of treason (hanjian, "traitors to the Han"), a label that expressed both genuine collaboration and political blame-shifting.

By August 1842 the British fleet lay off Nanjing. The Qing court, which had lost multiple coastal cities, faced the possibility of an attack on the former capital and the loss of the Yangtze commercial system. It sent negotiators, and the stage was set for the treaty. It is important to note that the court did not surrender because Qing society had collapsed. It chose to settle because continued war threatened the economic and strategic core of the empire and because it hoped the treaty would restore stability. That hope, as the next sections show, was only partly realized.

Section 52.7

The Treaty of Nanjing, 1842

The Treaty of Nanjing was signed on 29 August 1842 aboard the British warship HMS Cornwallis, anchored off the city. The principal negotiators were Sir Henry Pottinger for Britain and the imperial commissioners Qiying, Yilibu, and Niu Jian for the Qing, with translation by British interpreters such as John Robert Morrison and Robert Thom. The text existed in English and Chinese. Ratifications were exchanged in 1843. It is best read clause by clause, with attention to what each provision actually did.

Peace and the ports. The first article declared peace and friendship. Article II opened five ports, Guangzhou, Xiamen (Amoy), Fuzhou, Ningbo, and Shanghai, to British residence and trade, and provided for British consular officers at each. This was the heart of the commercial settlement: it ended the restriction of European trade to Guangzhou. Hong Kong. Article III ceded the island of Hong Kong to the British Crown, to be held in perpetuity. At the time it had a modest population of fishing, boat-dwelling, and quarrying communities, but its sheltered harbor made it valuable as an entrepôt and naval base.

Indemnities. Articles IV, V, and VI imposed payments totaling 21 million silver dollars: six million for the opium surrendered in 1839, three million for debts owed by Hong merchants to British creditors, and twelve million for the costs of the war. By ordinary conversion of the period, this was roughly fifteen million taels, a heavy burden but not by itself ruinous for the Qing fiscal system. Payment was spread over several years, with British occupation of Zhoushan and Gulangyu until completion. The Cohong. Article V also abolished the monopoly of the Hong merchants, allowing British merchants to deal with any Chinese merchants they chose. Prisoners, amnesty, and tariff. Further articles provided for release of prisoners, an amnesty for Chinese who had served the British, and a "fair and regular tariff" to be settled by agreement. A final provision established that correspondence between officials of equal rank would be conducted on a basis of equality.

Equally important is what the treaty did not say. It did not mention opium. It did not legalize the drug, though the trade continued and expanded. It did not establish extraterritorial jurisdiction, most-favored-nation treatment, or detailed rules for foreign residence, and it did not give foreigners the right to travel in the interior or to maintain resident ministers in Beijing. It did not mention missionary activity. Those developments came through supplementary agreements and later treaties (Section 52.8 onward). A student who assumes that the Treaty of Nanjing contained the whole treaty system will misunderstand how that system developed.

The Qing court's own view of the settlement is instructive. Daoguang accepted the terms reluctantly, and Qiying, who became the leading Qing negotiator in the following years, practiced a policy of conciliation, sometimes described by the term jimi (羁縻), a long-used idea of managing outside groups by loose reins, concessions, and cultivation of personal relationships. Qiying hoped that treating the British with accommodation would limit further demands and avoid renewed war. Others at court regarded this as dangerous weakness, and the divide between conciliators and hardliners would persist for two decades.

Historical Vocabulary
Unequal Treaties (不平等条约)

The phrase "unequal treaties" came into wide use in the early twentieth century, especially in the 1920s, when Chinese nationalists and the Nationalist government campaigned to abolish treaty privileges. It is a useful historical term, but students should learn what made the treaties unequal rather than treat the label as an explanation. The main features were: coercion (they were concluded under military threat or after defeat); one-sided rights (Britain and other treaty powers gained privileges without granting equivalent rights to the Qing in their territories); limits on sovereignty (tariffs fixed by treaty, consular jurisdiction over foreigners, and foreign residence in designated places); territorial cessions (Hong Kong Island, southern Kowloon); and the lack of unilateral exit (the Qing could not simply withdraw from them).

The term should not be used as though earlier Qing treaties, such as those with Russia, were also coerced in the same way, or as though the Qing had no capacity to negotiate. Contemporary British officials regarded the treaties as a legitimate settlement and as opening China to commerce and law. Understanding the debate over the term is part of understanding the historiography.

Section 52.8

Supplementary Treaties and the Expansion of Treaty Rights, 1843–1844

The Treaty of Nanjing was less a complete system than a framework that required further agreement. In 1843, Qiying and Pottinger negotiated the General Regulations of Trade and a Supplementary Treaty, known as the Treaty of the Bogue (October 1843). The regulations established a tariff schedule that set duties at about five percent ad valorem on most goods, in effect a "conventional tariff" that could be altered only by agreement. The Supplementary Treaty added provisions on residence, trade conduct, and, crucially, jurisdiction and equal treatment. It provided that British subjects accused of crimes against Chinese would be tried by British consuls under British law, and it contained a clause granting Britain any privileges the Qing might extend to other nations. Those two provisions would expand dramatically.

The Americans moved next. The United States sent Caleb Cushing, who negotiated the Treaty of Wangxia (July 1844) with Qiying. The American treaty extended consular jurisdiction to include more serious criminal and civil cases involving US citizens, provided for revision after twelve years, and addressed opium differently: American merchants who traded in contraband would not receive US government protection from Chinese authorities. In practice, many American firms continued to carry opium, and the clause was not rigorously enforced. The French followed with the Treaty of Whampoa in October 1844, negotiated by Théodore de Lagrené. Alongside commercial provisions, the French negotiations produced Qing edicts easing restrictions on Chinese Catholics and permitting Christianity to be practiced in the open ports, which were extended in 1846. Belgium, Sweden-Norway, and others signed treaties in subsequent years.

The concept that tied these agreements together was most-favored-nation (MFN) treatment. When the Qing granted a privilege to one treaty power, others who held MFN clauses could claim it. The effect was a ratchet: a concession granted in a negotiation with one power spread automatically to others, so that the network of treaties became more than the sum of individual agreements. This was not a Qing design. Qing negotiators seem to have regarded some of these provisions as ways of simplifying relations and avoiding disputes, and in Qiying's case perhaps of limiting British demands by granting equal treatment to all. But the combination of multiple powers, each with its own diplomatic interests and an MFN clause, meant that Qing officials negotiated against a background in which any gain for one might be claimed by all.

The supplementary treaties also reveal how treaty provisions evolved in practice. The Qing established a new office, the Commissioner for the Five Ports (Wukou tongshang dachen), usually held by the Liangguang governor-general, to manage foreign affairs outside the old Guangzhou structure. Consular officials were appointed, the first British consul arrived in Shanghai in 1843, and consuls became key points of contact between Qing local officials and foreign communities. The Hong Kong colonial government formally came into existence in 1843 under Pottinger as its first governor. By 1844 the outline of the treaty system was visible, though many practical questions about residence, jurisdiction, and tariff enforcement were still unresolved.

Cause and Effect: From Commercial Crisis to War to Treaty System

Causes (long-term)

Growth of the opium trade and silver outflow; the Canton System's restrictions on trade and diplomacy; the end of the East India Company monopoly (1834); disputes over jurisdiction and communication; Indian revenue interests; British commitment to expanded trade; Qing fiscal and administrative strain.

Causes (short-term)

Court decision for suppression (1838); Lin Zexu's confinement of the foreign factories and demand for opium; Elliot's pledge of compensation; destruction at Humen; Kowloon killing; failure to agree on bonds; Palmerston's decision to send a force.

The transformation

War across the coast and Yangtze, ending in the Treaty of Nanjing, which was then elaborated through the Bogue, Wangxia, and Whampoa agreements.

Effects

Five open ports; Hong Kong; indemnities; abolition of the Cohong; consular jurisdiction; MFN; conventional tariff; new Qing offices; local resistance; continued opium trade; unresolved disputes leading to renewed conflict in the 1850s.

The system that emerged reflects negotiation among multiple powers, Qing officials' attempts at conciliation, and the incentives created by MFN, not a single British blueprint.

Section 52.9

The Treaty Ports and Foreign Residence

The five ports opened by the Treaty of Nanjing differed greatly in their commercial significance and in how quickly foreign communities took root. Guangzhou had long been the center of foreign trade, but its population's hostility to foreign entry into the walled city, discussed in Section 52.13, limited the new freedoms. Xiamen was an old maritime commercial center with strong ties to Taiwan and Southeast Asia. Fuzhou, a provincial capital, became important for tea exports in the 1850s. Ningbo had a long history of commerce and of bankers and merchants who later played prominent roles in Shanghai. And Shanghai, a modest but strategically located county seat at the mouth of the Yangtze, became the most consequential of all.

A British consul, George Balfour, arrived at Shanghai in November 1843, and the port formally opened. The Qing circuit intendant (daotai) negotiated with him over where foreigners could live. In 1845, through the Land Regulations, an area north of the walled city along the Huangpu River was designated for British residence. Foreign merchants leased land from Chinese owners, usually through the Qing official, and built warehouses, offices, and homes. This was not a transfer of sovereignty. Qing officials remained responsible for governance of the surrounding territory, and the area was initially a place where foreigners were permitted to live and trade, not a foreign colony. But as other powers (the French in 1849, the Americans in the 1850s) established their own zones, and as foreign communities developed their own councils to manage roads, policing, and sanitation, the character of these enclaves evolved.

Consuls held an especially important position. Under the treaties, a consul represented a state's nationals, mediated disputes, and exercised jurisdiction over their legal affairs (Section 52.10). Consuls communicated with local Qing officials, and their ability to appeal to naval force when disputes occurred gave their words weight. Foreign merchants established firms, shipping agencies, insurance arrangements, and warehouses. Chinese merchants, many from Guangdong and Fujian, and Ningbo bankers took up the new opportunities, acting as intermediaries, suppliers, and partners. Chinese compradors (maiban, 买办) managed foreign firms' local dealings, handled cash and credit, and recruited staff. Compradors were intermediaries rather than subordinates in a simple sense: some became wealthy, powerful, and influential within the commercial community.

Hong Kong occupied a different category. It was a crown colony of Britain, with a British governor and British law, whereas the treaty ports remained under Qing sovereignty with foreign privileges. Hong Kong became a major entrepôt, a base for British naval and commercial interests, and a center of the opium trade, which moved from the old Lintin anchorage to other offshore locations, including near Shanghai at Wusong. Its population grew with migrants from Guangdong, who found work as laborers, traders, and artisans. The distinction between a colony and a treaty port is central to the chapter and is illustrated by the following vocabulary feature.

Historical Vocabulary
Treaty Port, Concession, Colony, and Leased Territory

These terms are often confused, but they describe different legal and political arrangements. A treaty port (通商口岸, tongshang kou'an) was a Chinese city opened by treaty to foreign trade and residence; Qing sovereignty continued, though foreign nationals enjoyed special privileges. A concession or settlement was a designated area within or beside a treaty port where foreigners leased land and, over time, often organized their own municipal administration; the legal form varied from port to port and changed over time. A colony was territory ceded to a foreign power and governed by it, as with Hong Kong Island (1842) and southern Kowloon with Stonecutters Island (1860). A leased territory was land granted to a foreign power for a fixed term, a development of the 1890s (Chapter 55).

Extraterritoriality, discussed next, is a different category again: it concerns the legal status of individual foreigners, not the status of territory. The Qing never ceded the treaty ports as territory, and treating them as colonies obscures the continued role of Qing officials, Chinese merchants, and local society.

Section 52.10

Extraterritoriality and Consular Jurisdiction

One of the most consequential features of the treaty system was extraterritoriality, the principle that foreigners of treaty powers were subject to the jurisdiction of their own consuls rather than Qing courts. Its origins lay in the jurisdiction disputes of the Canton era. In the Lady Hughes affair of 1784, the Qing demanded and ultimately obtained the execution of a British gunner after a fatal accident. In the 1839 Lin Weixi case, the Qing demanded surrender of the sailors responsible, and British authorities refused and tried them aboard ship. For the Qing, these were matters of territorial jurisdiction: crimes committed on Qing soil should be tried under Qing law. For British officials, the Qing legal process, which could involve harsh penalties, collective responsibility, and limited procedural protections as they understood it, was unacceptable for their subjects.

After 1842 the answer to this dispute was written into treaty law. The Supplementary Treaty of the Bogue provided for British consular jurisdiction in criminal matters involving British subjects, and the Wangxia treaty elaborated similar rules for Americans, including arrangements for disputes between Chinese and foreigners that were to be settled through the consul and local officials. The Bogue settlement and the associated General Regulations laid the foundations of British consular jurisdiction, and Britain supplied the domestic legal machinery through the Foreign Jurisdiction Act of 1843 and an Order in Council of 17 April 1844 empowering consuls and vice-consuls in China. The Wangxia treaty stated American extraterritoriality particularly clearly, and Britain's 1858 Tianjin treaty provided directly that British subjects committing crimes in China were to be tried by British officials under British law. Over the next decades, the system expanded in practice. Foreign residents who committed offenses against Chinese were tried in their own consular courts, and civil disputes involving foreign defendants were largely handled outside Qing courts. Qing officials could request the consul's assistance but could not compel it.

This was a major qualification of Qing jurisdiction, but it should be understood carefully. The Qing legal order was already pluralistic in several respects: Banner personnel had separate administrative and legal arrangements, and in frontier regions Mongols, Tibetans, and Muslim communities in Xinjiang were governed under distinct institutions (Chapter 48). The Qing thus had experience governing different groups through different rules and some officials viewed extraterritoriality as a practical way to avoid responsibility for the quarrels of foreigners. What made the treaty arrangement different was that it removed foreigners from Qing courts under the authority of foreign states, could not be revoked unilaterally, and applied in a context of military defeat. Chinese complainants and Qing officials frequently criticized the system as biased, because defendants were judged under the institutions of their own state, often by consuls who were themselves merchants or diplomats.

Extraterritoriality had consequences for Chinese society. Chinese subjects who had grievances against foreigners had to seek justice in foreign-run consular courts, which were slow, distant, and not always responsive. Foreign merchants could rely on their consuls to protect their business interests against local taxation or regulation. And the system created legal ambiguity at the boundaries of the treaty ports: foreign merchants who employed Chinese partners, or Chinese who sought foreign registration or protection, blurred the lines. Over time, mixed courts (such as the later Shanghai Mixed Court) and other arrangements developed, and extraterritoriality became a focus of Chinese reformers and nationalists in the twentieth century (Chapters 56 and 60 onward).

Historical Vocabulary
Extraterritoriality (治外法权, zhiwai faquan)

Extraterritoriality, or consular jurisdiction, was the legal principle that citizens of a treaty power living in China were subject to their own country's laws, enforced by their consul, rather than to Qing law and courts. It protected individuals, not territory, and it applied to foreigners from treaty powers, not to all outsiders. In Qing practice, the term describes a package of rights that developed through the 1843–1844 treaties and later agreements.

The concept was not unique to China. Earlier arrangements, such as the Ottoman capitulations, gave European merchants similar privileges, and the Qing had its own traditions of differentiated jurisdiction. What distinguished the Chinese case was its association with military defeat and its long persistence into the twentieth century.

Section 52.11

Tariffs, Most-Favored-Nation Rights, and the New Commercial Order

The treaty system was an institutional structure, not merely five open ports and Hong Kong. A central element was the tariff. Before 1842, the Qing had regulated foreign trade through a combination of formal duties, informal fees, and the monopoly role of the Hong merchants. The Treaty of Nanjing called for a "fair and regular tariff," and the 1843 General Regulations of Trade fixed duties at about five percent on imports and exports according to a schedule of goods. In itself, a low and predictable tariff might have been welcomed by merchants. The significance lay in the principle that the tariff was set by treaty and could not be changed by the Qing alone. This limited the Qing's ability to use customs duties as an instrument of revenue or protection, and made adjustment dependent on negotiation with each treaty power.

The MFN provisions magnified this effect. If the Qing agreed to change a duty or rule for one power, others with MFN rights could claim the same, but if a power had refused to agree, the Qing could not impose the change on it. The structure favored stability in terms of foreign merchants' expectations, and it also made Qing economic policy less flexible. Foreign merchants also complained of inland taxes imposed by Qing authorities on goods moving through the interior. When the Taiping war devastated the Qing treasury, provincial officials introduced the lijin (厘金) levy in 1853, a transit tax collected at inland barriers. Foreign merchants objected that this violated treaty tariff expectations, a dispute that would shape treaty revision in 1858.

The customs administration itself changed in this period. In 1853, the Small Swords Society, a secret-society rebel group, seized the Chinese walled city of Shanghai, driving out the Qing authorities, including customs officials. Foreign consuls, concerned about unpaid duties and bonds, arranged to collect customs revenue themselves. In 1854 this led to an arrangement in which foreign inspectors took on duties in the Shanghai customs, which in time developed into the Chinese Imperial Maritime Customs Service. At first it was an improvised response to a crisis; later it became a lasting institution, expanded under Horatio Nelson Lay in 1859 and, from 1863, under Robert Hart. Its growth is a good example of how the treaty system was built in stages from practical problems, and of how Qing officials cooperated in arrangements that gave foreigners an administrative role in Chinese finance.

Opium remained the great unresolved commodity. After 1842, despite its formal illegality, opium imports grew substantially, and Hong Kong, Shanghai, and other coastal points became centers of distribution. Qing officials found it politically impossible to legalize the trade in the 1840s, yet unable to suppress it. The question was finally addressed in the tariff arrangements negotiated at Shanghai in November 1858 in the aftermath of the Treaties of Tianjin, which placed opium on the schedule of dutiable imports. This did not end debate. Many Chinese officials and foreign critics regarded legalization as a defeat, while others saw it as a pragmatic acceptance of reality that allowed taxation of a trade the state could not eliminate.

Historical Vocabulary
Most-Favored-Nation Treatment (最惠国待遇)

Most-favored-nation (MFN) treatment is a treaty clause promising that any privilege granted by one party to a third state will also be extended to the other party. In the Chinese treaties it meant that rights won by one foreign power automatically became available to all other treaty powers with MFN clauses. In an equal, reciprocal treaty this can promote fair treatment. In the Chinese treaties it worked in one direction: the Qing did not obtain comparable privileges in treaty partners' territories, and the clause operated as a ratchet that spread concessions from one agreement to all.

Using MFN as a label without explaining its mechanism is a common error. Students should be able to explain, with an example, how a provision won by one power in a negotiation could become universal.

Section 52.12

Treaty-Port Society and the Rise of Shanghai

The social and economic consequences of the post-1842 order were visible first in the ports. Shanghai's rise was dramatic but should be understood in context. The city sat at the mouth of the Yangtze, on the Huangpu River, with access to the productive hinterland of Jiangnan, its silk, cotton, tea shipped from the interior, and a dense network of canals and market towns. Even before 1842, Shanghai was a busy coastal and river port, with a large junk trade, native-place associations (huiguan and gongsuo) representing merchants from Guangdong, Fujian, Ningbo, and elsewhere, and native banks (qianzhuang) that financed trade. The treaty opened it to deep-sea foreign shipping and foreign firms and gave it a new set of institutions without erasing the existing commercial world.

By the mid-1850s, Shanghai had overtaken Guangzhou as China's leading foreign-trade port. Several forces contributed. Shanghai was closer to the silk and tea districts that supplied exports. The Taiping Rebellion (from 1851), by disrupting inland routes to Guangzhou, redirected trade through the Yangtze and Shanghai. And British and other firms, drawn by the market, relocated. The foreign population remained small, only some hundreds in the early 1850s, but the commercial impact was large. Foreign firms built wharves and warehouses; steam shipping gradually supplemented sail; banks and insurance agencies appeared; and the treaty-port environment created a distinct commercial space with its own regulations and institutions, such as the foreign-run Municipal Council that developed in the settlements.

Chinese society in the port was far more numerous and varied than the foreign community. Merchants from Guangdong who had once worked at Guangzhou migrated to Shanghai. Ningbo bankers and shipowners built networks that financed trade. Compradors worked as intermediaries, and some accumulated large fortunes. Laborers, dockworkers, boatmen, rickshaw pullers (later), and domestic servants found employment, and artisans and shopkeepers served both Chinese and foreign customers. In 1853 and again in 1860, refugees from the Small Swords and Taiping conflicts poured into the foreign-administered areas, which had originally been intended for foreign residence only, and the settlements became places where large numbers of Chinese lived under conditions of relative security, a change that shaped the city's future.

Print and communication also changed. The London Missionary Society press, which had moved to Shanghai, published Chinese-language books on science, geography, and religion; English-language newspapers such as the North-China Herald (founded 1850) circulated foreign views; and Chinese readers encountered new information. The social effects were uneven. Some Chinese merchants prospered, but others, such as porters and boatmen on the old Guangzhou trade routes, lost work as trade shifted. Local gentry sometimes complained about foreign presence, rents, and the behavior of foreign sailors. The ports were places of exchange, opportunity, discrimination, and friction.

Students should not leap ahead to the late nineteenth-century Shanghai of factories and electric light. The purpose here is to see the immediate consequences of the post-1842 order: a shift in the geography of trade, the emergence of new commercial intermediaries, the beginnings of a distinctive treaty-port culture, and the layering of Qing, Chinese merchant, and foreign institutions. Later developments, including industry and the city's role in Republican culture, are discussed in Chapters 54, 63, and beyond.

Everyday Life and Social History

How the Wars and the Treaty Order Reached Ordinary People

Coastal and boat communities. Fishing families, boat-dwellers (such as the Tanka of the Pearl River delta), and stone-cutters in places like Hong Kong lived at the intersection of Qing, British, and local authority. Some benefited from provisioning and labor opportunities; others were displaced by fighting, blockade, and the establishment of new colonial and treaty institutions.

Towns under attack. At Dinghai, Zhenhai, Ningbo, Zhapu, Zhenjiang, and elsewhere, residents experienced bombardment, occupation, looting, and fear. Accounts describe families who killed themselves rather than face capture, and survivors who fled. These experiences were uneven: some towns capitulated with little fighting, while others endured sieges.

Opium consumers and dealers. Opium smoking cut across social groups, from officials and soldiers to merchants, artisans, and laborers. Addiction affected household economies, health, and labor, though the extent and social distribution of consumption in these years are difficult to quantify. Dealers and carriers faced shifting risks of arrest and extortion.

Merchants, porters, and workers. The shift of trade from Guangzhou toward Shanghai and the Yangtze altered the livelihoods of porters, boatmen, and shopkeepers. Some historians have argued that unemployment among workers along the old Guangzhou-interior trade routes contributed to social tensions in the south, though this interpretation is debated.

Women and families. Evidence about women's experiences is limited, but households bore the burdens of war, displacement, and economic change. In treaty ports, women worked as domestic servants, in textile work, and in markets, while wealthier merchant families adapted to new commercial opportunities. Foreign missionary women opened schools in the ports during these years, though their reach was small.

Local militias and gentry. Gentry-led village associations organized defense, as at Sanyuanli, and many continued to hold anti-foreign attitudes that shaped the Guangzhou city-entry controversy. For these communities, the wars were also a question of local order and the reach of the Qing state.

Section 52.13

Renewed Conflict in the 1840s and 1850s

The settlement of 1842–1844 did not produce stable relations. Both sides had expectations the treaties did not meet. British merchants had anticipated a vast new market, but sales of manufactured goods in China grew more slowly than hoped, and Chinese demand for British textiles was limited by competition with local cloth and by purchasing power. British officials also found enforcement difficult. Qing officials and local populations frequently resisted what they regarded as encroachments, and foreign diplomats found that treaty rights existed on paper more clearly than in daily practice. Meanwhile, the Qing court was divided between those who favored accommodation and those who considered the treaties humiliating expedients to be revised or reversed when circumstances allowed.

The Guangzhou city-entry controversy illustrated the tension. The treaties opened Guangzhou to British residence, and British officials interpreted this to include access to the walled city, which had been closed to foreigners. Guangzhou's gentry and population, shaped by the wartime experience of 1841 and by distrust, strongly opposed entry. In 1847, after incidents involving foreigners, a British force attacked forts along the river, and the governor-general Qiying agreed to open the city to entry in two years. When the time came in 1849, the new Qing governor-general, Xu Guangjin, supported by local militias and by popular sentiment, refused, and the British government, preoccupied elsewhere, backed down. The Daoguang emperor praised the Guangzhou officials. For the Qing, this was proof that firmness could succeed; for the British, it was a failure to enforce treaty rights. Both lessons shaped later events.

Treaty revision was another source of conflict. Britain and the United States argued that treaty provisions, including the twelve-year clause in the American treaty, justified negotiations for expanded rights: diplomatic residence in Beijing, additional ports, travel in the interior, legalization of opium, and clearer protection for missionaries. The Qing rejected these demands. The Xianfeng emperor, who came to the throne in 1850, and his advisers viewed concessions as dangerous, especially while the empire faced the Taiping Rebellion, which began in Guangxi in 1850–1851 and established a rival capital at Nanjing in 1853 (Chapter 53). Foreign officials saw the Qing as weak and regarded a firmer approach as necessary. Some also hoped that cooperation with the Qing against rebels might yield concessions, while others sympathized with the Taiping for their Christian-inflected ideology; foreign policy toward the rebellion shifted with circumstances.

Other irritants accumulated. The opium trade continued and grew. Piracy was widespread along the coast, and British and Chinese authorities disagreed about suppression and about the status of the small, locally registered European-style ships called lorchas, which often carried Chinese crews and registration from Hong Kong. Missionaries, both Protestant and Catholic, pressed beyond the treaty ports; the treaties allowed activity only in the ports, but missionaries traveled illegally into the interior, and local authorities arrested them. Anti-foreign incidents occurred in several places. Chinese emigration for labor, which would become a scandal in the following decade, also began to emerge from Xiamen and Hong Kong in this period.

This section should lead to the Arrow incident but must not imply that the Arrow alone caused another war. By 1856, the major issues were accumulated: unresolved questions about the status of foreigners and their ships, the denial of Guangzhou city entry, the gap between treaty text and local reality, British demands for diplomatic access, French concern for missionaries, and the Qing court's preoccupation with the Taiping rebellion. Britain and France were also freed from other commitments when the Crimean War ended in 1856. The Arrow incident supplied a pretext, not the cause.

Section 52.14

The Second Opium War Begins, 1856–1858

On 8 October 1856, Qing authorities at Guangzhou boarded the Arrow, a lorcha with a Chinese crew, owned by a Chinese resident of Hong Kong and registered there under British colonial regulations, and arrested twelve crew members on suspicion of piracy. The British consul, Harry Parkes, claimed that the ship was a British vessel flying the British flag and that the boarding was an insult. The legal facts were murky: the ship's registration had apparently expired, and whether the flag was flying at the time was disputed. The governor-general, Ye Mingchen, released most of the crew but refused the formal apology the British demanded. The British superintendent in Hong Kong, John Bowring, and Admiral Michael Seymour escalated, bombarding Guangzhou's defenses in late October and later breaching the city walls. In December, a mob burned the foreign factories.

The incident was a trigger, not the underlying cause. Parkes and Bowring used it to press for the entry into Guangzhou and the broader treaty revisions that had been blocked for years. In Britain, the incident provoked a parliamentary revolt: a motion critical of the government's handling passed in the Commons in early 1857, and Palmerston, then prime minister, called an election and won, interpreting the result as public support. The government decided to send a special envoy, Lord Elgin, with a naval and military force, and to act in concert with France. The Indian Rebellion of 1857 delayed their arrival, as troops were diverted to India.

France's participation grew out of its own grievance. In February 1856, the French Catholic missionary Auguste Chapdelaine had been arrested and executed by a county magistrate in Guangxi, a province where foreign missionary activity was prohibited. The French government, under Napoleon III, which presented itself as protector of Catholic missions, made the killing a justification for joining Britain. The episode shows how missionary activity, which the treaties permitted only in the open ports, had become a legal and diplomatic flashpoint, and how a county-level execution became an international incident.

Anglo-French forces captured Guangzhou on 28–29 December 1857 after a bombardment. Ye Mingchen was captured and exiled to Calcutta, where he died in 1859. The Allies established an occupation administration with a Qing official, Bo Gui, as nominal governor, a joint arrangement that lasted until 1861. The Cantonese gentry and population, who had resisted foreign entry for years, experienced the occupation as a humiliation, and local resistance continued. Meanwhile, the Allied ministers, accompanied by American and Russian envoys, moved north with the intention of negotiating directly with the court. They captured the Dagu forts at the mouth of the Hai River on 20 May 1858 and advanced to Tianjin, threatening the capital.

The Qing court was in a harder position than in 1842. The Taiping held Nanjing, the Nian rebels were active in the north (Chapter 53), and the treasury was strained. Xianfeng appointed Guiliang and Huashana as negotiators. Debates at court continued between moderates and hardliners, but the negotiators concluded that continued war would be dangerous. In June 1858, they signed a series of treaties with Britain, France, the United States, and Russia. The question was whether the court would honor them.

Section 52.15

The Treaties of Tianjin, 1858

The Treaties of Tianjin were concluded in June 1858 with Russia (13 June), the United States (18 June), Britain (26 June), and France (27 June). They represented a much deeper intrusion into the Qing diplomatic and legal order than the 1842 settlement. The core provisions included: the right to station diplomatic representatives in Beijing, ending the long Qing insistence that foreign envoys deal with provincial officials; additional ports; foreign navigation on the Yangtze; the right of foreigners with passports to travel in the interior; protection for Christian missionaries and Chinese converts; and substantial indemnities. The port provisions need to be read treaty by treaty. The British treaty itself named Niuzhuang (Newchwang), Dengzhou (Tangzhou), Taiwan, Chaozhou (Swatow), and Qiongzhou on Hainan, and separately provided for Zhenjiang and up to three further Yangtze ports as far as Hankou once peace was restored. The French treaty did not use exactly the same wording, and its additions are best checked against the text. What actually opened in practice depended on later implementation, most-favored-nation claims, the 1860 Convention (which added Tianjin), and the progress of the Taiping war along the Yangtze, so the list of ports operating in 1860 and in the early 1860s should not be read back as the list of any single treaty.

The treaties also dealt with the language of diplomacy. One article of the British treaty forbade Qing officials from using the character yi (夷), often translated "barbarian," to refer to the British government or its subjects in official documents. The provision shows that Western diplomats regarded Qing diplomatic vocabulary as a symbol of inequality. Modern scholars have noted that the meaning of yi was more complex than a simple insult and that the controversy reveals competing conceptions of diplomatic order and translation as much as outright contempt.

Commercial terms were refined in a separate set of tariff rules negotiated at Shanghai in November 1858. They revised the tariff schedule, set a commuted transit-duty rate for goods moving inland, and placed opium on the dutiable list. These measures integrated opium into the formal customs framework, marking a significant shift from the earlier illegal status. The Qing court may have accepted some of these terms in the belief that regulated imports would yield revenue and reduce smuggling. But the long debate over opium policy would continue in China, among reformers, officials, and later nationalists.

The Russian and American treaties had different emphases. Russia's treaty, negotiated by Count Putyatin, was accompanied by the Treaty of Aigun (May 1858), negotiated separately by Nikolai Muravyov with the Heilongjiang general Yishan, which transferred the left bank of the Amur River to Russia and was followed in 1860 by further territorial agreements in the Ussuri region. This reminds students that the Qing faced pressure from multiple directions, and that the northern and northeastern frontiers were also sites of imperial expansion (Chapters 48 and 55). The American treaty, negotiated by William Reed, extended MFN-style benefits and avoided direct military action but gained from the pressure the others exerted.

In Beijing, the response was divided. Many officials saw the treaties, especially the legation clause and the provisions about interior travel, as unacceptable. A faction led by the Mongol commander Sengge Rinchen advocated reinforcing the Dagu forts and resisting the exchange of ratifications in Beijing. Others, including Guiliang, Huashana, and Prince Gong (Yixin), urged compliance. The Xianfeng emperor, receptive to the first view and hoping to bargain the treaties down, sent mixed signals and, in 1859, directed the foreign ministers to approach by a route that avoided the Dagu forts.

China and the Wider World

The Opium Wars in a Global Frame

The British Empire and India. The wars were financed and staffed in part from British India. Indian opium revenue, Indian troops, Indian-built ships, and the governor-general's authority were integral. The Indian Rebellion of 1857 delayed the Second Opium War, showing how events across the empire interacted. Indian and Chinese histories were connected through the opium economy.
Russia and the northern frontier. While Britain and France pressed the Qing from the coast, Russia exploited Qing weakness to gain territory along the Amur and Ussuri. Russia's gains came through negotiation backed by the evident weakness of the Qing, not through a war of its own, and reshaped the northeastern frontier.
The United States and France. The United States sought commercial access and the benefits of MFN without large-scale force, while France combined commercial interest with its claim as protector of Catholic missions. Both illustrate that the treaty system was multilateral: it was built by several powers acting in competition and in loose cooperation.
Qing diplomacy as active process. Qing officials were not passive recipients. They negotiated, delayed, exploited rivalries among foreign powers, appealed to treaty text, and in 1859 inflicted a real defeat on the Allies at the Dagu forts. Their choices were constrained by war with domestic rebels and by limited information, but they were choices.
Global economy and ideas. The wars occurred amid the expansion of steam shipping, new naval technologies, and global flows of silver, tea, silk, and opium. They also coincided with debates in Britain about free trade, empire, and morality, and with the Christian missionary revival that sent missionaries to China.
Section 52.16

War Renewed: Taku, Beijing, and the Summer Palace, 1859–1860

In June 1859, the newly appointed British minister, Frederick Bruce, and the French minister, accompanied by a naval squadron under Admiral James Hope, sought to proceed to Beijing for ratification of the treaties. The Qing instructed them to land at Beitang, away from the Dagu forts. Bruce and Hope chose to force the river passage. On 25 June, the Qing garrison under Sengge Rinchen, which had strengthened and reorganized the forts, inflicted a sharp defeat on the Allied ships, sinking several gunboats and causing heavy casualties. This was a real Qing military success and reminds students that Qing forces could win when conditions favored them. The American minister, John Ward, followed the route the Qing prescribed and traveled to Beijing, where ratifications of the American treaty were exchanged after a dispute over ceremonial conduct.

Britain and France resolved to return in force. In 1860 they assembled a much larger expedition, commanded by Sir James Hope Grant and General Cousin-Montauban, with Elgin and Baron Gros as plenipotentiaries. Landing at Beitang in August, the Allies took the Dagu forts from the rear on 21 August and occupied Tianjin. Qing negotiators were sent to Tongzhou, near Beijing, to discuss terms. During the talks, on 18 September, a Qing detachment seized a party of Allied negotiators and escorts, including Harry Parkes and Henry Loch, and a group of soldiers and journalists. The captives were taken to Beijing and held under harsh conditions, and roughly half of them died in custody, a fact that deeply angered Allied commanders.

Military action followed. The Allies defeated Sengge Rinchen's cavalry at Zhangjiawan and Baliqiao (Palikao) in September, and the Xianfeng emperor left Beijing for the imperial hunting lodge at Rehe (Chengde) in the north, a departure the court called a hunting trip. Prince Gong was left in the capital to negotiate. On 6–7 October, French and British troops entered the Yuanmingyuan, the vast imperial garden palace northwest of Beijing, and looted it extensively. After the release of surviving captives and the discovery of the bodies of those who had died, Elgin decided on retaliation. On 18–19 October, British troops burned the Yuanmingyuan.

The burning should be explained critically, in its immediate military and diplomatic context. Elgin chose the Summer Palace as a target because it was the emperor's personal property and a way to punish the court rather than the Beijing population, and because he wished to avoid destroying the Forbidden City, which he feared would topple the dynasty and complicate negotiation. Others, including some British officers and later critics such as the French writer Victor Hugo, condemned the destruction as vandalism. Accounts also indicate that looting involved not only Allied soldiers but, afterward, local people and palace personnel. The garden contained architecture combining Chinese and European-influenced designs, great collections of art and documents, and gardens built over generations of Qing rulers. Its destruction removed irreplaceable cultural heritage, and many objects ended up in museums and private collections abroad, a matter of continuing debate. The Yuanmingyuan became in later Chinese memory an enduring symbol of foreign aggression, and its ruins were preserved as a commemorative site.

The destruction was not an inevitable act of war. It was a decision, justified by Allied commanders as retaliation and a means of pressure, and contested at the time. It also did not change Qing policy in the direction of resistance. It reinforced the view of Prince Gong and others that the Qing had to accept terms to avoid a worse outcome, particularly the possibility of an attack on the dynasty's ancestral and political core while the Taiping controlled the Yangtze.

Section 52.17

The Convention of Beijing and the Mature Treaty System

On 24 October 1860, Prince Gong and Lord Elgin signed the Convention of Beijing; the French convention followed the next day. The conventions confirmed the Treaties of Tianjin and added several terms. The indemnities to Britain and France were increased to eight million taels each. Tianjin was opened as a treaty port, giving the foreign powers access close to the capital. The southern part of the Kowloon Peninsula (south of what later became Boundary Street), together with Stonecutters Island, was ceded to Britain. The conventions recognized the right of Chinese to emigrate under contract to British colonies from treaty ports, legitimizing a labor migration that would become a source of abuse. The French text, through a Chinese-language addition that became notorious, granted missionaries the right to rent and buy land in the provinces, a provision whose origins and wording continued to cause disputes. In November 1860, Russia, whose envoy Nikolai Ignatiev had mediated in the crisis, secured a convention confirming the Russian acquisition of the Ussuri region and expanding Russian commercial rights in northwestern China.

The combined structure by 1860 can be called the mature treaty system. Its main components were: open ports, expanded from five to more than a dozen, and increasingly extending into the Yangtze and the north; extraterritoriality through consular jurisdiction; conventional tariffs set by treaty and an emerging customs service in which foreigners played an administrative role; MFN, which multiplied privileges; diplomatic residence in Beijing, symbolized by the creation of legations; missionary rights and protection; foreign navigation on the Yangtze and along the coast; territorial cessions at Hong Kong Island and southern Kowloon; and indemnities imposed after defeat. Each of these provisions limited the exercise of Qing sovereignty, and most could not be revoked by the Qing alone.

It is equally important to state what the system was not. The Qing was not colonized. It remained a functioning imperial government governing the great majority of its territory and population, collecting taxes, appointing officials, and suppressing rebellions. Foreign residence was limited to specified places; foreign merchants were few; and in the interior, Qing magistrates, gentry, and lineages continued to govern as before. The treaty ports were concentrated enclaves rather than blanket foreign control. What changed was that foreign powers had acquired legally entrenched privileges and institutional footholds that the Qing had not freely chosen and could not unilaterally remove. The effect on sovereignty was substantial, but it was partial and uneven.

Chinese responses in this period were varied. The court's immediate concern was survival and the suppression of the Taiping rebellion. Prince Gong, Guiliang, and Wenxiang, in a memorial in early 1861, argued that the Qing should observe the treaties to avoid further conflict, prioritize defeating the rebels, and establish a dedicated office to handle foreign affairs. The proposal was made in January 1861, and the Zongli Yamen was formally established in March (总理各国事务衙门), the Office for the General Management of the Affairs of All Nations, which would become the Qing's central foreign-policy institution (Chapter 54). Some officials favored a policy of cooperation, called by contemporaries and later historians conciliation or "accommodation"; others remained hostile and advocated firmness. Intellectuals such as Wei Yuan, whose Illustrated Treatise on the Maritime Kingdoms had advocated learning foreign techniques to resist foreign powers, supplied an intellectual framework, which later reformers extended. Gentry in Guangdong and elsewhere continued to resist local foreign encroachment. Merchants adapted to new opportunities. These responses did not form a single policy but a spectrum.

Regional Context

Four Regions Under the New Order

Guangzhou and the Pearl River delta. The old center of European trade lost relative position as Shanghai rose, but it remained a major port, and the city and its hinterland were the center of anti-foreign sentiment, militia organization, and resistance to city entry. It experienced occupation from 1857 to 1861. Hong Kong, next door, grew as a British colony and entrepôt, and Macao lost ground to it.

Shanghai and the lower Yangtze. Shanghai became the principal treaty port, benefitting from its position at the mouth of the Yangtze and its Jiangnan hinterland. The surrounding region, among the wealthiest in the empire, was also the area most affected by the Taiping Rebellion, making Shanghai both a refuge and a commercial center during civil war.

The Yangtze corridor. The Treaties of Tianjin opened Yangtze ports to foreign navigation and residence once rebel control ended, extending the treaty system inland along China's greatest commercial artery. The practical opening was delayed until the Taiping war eased and, for Hankou and others, until the early 1860s.

Beijing and Tianjin. The capital region had been largely insulated from foreign contact before 1858. The legation clause, the opening of Tianjin, and the occupation of Beijing in 1860 brought foreign diplomacy to the center of the Qing political world. For Qing court politics, this was a deeper intrusion than the opening of any port.

Continuity and Change Over Time: From the Canton System to the Treaty-Port Order

What Changed

  • Foreign trade expanded from one port to a growing list of ports on the coast, the Yangtze, and the north
  • The Cohong monopoly ended; merchants dealt with any Chinese partners
  • Tariffs became conventional, set by treaty, and no longer adjustable by the Qing alone
  • Foreigners of treaty powers came under their consuls' jurisdiction rather than Qing courts
  • Foreign diplomats gained residence in Beijing and correspondence on terms of equality
  • Hong Kong Island and southern Kowloon became British territory; foreign missionaries gained legal protection

What Stayed the Same

  • The Qing remained the governing authority over nearly all its territory and population
  • Chinese merchants, compradors, banks, and guild networks continued to control domestic commerce
  • Local officials, gentry, and lineages remained the main institutions of government and community in the interior
  • Opium continued to flow, now increasingly through ports and, after 1858, in a formal customs framework
  • The practice of managing foreign relations through intermediaries and negotiation persisted, adapted to new institutions

Who experienced change most directly? Port merchants, Hong merchants, compradors, coastal and boat communities, officials in treaty ports, and the court in Beijing. Who experienced continuity? Most villagers, farmers, and small-town residents, whose daily lives were affected only indirectly. Why it matters: The pattern shows that sovereignty can be eroded without a state ceasing to function, and that historical change may be concentrated in particular places, institutions, and groups.

Section 52.18

Chapter Review and Opium Wars / Treaty-Port System Checkpoint

This chapter has traced the movement from a dispute over trade, silver, law, and diplomacy to two wars and an institutional order. The synthesis question that organizes it is more demanding than "Who won?": How did military defeat transform a dispute over trade, law, and diplomacy into an international treaty system that changed the exercise of Qing sovereignty? A good answer must connect three layers. The first is the underlying conflict established in Chapter 51: British demands for wider access and diplomatic equality against the Qing's controlled Canton System, with opium as the commodity that sharpened every disagreement. The second is the series of decisions, in Guangzhou, London, Calcutta, and Beijing, that turned a crisis into war. The third is the process by which settlements negotiated under coercion were extended and multiplied through MFN, consular jurisdiction, and repeated treaty revision.

Second, extraterritoriality, treaty ports, concessions, colonies, and leased territories are different things, and "unequal treaties" is a label that requires an explanation of coercion, asymmetry, and limits on sovereignty. Third, the Qing was neither destroyed nor passive. It continued to govern, negotiated, resisted at Dagu, and began to rethink institutions, even as it fought rebellions that, as the next chapter shows, devastated the empire from within.

The chapter's boundary should also be clear. It ends with the treaty system as established through 1860 and the first Chinese responses. The Taiping Rebellion and other mid-century upheavals are the subject of Chapter 53; the Self-Strengthening Movement and the Zongli Yamen's development follow in Chapter 54; and the intensified imperialism of the 1890s, including leased territories and spheres of influence, is the subject of Chapter 55. Students should resist reading those later events back into 1842 or 1860 as if they were already determined.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Opium Wars were not simply wars about opium. They grew from the commercial, diplomatic, and legal conflicts of the Canton System, and military defeat forced the Qing into a treaty framework that altered tariffs, jurisdiction, diplomacy, residence, missionary activity, and access to Chinese ports. The treaty system constrained Qing sovereignty, but it did not immediately destroy the Qing state or reduce China to passive foreign control.

The crisis began with the Daoguang court's decision for strict suppression and the appointment of Lin Zexu as imperial commissioner. Lin, a capable official working within a long tradition of prohibition, confined the foreign factories at Guangzhou, demanded the surrender of opium, and destroyed more than 20,000 chests at Humen in 1839. Charles Elliot's pledge of compensation converted private contraband into a British government claim. Disputes over bonds and jurisdiction, such as the Lin Weixi case, broke down the Guangzhou arrangements.

Britain's decision for war reflected a convergence of motives: compensation, security for British subjects and trade, diplomatic equality, commercial access, the financial importance of Indian opium, merchant lobbying, and a free-trade ideology. British domestic opinion was divided, and the decision should be neither sanitized nor reduced to a single motive. Historians continue to debate how to weigh opium, trade and diplomacy, and Qing domestic politics in explaining the war.

The First Opium War unfolded as an expanding campaign. British naval mobility, steam vessels, artillery, and logistics allowed pressure at Zhoushan, the Bogue, Xiamen, Ningbo, Shanghai, and above all Zhenjiang and the Yangtze. Qing forces fought hard at specific sites, and gentry militias such as those at Sanyuanli resisted, but the empire lacked a coastal defense system, unified command, and reliable information. The court oscillated between conciliation and resistance, dismissed both Lin and Qishan, and settled when the Yangtze arteries were threatened.

The Treaty of Nanjing opened five ports, ceded Hong Kong, imposed indemnities, abolished the Cohong, and established a framework for a conventional tariff and official correspondence on equal terms. It did not yet establish extraterritoriality, MFN, or the full system of rights that later treaties would add. Supplementary treaties with Britain, the United States, and France in 1843–1844 introduced consular jurisdiction, MFN, a conventional tariff, and Catholic toleration, turning a bilateral settlement into a multilateral treaty system.

The treaty ports were not colonies. Shanghai rose rapidly as the most important, built on an existing commercial base with Chinese merchants, bankers, and compradors, and intensified by the Taiping disruption of older trade routes. Extraterritoriality removed foreigners of treaty powers from Qing courts. The tariff and customs arrangements limited Qing fiscal autonomy and, after the Small Swords seizure of Shanghai, led to a foreign role in customs administration. Opium remained at the center of the commerce, and the 1858 tariff placed it on the dutiable schedule.

The first settlement failed to produce stable relations. The Guangzhou city-entry controversy, treaty revision demands, missionary activity beyond the ports, piracy and lorcha disputes, and the court's preoccupation with the Taiping Rebellion fed a second war. The Arrow incident and the execution of the missionary Chapdelaine were triggers rather than causes. Anglo-French forces captured Guangzhou, took the Dagu forts, and compelled the Treaties of Tianjin in 1858, which extended foreign rights to Beijing, the interior, and the Yangtze.

When the Qing court resisted ratification and Sengge Rinchen repulsed the Allied attack at Dagu in 1859, Britain and France returned in 1860, captured the forts, defeated the Qing field army, and entered Beijing. The Xianfeng emperor fled to Rehe, the Allies looted and burned the Yuanmingyuan, and Prince Gong signed the Convention of Beijing, which ratified Tianjin, increased indemnities, opened Tianjin, ceded southern Kowloon and Stonecutters Island, and confirmed an expanding foreign presence. The burning of the palace was a decision justified as retaliation, contested then and since.

By 1860 the mature treaty system included open ports, consular jurisdiction, conventional tariffs, MFN, diplomatic residence, missionary protection, foreign navigation, territorial cessions, and indemnities. The Qing remained a functioning imperial government, and the system's effects were concentrated in particular places and groups, while most of the empire experienced the consequences indirectly. Chinese responses ranged from militia resistance and conciliation to intellectual reassessment, including Wei Yuan's work and the creation of the Zongli Yamen in 1861.

The chapter contributes to the larger history of Chinese civilization by showing how a long-established framework for managing foreign contact was replaced by a new institutional order through coercion and negotiation, and by demonstrating that the consequences were uneven, contested, and shaped by Chinese as well as foreign agency. It sets the stage for the rebellions and reforms of the following chapters, but it should not be read as an inevitable prelude to later Qing decline or to revolution.

Key Terms

Canton System
The Qing framework, from the mid-eighteenth century to 1842, restricting European maritime trade to Guangzhou under licensed Hong merchants and imperial officials.
Cohong (Gonghang, 公行)
The guild of licensed Hong merchants responsible for foreign trade and foreign merchants' conduct; abolished by the Treaty of Nanjing.
Hoppo
The imperial customs superintendent at Guangzhou, a key official in the Canton System.
Imperial commissioner (qinchai dachen, 钦差大臣)
A special envoy with authority from the emperor to deal with a specific problem, as Lin Zexu was in 1839.
Superintendent of Trade
The British government's representative in China after 1834, charged with overseeing British trade; Charles Elliot held the office in 1839.
Chest of opium
The standard shipping unit for opium, weighing roughly 60 kilograms; more than 20,000 were surrendered in 1839.
Humen (Bocca Tigris, the Bogue)
The narrows guarding the river approach to Guangzhou, site of forts and of the destruction of opium in 1839.
Chuanbi Convention
A draft agreement of January 1841 between Qishan and Elliot, rejected by both governments.
Sanyuanli
Village north of Guangzhou where gentry-led militias confronted British troops in May 1841, remembered as an episode of popular resistance.
Indemnity
A payment imposed on a defeated state to compensate the victor for losses and costs; the Treaty of Nanjing imposed 21 million silver dollars.
Tael (liang, 两)
A Chinese unit of silver weight used as a unit of account; by period conversion, roughly 1.4 Spanish dollars per tael.
Treaty port (tongshang kou'an)
A Chinese city opened by treaty to foreign trade and residence, remaining under Qing sovereignty.
Concession / settlement
A designated area in a treaty port where foreigners leased land and often developed their own municipal administration.
Colony
Territory ceded to and governed by a foreign power, such as Hong Kong Island and southern Kowloon.
Extraterritoriality (zhiwai faquan)
Treaty-based exemption of foreigners from Qing jurisdiction, with legal authority exercised by their consuls.
Consul
A state's official representative at a port, responsible for its nationals' affairs and, under the treaties, for jurisdiction over them.
Most-favored-nation clause (MFN)
A provision extending to one party any privilege granted to a third state; in China it worked as a ratchet spreading concessions among treaty powers.
Conventional tariff
A tariff fixed by treaty that could be changed only by agreement, limiting Qing fiscal autonomy.
Comprador (maiban, 买办)
A Chinese intermediary employed by a foreign firm to manage local business, credit, and staff.
Daotai
Circuit intendant; a Qing official who administered a region and often handled dealings with foreign consuls in treaty ports.
Lijin (厘金)
An inland transit tax introduced in 1853 to fund the war against rebels, disputed by foreign merchants.
Imperial Maritime Customs Service
A customs administration that evolved from the 1854 Shanghai arrangement, in which foreign inspectors collected Qing duties.
Unequal treaties
A term, popular from the early twentieth century, for treaties imposed under coercion that limited Chinese sovereignty without reciprocal rights.
Sovereignty
The authority of a state to govern its territory and population without external control; partially limited by treaty in this period.
Jimi (羁縻)
A traditional approach to outside groups, "loose rein" management through concession and cultivation, associated with Qiying's policy.
Yi (夷)
A Chinese term often translated "barbarian"; its range of meanings and the 1858 prohibition on its use in official documents illustrate disputes over diplomatic language.
Lorcha
A small vessel combining Western hull and Chinese rig, central to the Arrow dispute.
Legation
A diplomatic mission headed by a minister; the Treaties of Tianjin authorized legations in Beijing.
Zongli Yamen (总理各国事务衙门)
The Office for the General Management of the Affairs of All Nations, created in 1861 as the Qing's central foreign-affairs body.
Yuanmingyuan (圆明园)
The Qing imperial garden palace northwest of Beijing, looted and burned by Allied forces in October 1860.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a relationship governed for a century by one set of institutions was reorganized by another under military pressure. The Canton System had been a Qing creation, with its own logic of control and revenue. The treaty system was a joint but unequal product: negotiated with Qing officials, imposed under coercion, extended by competition among foreign powers, and adapted in practice by merchants, consuls, compradors, and local officials. Understanding how it was built, step by step, helps students avoid treating it as a single act of foreign domination.

The chapter also shaped later Chinese history. The treaty system created the legal and commercial setting for Shanghai's growth, for missionary expansion and its conflicts, for the debate over tariffs and customs, and for the long struggle by Chinese reformers, officials, and nationalists to regain tariff autonomy and end extraterritoriality. It influenced how Chinese intellectuals thought about foreign knowledge, from Wei Yuan to the officials of the next generation. At the same time, it unfolded alongside, and was altered by, the Taiping Rebellion, which is the subject of the next chapter.

The chapter reveals larger themes of this textbook: the interaction of state, commerce, and law; the importance of frontier and maritime spaces; the ways that sovereignty can be divided, limited, and contested; the role of Chinese agency within constraints; and the importance of reading sources across perspectives. It connects China to the British Empire and India, to Russian expansion, to the global circulation of silver, tea, and opium, and to the nineteenth-century expansion of steam power and missionary Christianity.

Finally, the chapter helps students see Chinese history as diverse, interconnected, contested, adaptive, and unfinished. Qing officials, gentry militias, Hong merchants, compradors, boat-dwellers, bankers, and princes did not experience the wars in the same way, and historians continue to debate their causes and meaning. The treaty system was neither the end of the Qing nor a minor episode. It was a profound change whose consequences unfolded unevenly over decades, and that is why careful, evidence-based history matters.
