Part IV — Division, Migration, and Religious Transformation
20

Western Jin, the Sixteen Kingdoms, and the Transformation of North China

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

In the spring of 280, the last ruler of Wu, Sun Hao, surrendered at Jianye on the lower Yangtze, and the Jin dynasty could claim what no government had achieved since the Han empire dissolved around 220: authority over the great river valleys of both north and south. Thirty-one years later, in 311, the Jin capital of Luoyang was captured and its emperor taken prisoner. Five years after that, in 316, the second capital, Chang'an, fell, and the Western Jin dynasty formally ended. How could a state that had just reunified the former Han world lose its northern capitals within a single generation?

This chapter studies that question, and it begins by refusing the easiest answer. The familiar short version of the story runs from a weak Chinese dynasty, to barbarian invasion, to chaos. Modern historians find that account inadequate. The Western Jin was undermined first from within. The founders' family, the Sima clan, had risen by seizing control of the Wei court, and its emperors then distributed troops, territory, and authority to imperial princes. After the death of Emperor Wu in 290, competition among princes, consort families, and court factions turned those resources against the dynasty itself. Between roughly 291 and 306 a sequence of coups and civil wars, conventionally called the War of the Eight Princes, drained the central government's fiscal and military capacity and drew regional armies, including cavalry recruited from communities along the northern frontier, into the struggle. New regimes were proclaimed in 304, while Western Jin still existed. The capitals fell to armies that were themselves products of the wars Jin's own elite had started.

What changed during this period is easy to list and hard to overstate. A reunified empire became a set of competing states in North China, some ruled by emperors and some by kings who nominally acknowledged Jin. Hundreds of thousands of people, and probably more, left the war-torn Yellow River plain, though most people stayed in the north or moved within it. Aristocratic lineages crossed the Yangtze and helped found a southern court, which Chapter 21 will examine. In the north, commanders and rulers from Xiongnu, Jie, Xianbei, Di, Qiang, and Han-descended backgrounds built governments that combined imperial titles and civil bureaucracies with military organizations rooted in Inner Asian traditions. What continued is equally important. Counties and commanderies, household registration as a state goal, agriculture as the fiscal foundation, the Chinese script, court ritual, and the prestige of great families all survived the fall of the dynasty, and successor governments reused them.

The people affected were nearly everyone in the northern plains and many beyond. Sima princes and their retainers, court officials in Luoyang, provincial commanders, and soldiers lived through the wars as participants, and often as victims. Farming households faced conscription, requisition, flight, and famine. Communities of Xiongnu, Di, Qiang, and Xianbei, many of whom had lived inside imperial territory for generations as soldiers, tenants, herders, and neighbors, faced suspicion, exploitation, and, eventually, opportunities to build power of their own. Women and children were captured, sold, or left behind. Monks and translators moved through the war zones and gradually found patrons among the new rulers. The chapter attempts to keep all of these groups in view rather than following only the fortunes of courts.

The evidence for this period is unusually difficult, and the chapter treats that difficulty as part of its subject. The main narrative source, the Book of Jin (Jin shu), was compiled under the Tang dynasty in the seventh century, roughly three hundred years after the events it describes. Sixth-century and later works on the northern states, and the eleventh-century Comprehensive Mirror for Aid in Government (Zizhi tongjian), also depend on earlier writings that were partly lost. Memorials, biographies, inscriptions, tomb finds, and excavated objects provide a check, but they are scattered. Categories that seem fundamental, such as "Western Jin," "Sixteen Kingdoms," and "Five Hu," were assembled by later writers who imposed order on a far messier political landscape. Students should ask of every source who wrote it, when, and from what political position.

This chapter connects Chinese history to wider Asian history in several ways. The northern states arose in a zone where farming societies and Inner Asian pastoral and mixed economies had interacted for centuries, and their rulers drew on traditions from both. Buddhist monks and translators from Central Asia and India worked in the capitals of the new states, a development taken up in Chapter 23. The movement of Xianbei groups from the northeast and the steppe edge fed into the rise of the Northern Wei, the subject of Chapter 22. The interval between 280 and 439 also belongs to an era in which several large agrarian states in Eurasia faced comparable pressures on their frontiers and comparable transformations of their armies, a comparison that should be made cautiously because each case had its own internal logic.

The chapter's argument can be stated briefly. Western Jin reunification was real but fragile. The dynasty inherited institutions, armies, aristocratic networks, and regional inequalities from the Three Kingdoms period examined in Chapter 19, and its decision to strengthen the imperial clan through powerful princely commands created new points of failure. Civil war among the Sima then opened the way for new coalitions and states. The collapse of Western Jin did not end political life in North China. It destroyed one imperial center while accelerating migration, local militarization, and the formation of new multiethnic states, and it began a durable north-south division that was not simply an age of disorder but the start of a profound reorganization of Chinese society and government. Chapter 21 follows the southern successor and Chapter 22 the northern consolidation.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace the rise of the Sima family from Sima Yi's seizure of power at the Wei court in 249 to the founding of Jin by Sima Yan in 265–266, and explain why Sima Yi did not found the Jin dynasty himself.
  2. Explain the difference between the founding of Jin (265–266) and the reunification of the realm with the conquest of Wu in 280.
  3. Describe Western Jin institutions, including princely enfeoffment, regional military commands, land and tax policies, and aristocratic recruitment, and assess their strengths and weaknesses.
  4. Analyze the War of the Eight Princes (291–306) as a sequence of overlapping coups and civil wars rather than a single eight-sided conflict.
  5. Distinguish the fall of Luoyang (311) from the fall of Chang'an and the formal end of Western Jin (316), and explain why the difference matters.
  6. Assess the patterns of migration and displacement after 300, including the fact that most people did not flee south.
  7. Explain why "Sixteen Kingdoms" is a retrospective label, and compare representative northern states such as Han-Zhao, Cheng-Han, Later Zhao, Former Yan, and Former Qin.
  8. Evaluate the labels Xiongnu, Jie, Xianbei, Di, and Qiang, and the retrospective grouping "Five Hu," as evidence rather than as fixed ethnic categories.
  9. Compare destruction and reconstruction in northern society, including fortified settlements, resettlement, and mixed farming and herding economies.
  10. Interpret Jiang Tong's Discourse on Moving the Rong as evidence of elite anxiety before the collapse, while identifying the prejudice in the source.
  11. Connect the collapse of Western Jin to the political division of north and south and to the topics of Chapters 21 and 22, without treating that division as inevitable.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did a dynasty that reunified the former Han world in 280 lose effective control of North China within about thirty years?
  2. How did the Sima family's own rise to power shape the institutions and the political vulnerabilities of Western Jin?
  3. Why did the Jin court grant military and territorial authority to imperial princes, and how did that decision affect the crisis after 290?
  4. Why is "invasion" an inadequate description of the events of 304–316?
  5. How did ordinary people, from farmers to refugee lineages to military families, experience the collapse of central authority?
  6. What does the label "Sixteen Kingdoms" reveal, and conceal, about the political world of North China between 304 and 439?
  7. How did northern states combine inherited imperial institutions with the traditions of Inner Asian military and political communities?
  8. What kinds of evidence allow historians to reconstruct a period that later dynastic histories tell in an orderly but retrospective form?
  9. In what senses was the north-south division after 316 new, and in what senses was it a reorganization of older regional differences?

Historical Context

Chapter 18 traced the fall of the Han dynasty, and Chapter 19 showed how three successor states, Cao Wei in the north, Shu Han in the southwest, and Eastern Wu in the lower and middle Yangtze, built functioning governments out of Han fragmentation. Each state solved the problems of survival differently. Wei, the largest, relied on registered military households, agricultural colonies (tuntian), and cooperation with prominent families through a system of official recruitment that, in its mature form, ranked candidates in nine grades on the recommendation of local assessors. Wu governed a Yangtze world of great local families, private followers, and river-based armies. Shu governed the Sichuan basin and its approaches. By the 260s, Wei's territory included the Central Plains, Guanzhong, the northern frontier provinces, and, after 263, Sichuan; Wu remained the last rival.

The geographic space of this chapter is essentially the space that Chapter 2 introduced as North China and its neighbors: the North China Plain and the middle Yellow River, the loess highlands of Bing province (roughly modern Shanxi), the Guanzhong basin around Chang'an, the Hexi Corridor in the northwest, the Liaoxi and Liaodong regions in the northeast, and the Sichuan basin, with the Yangtze valley as the southern setting. Students should not equate the provinces of the third and fourth centuries with modern provinces, and should treat the place names used here as historical regions whose boundaries shifted repeatedly. The political meaning of "China" was equally unsettled. Contemporaries spoke of the Jin, of the Central Plains, of the imperial legacy of the Han and Wei, and of specific states and titles, and had no single word for a national territory. The idea that legitimate rule descended through a line of dynasties was, in this period, a live claim that competing rulers argued over rather than an agreed fact.

The population of the northern frontier had been changing for two centuries. After the Southern Xiongnu submitted to the Han in the first century, they were settled inside the empire, especially in Bing province, and by the late second and early third centuries the Xiongnu, together with Wuhuan, Xianbei, Di, and Qiang communities, lived within or along the Han and Wei borders. Cao Cao divided the Xiongnu of Bing province into several sections under Han-appointed supervision, and Wei and Jin armies used cavalry drawn from these communities. Other communities of Di and Qiang lived in Guanzhong and the west, where they farmed, served as auxiliaries, and were sometimes exploited by local officials. These circumstances matter because the collapse of the Western Jin cannot be understood as the arrival of strangers. The men who founded new states after 304 were often people who had been raised inside the Jin political and military system.

Politically, the Western Jin inherited a distinctive aristocratic order. Under Wei, the nine-rank system had given the assessment of candidates to officials who often came from established families, and by the third century a small number of lineages dominated high office and passed status across generations. Jin continued this arrangement. Families that combined landholding, dependents, classical learning, and office held a position that the government both relied upon and had difficulty controlling. Intellectual life in Luoyang combined Confucian scholarship with xuanxue (“Dark Learning”), philosophical interpretation that engaged texts such as the Laozi and Zhuangzi, as well as qingtan (“pure conversation”), an elite style of discussion associated with some of the same intellectual circles; the political stakes of such circles were real, as the execution of the writer Ji Kang under Sima Zhao's regime in 262–263 shows. The Sima family's legitimacy was also fragile, since it had risen through coups and the killing of a Wei emperor.

Economically, the wars of the late second and third centuries had reduced registered population and disrupted the irrigation and market systems of the north. The Jin government's registers of 280 record on the order of two and a half million households and some sixteen million people, but such figures reflect the households the state could enumerate and tax, not the whole population, and students should treat them as evidence of administrative reach rather than exact counts. Regional recovery was uneven: the Central Plains and Guanzhong remained scarred, the lower Yangtze had grown in the Wu period, and the Sichuan basin retained its own resources. With these conditions in mind, the sections that follow ask how a reunified dynasty attempted to govern them, and why its solutions produced new vulnerabilities.

Historical Evidence

Historical Evidence

Reading a Period That Later Histories Made Orderly

Few periods in Chinese history illustrate more clearly the gap between events and the accounts of them. Students should sort the evidence into three groups and remember the different kinds of caution each requires.

Contemporary and near-contemporary evidence. Memorials and letters preserved in later collections, inscriptions on stone and brick, dated tomb contents, excavated tombs such as the wall-painted brick tombs of the Hexi Corridor, translators' colophons in Buddhist manuscripts, and the material remains of cities and fortifications belong to the period itself. They are fragmentary, and often local, but they were not written with hindsight about the dynasty's fate. Their limit is scale: a tomb speaks for one family, and a memorial for one official's argument.

Later transmitted histories. The Book of Jin was compiled in 648 under the Tang dynasty by a commission drawing on earlier, now largely lost, histories. It is indispensable, and it contains both careful documentary material and moralizing narrative, in which the failings of individual rulers and consorts explain dynastic decline. Sixth-century and later works on the northern states, such as the northern histories compiled after the Northern Wei, and the Zizhi tongjian of 1084, arranged and evaluated their materials according to the political concerns of their own eras. The history of the northern states was also written by historians working under dynasties that traced their legitimacy to specific predecessors, which shaped what they emphasized.

Retrospective categories. "Western Jin," "Eastern Jin," "Sixteen Kingdoms," and "Five Hu" are labels that later writers assigned. The list of sixteen states traces to a sixth-century work by the historian Cui Hong, and modern scholars identify more significant regimes than sixteen. These tools help students organize the period, but they can suggest an order and a clarity that contemporaries lacked.

A habit to practice throughout this chapter:

When a source describes people as "Hu," "rebels," "bandits," or "barbarians," ask whether the term identifies a group in an objective sense or expresses the compiler's political viewpoint. The same commander could be a loyal general in one text and a bandit in another, depending on who won.

Section 20.1

The Sima Clan and the Founding of Western Jin

The founding of the Jin dynasty in 265–266 is often presented as an event of a single moment, when the last Wei emperor, Cao Huan, ceremonially yielded the throne to Sima Yan. A better account begins earlier, with a process that lasted for roughly seventeen years. The dynasty's founder was Sima Yan, but the political position he inherited had been built by his grandfather, his uncle, and his father. Students should be especially careful on one point. Sima Yi, the grandfather, did not found Jin. He died in 251, more than a decade before the dynasty existed, and his own career was spent as a Wei general and regent whose authority rested on the loyalty of armies and officials he had won in Wei service.

The Sima were a family from Henei, in the Central Plains, with a tradition of classical learning and official service, and Sima Yi rose through Wei ranks under Cao Cao and his successors. He commanded armies against Shu and, in the 230s, against the Liaodong warlord Gongsun Yuan. When the emperor Cao Rui died in 239, leaving a child, Cao Fang, on the throne, Sima Yi and the imperial relative Cao Shuang were appointed regents. Their rivalry produced the crisis of 249. While the young emperor and Cao Shuang were away from Luoyang visiting the tomb of Cao Rui, Sima Yi took control of the capital gates and the palace, and Cao Shuang, after negotiation, surrendered and was executed with his associates. This event, known from the location of the tombs as the Gaoping Tombs incident, is the true beginning of the Sima takeover. Sima Yi then defeated the plot of the general Wang Ling in 251 and died shortly thereafter.

His sons continued the work. Sima Shi, as regent, deposed Cao Fang in 254 and installed a new emperor, Cao Mao, and suppressed the revolt of Guanqiu Jian in 255, dying in the same year. Sima Zhao then held the regency and dealt with the revolt of Zhuge Dan at Shouchun in 257–258, an uprising supported by Wu forces. In 260, Cao Mao, apparently attempting to strike against Sima Zhao, was killed by Sima Zhao's men, an act that shocked contemporaries and left a lasting stain on the family's legitimacy; Cao Huan was then installed. In 263, Wei armies under Deng Ai and Zhong Hui conquered Shu, and the surrender of the Shu ruler Liu Shan gave Sima Zhao a triumph, though the aftermath, in which Zhong Hui revolted in 264 and was killed, showed the risks of relying on commanders. Sima Zhao took the title of Prince of Jin in 264 and died in 265. The conquest of Shu was therefore a Wei achievement, and it belongs to the pre-Jin history of the Sima ascent.

In the winter of 265–266 by the lunar calendar (the date falls in early 266 by the modern calendar), Sima Yan, Sima Zhao's son, received the abdication. The ceremony imitated the abdication by which the Han emperor had given way to Cao Pi in 220, and the imitation was deliberate: the Jin claimed a legitimate transfer of the mandate from Wei, as Wei had claimed one from Han. Historians distinguish the ritual form of the transfer from its political substance. The substance was the accumulated control of the army, the central offices, and the leading aristocratic families that the Sima had assembled over seventeen years, including prominent supporters such as Jia Chong and Pei Xiu, whose families and careers become important later in the chapter. Jin's rulers took Emperor Wu as the posthumous title of Sima Yan and established their capital at Luoyang.

Historical Vocabulary
Fengjian (封建) — enfeoffment; and "princely command"

In classical Chinese, fengjian referred to the practice, attributed to the early Zhou (Chapter 8), of granting territories and titles to kinsmen and allies who owed the ruler loyalty and service. The word has often been translated as "feudalism," but students should be cautious, because Jin practice bore little resemblance to medieval European arrangements of land-based obligation between lords and vassals. In the Jin, imperial princes received titles and territorial "kingdoms" (guo) based on commanderies, with income from their fiefs, small forces of their own, and the right to appoint some officials. What made the system dangerous was a second, separate authority: many princes also held appointments as regional commissioners (dudu) commanding the troops of several provinces. Combining a princely title with a regional command gave individual Sima princes resources that no earlier Wei prince had held. The two things, title and command, should be kept apart in analysis.

The decision to enfeoff imperial relatives was a considered response to the recent past. Cao Wei had kept the Cao princes under close supervision, denying them offices and forces, and the Sima and their advisers concluded that this weakness of the imperial clan had left the dynasty defenseless when a regent with an army moved against it. Contemporary writers argued that a ruling house needed relatives with real resources as a "screen and fence" for the throne. At the dynasty's founding in 265–266, Emperor Wu enfeoffed large numbers of imperial relatives; in a major reorganization in 277, the princely domains, troop establishments, and regional positions were rearranged, with commanderies divided into kingdoms and troops assigned according to the size of each, reportedly at several graded levels, and many princes were sent from the capital to their kingdoms. Several were also placed in charge of the major military commands. The Sima were reasoning from an accurate diagnosis of what had happened to Wei, and they applied a remedy that would fail in a different way.

The Jin dynasty also began with a substantial legal and administrative effort. A new legal code was promulgated in 268, with commentary by the scholar-official Du Yu and others, which is generally regarded as a milestone in the development of Chinese law and a source for the later Northern and Tang codes discussed in later chapters. The government retained the nine-rank recruitment system, the Wei bureaucratic offices, and the network of aristocratic patrons that had sustained Sima ambitions. Students should therefore see the early Jin as a dynasty that combined genuine institutional creativity with a political settlement in which the imperial family, consort families, and great families all held resources that could be turned against one another. The next section examines how those resources were used to reunify the realm before they were used to divide it.

Section 20.2

Reunification After the Three Kingdoms

Jin was founded in 265–266, but the realm was not reunified until 280. For fourteen years, Wu, ruling the middle and lower Yangtze and the far south from Jianye, remained an independent state. Its last ruler, Sun Hao, had a reputation in later histories for cruelty, but historians should treat that reputation with the caution appropriate to accounts written by the conquerors. The more secure facts are that Wu was militarily formidable along the Yangtze, that its elites were divided, and that the Jin court was cautious. Emperor Wu authorized a campaign only after long preparation and debate among his ministers, in which some, such as Jia Chong, resisted, while others, including Du Yu and Zhang Hua, pressed for action.

In the winter of 279–280, Jin armies advanced in several columns. Du Yu moved from the middle Yangtze region around Xiangyang; Wang Hun and Sima Zhou, a prince, advanced from the north; and Wang Jun, whose fleet had been built in Sichuan, sailed down the Yangtze, overcoming obstacles that Wu had placed in the river. The campaign was short. In the spring of 280, Sun Hao surrendered at Jianye, and the registers of Wu, recording roughly half a million households, passed to the Jin. The conquest was a real accomplishment, and Jin's contemporaries celebrated it as the restoration of unity. Students should resist the later temptation to view it as an empty triumph, doomed from the beginning. The conquest brought under a single government a population and a set of resources that the divided states of the previous sixty years had not been able to combine, and the following decade, sometimes called the Taikang era after the reign title, was remembered by later historians as a period of relative peace and administrative regularity.

Reunification required reintegration. Wu's officials and leading families, including intellectuals such as Lu Ji, Lu Yun, and Gu Rong, entered the Jin service, and some traveled to Luoyang, where they became known in literary and official circles. Their reception was mixed. Northern aristocrats often regarded southerners as provincial, and southern families lacked the networks that northern lineages had built in the capital. The tension between northern and southern elites would matter again after 311, when northern refugees arrived in the south. Recognizing that the south was already a society with its own great families, its own local communities, and its own political memory, rather than a blank space awaiting settlers, is one of the central lessons of the next chapter.

The Jin government's postwar policies addressed the practical challenges of ruling a larger territory. It issued land regulations conventionally described as zhantian (“occupation” or registration of fields) and ketian (allocated or taxable cultivation). Surviving descriptions set standards for landholding and cultivation according to age, sex, and official rank, while also defining household obligations in cloth, silk, and grain. The aim was to encourage cultivation, to register households, and to limit the amount of land and labor that powerful families could withdraw from the tax base, but the policy also confirmed privileges: officials, according to rank, received rights to land and to exempt dependents from taxation. Such provisions reveal the government's dependence on the very families that reduced its revenues. Historians debate how fully the policy was implemented, since surviving descriptions are brief and the concrete numbers come from later summaries.

The demobilization of armies was a second major decision. After 280, the Jin court ordered a reduction of the military forces held by provincial governors, on the reasoning that peace no longer required large standing garrisons and that the expense could be reduced. Later writers, aware of what followed, criticized this decision, since the reduction of provincial forces coincided with the growth of the princes' commands and left provincial officials with few means to defend their regions when large armies later appeared. Students should be careful with this hindsight. Emperor Wu had reasons for the policy, and it was not obviously reckless in 280. The result, however, was a military balance in which, in many regions, the principal armies were those of the princes and of a few frontier commanders.

The unresolved tensions of the reunified realm can now be listed. Aristocratic families were powerful, and their privileges limited the government's fiscal reach. Princes held titles, fiefs, and, in some cases, regional commands. Empresses' families were prominent, as the family of Emperor Wu's consort Yang Yan showed; after her death in 274, her relatives, especially her father Yang Jun, retained influence. Succession was uncertain, since the crown prince, Sima Zhong, was regarded by some contemporaries as unsuited to rule, though the surviving judgments come from histories written after his reign became a period of crisis, and students should not rely on them. Registered population and tax revenue had recovered unevenly across regions. Communities of Xiongnu, Di, Qiang, and others living within the empire's borders had grievances and military skills. Wu's recently absorbed elites had unclear positions. In the Jin court of the 280s, none of these was a crisis by itself. Together, they left the dynasty with little margin for error when its founder died. In 281, an ancient royal tomb at Ji County in the old territory of Wei was opened, reportedly by tomb robbers. Among the bamboo manuscripts subsequently recovered and studied at the Jin court was the chronicle later known as the Bamboo Annals, a reminder that the Jin court was also a center of scholarship.

Section 20.3

Aristocratic Politics and the War of the Eight Princes

Students should understand the political system before following the fighting, because the sequence of coups that began in 290 cannot be understood as a story of individual villains. Emperor Wu died in the spring of 290, leaving the throne to Sima Zhong, Emperor Hui. Real authority fell to whoever controlled the court and the palace guards. In principle, there were four kinds of power holders: regents appointed by the previous emperor, consort kin such as the Yang and, later, the Jia families, imperial princes with regional armies, and leading officials of great aristocratic families. Any one could ally with another against a third, and the emperor himself, whatever his personal abilities, functioned as a source of legitimacy whom rivals sought to control.

The conventional name, War of the Eight Princes, dates the conflict from 291 to 306, but it should not be taken literally. It was not a single war between eight men. It was a succession of coups, counter-coups, and campaigns involving different princes at different times, some of whom did not appear until the later years. The traditional list, drawn from the Book of Jin, names Sima Liang of Runan, Sima Wei of Chu, Sima Lun of Zhao, Sima Jiong of Qi, Sima Ai of Changsha, Sima Ying of Chengdu, Sima Yong of Hejian, and Sima Yue of Donghai, but many other princes, ministers, and generals took part. Historians sometimes prefer the term Disturbances of the Eight Princes for this reason.

The sequence began with a struggle over the regency. Yang Jun, the father of the late empress, took charge in 290 and alienated other members of the imperial family, including Sima Liang. In 291, the new empress, Jia Nanfeng, the daughter of the minister Jia Chong and consort of Emperor Hui, worked with the prince Sima Wei, whose troops entered Luoyang, to eliminate Yang Jun and his associates. Sima Liang and the official Wei Guan then served as regents, but Empress Jia and Sima Wei turned against them, and both were killed; Sima Wei was then eliminated as well, on the accusation that he had acted without authority. What followed, from 291 to about 299, was a period in which Empress Jia's faction, together with capable officials including Zhang Hua and Pei Wei, held the government together with relative stability. Students should note the point: the years often described simply as a disastrous rule by an empress and her family were, on the evidence of the sources themselves, years in which administration continued, and the later condemnation of her reflects the outcome of what followed.

The next stage began in 299–300, when the crown prince Sima Yu, who was not her son, was deposed and, in 300, killed, apparently at Empress Jia's initiative. The prince Sima Lun of Zhao, together with his adviser Sun Xiu, moved against her, and she was killed. In 301, Sima Lun took the throne himself, placing Emperor Hui in retirement, a step too far for other princes: Sima Jiong at Xuchang, Sima Ying at Ye, and Sima Yong at Chang'an raised armies and overthrew him, restoring Hui. The victors then quarreled. Sima Jiong held the capital until 302, when Sima Ai of Changsha, in turn, eliminated him. In 303–304, Sima Ying and Sima Yong, from their regional bases, fought Sima Ai, whose forces controlled Emperor Hui at Luoyang; Sima Ai was killed in 304. Sima Ying then controlled the court from Ye, though his position was challenged by Sima Yue of Donghai, who gathered a coalition that, in 304, marched Hui and his army to a defeat at Dangyin, north of Luoyang, and then broke apart.

The next stage brought outside forces into the struggle in a manner that the founders of Jin had not anticipated. Regional commanders in the north, including Wang Jun in the Youzhou region and Sima Teng in Bing province, recruited cavalry from Wuhuan and Xianbei communities and attacked Ye. Sima Ying took the emperor and fled toward Luoyang, and the court was then seized by forces loyal to Sima Yong and taken to Chang'an in 304. In 306, Sima Yue's coalition took Chang'an and returned Emperor Hui to Luoyang. Hui died in 307, and the throne passed to his brother, Emperor Huai, with Sima Yue the most powerful figure in the government. In the same years, Sima Ying had also, according to the sources, accepted an offer from a Xiongnu noble, Liu Yuan, to raise troops among his people in Bing province. Liu Yuan's decision to build a state of his own belongs to the next section.

What the conflict did to the state deserves more analysis than the names of the participants. The war involved repeated occupations of Luoyang and Ye, the two principal cities of the Central Plains, and the movement of large armies across the same territory. It removed much of the administrative and military leadership of the empire, including many of the princes who were supposed to defend the throne. It disrupted the collection of taxes and the maintenance of registers, since armies lived off the countryside and successive rulers issued their own appointments and demands. It taught regional commanders to build followings independent of the court. And it established that armies drawn from frontier communities were decisive in the outcomes of court struggles. These developments turned a succession crisis into a structural breakdown. Students should also note that competition for the imperial court was not itself unusual: comparable crises occurred at the end of the Han. What distinguished the Jin case was the combination of princely commands, a divided elite, and the availability of experienced frontier armies.

Section 20.4

The Yongjia Crisis and Collapse of Western Jin

The collapse of Western Jin took place in two stages, separated by five years, and the distinction is one of the chapter's most important lessons. In 311, the main capital, Luoyang, fell and the emperor was captured. In 316, the second capital, Chang'an, fell and the last emperor surrendered. The first is the destruction of the dynasty's center, and the second is the formal end of Western Jin. Historians speak of the Yongjia crisis, after the reign title Yongjia (307–313) of Emperor Huai, but the phrase should not be taken to mean that everything happened in a single year or a single battle.

New regimes were appearing before either capital fell. In 304, Liu Yuan, a Xiongnu noble who had lived in Luoyang and Ye, whose family belonged to the Southern Xiongnu communities settled in Bing province, gathered followers at Lishi, in Bing province, and proclaimed himself king of Han. The name was deliberate. Liu Yuan's family, according to the sources, had adopted the Liu surname on the basis of intermarriage with Han imperial women in the early Han, and he presented his state as the continuation of the Han dynasty, offering sacrifices to Han emperors. In 308, he took the title of emperor at Pingyang. Historians call this state Han-Zhao, a name it acquired after Liu Yuan's successor, Liu Yao, changed the dynastic name to Zhao in 319, in order to distinguish it from the earlier Han dynasties and from Cheng-Han. In the same year, 304, Li Xiong, a leader of Ba-Di refugees who had entered Sichuan in 301 under his father, Li Te, established a state in the Chengdu region, taking the title of emperor in 306; this state, which later took the name Han, is known as Cheng-Han. Its location in the southwest shows why a conventional list of the "Sixteen Kingdoms of the North" can include a polity in Sichuan.

Meanwhile, Jin authority in the north was crumbling under the pressure of famine, banditry, and the presence of independent commanders. Groups of armed followers, such as those of Shi Le, a Jie military leader who had been enslaved and then rose to command, and of Wang Mi, a Han rebel, operated across Hebei and Shandong. Sima Yue, the dominant figure in the government of Emperor Huai after 307, struggled to control these forces while suppressing rivals within the court, and in 310–311 he took a large army out of Luoyang, reportedly to confront Shi Le, leaving the capital poorly defended and Emperor Huai dissatisfied. Sima Yue died in 311, and Shi Le's forces overtook and destroyed the army escorting his funeral at Ningping, killing many princes and high officials. Meanwhile, Liu Yuan had died in 310, and his son Liu Cong, with generals including Liu Yao, Wang Mi, and Shi Le, directed the attack on Luoyang. In the summer of 311, the city fell. The palace and imperial offices were plundered, and the sources report large numbers of dead, though such figures, which reach into the tens of thousands, should be treated with caution. Emperor Huai was taken to Pingyang, where he was later killed.

The Western Jin nevertheless continued. Jin officials and commanders elsewhere refused to accept the loss. Sima Ye, a nephew of Emperor Huai, was enthroned at Chang'an in 313 as Emperor Min, with the support of loyalist officials. Liu Kun in Bing province maintained a Jin position for some years, in alliance with Tuoba Xianbei leaders who received Jin titles, and Zhang Gui in Liang province, in the northwest, governed the Hexi Corridor in the Jin name. Chang'an itself, however, was a depleted city, with a small population and scarce provisions, and it was repeatedly attacked by Han-Zhao. In late 316, Liu Yao's army entered the city, and Emperor Min surrendered; he was taken to Pingyang and killed in 318. The Western Jin dynasty, in the sense of a government ruling from the Central Plains, was finished.

The end of one dynasty did not imply the end of all Jin. In 307, a Sima prince, Sima Rui, had been sent to the lower Yangtze, where he took up residence at Jianye (renamed Jiankang in 313, to avoid the personal name of Emperor Min). After the fall of Chang'an, Sima Rui was recognized by his supporters as leader and took the title of Prince of Jin in 317; in 318, on news of the death of Emperor Min, he took the imperial title. That later Jin state, known to historians as Eastern Jin, is the subject of Chapter 21. The present chapter treats Sima Rui only as one consequence of the northern collapse.

Why did the loss of the capitals matter so much? The main reason was that the capitals, especially Luoyang, were not merely symbolic. They held the archives and registers, the imperial ritual apparatus, the great families' houses, the bureaucracy, and the principal granaries and markets of the Central Plains. Their destruction ended the practical capacity of the Jin to manage the northern provinces, and it scattered the officials and scholars who had carried its institutions. Their fall also carried heavy symbolic weight, since the capture of two successive emperors demonstrated that the dynasty's ritual center could not protect its ruler. But the effect on the north was not a simple vacuum. It was a contest among commanders, kings, and emperors, with the survival of their governments dependent on how well they could secure households, grain, and soldiers.

Section 20.5

Migration and Population Displacement

The wars and state failures of the early fourth century displaced people on a scale that contemporaries recorded and later historians have tried to measure. The most famous movement was the flight of northern families toward the Yangtze, but treating that flight as the whole story would distort what happened. Most people in North China did not leave it. Others moved within the north, to safer valleys, to walled communities, to the protection of a local commander, or to the territory of a state that offered land and security. Some went west and northwest, toward Liang province and the Hexi Corridor, where Zhang Gui's government offered relative stability. Others went northeast to Liaoxi and Liaodong, where the Murong Xianbei leaders who held Jin titles attracted refugees, and southwest into Sichuan or the Hanzhong region. Migration was multidirectional, and its causes included war, famine, forced resettlement, and opportunity.

Forced movement was also common. States that captured populations moved them to their own centers, since households and skilled workers were the resources that rulers needed. Later Zhao, for example, resettled large numbers of households around its capital at Ye, and Former Qin moved many families, including Di and Xianbei groups, to garrison newly conquered regions. Armies traveled with their families, and military households were assigned to particular locations. Captives, including women and children, were enslaved or sold, and the sources contain many references to the sale of people in years of famine. Historians have to be cautious about numbers, but the pattern of population movement, both voluntary and coerced, is well documented.

The southward flight had a particular character. It included ordinary families from Henan, Shandong, Hebei, and Guanzhong, who traveled in stages and often settled first in the Huai valley or the region north of the Yangtze, and it included aristocratic lineages that moved with kin, dependents, and armed followers, forming what contemporaries later called refugees of the "clothes and caps," that is, families with rank. Using a government record from 464 CE, the modern historian Tan Qixiang estimated that successive waves of southward migration since the Yongjia crisis had added roughly one million northerners to the southern population. The figure is a reconstruction from later registration evidence rather than a direct count and should be treated as approximate. The movement was not a single event: it continued for decades, in waves that responded to particular campaigns and famines. It was also not a settlement of an empty land. The Yangtze valley and the far south already held substantial populations, including Wu-era great families, ordinary farming households, and non-Han communities such as the Shanyue and Man in the hills, and the arrival of northerners created competition for land, office, and status. These tensions form the subject of Chapter 21.

The role of elite lineages deserves attention. The great northern families, such as the Wang of Langye and the Xie of Chenjun, carried with them their claims to office, their genealogical records, and their traditions of learning and ritual, and in the south they later became the core of a new aristocratic order. Sima Rui, the Prince of Langye who established himself at Jianye, was joined by Wang Dao and other northerners, and the network of refugee families around him helped make the southern court viable. This chapter treats those developments only as a bridge to Chapter 21. The essential point is that the movement of elite lineages was politically consequential because it transferred to the south the administrative expertise and the social prestige that had been concentrated in the northern capitals, while leaving the northern population to be governed by new regimes.

Experiences varied by class, sex, and region. A family with kin in the south, and resources to travel, could move in an organized column. A poor household, with little grain and no armed followers, was more likely to remain, to seek shelter with a local leader, or to be swept up by a passing army. Women, children, and the elderly were exposed to hunger, disease, and abduction, and many refugees died on the road. Those who stayed in the north experienced not only the destruction of war but also the arrival of new officials, new taxes, and new demands for service under whichever state came to control the region. The sources, written largely by members of families that survived and prospered, tend to highlight elite flight. The evidence for those who stayed is thinner, and reconstructing it requires attention to tomb finds, to the local defensive communities discussed in Section 20.8, and to the administrative practices of the northern states.

Section 20.6

The Sixteen Kingdoms

"Sixteen Kingdoms" is a retrospective historical label. It derives from a sixth-century work by the historian Cui Hong, who organized the histories of the northern regimes into a set of sixteen, and it conventionally covers the period from 304 to 439. It does not mean that exactly sixteen states existed. Modern scholarship identifies as many as twenty-two significant regimes in these years, ranging from large states that claimed imperial titles to smaller governments that maintained a nominal allegiance to Jin or to a more powerful neighbor. Some of them, such as the state of Dai ruled by the Tuoba, do not appear on the traditional list, and others, like Cheng-Han in Sichuan, lay outside the north. The label is useful shorthand, provided students remember what it hides: many regimes, shifting borders, and changing alliances.

Rather than memorizing a list, students will learn more by studying representative cases. The first was Han-Zhao (304–329), the state founded by Liu Yuan and continued by Liu Cong and Liu Yao, which destroyed the Western Jin capitals and controlled southern Bing province and parts of the Central Plains and Guanzhong. Its rulers used the Han imperial title, sacrificed to Han emperors, and maintained a Chinese-style civil bureaucracy at the center, while also keeping a separate structure of chanyu-style governance for tribal and military subjects. After 319, when Liu Yao adopted the name Zhao, the state fought a long war against a former subordinate, Shi Le, who destroyed it in 329 and established Later Zhao. That state (319–351) controlled much of North China. Shi Le, of Jie background, had risen from slavery through military service, and he built a state that employed Han literate advisers, established schools and a "gentlemen's camp" for educated men, and patronized the Buddhist teacher Fotudeng, while retaining a Jie-led military core. After Shi Le's death in 333, his nephew Shi Hu ruled with great violence and extensive levies, and after Shi Hu died in 349 the state collapsed amid succession wars and a massacre of "Hu" people ordered in 350 by the general Ran Min, a Han commander who briefly ruled the short-lived state of Ran Wei. Accounts of the massacre report very large death tolls, figures that historians treat with caution, but the episode shows how ethnic labels could be turned into weapons in the aftermath of a state's failure.

In the northeast, the Murong Xianbei, whose leaders had held Jin titles in Liaoxi, built the state of Former Yan (337–370). Under Murong Huang, and later Murong Jun, who took the imperial title in 352, Former Yan attracted refugees from the Central Plains, drew on the skills of Han officials, and in 350–352 took over Ye and much of the Hebei plain. In the west, the state of Former Liang (301–376) was established by Zhang Gui and his successors in the Hexi Corridor, a Han-descended family that maintained nominal allegiance to Jin and continued to use its calendar for decades, illustrating a common form of "nominal allegiance": recognition of a distant court that gave a local government legitimacy without any real subordination. In the southwest, Cheng-Han (304–347) was conquered by the Eastern Jin general Huan Wen in 347, a campaign that belongs to Chapter 21.

The most ambitious northern state was Former Qin (351–394), founded by a Di leader of the Fu family. Under Fu Jian (r. 357–385), and with the advice of the Han minister Wang Meng, Former Qin promoted agriculture, irrigation, and schools, and in a series of campaigns conquered Former Yan in 370, and Former Liang and the state of Dai in 376, bringing most of North China under one government for the first time since 316. Fu Jian resettled large numbers of households, including Di and Xianbei, to hold newly conquered regions. In 383, he led a large army against the Eastern Jin and was defeated at the Fei River, a battle whose Eastern Jin side belongs to Chapter 21. Former Qin's collapse followed quickly. Fu Jian's tributary or subordinate leaders reasserted independence: the Murong Xianbei founded Later Yan and Western Yan, the Qiang leader Yao Chang killed Fu Jian in 385 and founded Later Qin, and other states emerged, including Western Qin, Later Liang, Southern Liang, Northern Liang, and Southern Yan. The Xiongnu leader Helian Bobo established Xia in 407, and the Han-descended Feng Ba founded Northern Yan. In 386, the Tuoba leader Tuoba Gui, reviving the state of Dai, adopted the name Wei, the beginning of the Northern Wei. In 439, its emperor Taiwu conquered the last major rival, Northern Liang in the Hexi Corridor, the date that conventionally ends the era of the Sixteen Kingdoms and marks the endpoint of this chapter's chronology. Its explanation belongs to Chapter 22.

Several features of these states deserve emphasis. First, they were unstable. Most lasted for one or two generations, and their fortunes depended on the ability of the ruler to hold together coalitions of military commanders, whose loyalty was often personal. Second, they shared a set of political practices: rulers adopted imperial or royal titles, appointed ministers according to Chinese models, and often took great care to obtain the services of literate men from prominent Han families, yet they also relied on cavalry forces and on tribal or military leaders whose authority derived from other traditions. Third, alliances changed: states that allied against one power fought each other a decade later, and marriage alliances, hostages, and shifting titles were the instruments of diplomacy. Fourth, the states also interacted with the south, whether by fighting Eastern Jin expeditions, exchanging envoys, or receiving refugees. The period is a real multi-state system, and students should think of it as such, rather than as a waiting room for the Northern Wei.

Section 20.7

Inner Asian Peoples and Northern State Formation

This section addresses a question of both terminology and method. The names Xiongnu, Jie, Xianbei, Di, and Qiang, later grouped by writers as the "Five Hu," identify communities that the sources associated with the founders of northern states. It would be a serious error to read these names as five biologically fixed peoples, or as a single coalition that acted together against the Jin. They were different communities, with different histories, languages, economies, and political traditions, and they did not act in concert. Even within each name there was diversity, and the boundaries between groups shifted through intermarriage, common military service, and political alliance.

Historical Vocabulary
Hu (胡) — and "Five Hu" (Wu Hu, 五胡)

In early Chinese texts, hu referred to peoples of the north and northwest, and by the Han period it was applied broadly to nomadic or semi-nomadic neighbors, and later also to people from Central Asia. It was a term of outsiders, used by Chinese writers, and its meaning varied with context and with the author's attitude. "Five Hu" is a later grouping of Xiongnu, Jie, Xianbei, Di, and Qiang, used by historians writing after the events. Students should use both terms with care: they tell us how compilers classified people, which is evidence about compilers, and they cannot be treated as objective descriptions of the communities they name.

The Xiongnu of this period were mainly the descendants of the Southern Xiongnu who had submitted to the Han in the first century and settled in Bing province and neighboring regions. They kept some of the organization of their leading lineages, including the title of chanyu for a ruler, but they lived in a landscape of farms and towns and were subject to Chinese officials. The Jie, who included Shi Le's family, are usually described as a group associated with the Xiongnu confederation in the Shangdang region of Bing province, and their origins are debated; some scholars suggest a link to communities from farther west, but the evidence is thin. The Xianbei, in contrast, comprised many separate groups. The Murong, Duan, and Yuwen lived in Liaoxi and the northeast, the Tuoba lived farther west along the steppe edge in the region of Dai, and later groups such as the Qifu and Tufa appeared in the west. Di and Qiang communities lived in the Guanzhong, Longyou, and Sichuan regions, and included clans such as the Fu and Lü among the Di and the Yao among the Qiang. Many were farmers, herders, or both, and some served for generations in imperial armies.

The most important point is that these communities did not arrive suddenly from outside. Many had lived within Han, Wei, and Jin territory for generations, serving as soldiers, farming as tenants, trading, marrying, and learning Chinese. Liu Yuan grew up in Luoyang and, according to the sources, received a classical education. Shi Le was a slave sold by Jin authorities and later an independent commander. Xianbei and Wuhuan cavalry fought on both sides of the Eight Princes' conflicts. The Tuoba leader Tuoba Yilu received a Jin title, Duke and later Prince of Dai, for his support against Han-Zhao. Frontier commanders such as Wang Jun recruited Xianbei to fight against other Jin commanders. The northern states came into being through the interaction of Jin political breakdown with the military capacities and political ambitions of communities that had already been incorporated into the empire's armies and economies. "Foreign barbarians suddenly invaded China" is therefore not an adequate explanation of the collapse. Many leaders whom later Chinese histories classified as foreign or non-Han had long-standing political, military, economic, and social ties within Han, Wei, or Jin territory.

These states were creative political constructions. Their rulers drew on Chinese imperial titles and bureaucratic forms, since the government of a large agrarian population required registers, tax collection, and civil officials, and educated Han men were essential. They also used cavalry organizations, hereditary military households, and, in some cases, distinct legal and administrative structures for members of the ruling group. In Later Zhao, the ruling Jie and other "state people" (guoren) held a privileged position, and the state maintained separate institutions for Han and non-Han subjects. Han-Zhao used chanyu-style offices alongside a Han-style bureaucracy. Rulers legitimated themselves in multiple ways at once: Liu Yuan through Han ancestry, Shi Le through Buddhist and Confucian patrons, Fu Jian through Confucian learning and claims of universal rule, and the Tuoba through both steppe and Chinese titles. Sometimes the mixture caused tension, as when literate Han officials complained of the influence of military commanders, or when soldiers resented the privileges of civil elites. It nevertheless produced a genuinely new political culture, whose consequences later chapters trace.

Students should also avoid describing the successor governments as purely "Chinese" or purely "nomadic." Ruling groups were multiethnic and multilingual, and identities were malleable: individuals could be described as Xiongnu in one source and as Han in another, families adopted Chinese surnames, and new identities such as the "Xianbei" of the northern states were themselves partly political creations. The concept of identity as a matter of political allegiance, language, dress, and descent, which shifted with circumstances, is one of the principal ideas of Chapter 23. Here it is enough to insist on the methodological point: names in the sources are evidence for how contemporaries and later writers sorted people, and historians must reconstruct the actual communities from many kinds of evidence at once.

Section 20.8

Warfare, Agriculture, and Social Reconstruction

Warfare in the early fourth century destroyed, but it did not empty the north. The Jin shu and other sources describe famine, epidemics, and the burning of cities and granaries, and archaeologists find evidence of abandoned settlements and reduced population in some regions. Irrigation works fell into disrepair, markets contracted, and the registers on which taxation depended were lost or became meaningless. Coins were scarce, and cloth and grain served more often as media of exchange. Some historians link the disasters of the period to climatic stress, including cooler and drier conditions in parts of the north, but the evidence is debated and cannot substitute for political explanation. Any account that stresses destruction alone, however, distorts the period. Reconstruction was as important as destruction, and it was carried out at several levels at once.

Historical Vocabulary
Wubi (塢壁) — fortified settlement

Wubi refers to a walled or fortified local community, often organized around a leading lineage or a local strongman, that provided defense, storage, and a measure of order when state authority failed. Such communities had appeared in the late Han, and they multiplied in the fourth century. Their inhabitants included kin, dependents, neighbors, and refugees who accepted the leader's authority in return for protection. Historians use the term to describe the local basis on which many northern communities survived, and to explain how new states obtained cooperation from local leaders by offering titles in exchange for tax and military service.

The first level was local. When state protection failed, families and neighbors built fortified settlements, stored grain, and organized armed defense under the direction of local leaders, some of whom held official titles from whichever government was nearest. The biographies in the Book of Jin record leaders who gathered kin and neighbors into defended communities and negotiated, according to circumstances, with Jin loyalists, with Han-Zhao, or with Later Zhao. Such communities often prevented complete social collapse, but they also concentrated power in the hands of local strongmen, who could become the partners or rivals of the new states. New governments therefore had to deal with local elites, granting titles and confirming authority in exchange for taxes, grain, and soldiers.

The second level was that of the states themselves, which competed for taxable households and for skilled workers, as households were the basic resource of any regime. They resettled farmers, moved soldiers and their families to garrisons and agricultural districts, and established officials to promote cultivation. Former Yan, in Liaoxi, drew refugees and had to decide how to assign land and tenancy arrangements to them. Former Qin under Fu Jian and Wang Meng emphasized agriculture and irrigation in Guanzhong. Later Zhao sent officials to encourage farming while also imposing heavy levies for military campaigns and building projects. The states also maintained household registration, since registers were the tool that made taxation and conscription possible, and the practice continued across changes of regime, providing a thread of administrative continuity. In these processes, states borrowed freely from one another and from the Han and Wei traditions that educated advisers remembered.

The economy of the north was also altered. Herding and mixed agrarian-pastoral economies became more prominent in some regions, especially along the northern edge and in the territories of the Tuoba, where livestock, horses, and hides were important, and where captured populations were sometimes assigned to herds or to farms. Horses were valuable for warfare and for communication, and the demand for them shaped both military organization and trade with steppe communities. Mixed economies did not mean a decline of farming: much of the Central Plains, Guanzhong, and Hebei remained agricultural, and rulers, even those from pastoral backgrounds, depended on grain taxes. But the presence of significant herds and cavalry shaped the political and military structure of the northern states.

Religion and learning also continued. Buddhist monks and translators traveled among the courts and, as the sources indicate, found patrons among the northern rulers: Fotudeng in Later Zhao, Dao'an in the Former Qin territories, and, at the end of the period, the translator Kumarajiva, brought to Chang'an by the Later Qin in 401. Confucian schools and classical learning were preserved in some courts and by refugee scholars, and Daoist practitioners and local cults continued in communities that lacked any central authority. The fuller history of these developments belongs to Chapter 23. What matters here is that cultural life did not end with the fall of Luoyang: it moved, was reorganized, and found new patrons, some of whom were themselves products of a mixed and shifting society.

Section 20.9

The Emergence of a Divided North and South

The collapse of Western Jin produced a durable political distinction between northern and southern regimes, which lasted, with interruptions, until the Sui reunification in 589 (Chapter 24). The division was real. After 317–318, a Jin court sat at Jiankang, claiming continuity with the Western Jin and an obligation to recover the north, while in the north a succession of states, of the kind described in Sections 20.6 and 20.7, competed for control. The two areas developed different political cultures, different military systems, different relationships with Inner Asia, and different patterns of aristocratic society. Students should nevertheless be careful not to describe the distinction as an impermeable border between two homogeneous civilizations.

The line between the two systems moved. In the early decades, the Jin court at Jiankang held the Yangtze and the region south of the Huai, and its generals conducted campaigns northward, including Zu Ti's efforts in the 310s and 320s, Huan Wen's expeditions in the 350s and 360s, and the conquest of Cheng-Han in Sichuan in 347. After the Fei River in 383, Eastern Jin forces temporarily advanced into parts of Henan. In 416–417, the general Liu Yu took Luoyang and Chang'an from Later Qin, though the gains were lost soon afterward. Communities near the frontier, including the fortified settlements of the Huai and Han valleys, changed allegiance repeatedly, and trade, diplomacy, and the movement of individuals continued across the line. The north-south division was a zone of interaction as well as conflict.

The states on each side also made competing claims of legitimacy. The Jin court in the south presented itself as the true continuation of the imperial line, custodian of the Han-Wei-Jin heritage, and often described the north as occupied by non-Chinese usurpers. Northern rulers, on their side, cited their own titles, their possession of the Central Plains and the old capitals, their patronage of Confucian and Buddhist learning, and, in some cases, their descent from Han imperial families. Modern students should treat both sets of claims as political arguments and should avoid adopting either as a neutral description. The terminology of "Han" and "barbarian," frequent in southern writings, reflects the outlook of a court whose authority depended on the claim to continuity, and the narrative of southern refuge should be read with that in mind.

Differences developed in society and economy, as well. In the south, an aristocracy of refugee and local families, organized around the court at Jiankang, governed a region of expanding agriculture and commerce, and the Yangtze valley acquired the demographic and economic weight that made it central to later Chinese history. In the north, military households, mixed populations, fortified communities, and rulers who combined Chinese and Inner Asian traditions created the institutions that eventually shaped the Northern Wei, the Northern Zhou, and the Sui-Tang synthesis. The two societies were not sealed off from each other. They shared a script, a classical heritage, a Buddhist religious world, and, increasingly, a memory of a lost unity. Historians now recognize that later imperial unification drew on both.

This section should therefore end by fixing the new geography without narrating either successor system in detail. To the south stood the Eastern Jin, whose politics, aristocracy, and economic development are examined in Chapter 21, and, after it, the Southern Dynasties. To the north, competing states gave way to the consolidation carried out by the Northern Wei, which took Pingcheng as its capital, unified the north in 439, and eventually moved to Luoyang, as Chapter 22 will show. Between them lay a shifting frontier, and beyond them lay the Inner Asian societies whose political and military traditions helped shape the north. The collapse of the Western Jin, in short, destroyed an imperial center, but it also created a new political landscape from which later regimes would draw, and the next chapter turns to the south.

Section 20.10

Chapter Review and Western Jin and Sixteen Kingdoms Checkpoint

Students should be able to explain the following causal sequence: the Sima takeover of Wei (249–265); the founding of Jin (265–266); reunification in 280; princely and aristocratic competition after 290; civil war between 291 and 306; the weakening of central authority; the appearance of new northern states from 304; the fall of Luoyang in 311 and Chang'an in 316; migration and local reconstruction; and the emergence of a durable, though permeable, north-south division. Each link should be explained, not merely listed, and students should be prepared to say why each step made the next possible without making it inevitable.

The checkpoint also requires students to reject two monocausal explanations. It is not sufficient to say that the Eight Princes alone destroyed the Jin: the princes' wars were made possible by structures the Jin itself had built, and the dynasty's fall depended on the actions of commanders outside the imperial family. Nor is it sufficient to say that barbarian invasion destroyed the Jin: the new states were created by people who had lived within the Jin system and who found opportunity in its breakdown, and the collapse of the court preceded the fall of the capitals. A satisfactory explanation combines institutional design, elite competition, regional militarization, fiscal disruption, migration, and the political choices of many actors.

Primary Source Window

Jiang Tong, Discourse on Moving the Rong (Xi Rong lun), c. 299

Creator: Jiang Tong, a Jin official · Date: c. 299 · Type: Political essay or memorial preserved in later historical works, including his biography in the Book of Jin and the Zizhi tongjian

Context: After a revolt by Di and Qiang communities in Guanzhong between 296 and 299, Jiang Tong argued that such populations should be removed from the interior and returned to regions he considered their proper homes. His proposal was not adopted. The text is preserved through later transmission, and the version students read is a later copy, not an original manuscript. The essay is valuable as an example of elite reasoning before the collapse of the Western Jin.

Paraphrase (not a translated quotation):

Jiang Tong argues that communities of Di, Qiang, and other peoples who have settled within the empire's interior are a danger, that their numbers have grown, that officials have exploited them and provoked revolt, and that the government should move them out of the interior provinces while it still can. He appeals to the example of earlier rulers, and he portrays the peoples he names in hostile and stereotyped terms.

Analysis: The essay is significant because it shows that elite writers in Jin recognized demographic and political change along the frontier before the crisis, and that some saw it as a threat. Its stereotypes reflect the author's prejudice, not observation, and the textbook paraphrases rather than reproduces the insulting language the original uses. The essay tells us little about the actual customs, motivations, or grievances of the communities it describes; it does not include their own voices, and its account of their character is an argument, not ethnography. It also reflects the author's own political purposes, since arguments about frontier policy were also arguments about the competence of officials. Its argument that the communities had grown numerous and had been mistreated by officials actually suggests that policies and local abuses, rather than any fixed character, contributed to unrest.

Historical Debate

Why Did Western Jin Collapse?

The question: Historians agree that Western Jin lost the north between 291 and 316. They disagree about which factors were decisive.

Interpretation A — Structure of Sima enfeoffment and princely power: The dynasty's design, in which princes held fiefs and regional commands, made civil war possible, and provincial demobilization left few counterweights.
Interpretation B — Court succession and aristocratic factionalism: The crisis came from rivalry among regents, consort families, and great officials over control of an emperor who could not govern independently, and princes acted within that struggle.
Interpretation C — Fiscal disruption, displacement, and autonomous military leaders: Long wars, famine, and the collapse of registration created conditions in which commanders and local leaders operated independently of any court.
Interpretation D — The role of the new states and their political communities: The capacity of Xiongnu, Di, Xianbei, and mixed communities to form governments determined the outcome and shaped what followed.

Evidence: Each view draws on the same narrative sources, notably the Book of Jin, supplemented by biographies, memorials, and archaeological evidence for regional conditions. They differ in emphasis on institutions, politics, economy, or state formation.

Points of disagreement: Historians differ over how much causal weight to give the Jin princely system itself as opposed to contingent court struggles after 290; how seriously the demobilization of provincial forces weakened resistance; whether fiscal and demographic stresses were already severe enough to threaten the dynasty before the civil wars; and whether the new northern states should be understood chiefly as consequences of Jin collapse or as independent political actors that helped produce the new order.

Why it matters: How students explain the collapse determines whether they see it as a failure of design, of politics, of resources, or as the beginning of new state formation. The explanations need not be mutually exclusive.

Student task: Combine at least two interpretations into a coherent argument, and identify what additional evidence would help decide which mattered most.

Multiple Perspectives

Living Through Western Jin's Collapse

Note: these are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns in the evidence, not quotations from named individuals.

A retainer of a Sima prince: His fortunes rose and fell with his patron; victory could mean office, and defeat execution, and his loyalty was tied to a household rather than the dynasty.
A Luoyang court official: He served regents who changed repeatedly and faced accusations after each coup; the capital, his archives, and his family's standing were at risk when armies entered the city.
A northern farming household: Conscription, requisitions, and famine threatened survival, and its choices, to stay, flee, or seek protection, depended on grain, kin, and the proximity of armies.
A Xiongnu or Di military family already resident in Jin territory: Long service in imperial armies, local exploitation, and the opportunities created by the civil wars shaped its decisions, and its members could be both victims of suspicion and agents of new states.
A refugee lineage considering migration south: It weighed the cost of leaving land and graves against war and hunger, and its resources, kin, and connections determined whether it could move as an organized column.
A family sheltering in a fortified settlement: It traded labor and loyalty to a local leader for defense, and it negotiated with whichever state controlled the surrounding region. Its power to shape events was small compared with that of commanders and rulers, and the sources rarely record its voice.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Reunified Jin to a Multi-State North

What Changed

  • Dynastic authority ended in the north, and imperial capitals were destroyed or lost
  • Political geography shifted from a single empire to several competing states and a southern court
  • Military power passed to princes, regional commanders, and leaders of frontier communities
  • Populations moved, with refugee lineages settling in the south and captives resettled in the north
  • Ruling coalitions became multiethnic and mixed Chinese and Inner Asian political forms

What Stayed the Same

  • Agriculture remained the fiscal foundation of the states
  • Counties and commanderies continued as units of administration
  • Chinese writing, court ritual, and imperial titles were adopted by new rulers
  • Household registration remained a goal of every state that could attempt it
  • Aristocratic families kept their standing in many regions

Who experienced what: Officials and princes at the capitals experienced sharp discontinuity; many rural households experienced changes of masters more than of daily routine, but with heavier demands. Timing: gradual stress in the late third century, acceleration in 291–306, crisis in 304–316, and reconstruction across the following century. Why it matters: the pattern shows that dynastic collapse and social disappearance are different things.

Cause and Effect

Why Did the Reunification of 280 Fail?

Long-term causes

The Sima political structure, with princely enfeoffment and regional commands; powerful aristocratic families; uneven regional recovery; frontier communities inside the empire's armies.

Short-term causes

The succession after 290: a ruler dependent on regents, competing consort families, and princes with armies.

Trigger and context

The early coups after Emperor Wu's death (290–291), which established the pattern of using armies to control the court.

Event or transformation

The War of the Eight Princes (291–306), a sequence of coups and civil wars across the Central Plains and beyond.

Immediate effects

Devastation of Luoyang, Ye, and their regions; competing armies; loss of revenue and registers; recruitment of frontier cavalry.

Long-term consequences

The weakening of Jin; autonomous regimes from 304; the fall of the capitals in 311 and 316; migration; a durable north-south division.

Historical complexity

The collapse resulted from interacting internal and regional processes, not from a single ethnic or military cause. Institutions designed to protect the dynasty helped destroy it, and outsiders to the court, many of them long resident within the empire, turned the crisis into new state formation.
Everyday Life and Social History

Behind the Walls of a Northern Fortified Settlement

No source describes a single named fortified community in complete detail, so this account is a cautious reconstruction from scattered evidence in biographies, histories, and archaeological remains. A settlement of this kind was organized around a walled compound, with towers and a gate, that enclosed houses, wells, and granaries. Its inhabitants were principally members of a leading kin group, together with dependents, tenants, and neighbors who had sought shelter. Stored grain was the settlement's most important asset, and its management was a source of the leader's authority.

Daily life combined farming with defense. Members went out to cultivate fields near the walls when conditions permitted and retreated when raiders or armies approached. Some carried arms, and the settlement's leader could call on them for defense. Markets were damaged and coin was scarce, so exchange took the form of barter and the exchange of cloth and grain. The settlement depended on its leader, who negotiated with whichever state or commander controlled the surrounding area, and the terms of that negotiation, whether payment of grain, supply of soldiers, or acceptance of an official title, decided the community's fate.

The costs were real. Dependents owed labor and obedience, and those who had joined for protection had few options if the leader demanded more. Women and children managed households under uncertain conditions, and the sick and old were vulnerable to shortages. Yet such communities also preserved family ties, ritual practices, and local knowledge, and they served as a foundation from which later governments rebuilt administration. Students should remember that the sources speak mostly through leaders, and that ordinary members of these settlements are visible only indirectly.

Regional and Frontier Context

Regions Experienced the Collapse Differently

The Central Plains and Hebei suffered repeated occupations and the destruction of Luoyang and Ye. Guanzhong faced the Di and Qiang revolt of 296–299, the conflicts around Chang'an, and, after 316, competition among Han-Zhao and successors. Bing province was the base of Liu Yuan's Han and the site of the Jin loyalist effort under Liu Kun. Liaoxi and Liaodong saw the rise of the Murong Xianbei and became refuges. The Hexi Corridor was governed by the Zhang family under nominal Jin allegiance. Sichuan was the base of Cheng-Han. The lower Yangtze received refugees and became the seat of Eastern Jin. Students should avoid treating the capital region as representative of all others.

Frontier zones were places of interaction as much as conflict: Xiongnu, Xianbei, Di, Qiang, and Han communities traded, intermarried, served in one another's armies, and shifted allegiance. Their history should be read from their own political circumstances as well as from the viewpoint of Jin officials.

China and the Wider World

Inner Asia, Central Asia, and Buddhism

The northern states were part of a wider Eurasian zone. Their cavalry traditions and titles drew on steppe practice, their courts hosted Central Asian and Indian monks, and their armies and merchants participated in exchange networks connected to the Silk Roads. Zhu Fahu (Dharmaraksa), active in the Western Jin period, worked as a translator, and Fotudeng, Dao'an, and Kumarajiva worked under northern patronage. These processes were active choices by rulers, monks, and traders, not events done to China. The rise of the Tuoba and other Xianbei groups on the steppe edge belongs to a wider pattern in which pastoral and mixed societies built states in interaction with agrarian neighbors, a comparison that historians draw carefully, since each case had its own local causes.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how a dynasty that reunified the former Han world in 280 lost the north within a generation, and how that loss produced new multiethnic states rather than simple chaos. Its central argument is that Western Jin reunification was real but fragile. The Sima family inherited institutions, armies, aristocratic networks, and regional inequalities from the Three Kingdoms, and its choices, especially the strengthening of imperial princes through titles and regional commands, created new risks.

The Sima rise was a process, not an event. It began with Sima Yi's seizure of the Wei court in 249, continued through Sima Shi and Sima Zhao, included the Wei conquest of Shu in 263, and culminated in Sima Yan's founding of Jin in 265–266. Sima Yi did not found Jin. The dynasty's genuine accomplishment came in 280, when the conquest of Wu completed reunification, and the following decade brought administrative regularity, a new legal code, and land and tax policies.

The same settlement contained unresolved tensions: powerful aristocratic families, princes with regional resources, consort families, uneven recovery, and armies of frontier communities. After Emperor Wu's death in 290, competition among regents, consort kin, and princes produced the sequence of coups and civil wars conventionally called the War of the Eight Princes (291–306). It was not eight men fighting simultaneously, and it should not be reduced to the personality of any one figure, including Empress Jia, whose years of influence were also years of administrative continuity.

The wars devastated the Central Plains, drained central fiscal and military capacity, and drew frontier cavalry into court struggles. New regimes, including Han-Zhao and Cheng-Han, appeared in 304, while Western Jin still existed. In 311, Luoyang fell and Emperor Huai was captured; in 316, Chang'an fell and Emperor Min surrendered, ending Western Jin. The distinction between the two dates is essential, and the Yongjia crisis is best understood as a multi-year process.

Migration and displacement followed. Some lineages and many families moved south toward the Yangtze, but most people remained in the north, moved within it, or moved west and northeast. Forced resettlement by states, captivity, and famine were common. Southward migration was not the settlement of an empty land, since the south already held substantial populations and elites, and the arrival of northern refugees prepared the conflicts and accommodations of Chapter 21.

The political world of North China between 304 and 439 is called the Sixteen Kingdoms, a retrospective label that hides at least twenty-two significant regimes. Representative states, such as Han-Zhao, Later Zhao, Former Yan, Former Qin, and Cheng-Han, combined Chinese imperial titles and bureaucracies with military organizations rooted in Inner Asian traditions. Their rulers came from Xiongnu, Jie, Xianbei, Di, Qiang, and Han-descended backgrounds, groups that were not a single coalition and whose identities were malleable. Many had lived within imperial territory for generations, so the "barbarian invasion" explanation cannot stand.

Reconstruction was as important as destruction. Local leaders organized fortified settlements, states resettled farmers and soldiers and competed for taxable households, herding and mixed economies grew in some regions, and Buddhist monks and classical scholars found new patrons. Administrative practices, such as household registration, counties and commanderies, and imperial titles, continued. The collapse of Western Jin destroyed an imperial center but not political and social life in the north.

By the fifth century, the north and south had become distinct political systems, though not impermeable ones. The Eastern Jin and the Southern Dynasties developed at Jiankang (Chapter 21), and the Northern Wei consolidated the north by 439 (Chapter 22). Chapter 23 will treat the broader transformations in religion, migration, and culture. Together they show that the age of division was a creative period in which political fragmentation reorganized Chinese society and government rather than merely interrupting a continuous story of unity.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a reunified empire can fail without disappearing into disorder. The Western Jin story is not one of inevitable decline: it was shaped by choices about princes, armies, succession, and registration, made by people with reasonable aims and limited information. Understanding those choices helps students see dynastic history as a matter of institutions and decisions rather than fate.

The chapter also shaped later Chinese history. The northern states created political forms that combined imperial and Inner Asian traditions, and those forms fed directly into the Northern Wei, the Northern Zhou, the Sui, and the Tang. The southward movement of elite lineages helped build the southern courts and the economic weight of the Yangtze valley. The very idea of a divided north and south, and the ambition to reunite them, became lasting features of Chinese political thought.

The chapter connects Chinese history to Asian and global history. Northern rulers governed societies that linked farmland, steppe, oases, and mountain frontiers, and their courts received Buddhist monks from Central Asia and India. Their world belonged to a Eurasian era in which agrarian empires and mobile societies transformed each other, and it reminds students that Chinese history cannot be told from the Central Plains alone.

Finally, the chapter helps students understand Chinese history as diverse, contested, and unfinished. The people of the fourth century did not know that a later unification would come, and they did not think of themselves as living in an interlude. They built states, defended communities, wrote memorials, translated scriptures, and moved their families, and their choices created the world that Chapters 21 through 23 will describe.
