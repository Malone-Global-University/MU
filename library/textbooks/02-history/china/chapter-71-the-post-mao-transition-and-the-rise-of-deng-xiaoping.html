Part XII — Reform and Contemporary China
71

The Post-Mao Transition and the Rise of Deng Xiaoping

China: A History of a Civilization · Principal chronology 1976–1979, with retrospective coverage of 1980–1981

Chapter Opening

Chapter purpose: This chapter explains how China moved from the political and institutional upheaval of the Cultural Revolution toward a new governing strategy that emphasized economic modernization, institutional reconstruction, practical experimentation, and engagement with the outside world, and why that transition produced economic experimentation without an equivalent movement toward political pluralism.

On the evening of 6 October 1976, less than a month after Mao Zedong's death, a small group of senior leaders and security officers carried out an operation in Beijing that few ordinary citizens knew about until days afterward. Four of the most prominent figures of the Cultural Revolution (Jiang Qing, Zhang Chunqiao, Wang Hongwen, and Yao Wenyuan) were taken into custody. There was no election, no public vote, and no open debate. Yet when the news spread, many Chinese people celebrated in the streets. In cities across the country, crowds marched, drank, and set off firecrackers. The celebration reflected less any clear vision of what would come next than relief that a decade of campaigns, factional violence, and political fear might be ending.

What came next was neither quick nor predetermined. The arrest of the "Gang of Four" removed the most visible radical leaders, but it did not by itself create the reform system later associated with Deng Xiaoping. The new Party chairman, Hua Guofeng, had risen under Mao and relied on Mao's legitimacy. Deng Xiaoping, twice purged, was in 1976 an official in disgrace. Between 1976 and 1979 these leaders, together with veteran officials such as Ye Jianying and Chen Yun and with younger figures such as Hu Yaobang, worked out a political settlement through disputes over history, ideology, personnel, education, economics, and law. This chapter studies that process as a gradual and contested transition rather than as a single moment of rupture.

The chapter distinguishes three processes that moved at different speeds. The first was the removal of the Cultural Revolution's radical leadership, which happened in weeks. The second was the rehabilitation and reorganization of the Communist Party and state, which took years and affected millions of people. The third was the emergence of a new economic development strategy, which began with debates and local experiments in 1977–1979 and developed into the national reforms of the 1980s treated in Chapter 72. Keeping these processes apart helps students avoid a common error: assuming that the fall of the Gang of Four, Deng's return, and the beginning of market-oriented reform were one event.

The central turning point was the Third Plenary Session of the Eleventh Central Committee, held from 18 to 22 December 1978. Its communiqué redirected the Party's main work toward socialist modernization and set in motion changes in agricultural policy, Party organization, and legal development. But the plenum did not transfer all power to Deng, and it did not create a market economy. Hua Guofeng still formally chaired the Party. Many reforms were adopted only in outline, and their implementation took years. Students should therefore treat 1978 as a decisive redirection of priorities, not the completion of reform.

The transition also had a human dimension that official documents capture only partly. Former students who had spent years in the countryside prepared for a restored college entrance examination. Officials who had been denounced, imprisoned, or sent to labor returned to office, or died before their names were cleared. Families of the persecuted tried to reclaim reputations and housing. Villagers in provinces such as Anhui and Sichuan experimented with farming arrangements that ran against the logic of the collective. Intellectuals debated what Mao's legacy implied for policy. These changes affected urban and rural people, men and women, officials and non-officials, and communities with very different degrees of access to the centers of power.

The chapter also asks why political and economic change diverged. The Party leadership that corrected many Mao-era injustices also affirmed in March 1979 that China must uphold the socialist road, the people's democratic dictatorship, the leadership of the Communist Party, and Marxism-Leninism-Mao Zedong Thought. The same leadership that encouraged experiment in the countryside and the opening to foreign technology imprisoned Democracy Wall activist Wei Jingsheng. Historians interpret this pairing in different ways. Some stress the leadership's commitment to preserving Party rule after a decade of chaos; others stress the practical constraints and competing views within the leadership itself.

Historians draw on a wide range of evidence for this period: Party and state documents, speeches and communiqués, newspaper editorials, memoirs of participants, oral histories, archival materials gradually available to researchers, statistical records, local gazetteers, unofficial journals, and later official historical resolutions. Each carries its own limits. Official documents show what the leadership chose to announce, not necessarily how decisions were made. Memoirs are often written decades later, shaped by hindsight and by later political positions. The chapter therefore asks students to compare sources, to note who produced them and when, and to distinguish contemporary events from later interpretations, especially the Party's 1981 historical resolution. The chapter also connects China's domestic transition to wider Asian and global developments, including the normalization of relations with the United States in 1979, relations with Japan, the Soviet-Vietnamese alignment, and the search for foreign technology and capital.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how the arrest of the Gang of Four in October 1976 changed the leadership of the People's Republic without immediately creating a new governing consensus.
  2. Distinguish among a change of leaders, a change in political legitimacy, institutional rehabilitation, and the beginning of economic reform.
  3. Analyze Hua Guofeng's program and the "Two Whatevers" formula, and evaluate competing interpretations of Hua's role in the transition.
  4. Trace Deng Xiaoping's rehabilitation in 1977 and his gradual rise to practical influence, explaining why his rise was not an automatic succession to Mao.
  5. Assess the social and intellectual significance of the restored college entrance examination and the rehabilitation of expertise.
  6. Interpret the 1978 debate on "practice as the sole criterion of truth" as both an intellectual argument and a political contest.
  7. Explain what the Third Plenum of December 1978 decided, what it did not decide, and why it is regarded as a turning point.
  8. Evaluate the process and limits of political rehabilitation, including its effects on officials, intellectuals, families, and former sent-down youth.
  9. Distinguish the Four Modernizations (development priorities) from the Four Cardinal Principles (political limits).
  10. Describe early rural experiments and the first steps of external opening, separating local experiments from the national policies of Chapter 72.
  11. Compare contemporary criticism of the Cultural Revolution in 1976–1979 with the retrospective 1981 Party resolution.
  12. Contextualize the transition within Cold War diplomacy and Asia's regional politics.

Historical Context

To understand the transition, students need the political and economic situation inherited from the Mao era (Chapters 66–70). After 1949 the People's Republic had built a Party-state with a planned economy, collectivized agriculture, urban work units, and a powerful ideology centered on class struggle and the thought of Mao Zedong. Industrialization had produced real gains in heavy industry, basic education, and public health. But campaigns such as the Anti-Rightist Campaign (1957), the Great Leap Forward and the famine that followed (Chapter 68), and the Cultural Revolution (Chapter 70) had killed or persecuted many people and disrupted institutions. The Cultural Revolution in particular shut or distorted schools and universities, removed large numbers of officials, divided communities into factions, and left the Party and state organization damaged. Estimates of the numbers killed and persecuted vary and remain debated; scholars agree only that the toll was very large.

The political situation in 1976 was unusually unstable. Premier Zhou Enlai died in January 1976, and Zhu De died in July. In April 1976 mourning for Zhou in Tiananmen Square turned into a mass gathering that authorities treated as a counter-revolutionary incident; Deng Xiaoping, who had been restored to authority in 1973 and had led a program of rectification in 1975, was dismissed from his posts, and Hua Guofeng, previously a lesser-known security and provincial official, was named premier. In July the Tangshan earthquake killed hundreds of thousands of people and damaged an important industrial city. Mao died on 9 September 1976. The country therefore faced succession, disaster recovery, and an unresolved dispute over the direction of the revolution all at the same time.

Economically, China in 1976 was poor by international standards. Agriculture was organized in people's communes, production brigades, and production teams, and farmers' incomes were low in many regions. The state procured grain at fixed prices and prioritized heavy industry. Technology imports had resumed on a limited scale in the early 1970s, following the shift in foreign policy that produced the 1972 meeting between Mao and Richard Nixon (Chapter 70). But the economy had little exposure to foreign investment or markets, and the education system had lost years of trained personnel.

Geographically and politically, "China" here means the People's Republic of China on the mainland, governed from Beijing. The Republic of China government on Taiwan (Chapter 65) continued to exist under separate rule, and relations across the Taiwan Strait were a continuing diplomatic problem in 1978–1979. The period also falls within the Cold War: the PRC was in tension with the Soviet Union since the Sino-Soviet split (Chapter 69), had moved toward the United States in the early 1970s, and faced shifting alignments in Southeast Asia. These conditions shaped both the opportunities and the constraints of the transition.

Division I — Mao's Succession and the Fall of the Gang of Four, 1976–1981

Purpose: to explain why the removal of the Gang of Four did not itself establish the reform system associated with Deng Xiaoping.

Section 71.1

China After Mao Zedong's Death

Mao Zedong died in Beijing on 9 September 1976. The Party announced his death the same day, and the following week was filled with organized mourning: memorial gatherings in workplaces, schools, and communes, black armbands, and a large ceremony in Tiananmen Square on 18 September at which Hua Guofeng delivered the eulogy. For many people the grief was genuine; Mao had been the central political figure of their lives. For others, mourning was a public obligation that concealed fear, relief, or uncertainty. Historians cannot reduce the national mood to one emotion, and the surviving evidence (diaries, memoirs, oral histories, and official reports) reflects that variety.

The political system Mao left behind had no settled procedure for succession. The 1975 State Constitution and the Party constitution described institutions, but real authority had depended heavily on Mao personally. In the Politburo, several groups held different positions. The radicals associated with Jiang Qing, Zhang Chunqiao, Wang Hongwen, and Yao Wenyuan controlled important parts of the propaganda system and had a base in Shanghai. A group of veteran officials, many of whom had been attacked during the Cultural Revolution, wanted to restore stable administration. Military leaders, regional commanders, and security officials held their own interests. Hua Guofeng, newly named premier and then first vice-chairman of the Party, occupied the formal center but lacked a long personal political base.

Institutions inherited from the Cultural Revolution still shaped daily life. Revolutionary committees governed many provinces, cities, and workplaces; work-unit and household-registration controls structured urban life; schools used revised curricula; and political study sessions remained part of working life. Millions of former urban students remained in rural areas, and many officials remained under investigation or in labor settings. A leadership change at the top did not instantly alter any of these arrangements.

Students should note an important point of method. Later narratives often present the months after September 1976 as the opening of "the Deng era." That is hindsight. At the time, Deng Xiaoping was under criticism and out of office, and the immediate question was who would hold the leadership and on what claim. Evidence for the early period must therefore be read for what contemporaries knew and feared in 1976, not for what later events made obvious.

The leadership crisis was also a crisis of information. Party newspapers, work units, and neighborhood committees were accustomed to transmitting official verdicts rather than inviting open evaluation of them. People who wanted to know whether a new political campaign was coming often learned more from alterations in slogans, meetings, and personnel than from an explicit statement of future policy. At the local level, the difference between a revolutionary committee, a restored Party committee, and a functioning ministry could determine who issued travel permits, cleared personnel files, or authorized the return of a dismissed employee. Consequently, succession cannot be understood solely from lists of top offices; it involved the gradual reconstruction of ordinary administrative authority.

Section 71.2

Hua Guofeng and the Immediate Post-Mao Leadership

Hua Guofeng was born in Shanxi in 1921 and rose through local and provincial posts, including a period in Hunan, before entering the central leadership and later heading the Ministry of Public Security. His elevation in 1976 reflected Mao's judgment: after Zhou Enlai's death, Mao chose Hua rather than Deng or one of the radicals as acting premier. Hua's supporters later cited a note attributed to Mao in April 1976 expressing confidence in Hua; the exact circumstances and use of that note were subjects of later dispute, and students should treat it as a contested piece of political evidence.

After the October arrests Hua became Chairman of the Party Central Committee and Chairman of the Central Military Commission, in addition to serving as premier. These posts gave him formal supremacy. But his authority depended on three supports: Mao's endorsement, the backing of senior figures such as Ye Jianying, and the cooperation of the security and military apparatus. He also needed the veteran cadres whose experience he lacked. This dependence shaped his political choices. Because his legitimacy derived from Mao, he could not easily repudiate Mao's decisions; because he needed veterans and administrative competence, he could not ignore pressure to restore officials and stabilize governance.

Hua's government pursued several lines at once. It publicized the exposure and criticism of the Gang of Four, supported the publication of Mao's writings and the construction of a memorial hall, restored administrative routines, and called for economic recovery after the disruption and the earthquake. It also continued to use the language of class struggle in some contexts. This combination can look inconsistent, but it fits a leadership trying to preserve legitimacy while moving away from the radicals' most disruptive practices. Section 71.6 examines the program in detail.

Hua's unusual combination of Party chairmanship, premiership, and chairmanship of the Central Military Commission made him formally powerful, but it did not eliminate the need for consultation with veteran revolutionary leaders. The October 1976 coalition had been assembled to contain a specific threat, not to endorse a complete economic or political program. Its members agreed that political turbulence should cease while differing about how far Mao's decisions could be reexamined. This helps explain the apparent contradiction in Hua's record: promoting industrial imports and professional management while defending the ideological language that had legitimized earlier campaigns. Historians should compare actual budgets, personnel decisions, and policy directives with the labels applied to Hua by his subsequent political rivals.

Section 71.3

The Arrest of the Gang of Four, October 1976

The arrests of 6 October 1976 were prepared by a small group. According to later accounts by participants and historians, Hua Guofeng, Ye Jianying, and Wang Dongxing, commander of the central guard unit (the 8341 unit), coordinated the plan, with the support of other senior figures including Li Xiannian. A Politburo meeting was scheduled in the leadership compound at Zhongnanhai; as the intended targets arrived or were located, security personnel detained them. Mao Yuanxin, Mao's nephew and a political supporter of the radicals, and others close to the Gang were also detained in the following hours and days.

Why did the leaders act when they did? Historians point to several factors. Jiang Qing and her associates held positions in propaganda and in Shanghai's political and militia networks, and the leaders feared they would use Mao's death to seize power or to name a successor on their terms. Hua and the veterans also had personal and policy reasons: many veterans had been persecuted during the Cultural Revolution, and many officials resented the radicals' methods. The operation was also made possible by the fact that the Gang lacked broad control of the army and the security forces. The evidence for private planning comes largely from memoirs and later interviews, so details differ and some claims cannot be independently verified.

The arrests were not a popular uprising or a constitutional process. They were a leadership action carried out by a coalition within the Party-state. At the same time, public reaction mattered. When the news became known, large rallies and celebrations followed, suggesting that many people welcomed the end of the radicals' prominence. Official media launched a campaign portraying the four as a conspiratorial faction that had distorted Mao's line. That official portrayal had a political purpose: it allowed the new leadership to blame the Cultural Revolution's excesses on a small group while preserving Mao's authority. Later sections show how limited that formula became.

Security control was a decisive practical element. The leadership compound, the central guard unit, the army command structure, and the communications apparatus were not equally accessible to the competing factions. Once senior military figures agreed to the operation, Jiang Qing's access to media and cultural organizations provided little protection. Accounts of the arrests nevertheless differ over the order in which individuals were detained and the exact words exchanged in private meetings. A good historical reconstruction therefore establishes the firm chronology first, then identifies which details depend chiefly on recollections published after the victorious coalition had consolidated its power.

Section 71.4

The End of the Cultural Revolution and the Reorganization of Authority

After the arrests, the leadership moved to reorganize authority. Central directives instructed local Party committees to support the center and to investigate those connected with the radicals. In Shanghai, where the radicals had a strong base, leaders were replaced and militia structures were brought under control. Newspapers and radio carried criticism of the "Gang of Four," and a nationwide campaign investigated their followers, with consequences that varied by region and local factional history.

It is important to distinguish a change of leadership from a change of governing system. In late 1976 and 1977 the central leadership declared the end of the Cultural Revolution's disorder but not the repudiation of the Cultural Revolution itself. The Eleventh Party Congress in August 1977 described the movement as having concluded, while still treating it as a legitimate stage of revolution. Many of its institutions, including revolutionary committees and certain cadre-selection practices, remained in place until later reforms. Many officials who had risen during the movement stayed in office, and many who had been attacked remained in limbo.

This was, in effect, a stabilization phase. Factories and schools returned to regular schedules, ministries reopened and reorganized, and the army reasserted its role in maintaining order. But the question of what to do about those wrongly persecuted, about the legacy of Mao's policies, and about the proper relationship between politics and economic work remained open. Those questions occupy the rest of the chapter.

The transition from revolutionary committees to restored government organs took different forms in different places. A provincial official who had been promoted during the Cultural Revolution was not automatically removed, just as an official who had been persecuted was not immediately reinstated. Personnel offices had to recheck political files, evaluate accusations, and decide whether dismissals reflected factional retaliation, ordinary disciplinary problems, or both. Local authorities also faced pressure to restore rail services, stable school calendars, and predictable factory management. These administrative questions mattered to the credibility of the new leadership because national proclamations had little effect unless local institutions could implement them.

Section 71.5

The Trial of the Gang of Four and the Legal Reckoning, 1980–1981

This section is a deliberate retrospective extension beyond 1979. The Gang of Four were arrested in October 1976; they were not tried until November 1980–January 1981, more than four years later. During that period they were held in custody and investigated. Students should not narrate the arrests and the trial as a single continuous legal process.

The trial was conducted by a Special Procuratorate and a Special Court established under the Supreme People's Procuratorate and Supreme People's Court. Ten defendants were tried together: Jiang Qing, Zhang Chunqiao, Wang Hongwen, and Yao Wenyuan, along with Chen Boda and five military figures associated with Lin Biao (Huang Yongsheng, Wu Faxian, Li Zuopeng, Qiu Huizuo, and Jiang Tengjiao). The charges centered on counter-revolutionary crimes, including persecution of officials and citizens and, in the case of the Lin Biao group, alleged plotting against Mao. The proceedings were partly publicized, with edited excerpts broadcast and reported. Jiang Qing defended herself defiantly, arguing that she had acted under Mao's authority; Zhang Chunqiao largely refused to speak.

In January 1981 the court sentenced Jiang Qing and Zhang Chunqiao to death with a two-year suspension (later commuted to life imprisonment), Wang Hongwen to life imprisonment, Yao Wenyuan to twenty years, and the others to prison terms of sixteen to eighteen years. Observers inside and outside China noted procedural questions, including the application of the 1979 Criminal Law to earlier acts, the court's independence from the Party, and the fact that the accused were tried as members of two "counter-revolutionary cliques" while Mao himself, who had authorized many of the movement's actions, stood outside the dock.

The trial's historical importance lies in its political function. It created an official account that located responsibility in identifiable individuals and distinguished Mao's "mistakes" from the "crimes" of the cliques. This distinction was refined in the 1981 Party resolution (see the interpretation box after §71.22). Historians continue to study the trial as evidence both of a real effort at legal reckoning and of the limits of that effort under Party control.

The distinction between the judicial record and a broad historical judgment is particularly important here. The defendants were selected through a political process, yet the proceedings also produced testimony and evidence about serious acts of persecution. The court's verdicts can therefore be used to study both individual allegations and the Party's decision to channel accountability toward particular groups. Neither a defendant's conviction nor an official narrative of the prosecution settles the wider question of Mao's responsibility or that of officials who were not charged. Compare the indictment, contemporaneous reporting, and the June 1981 Party history resolution before drawing conclusions about how this legal reckoning shaped collective memory.

Division II — Hua Guofeng, Deng Xiaoping, and the Political Transition, 1976–1977

Purpose: to explain why Deng's political rise was a gradual process rather than an immediate succession to Mao.

Section 71.6

Hua Guofeng's Initial Program of Government

Hua's program had three elements. First, he sought to restore administration after years of disorder: reopening ministries, reorganizing provincial leadership, and demanding discipline. Second, he sought economic revival. In 1977–1978 the leadership announced ambitious industrial and agricultural targets, emphasized steel, energy, and mechanization, and renewed the call to build China into a modern socialist country by the end of the century, drawing on the Four Modernizations formula associated with Zhou Enlai's 1975 report (§71.25). Third, he sought political continuity with Mao, emphasizing study of Mao's works and defending the legitimacy of the Cultural Revolution's goals. Hua also presided over the publication of the fifth volume of Mao's Selected Works and the opening of the Mao Zedong Memorial Hall in 1977.

Hua's approach was not simply reactionary. He supported the reinstatement of experienced officials, encouraged technological imports, and promoted agricultural mechanization and model areas such as Dazhai and Daqing, which he treated as examples of self-reliant development. The model of Dazhai was later widely criticized as an example of overly political and impractical rural policy, but in 1976–1977 it was still official orthodoxy. Hua's government thus combined elements of recovery, continuity, and a more ambitious state-directed development program.

The scale of Hua's development ambitions introduced economic contradictions of its own. Large imported industrial plants required foreign exchange, transport capacity, energy, and skilled labor; orders for equipment could be signed more quickly than the supporting infrastructure could be built. Officials who favored modernization did not agree about whether the priority should be spectacular heavy-industry projects or the repair of agriculture, light industry, and household consumption. The later reduction of some investment plans should consequently be described as a revision of priorities within a continuing drive for modernization, not as evidence that modernization began only when Hua lost influence.

Section 71.7

The Two Whatevers and the Problem of Mao's Legacy

In February 1977 the Party's main publications (People's Daily, Red Flag, and Liberation Army Daily) carried a joint editorial that came to be known by the shorthand "Two Whatevers." In substance it said that the Party would firmly uphold whatever decisions Chairman Mao had made and would follow whatever instructions he had given. The formula was associated with Hua and with officials close to him, including Wang Dongxing. Its political purpose was clear: to protect the legitimacy of the new leadership by tying it to Mao and to forestall attacks on Mao's decisions, which would also have undermined Hua.

The formula created a difficulty. Mao had decided to purge Deng in 1976 and had approved the characterization of the April Tiananmen events as a counter-revolutionary incident. If every Mao decision had to be upheld, Deng could not be rehabilitated and the Tiananmen verdict could not be reversed. Veteran officials objected. Deng, in letters to the Party center in spring 1977 and in remarks he made afterward, argued that Mao Zedong Thought should be understood in an "accurate and complete" way, as a system, rather than treated as a set of isolated instructions. This was a careful move: it affirmed Mao's thought while creating room to revise policies.

Students should note that the dispute was not simply "Hua for Mao, Deng against Mao." Deng also depended on Mao's name; he never repudiated Mao as the founder of the People's Republic. The disagreement concerned how Mao's legacy could be interpreted and whether practical results or fidelity to specific instructions should guide policy.

The strength of the Two Whatevers was also its weakness. As a principle of succession it offered continuity at a time when continuity seemed reassuring to many officials. As a method for settling policy it could not easily accommodate contradictions among Mao's instructions or changing practical conditions. Deng's argument for understanding Mao Zedong Thought as an integrated system created a route through this difficulty: one could claim fidelity to Mao's emphasis on investigation and practice while rejecting specific decisions made under particular circumstances. That rhetorical strategy later gave supporters of reform a language of continuity as well as change; its success depended on shifting alliances and administrative results, not on philosophical argument alone.

Historical Vocabulary
Two Whatevers and Seeking Truth from Facts

The Two Whatevers was a political formula associated with defense of Mao’s decisions and instructions. Seeking truth from facts became a competing principle of policy judgment, emphasizing investigation and practical results. Both were interpreted within the Communist Party’s political language; neither by itself constituted a program of multiparty democracy.

Section 71.8

Senior Party Veterans and the Reconstruction of Political Coalitions

The transition cannot be understood without the older generation of revolutionary leaders. Ye Jianying, a senior marshal, helped organize the October arrests and later served as chairman of the National People's Congress Standing Committee. Chen Yun, an economic planner associated with a cautious, balanced approach to planning, returned to prominence in 1977–1978. Li Xiannian, a veteran administrator, supported economic reconstruction. Wang Zhen, Peng Zhen, Bo Yibo, and others pressed for the rehabilitation of those wrongly attacked. Hu Yaobang, head of the Central Party School and then of the Organization Department, directed investigations that overturned wrongful verdicts and promoted discussion of the practice criterion.

These leaders did not hold identical views. Some stressed economic recovery and planning discipline, others political rehabilitation, others military and security concerns. Their common interest was to restore Party institutions, protect surviving veterans, and prevent a repeat of Cultural Revolution-style purges. That shared interest produced a coalition that could support both rehabilitation and modernization but could disagree about pace, markets, and the treatment of Mao. This coalition is one reason the Third Plenum could produce a consensus on priorities while leaving many questions open.

At the same time, networks of local officials, military commanders, and provincial leaders determined whether central decisions were implemented. Reformist provincial leaders such as Wan Li in Anhui and Zhao Ziyang in Sichuan used their positions to test new methods, as later sections show.

Veteran networks crossed formal institutional boundaries. Ye Jianying's military prestige, Chen Yun's economic reputation, Li Xiannian's administrative experience, and Hu Yaobang's emerging role in personnel rehabilitation made them influential in different arenas. Their interests did not always coincide. Some wanted faster reassessment of Mao-era injustices, others prioritized a stable bureaucracy, and still others warned against repeating the investment imbalances of the Great Leap period. This should be interpreted as a changing coalition rather than a unified reform faction possessing a fully developed economic blueprint from the start. Hua's formal authority and the veterans' political weight coexisted, producing negotiation and gradual rather than instantaneous change.

Section 71.9

Deng Xiaoping's Rehabilitation and Return to Leadership in 1977

Deng Xiaoping, born in Sichuan in 1904, had been a senior Party figure since the revolutionary era. He was purged as the "second-ranking capitalist roader" in the Cultural Revolution, restored to office in 1973 with Mao's consent, and placed in charge of daily administration in 1975, when he promoted a program of rectification in industry, science, and the army. After Zhou Enlai's death and the April 1976 Tiananmen events, Deng was dismissed from his posts and went south to Guangzhou, where he was protected by military leaders such as Xu Shiyou.

Deng sought a return after the October arrests, including through written communication with the leadership. Veterans such as Ye Jianying, Chen Yun, and Wang Zhen argued for it at central work meetings in March 1977. Hua initially resisted a quick restoration, in part because the Two Whatevers stance implied that Mao's verdict stood. A compromise made Deng's return conditional on accepting a statement of support for Hua. In July 1977 the Third Plenum of the Tenth Central Committee formally restored Deng to his posts, including Vice-Chairman of the Party, Vice-Premier, Vice-Chairman of the Central Military Commission, and Chief of the General Staff. The plenum also confirmed Hua as Party chairman and formally expelled the Gang of Four.

Deng's return mattered because he brought three assets: decades of administrative experience, personal ties in the army, and a record, from 1975, of favoring practical rectification and expertise. His priorities in 1977 were education and science, the restoration of discipline and competence, and a reinterpretation of Mao Zedong Thought that allowed policy revision. He did not displace Hua; instead, a dual structure emerged, with Hua holding the top formal offices and Deng increasingly shaping policy in specific areas.

Deng's return was institutional as well as personal. It allowed a leader with experience in military affairs, diplomacy, and economic coordination to participate again in top-level decisions and to advocate restoring examination-based education and professional competence. Yet in 1977 his colleagues still held important offices and could veto or delay programs. His increasing prominence depended on persuading other senior leaders that the Party's legitimacy could be renewed through effective administration. Students should distinguish the precise dates of reinstatement from later retrospective phrases such as 'Deng took power,' which compress a complicated transition lasting several years.

Section 71.10

The Eleventh Party Congress and the Post-Mao Political Settlement

The Eleventh National Congress of the Chinese Communist Party met from 12 to 18 August 1977. It produced a new Central Committee and Politburo, a Standing Committee headed by Hua with Ye Jianying, Deng Xiaoping, Li Xiannian, and Wang Dongxing, and a revised Party constitution. The congress declared that the Cultural Revolution had ended with the defeat of the Gang of Four, while insisting that its "great achievements" were real and that similar movements might recur. It called for development through the Four Modernizations and for a "great order" restored to the country.

The settlement was unstable. Hua remained chairman and the Two Whatevers had not been repudiated. Deng had regained office and was beginning to act on education and science. Many cadres were still unrehabilitated, the Tiananmen verdict had not been reversed, and Wang Dongxing and others associated with the security apparatus held senior positions. In this sense 1977 was a compromise year. The compromise could be sustained only so long as the questions it left open (Mao's legacy, the Tiananmen verdict, rehabilitation, economic priorities) did not demand answers. In 1978 they did.

The Eleventh Congress also illustrates the distinction between an official periodization and a settled historical consensus. Declaring the Cultural Revolution complete did not yet amount to acknowledging that its organizing theory and methods had been fundamentally mistaken. Congress language continued to defend Mao's general revolutionary line and emphasized the defeat of the Gang of Four as proof of the Party's capacity for correction. This allowed officials to participate in rebuilding without openly repudiating the recent past. The deeper historical reassessment came through the political struggles of 1978 and the resolution of 1981, so the 1977 congress should be treated as an intermediate settlement rather than the decisive moment of ideological change.

Division III — Education, Modernization, and the Struggle over Political Direction, 1977–1978

Purpose: to demonstrate that the reform transition began through changing approaches to knowledge, expertise, education, and political legitimacy, not simply through a new economic program.

Section 71.11

The Restoration of the National College Entrance Examination

During the Cultural Revolution, university admission had largely been replaced by recommendation based on political background and work-unit approval, and universities had been closed or severely disrupted. In August 1977 Deng Xiaoping, who had taken responsibility for science and education, convened a forum of scientists and educators. After discussion, the leadership decided that university admission in 1977 would again be based on competitive examination and school performance, rather than recommendation. The decision was announced in October, and examinations took place in November and December 1977, with arrangements differing by province.

The response was enormous. Approximately 5.7 million people sat the examinations, and roughly 273,000 were admitted, so the great majority were not. Applicants included recent school leavers, former sent-down youth in their twenties and thirties, and workers and farmers who had waited years. A second examination in summer 1978 drew still more candidates (approximately 6.1 million) and admitted about 402,000, since universities soon expanded places. The 1977 and 1978 entrants are often called the "Class of '77" and "Class of '78"; they entered university in early 1978 and autumn 1978 respectively and later became prominent in scholarship, government, and business.

The restoration was both a reopening and a selection. It gave many talented people access to education from which they had been excluded, and it reasserted the principle that academic performance mattered. But the competition was severe, preparation was uneven, and access differed by region, school quality, and family circumstances. Urban candidates with better schooling and rural candidates with few textbooks did not compete on equal terms. The policy also ended the formal use of "class background" as the main criterion, a change significant for families that had been classified as politically suspect, though political review of applicants did not vanish immediately.

Because provincial education authorities organized the examination under difficult conditions, the test was not administered through a fully standardized nationwide procedure in 1977. Syllabi, questions, and arrangements differed across regions, and late notice left applicants to prepare with whatever textbooks or notes they could obtain. The admission results also expose a distinction between reestablishing a principle and providing equal opportunity. Selection by examination could reduce the importance of a family's political label, but working adults and villagers who had been denied schooling still competed against those with better educational resources. The following year's expansion to about 6.1 million candidates and 402,000 admissions showed the demand for schooling and the shortage of university places. The return of competitive admissions was therefore both a social opening and the beginning of a long rebuilding process.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Student Taking the Restored Entrance Examination

The following is a composite scenario built from patterns documented in memoirs and oral histories; it is not the testimony of one named person. A twenty-six-year-old man, sent from a city to a village in 1969, hears of the announcement on the commune loudspeaker. He has no textbooks, so he borrows old school books from a teacher and studies at night by oil lamp after farm work. His production team leader must approve his leave, and his household background is checked. He travels to the county town to sit the examination in winter, in a cold classroom with hundreds of other candidates, some older than the invigilators. Months later a notice arrives; if successful, he will leave the village for a university and a new status, while friends who failed remain.

Women candidates faced similar competition but also pressures of marriage and family expectations; rural candidates faced thinner schools; and those with a "bad" family background faced remnants of political screening. The experience shows how a national policy changed individual lives unevenly.

Historical Evidence

Education and University Admissions, 1977–1979

Historians use several kinds of evidence: official announcements and statistics (which give national totals of applicants and admissions), provincial education bureau records, university archives and class lists, newspaper coverage, memoirs and oral histories of the "Class of '77," and contemporary letters. Official figures are valuable for scale, but they do not show who was excluded, how unevenly preparation was distributed, or how candidates experienced the process. Memoirs are vivid but are written long after the events and tend to emphasize success. A careful account compares these sources and notes that the numbers cited (5.7 million candidates, about 273,000 admitted) are widely used approximations from official reporting.

Section 71.12

Rebuilding Universities, Research Institutions, and Professional Expertise

The examination was one part of a broader effort to rebuild higher education and research. Universities reopened classes, reinstated academic ranks and departments, and began to restore libraries and laboratories. Research institutes, including those of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, were reorganized after years in which many scientists had been criticized, reassigned, or sent to manual labor. Graduate admissions resumed in 1978. Many senior scholars returned to work; many younger scholars had lost years of training, creating a gap between generations that would shape academic life for decades.

This effort rested on a change in how intellectuals were viewed. During much of the Cultural Revolution, intellectuals had been treated as suspect. The new leadership reversed this language and treated expertise as a national resource. The shift was partly pragmatic: modernization required engineers, scientists, doctors, and teachers. It was also political, since it implied reevaluating the verdicts passed on intellectuals in earlier campaigns, including the Anti-Rightist Campaign (see §71.23).

Resources were limited, and many classrooms and laboratories were short of equipment. Universities also faced pressure to teach political courses while restoring academic standards. The rebuilding was therefore uneven, with elite institutions in Beijing and Shanghai recovering faster than provincial colleges.

Rebuilding higher education required more than reopening entrance examinations. Faculties had lost teachers through political persecution, retirement, interrupted research, and the breakdown of specialist training. Universities needed laboratories, libraries, academic journals, revised curricula, and credible criteria for hiring and promotion. Scientific institutions also faced a question of political status: could an expert's technical judgment carry weight when it conflicted with an official's ideological expectations? Restoring research organizations and professional titles was an attempt to answer yes within the framework of Party leadership. Progress varied sharply by discipline and institution, especially where equipment or trained staff had been lost.

Section 71.13

The National Science Conference of 1978 and the Rehabilitation of Expertise

The National Science Conference, held in Beijing from 18 to 31 March 1978 with several thousand participants, was a public statement of the new attitude. Deng Xiaoping told the conference that science and technology are productive forces and that intellectuals who worked with their minds were part of the working class. This was a significant ideological point: it removed the label of political suspicion from professional work and linked intellectual labor to the socialist economy. A national science plan and recognition of numerous scientists and research achievements followed.

Public culture reflected the shift. Popular writing in early 1978 praised scientists and mathematicians, including reportage about the mathematician Chen Jingrun that attracted a wide readership. Newspapers published stories of scientists whose careers had been interrupted. The mood was celebratory but also reflected a need for official reassurance; many intellectuals remained cautious about whether the new line would last.

The conference also illustrates a general pattern of the period: new policy often came framed as a restoration of properly Marxist understanding, not as a break with the past. Deng's phrasing connected to the socialist tradition while changing its practical meaning.

The science conference's importance was partly symbolic. By bringing researchers back into public view and identifying scientific and technical achievement as a national development priority, it challenged the atmosphere in which professional knowledge had often been treated with suspicion. Yet the conference should not be mistaken for an immediate end to political supervision of research. Institutions remained dependent on state funding and appointments, and senior leaders continued to set strategic priorities. The central change was an increasingly explicit argument that modernization required stable education, sustained research, and respect for specialists' professional competence. This argument linked the human consequences of rehabilitation to the economic ambitions of the Four Modernizations.

Section 71.14

Hua's Economic Ambitions and the Problems of Accelerated Development

In February–March 1978 the Fifth National People's Congress approved a Ten-Year Plan outline (1976–1985) and a revised State Constitution. The plan set high targets for steel, coal, oil, grain, and mechanization and called for dozens of large industrial projects (often described as about 120) built with imported technology and equipment. Supporters described it as a way to catch up quickly; critics later nicknamed the effort a "foreign leap forward," comparing it to the Great Leap Forward's haste.

The plan ran into constraints. Domestic savings, foreign exchange, energy supply, transportation, and managerial capacity were limited, and contracts with foreign firms strained finances. Some leaders, notably Chen Yun and Li Xiannian, argued for slowing down, emphasizing balance among sectors. Deng supported modernization but also became concerned about pace and efficiency. By late 1978 a debate was under way within the leadership about how fast to proceed, which sectors to emphasize, and how to finance imports. That debate fed into the adjustment policies of 1979 (§71.32).

Students should avoid two simplifications: that Hua's economic policy was merely a repetition of Mao-era mobilization, or that it was entirely a failure. It included real efforts to import technology and rebuild industry, and it opened channels (such as trade contracts with Japan and Western Europe) that later policy used. But its dependence on large state-led projects and optimistic targets exposed structural limits that later reforms sought to address.

Debates over investment also connected Hua's program to earlier Chinese development history. Leaders remembered the rapid industrial expansion of the First Five-Year Plan but also the catastrophic consequences of unrealistic targets during the Great Leap. These memories supported different conclusions: some urged bold purchases of advanced foreign equipment, while others emphasized the need to improve agriculture, transport, fuel supplies, and household consumption first. A modern factory purchased abroad might improve productivity, but it could also become an expensive burden if spare parts, managers, foreign exchange, or energy were unavailable. By 1979 the government was already readjusting some plans, showing that reform encompassed renewed caution about investment as well as experimentation with enterprise management.

Section 71.15

Practice as the Criterion of Truth and the Ideological Debate of 1978

On 11 May 1978 the newspaper Guangming Daily published an article, credited to a "special commentator," titled "Practice Is the Sole Criterion for Testing Truth." It had been drafted by Hu Fuming, a philosophy teacher at Nanjing University, and revised with the involvement of scholars at the Central Party School, where Hu Yaobang was a leading figure. Xinhua and other newspapers reprinted it the next day. The article argued, drawing on Marx, Lenin, and Mao's essay "On Practice," that no theory could be treated as true simply because an authority had stated it; social practice was the test.

The political meaning was clear to contemporary readers. If practice, not quotation, decided truth, then Mao's specific decisions and the Two Whatevers could be examined. Wang Dongxing and others associated with Hua's line criticized the article as dangerous. Deng Xiaoping supported it publicly, notably at the All-Army Political Work Conference in June 1978, where he stressed "seeking truth from facts." Over the summer and autumn, provincial and military leaders expressed positions, and the debate spread through the Party. Its outcome was not predetermined; support came from a mixture of conviction, calculation, and local experience of the costs of earlier policies.

As historical evidence, the truth-criterion debate illustrates how apparently abstract ideas can change the range of acceptable political choices. Supporters did not simply abandon Marxism or Mao's writings; they used the Marxist idea that knowledge must be tested against practice to challenge the authority of individual quotations. This offered a way to reconsider purges and failed economic policies while retaining ideological continuity. The article's effect depended on endorsement by influential Party officials and its circulation through authorized newspapers. It did not create an independent sphere of unrestricted debate; rather, it altered the terms of argument within a changing political leadership. Students should read both the article and the contemporary reactions to it.

Division IV — The Third Plenum and the Political Turning Point of 1978

Purpose: to explain why the Third Plenum is considered a major turning point without depicting it as an immediate transfer of all power to Deng or the establishment of a market economy.

Section 71.16

The Central Work Conference of November–December 1978

The Central Work Conference convened in Beijing on 10 November 1978 and lasted until 15 December. Its announced agenda was economic: agricultural development, the 1979–1980 economic plans, and related matters. But early in the meeting Chen Yun, speaking in a regional discussion group, urged that unresolved historical cases be addressed, including the April 1976 Tiananmen Incident and the cases of purged officials. Other participants supported him, and the agenda widened. By late November Hua Guofeng announced that the Beijing leadership had revised its judgment on the Tiananmen events, describing them as a revolutionary mass action. Participants also pressed for reconsidering other cases and criticized the Two Whatevers.

The conference matters because it shows how a meeting called for one purpose changed direction through group discussion, personal interventions, and shifting alignments. It also shows that Hua accepted significant concessions, including partial self-criticism, while remaining chairman. Meanwhile events outside the hall, including wall posters in Beijing, Xiaogang's agreement, and Deng's travels abroad, formed part of the setting.

The conference was unusually important because personnel cases and economic planning were considered together. Rehabilitating an official did not merely correct the past; it restored an experienced administrator who might then influence future agricultural or industrial policy. Likewise, policy debates brought suppressed evidence of earlier failures into the meeting. Chen Yun's interventions made it harder to discuss future investment or agriculture without revisiting the political decisions that had damaged administration. The conference's participants were not simply ratifying a decision already made by Deng; they were testing the limits of consensus, forming alliances, and determining which past verdicts could be changed without undermining Party authority.

Historical Debate

What Made the 1978 Political Shift Possible?

The question: Why did the Party's direction change in late 1978 rather than earlier or later?

Interpretation A — Leadership politics and coalition-building: The shift resulted from the alignment of veterans (Chen Yun, Ye Jianying, Hu Yaobang) and Deng, skillful use of the practice debate, and Hua's weakened position.

Section 71.17

Deng Xiaoping's December 1978 Speech and the Call to Emancipate the Mind

On 13 December 1978, at the close of the Central Work Conference, Deng gave a speech titled "Emancipate the Mind, Seek Truth from Facts, and Unite as One in Looking to the Future." It served as a keynote for the plenum. Deng argued that the Party needed to think independently, adhere to facts, and focus on practical results. He called for greater democracy within the Party, for law that did not change with each leader's views, and for decentralizing some decision-making to localities and enterprises. He also said that some regions, enterprises, and individuals should be allowed to become prosperous first, with the expectation that others would follow. On the past, he urged the Party to resolve major historical problems but not to be consumed by them, so as to concentrate on the future.

Deng's speech paired a demand for greater intellectual freedom within political decision-making with explicit concern about order and effective administration. He criticized excessive centralization and the tendency of officials to wait for instructions, arguing that enterprises and local authorities needed room to solve practical problems. Yet his appeal to democracy principally addressed the Party's internal deliberation and the regular functioning of law, not multiparty competition. This distinction helps explain why the speech could become a foundational reform text while the political system's basic monopoly of power remained intact. Its treatment of incentives also foreshadowed later disputes over inequality: allowing some regions or workers to improve their living standards first was presented as a means of encouraging wider progress, not as a fully specified market reform program.

Section 71.18

The Third Plenary Session of December 18–22, 1978

The Third Plenary Session of the Eleventh Central Committee met in Beijing from 18 to 22 December 1978. Hua Guofeng, as Party chairman, presided. The plenum reviewed the work conference's results and adopted several decisions. Its communiqué declared that the large-scale mass movement to expose and criticize the Gang of Four had basically concluded, and that the Party's focus should shift from "class struggle as the key link" to socialist modernization starting in 1979. It endorsed the discussion on the criterion of truth in spirit, called for strengthening democracy and the legal system, and approved the reexamination of a number of cases, including the Tiananmen Incident and the cases of senior figures such as Peng Dehuai.

In agriculture, the plenum approved draft documents for discussion and trial implementation and announced a rise in state procurement prices for grain and other farm products starting in 1979, with a higher price for above-quota deliveries. It discussed Party organization, including the creation of a Central Discipline Inspection Commission, and elected additional leaders to the Politburo and its Standing Committee.

The plenum's published communiqué is valuable because it records both a substantive shift and the limits of that shift. It explicitly credited Hua Guofeng with presenting the Political Bureau's decision to redirect the Party's work, while also raising Chen Yun and other rehabilitated figures to new positions. Agricultural documents were distributed as drafts for discussion and trial use, not enacted as a nationwide household-contract system. The communiqué addressed economic management, stronger legal institutions, and a more orderly division of responsibilities between Party, government, and enterprises. That breadth is why its significance cannot be reduced to one slogan or one leader's victory. Students should compare the communiqué with the subsequent laws and rural directives to determine what changed immediately and what changed later.

Section 71.19

The Reconfiguration of Communist Party Leadership

At the Third Plenum Chen Yun was added to the Politburo Standing Committee and made a Party vice-chairman, and he became first secretary of the new Central Discipline Inspection Commission. Hu Yaobang, Wang Zhen, and others joined the Politburo, and a number of rehabilitated veterans returned to leadership posts. These changes strengthened leaders who favored reassessment and economic reconstruction. Hua Guofeng retained his offices, and Wang Dongxing and other figures aligned with the earlier line retained posts for the time being.

This is an example of the difference between political influence and official office. Deng Xiaoping did not become Party chairman or premier. Yet his authority in practice grew, partly because of his seniority, his alliances, and his capacity to define policy. Over the next two and a half years the formal structure caught up: in February 1980 the Fifth Plenum rehabilitated Liu Shaoqi, removed Wang Dongxing and several others from the Politburo, restored the Party Secretariat with Hu Yaobang as General Secretary; Zhao Ziyang replaced Hua as premier in 1980; and in June 1981 Hu Yaobang succeeded Hua as chairman while Deng became chairman of the Central Military Commission. These later changes are previewed here and treated more fully in Chapter 72.

These changes were the outcome of a political process in which formal rank, revolutionary seniority, administrative networks, and influence over appointments all mattered. A new committee membership did not automatically guarantee control of provincial officials or economic ministries. Reform-minded leaders had to persuade, replace, or rehabilitate people within an immense Party-state hierarchy. The subsequent transfers of offices in 1980 and 1981 belong to the retrospective part of the chapter: they demonstrate how practical influence became formal authority after the shift in policy, but they must not be projected backward onto the December 1978 meeting. Hua's continued position at that meeting is an essential chronological fact.

Section 71.20

What Did the Third Plenum Actually Change?

The Third Plenum changed priorities, personnel, and the permissible range of discussion. It ended the formal primacy of class struggle as the Party's central task, and it opened the way for the reassessment of Mao-era cases and for institutional rebuilding. It supported practical measures in agriculture, such as higher procurement prices, and encouraged legal development.

What it did not do is equally important. It did not introduce the Household Responsibility System as national policy, and its draft agricultural documents still reflected the collective framework. It did not create special economic zones or authorize extensive market pricing. It did not displace Hua from the chairmanship or repudiate Mao. Many of the changes commonly attributed to "1978" were decided in 1979–1981 or later. Students should therefore treat the plenum as a point where direction changed, with implementation spread over subsequent years.

One way to assess the plenum is to classify its results. The change in public priorities was immediate: modernization displaced continuing class struggle as the organizing focus of national policy. Institutional change was partly immediate and partly prospective: a new discipline commission and rehabilitations were approved, but the restoration of laws, ministries, and procedures took much longer. Economic change was initially experimental: agricultural purchase prices, management initiatives, and local trials could expand without dismantling collective ownership. This distinction gives students an evidence-based method for evaluating the common claim that reform began in December 1978 rather than treating the date as either a complete revolution or an empty symbol.

Cause and Effect

From Ideological Debate to Policy Change

Long-term causes

Economic stagnation and rural poverty after decades of collectivist planning; the human and institutional costs of the Cultural Revolution; the presence of veteran leaders with administrative experience; the example of neighboring East Asian economies and opportunities in trade with the West and Japan.

Short-term causes

Mao's death and the arrest of the Gang of Four; Deng's 1977 return; the practice debate of 1978; the Central Work Conference's widened agenda; pressure from the rehabilitated and from local experiments; the 1978 exposure of Hua's accelerated development plan to financial limits.

Immediate effects

A shift of Party priorities toward modernization; changes in personnel; agricultural price increases; reconsideration of verdicts; institutional and legal development.

Longer-term effects

The reforms of the 1980s; rural decollectivization; opening to foreign trade and investment; a Party leadership committed to economic growth but not to political pluralism.

Historical complexity: No single argument, leader, or meeting caused the shift. Ideas, institutions, individuals, local initiatives, and external conditions interacted, and the outcome was not inevitable.

Division V — Reversal of Mao-Era Policies and Institutional Reconstruction, 1978–1981

Purpose: to demonstrate that the post-Mao transition involved the correction of political injustices, restoration of state institutions, and a major change in development priorities, but not the abandonment of Communist Party rule.

Section 71.21

Boluan Fanzheng: Correcting Disorder and Restoring Institutions

Boluan fanzheng (拨乱反正) is a classical phrase meaning roughly "to remove chaos and return to the correct path." In the late 1970s Party leaders used it to describe a broad program: ending the disorder of the Cultural Revolution, reexamining mistaken policies and verdicts, and restoring normal procedures in Party and state. It was a flexible slogan. For some leaders it meant a return to the institutions and norms of the early 1950s or the period before 1957; for others it justified substantial new policy. The phrase also served a legitimizing function by presenting change as correction of deviation rather than rejection of the revolution.

The program had several parts: reinstating discipline and regular meetings in Party committees; restoring ministries and professional appointments; reviewing wrongful cases; reopening schools and research institutions; and reasserting rules governing the army and security services. It did not mean that Mao's role was reversed or that all Mao-era policies ended. In agriculture, for example, collective structures remained in place into the early 1980s even as incentives began to change.

The process of correcting disorder depended on investigations, documentary review, and the restoration of records that could be used to reverse verdicts. In many cases there was no single reliable case file: denunciations, confession statements, and recommendations had been produced under pressure during earlier campaigns. Reviewers had to decide which records were credible and whether political labels had any lawful foundation. Rehabilitation could restore a position or remove a stigma, but it could not reverse a death or return years of education and family life. The campaign therefore had practical as well as ethical limits, and different local institutions carried it out at different speeds.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Mao-Era Political Institutions and the Post-Mao State

What Changed

  • Class struggle ceased to be the Party's declared central task (1978)
  • Revolutionary committees were replaced by regular local governments and standing committees of people's congresses (1979)
  • Criminal and procedural codes and other laws were adopted (1979); the procuracy and legal institutions were rebuilt
  • Universities, examinations, and professional expertise were restored
  • Large numbers of officials and others were rehabilitated, and personnel policy shifted toward experience and competence

What Stayed the Same

  • The monopoly of the Communist Party over political leadership
  • Collective agriculture and a planned economy, still dominant in 1979
  • Household registration, work-unit structures, and state control of the media
  • The official status of Mao as founder and of Mao Zedong Thought as a guiding doctrine

Who experienced what: Rehabilitated officials and intellectuals experienced change most directly; farmers saw limited change until household contracting spread; urban workers largely continued within work-unit systems; critics of the Party experienced continuity in restrictions on political opposition. Why it matters: The pattern shows how a major change in policy coexisted with continuity in political structure.

Historical Vocabulary
Boluan Fanzheng, Rehabilitation, and Socialist Legality

Boluan fanzheng means correcting disorder and returning to a proper course. Rehabilitation involved reversing political verdicts and restoring rights, reputations, or offices. Socialist legality described reconstruction of codified law and judicial procedure under continued Party leadership. These processes overlapped but were not identical.

Section 71.22

Reversing Political Verdicts and Rehabilitating Purged Officials

The review of cases was among the most consequential tasks of the period. It began with high-profile cases. The April 1976 Tiananmen Incident, initially labeled counter-revolutionary, was reassessed in November 1978 as a revolutionary mass action, and participants who had been detained were released and cleared. Senior leaders such as Peng Dehuai, Tao Zhu, and Bo Yibo were rehabilitated or restored to honor, and in February 1980 Liu Shaoqi, the former head of state who died in custody in 1969, was formally rehabilitated. Hu Yaobang's Organization Department, along with local Party organizations and Central Discipline bodies, oversaw reviews of many thousands of cases.

Rehabilitation was bounded by Party control. Reviews were conducted by Party and state organs, not independent courts. Decisions often depended on the political attitudes of local officials, some of whom had participated in earlier persecutions. Authorities distinguished categories: those to be fully cleared, those whose cases were reduced, and "three types of people" (followers of Lin Biao and the Gang, those who had risen through factional activity, and those who had committed beatings, smashing, and looting) who were to be investigated. The Party also reclassified most of those labeled "Rightists" in 1957–1958, removing the label in nearly all cases from 1978–1979.

Reassessing famous political cases had implications beyond the individuals named. A reversal involving a senior figure signaled that local officials might safely reconsider hundreds of subordinate cases that had previously depended on the same accusation. Hu Yaobang's work in the Party Organization Department became important because political rehabilitation required an administrative apparatus capable of processing files across institutions. Yet the process remained politically directed and selective: official authorities decided what counted as a wrong verdict, and citizens had no independent tribunal able to compel the Party to revise every past decision. Students should therefore distinguish the broad impact of rehabilitation from the absence of a fully independent mechanism for accountability.

Historical Interpretation

The 1981 Party Resolution on History

In June 1981 the Sixth Plenum of the Eleventh Central Committee adopted a "Resolution on Certain Questions in the History of Our Party Since the Founding of the People's Republic of China." It was drafted under Deng Xiaoping's guidance and aimed to provide an authoritative account. In substance it affirmed Mao's contributions as primary and his errors as secondary, judged the Cultural Revolution a severe setback for which responsibility lay partly with Mao and partly with leading cliques, and described Mao Zedong Thought as the product of collective Party experience rather than the work of one man. It also confirmed the shift to modernization and economic construction.

Why students must treat it carefully: The resolution is a retrospective political document, issued in 1981, not the consensus of 1978. In 1976–1978 official discourse blamed the Gang of Four while upholding Mao's decisions; by 1981 the leadership accepted more extensive criticism but limited it. Scholars inside and outside China have interpreted the resolution as a compromise that preserved Party legitimacy while condemning policies. It is both evidence and an interpretation to be examined.

Section 71.23

The Human Consequences of Rehabilitation

For individuals, rehabilitation could mean the return of a job, an apartment, a salary, a pension, the right to publish or teach, or a restored reputation, sometimes posthumously for families. But the process was often slow and incomplete. Many petitioners traveled to Beijing and provincial capitals in 1978–1979 to seek review of cases through the "letters and visits" system, and bureaucracies struggled with volume. Some people had died, some documents were lost, and local officials who had implemented earlier verdicts sometimes resisted reversal.

Sent-down youth presented a major social challenge. Estimates suggest that roughly seventeen million urban young people were sent to the countryside between the late 1960s and 1970s. By 1978 many were in their late twenties or thirties, with few prospects of urban employment, and many sought to return. Protests and petitions, including a widely reported movement among youth in Yunnan in late 1978 and early 1979, drew attention. Policies allowed returns through examinations, family job succession, and other channels, but the transition produced unemployment, housing shortages, and resentment. Former rural-educated youth entered a labor market that did not have enough positions.

The wider social effect was uneven. Intellectuals regained status and publication opportunities, and literature known as "scar literature" began to depict the Cultural Revolution's suffering. But victims often lived alongside those who had persecuted them, and public accounting was limited. Many families dealt with loss without formal acknowledgment of responsibility.

For a person who had been labeled a Rightist, relief could mean regaining permission to teach, travel, or work in a skilled profession. For the child of a persecuted official, the effects might include access to examination-based education and an improvement in the family's standing with a work unit. But reintegration could be painful. Rehired teachers returned to classrooms transformed by years of interruption; families sought information about relatives who had died in detention; surviving victims encountered former accusers in the same workplaces. These experiences remind students that official rehabilitation was a process of social reconstruction rather than a simple administrative stamp. Personal testimonies should be identified by source, and invented composites must never be represented as actual interviews.

Multiple Perspectives

Rehabilitated Officials, Intellectuals, and Families

The viewpoints below are analytical syntheses of patterns in memoirs and oral histories, not quotations from named individuals.

A senior official restored to office: Rehabilitation meant return to authority and a chance to reshape policy, together with a strong interest in preventing another purge. This shaped support for institutional rules and for limiting political campaigns.
A former "Rightist" or intellectual: Rehabilitation could restore a career after twenty years, but also exposed loss of time and uncertainty about whether policy would last. Many were cautious and welcomed professional recognition while remaining wary of political risk.
A family of a person who died in persecution: Rehabilitation could bring a formal clearing of the name, restitution, and sometimes housing or pensions. It did not return the dead or identify individual responsibility, and some families received little recognition.
A returning sent-down youth: Return meant escape from rural hardship but entry into a competitive, constrained urban labor market, often with delayed education and marriage.

Note on power: These experiences are not equivalent to those of officials who carried out persecution or local cadres who resisted reviews. Perpetrators and victims did not stand on equal moral or political ground, and the process of accountability was limited.

Section 71.24

Rebuilding Law, Courts, and State Administration

The Cultural Revolution had severely damaged legal institutions: courts and procuracies had been reduced, law schools closed, and legal professionals persecuted. The 1975 Constitution had abolished the procuracy. The 1978 State Constitution, adopted in March 1978, restored the procuracy and reaffirmed the National People's Congress, but it retained Cultural Revolution language, including the concept of continuing revolution and the "four great freedoms" of speaking out, airing views, great debates, and big-character posters. It was a transitional constitution, and it would be replaced in 1982 (Chapter 72).

In July 1979 the Second Session of the Fifth NPC adopted a package of laws: a Criminal Law, a Criminal Procedure Law, laws on courts and procuracies, an electoral law, an organic law of local people's congresses and local governments, and the Law on Joint Ventures Using Chinese and Foreign Investment. Most took effect in January 1980. The organic law replaced revolutionary committees with local people's governments and created standing committees for local congresses. The Ministry of Justice was reestablished, law faculties reopened, and a lawyer system was gradually revived in the early 1980s.

These changes were significant but must be read within the concept of "socialist legality," in which law was expected to serve Party leadership. The Criminal Law retained "counter-revolutionary" offenses and allowed punishment by analogy for acts not specified. The legal system lacked judicial independence, and Party committees continued to influence outcomes. The reforms nevertheless provided procedures, written norms, and a professional basis that later developed.

The 1979 criminal and criminal-procedure laws were significant because China had long relied heavily on political and administrative directives where codified procedure was weak. The new statutes sought to define offenses, organize investigation and prosecution, and give courts a clearer legal framework. They did not establish judicial independence from Communist Party leadership or fully resolve the use of administrative detention and political policing. Nor was the system transformed overnight: trained judges, prosecutors, defense lawyers, and standardized procedures were scarce after the Cultural Revolution. The transition is best described as rebuilding socialist legality within a one-party state, a major institutional change that still had important limits.

Section 71.25

The Four Modernizations as a National Development Program

The "Four Modernizations" refers to the modernization of agriculture, industry, national defense, and science and technology. The idea was introduced in 1964 and restated in January 1975 in Zhou Enlai's report to the Fourth National People's Congress, which set the aim of comprehensive modernization by the end of the century. During the Cultural Revolution the radicals attacked it; after 1976 it became central to official policy, associated first with Hua and then with Deng. The Third Plenum made it the Party's main task.

Priorities within the program were debated. Hua's plan stressed heavy industry and large imported projects. Deng emphasized efficiency, science and technology, and training, and he later spoke of a more modest "Chinese-style modernization" aiming first at a "moderately prosperous" level of income (xiaokang), a formulation he used in December 1979 in talks with the Japanese prime minister. Chen Yun stressed balance and caution. Defense modernization was given a lower practical priority than agriculture, industry, and science, reflecting limited resources.

The Four Modernizations shifted the legitimacy of the Party from ideological mobilization toward economic performance. That shift helps explain why economic experiment could advance more readily than political reform: results could be measured in output, while political change threatened Party control.

The four sectors had different starting conditions and time horizons. Agricultural modernization involved yields, irrigation, rural incentives, and scientific farming; industrial modernization involved equipment, management, energy, and efficiency; defense modernization required costly technical capabilities; and scientific and technological modernization depended on education and research institutions. Investment choices therefore created trade-offs. Spending scarce foreign exchange on a large plant might reduce resources available for agriculture or transport, and importing advanced machinery would accomplish little without trained workers. Treating the Four Modernizations as an interdependent program helps explain why educational reform, legal stabilization, and cautious economic readjustment accompanied the new policy direction.

Section 71.26

Democracy Wall, Political Criticism, and the Limits of Reform

From November 1978, citizens posted handwritten essays and posters on a brick wall at Xidan in central Beijing. Topics included Cultural Revolution grievances, the Tiananmen Incident, rehabilitation, democracy, human rights, and criticism of leaders. Unofficial journals such as Explorations and Beijing Spring appeared. Foreign journalists reported on the wall, and Deng initially gave tacit tolerance, in part because criticism served to pressure opponents of reform. In December 1978 Wei Jingsheng, a former Red Guard and electrician, posted "The Fifth Modernization," arguing that democracy was necessary for the success of the other four.

In March 1979 the climate shifted. Authorities arrested Wei Jingsheng and others, and on 30 March Deng gave a speech at a theory work conference affirming four principles that must be upheld: the socialist road, the people's democratic (or proletarian) dictatorship, the leadership of the Communist Party, and Marxism-Leninism-Mao Zedong Thought. Wei was tried in October 1979 on charges including counter-revolutionary propaganda and giving military information to foreigners, and was sentenced to fifteen years. Posting on the wall was restricted at the end of 1979 and moved to a park.

The Four Modernizations and Four Cardinal Principles must be distinguished: the first set development goals; the second set political limits. Together they define the character of the post-Mao reform: Party-led economic and institutional change with restricted political pluralism.

Democracy Wall offered a revealing test of the new leadership's commitment to open criticism. Some posters supported the dismantling of Mao-era practices and called for legal protection against arbitrary detention; others raised more fundamental questions about political power. These arguments did not form a single movement with one program. The government's response also changed. Initially, some criticism was tolerated because it supported the challenge to entrenched political practices. As activists pressed for independent political rights, the leadership treated them as threatening stability and Party authority. Deng's Four Cardinal Principles marked the boundary between participation in a Party-led modernization program and opposition to the Party's monopoly of power. The arrest of Wei Jingsheng exposed how quickly that boundary could be enforced.

Division VI — Early Rural Reform, Opening, and the New Development Strategy, 1977–1979

Purpose: to explain how new policy priorities began to generate concrete experiments before the broad economic reforms of the 1980s.

Section 71.27

Rural Conditions at the Beginning of Reform

In 1978 most Chinese people lived in the countryside, and agriculture was organized through the people's commune system created in 1958 and revised after the Great Leap famine (Chapter 68). Rural society was divided into communes, production brigades, and production teams, with the team, usually a village or part of one, serving as the basic unit for farming and distribution. Members earned "work points" for labor, and the value of a work point depended on the team's output. The state purchased grain and other products at fixed prices, and rural people had little ability to leave the countryside because of household registration controls.

The system had achieved some gains, including irrigation works, basic rural education and health networks, and the spread of improved seeds and fertilizer. But growth in rural incomes was slow, and per-capita grain output had risen little for many years. Official estimates later put the rural population below the national poverty line at roughly a quarter of a billion people. Incentives were weak because pay was only loosely tied to individual effort, supervision was costly, and teams were often subject to political direction in what to plant. Conditions varied: the Yangtze delta and parts of Sichuan and Guangdong were comparatively productive, while parts of Anhui, Gansu, Guizhou, and the northwest faced recurrent shortages.

Agricultural reform became central because of this poverty, because the state needed more food and raw materials for industrial modernization, and because rural conditions offered local leaders both motive and opportunity to experiment. Reform of the countryside also required little new state investment compared with industry, an important consideration given limited funds.

The economic stakes were substantial because communes varied greatly in soil quality, access to water, transport, and local market opportunities. Production teams carried collective obligations while villagers often cultivated small private plots and raised animals on the side. Income depended on work-point distribution and the amount of grain left after state procurement. Some farmers believed that collective work could be productive under capable management; others complained that effort and reward were too weakly connected. The pressure for change was therefore uneven. In more favorable areas the immediate priority might be better collective accounting, whereas very poor villages regarded household responsibility arrangements as a way to prevent hunger and make work worthwhile.

Section 71.28

Local Experiments in Anhui, Sichuan, and Other Provinces

Experiments with rural management began under provincial leaders who had been promoted or restored after 1976. Wan Li became Party first secretary of Anhui in 1977 and promoted a set of provincial rural policies that gave production teams more autonomy over planting and distribution and reduced political interference. When a severe drought struck in 1978, Anhui allowed peasants to farm "borrowed" plots to plant wheat for the next season. In Sichuan, Zhao Ziyang, first secretary from the mid-1970s, supported policies expanding the autonomy of teams and permitting private sideline production, and in industry the province piloted expanded enterprise autonomy beginning in 1978. Reports describe similar local arrangements in other provinces, including Guizhou and Inner Mongolia, though the quality of documentation varies.

Responsibility systems took different forms: assigning tasks to groups, linking pay to output, contracting land to small groups, or contracting to households. Some of these ideas had antecedents: in the 1950s and early 1960s, households or small groups had been allowed to contract land in places such as Zhejiang and Anhui, but those experiments were criticized and ended. In 1978–1979 the political environment changed, but the status of household contracting remained contested. A letter published in the People's Daily in March 1979 criticized the practice, and a Party decision of September 1979 on agricultural development still stated that land should not be divided among households, with exceptions for certain remote and poor areas. National approval of broader household contracting came later (Chapter 72).

The key point is the interaction between levels. Local cadres and villagers took risks; provincial leaders provided cover; the center sometimes tolerated and sometimes limited experiments; and the policies of 1978–1979, including higher procurement prices, altered incentives. No single actor created reform.

Responsibility systems should be defined precisely. A contract to a work group, with accounting retained at the production-team level, was not identical to allocating land management to individual households. Officials could tolerate one arrangement while denouncing another, even when both rewarded production above a target. The March 1979 public dispute over contracting demonstrates this uncertainty: the Zhang Hao letter in the People's Daily generated anxiety among local experimenters, and a subsequent newspaper response recognized that some of its claims had been too sweeping. Local experimentation thus proceeded through political negotiation and protection as much as through direct changes in economic incentives. The development of national approval in the early 1980s should remain with Chapter 72.

Section 71.29

Xiaogang Village and the Origins of Household Contracting

Xiaogang, a village in Fengyang County in northern Anhui, became a symbol of rural reform. The familiar account says that in the winter of 1978, after years of poverty and a severe drought, farmers of a production team of about twenty households secretly agreed to divide the team's land among households, each promising to deliver its share of the state quota and to keep the rest, and that they pledged to care for the families of any leaders punished for the arrangement. Later accounts distinguish approximately twenty households in the team from eighteen representatives said to have attended the initial meeting; the number of marks on the surviving agreement does not by itself prove that eighteen different households signed it on a single day. A team leader, Yan Hongchang, is often credited with leading the initiative. The arrangement is said to have raised output sharply in the following harvest.

Scholars have qualified this story. Other villages and counties had already tried household or group contracting; local officials, including Wan Li's provincial administration, gave varying degrees of protection; and aspects of the surviving documentation, including the dating and exact wording of the agreement and later reconstructions, have been questioned. The village's symbolic role was strengthened by later publicity and commemoration. Xiaogang is therefore best understood as an important local case that became emblematic, not as the single origin of national reform.

The distinction between local experiment and national policy matters. In 1978–1979, household contracting in Xiaogang and elsewhere was an exception, tolerated in some places. The nationwide Household Responsibility System developed after 1980 with central approval, and is treated in Chapter 72.

The numerical details of the Xiaogang agreement are a useful lesson in source criticism. Official museum accounts speak of twenty households in the production team, with eighteen represented at the meeting, whereas a published scholarly analysis describes twenty participants, including two cadres. The surviving document is commonly displayed with a date in December 1978, but later local accounts have identified a late-November meeting. Some scholars question the authenticity or reconstruction of the documentary artifact. These differences do not erase the evidence for local contracting; they show that celebrated national origin stories acquire simplified numbers and dates over time. Students should separate the contemporary experiment, the later physical document, and the commemorative narrative that turned Xiaogang into a national symbol.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Household in Early Rural Reform

The following is a composite scenario based on patterns reported in rural accounts; it is not a record of one family. Before contracting, a household's day began with a team bell calling members to work in groups, with work points recorded at day's end. After a contracting arrangement, the family plans its own planting and labor, with the husband and wife and older children dividing tasks. They must still deliver grain to the state and the team, but surplus can be kept or sold. The household may invest more effort in fields, fertilizer, and sideline production, such as pigs or vegetables, but also carries risks of crop failure and dependence on family labor. Women's work in farming and household production increased in visibility, but decisions often remained under male household heads. Some families gained while others, with less labor or poorer land, struggled.

Historical Evidence

Xiaogang's 1978 Agreement and Later Accounts

Sources include the agreement itself (a handwritten document, later displayed in museums), local Party and county records, official reports on Anhui's rural policy, journalistic accounts from the 1980s onward, memoirs of participants, and interviews. These sources differ in date and purpose. Contemporary documents show local practices but may be incomplete; later accounts, produced when Xiaogang had become a national symbol, may simplify or dramatize events and may reflect commemorative aims. Researchers have noted uncertainty about when the surviving text was written and how it relates to earlier arrangements. A careful historian compares sources, notes who produced them, and avoids stating disputed details as established fact.

Section 71.30

The First Steps Toward Opening China's Economy

External opening began in 1978 with exploratory visits and agreements. In May–June 1978 a senior delegation led by Vice-Premier Gu Mu visited several West European countries to study technology and management and discuss trade and credit. In October Deng visited Japan, toured industrial plants, and met business leaders; in November he visited Southeast Asian states including Singapore. Late in 1978 China concluded major contracts, including with Japanese firms for the Baoshan steel complex near Shanghai. The Third Plenum communiqué endorsed expanding economic cooperation with other countries on terms of equality and mutual benefit.

In Guangdong, local officials and entrepreneurs from Hong Kong began processing and assembly arrangements in which foreign partners supplied materials, equipment, and orders and Chinese factories supplied land and labor. A handbag factory in Dongguan is often cited as an early example in 1978. In July 1979 the National People's Congress passed the Joint Venture Law, creating a legal framework for equity joint ventures with foreign firms, and the Party center issued a document granting Guangdong and Fujian special policies and flexible measures in foreign trade and investment, including the idea of export-oriented special zones. In Shenzhen's Shekou area, a zone for industry was begun in 1979 by a Hong Kong-linked state enterprise. The formal designation of Special Economic Zones in 1980 and their development belong to Chapter 72.

It is important to separate three stages in external opening. First came study missions and negotiations that helped officials identify new technologies and methods. Second came national legislation and local policy permissions, such as the 1979 equity joint-venture law and the special flexibility granted to Guangdong and Fujian. Third came the establishment and growth of Special Economic Zones, which should be discussed in Chapter 72. Even in the first two stages, opening was not a simple abandonment of state planning: officials negotiated trade, investment, and technology arrangements while retaining central control over major decisions. Hong Kong-based enterprises and overseas Chinese commercial links mattered because they offered nearby sources of capital and practical business experience.

Section 71.31

Diplomatic Reorientation and the International Setting of Reform

On 15 December 1978 the United States and the PRC announced that they would establish formal diplomatic relations from 1 January 1979. The United States recognized the PRC as the government of China, ended formal diplomatic relations and its defense treaty with the Republic of China on Taiwan, and maintained unofficial ties. The U.S. Congress passed the Taiwan Relations Act in April 1979, which set out a framework for continuing relations with Taiwan. On 1 January 1979 the PRC's National People's Congress Standing Committee issued a "Message to Compatriots in Taiwan," proposing contact and exchange and signaling a reduced emphasis on military confrontation. Deng visited the United States from 28 January to 5 February 1979, signing agreements on science, education, and consular matters.

Relations with Japan also developed. A Treaty of Peace and Friendship was signed in August 1978, and Japan later became an important source of loans and technology. Meanwhile, tension with the Soviet Union and Vietnam intensified. Vietnam signed a treaty with the Soviet Union in November 1978 and invaded Cambodia in late December, overthrowing the Khmer Rouge government that China had supported. In February–March 1979 China launched a limited military campaign into northern Vietnam, which it described as a punitive action. Both sides suffered substantial casualties, estimates of which differ, and the campaign revealed shortcomings in the PLA that contributed to later military reform. China also announced that it would not renew the 1950 Sino-Soviet treaty.

Normalization with Washington was internationally important but did not dissolve the Taiwan dispute. The United States changed its diplomatic recognition and continued unofficial relations with Taiwan under a new domestic legal framework. At the same time, Chinese relations with Japan created opportunities for technology and financing that were directly related to industrial modernization. Relations with Vietnam and the Soviet Union demonstrate the limits of a narrative focused only on cooperation with the West: the 1979 conflict was costly and politically significant, and Beijing's decisions were shaped by regional security concerns as well as reform objectives. Domestic economic opening and international strategic confrontation therefore developed simultaneously rather than successively.

China and the Wider World

The Strategic Setting of Opening

The opening was not only an economic choice. Normalization with the United States reduced a major source of strategic hostility, gave access to technology and markets, and fit with the Chinese leadership's concern about the Soviet Union. Relations with Japan provided credit and industrial equipment. Regional events, including the Vietnamese invasion of Cambodia and the border war, show that security concerns shaped the period and that China was an active participant, not simply a recipient of foreign influence. China's leaders pursued diplomacy, negotiated terms, and used military force according to their own assessments. The Cold War context offered both opportunities and constraints, and the decision to open to foreign capital and technology reflected the leadership's view that modernization required international cooperation.

Section 71.32

From the Post-Mao Transition to Reform and Opening

By the end of 1979 the Party had redirected its priorities, rehabilitated many people, begun to rebuild legal institutions, and approved early steps in agriculture and external opening. In April 1979 a Central Work Conference adopted a policy of "readjusting, restructuring, consolidating, and improving" the economy, reflecting concern about imbalances and the large deficits created by the accelerated plan. This was a cautious move that slowed some imported projects and emphasized agriculture and light industry. In September 1979 the Fourth Plenum approved a decision on accelerating agricultural development, and Ye Jianying, in a speech marking the thirtieth anniversary of the PRC, described the Cultural Revolution in strongly negative terms, a sign of a developing official reassessment.

Yet much remained unresolved. Hua was still chairman. The status of Mao had not been settled, and the 1981 Resolution lay ahead. Household contracting was contested. Prices were controlled. State enterprises operated under the plan, and most foreign trade was in state hands. Political opposition was limited by the Four Cardinal Principles. Chapter 72 follows the development of the Household Responsibility System, township and village enterprises, Special Economic Zones, foreign trade and investment, and price reform; Chapter 73 examines the tensions that emerged by 1989.

The year 1979 is best understood as an open-ended turning point rather than the completion of a new economic model. National officials still disagreed over investment speed, local experiments remained politically controversial, and the precise limits of legal and political criticism were being tested. The 1979 readjustment illustrates that reform included restraints on overambitious industrial projects as well as new incentives and international contacts. The transition was durable because it gradually connected leadership changes, revised political judgments, legal institutions, schools, and rural experimentation; it was incomplete because later decisions would still determine the reach of household contracting, enterprise autonomy, foreign investment, and Party control. Chapter 72 should therefore begin with implementation and expansion, not with the claim that nothing had changed before 1980.

Division VII — Final Review

Section 71.33

Chapter Review and Post-Mao Transition Checkpoint

Central question: How did the Communist Party preserve its political monopoly while replacing central elements of Mao-era governance and initiating a new economic development strategy?

Students should distinguish four things: a change of leaders (the arrests of October 1976 and later changes in office), a change in political legitimacy (from fidelity to Mao's instructions toward practice and results), institutional rehabilitation (review of cases, reopening of schools, legal codes), and the beginning of economic reform (price incentives, local experiments, external opening). These processes overlapped but did not coincide, and none of them eliminated Party control of politics.

Chapter Summary

The years after Mao Zedong's death in September 1976 were a transition, not a single event. The arrest of Jiang Qing, Zhang Chunqiao, Wang Hongwen, and Yao Wenyuan on 6 October 1976 removed the most prominent radical leaders, but it was an action within the leadership, not a popular or constitutional change, and it did not by itself define a new governing system.

Hua Guofeng, who held the top offices, tried to restore order, revive economic development, and preserve Mao's legitimacy. His program contained real elements of recovery and modernization, but its dependence on Mao's authority, expressed in the "Two Whatevers," limited its ability to address past injustices. Historians debate whether Hua was mainly an obstacle or a transitional enabler, and the evidence supports a more complex picture than a simple contrast with Deng.

Deng Xiaoping's restoration in 1977 and the return of veteran officials created new centers of influence. Deng's rise was gradual and did not rest on formal succession: he regained offices, but Hua remained chairman until 1981. The shift in political direction began in areas that did not directly challenge the leadership's legitimacy, such as education, science, and expertise, and then developed into an ideological debate over practice as the criterion of truth.

The restoration of the college entrance examination in 1977 and the National Science Conference of 1978 reflected a change in how knowledge and expertise were valued. They affected millions of people, opened opportunities, and also revealed inequalities in access. These changes show that the transition involved social and intellectual transformations as well as high politics.

The Central Work Conference and the Third Plenum of December 1978 were the political turning point. They redirected the Party's central task from class struggle to socialist modernization, endorsed reexamination of historical cases, and supported measures such as higher procurement prices. At the same time, the plenum did not create a market economy, authorize national household contracting, or transfer all authority to Deng.

Rehabilitation and institutional reconstruction were central to the period. Verdicts on the Tiananmen Incident and on numerous officials and intellectuals were reversed, former Rightists were cleared, and legal institutions and codes were rebuilt in 1978–1980. Yet review was controlled by the Party, remained incomplete, and left many families without full redress, while the legal system remained subordinate to Party leadership.

The Four Modernizations and the Four Cardinal Principles together define the character of the transition: a commitment to economic development and institutional order, combined with firm limits on political challenge. The treatment of Democracy Wall and Wei Jingsheng in 1979 shows how those limits were enforced, and historians interpret the reasons differently.

Early rural experiments in Anhui, Sichuan, and elsewhere, including Xiaogang, and the first steps of external opening in Guangdong and Fujian, show how local initiative and national policy interacted. These developments were not yet the national reforms of the 1980s, but they created experience and precedents. International events, including U.S.–PRC normalization, relations with Japan, and the Sino-Vietnamese War, shaped both opportunity and constraint.

The 1976–1981 sequence also shows the difference between contemporary criticism and later official interpretation. The 1981 Party resolution, which judged Mao's contributions primary and his mistakes secondary and condemned the Cultural Revolution, reflected a compromise reached after years of debate, not the consensus of 1978.

For the larger history of Chinese civilization, this chapter marks the transition from the Mao era to the reform era without treating either as a seamless whole. It illustrates how change can occur through reinterpretation, personnel change, institutional rebuilding, and experiment, and how political continuity can coexist with major shifts in policy. It prepares for Chapter 72's account of the reforms of the 1980s and Chapter 73's account of the tensions of 1989.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it explains how a political system changed direction without ending its own rule. The transition combined real correction of injustices, the rebuilding of institutions, and a new emphasis on development, within limits set by the Party. Understanding that combination is essential to interpreting the reform era.

The chapter shaped later Chinese history by establishing priorities, personnel, and precedents: the emphasis on practical results, the rehabilitation of expertise, early legal codes, rural incentives, and external opening. It also left unresolved questions about Mao's legacy, the scope of law, and the boundaries of political criticism, which reappear in Chapters 72 and 73.

The chapter shows themes that run through the textbook: the relationship between state and society, the importance of local initiative, the role of legitimacy and ideology, and the interaction of domestic change with global conditions. It also shows that historical change often proceeds through reinterpretation and negotiation, not only rupture.

Finally, it helps students see Chinese history as diverse, contested, and unfinished. The transition was shaped by many people, including leaders, officials, intellectuals, students, farmers, petitioners, and activists, and its meaning continues to be debated. Reading the evidence carefully, and separating what happened from how it was later remembered, is the task the chapter asks of its readers.
