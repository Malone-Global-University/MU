Part IX — Nineteenth-Century Crisis and the End of the Qing
55

The Sino-Japanese War and Foreign Imperialism

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

In February 1895, the warships of the Beiyang Fleet, the proudest product of three decades of Qing self-strengthening, lay trapped in the harbor of Weihaiwei, their land forts in enemy hands and their commander negotiating terms of surrender. Within three years, the same coastline would be dotted with foreign naval bases.

The commander at Weihaiwei was Admiral Ding Ruchang, a former cavalry officer from Anhui who had risen through the Huai Army networks of Li Hongzhang and been entrusted with a modern fleet. In the first days of February 1895, Japanese troops who had landed on the Shandong coast had captured the forts overlooking the harbor and turned their guns against the ships. Japanese torpedo boats slipped past the defensive boom in the darkness and badly damaged the fleet's flagship, the battleship Dingyuan, which grounded and was subsequently scuttled by its crew to prevent capture. With the situation hopeless, Ding authorized surrender terms on 12 February and then took his own life; surviving officers completed the formal surrender, and the transfer of the fleet and installations was concluded on 17 February. His death, and the fate of the fleet he commanded, became one of the most powerful images in modern Chinese historical memory: a symbol of the defeat of an entire reform program, and of a state that seemed to have been caught unprepared by a neighbor it had long regarded as junior.

This chapter studies the First Sino-Japanese War of 1894–1895 and the wave of foreign imperial pressure that followed it in the late 1890s. It asks how a dispute over the stability of Korea, a kingdom that had been bound to the Qing by centuries of ritual and diplomatic relationship, escalated into a war that reshaped the East Asian order; why the Qing lost; what the Treaty of Shimonoseki changed; and how the defeat of 1895 opened a period in which Germany, Russia, Britain, France, Japan, and other powers competed for leaseholds, railway concessions, mining rights, loans, and spheres of influence. It also studies the people on whom these decisions fell: Korean peasants who had risen against their own government, Chinese soldiers and sailors, Taiwanese families whose island was transferred from one empire to another without their consent, Chinese Christians and the neighbors who resented them, merchants in the treaty ports, and the reform-minded scholars who concluded that the survival of the Qing state required deeper institutional change.

The war matters because it changed both power and perception. It ended the long-standing, though always flexible, Qing position as the principal ceremonial and diplomatic patron of the Korean court. It transferred Taiwan to Japan, beginning a colonial relationship that would last until 1945. It imposed an indemnity of 200 million taels, forcing the Qing into heavy foreign borrowing. And it revealed that the institutional weaknesses of the Qing state, including divided command, regionally financed armies, inadequate logistics, and fragile finance, could not be solved by the purchase of ships and guns alone. These were the lessons that Chapter 54 prepared students to see: the Self-Strengthening Movement had produced real shipyards, arsenals, telegraphs, and trained personnel, and the war tested whether those achievements had been integrated into a coherent national system.

At the same time, students should resist two simplifications. The first is the idea that the defeat was simply the result of Chinese rejection of modernity. Qing officials had spent thirty years importing and adapting foreign technology, and the Japanese victory depended on many other factors as well, including state mobilization, naval doctrine, financing, command coordination, and contingency. The second is the idea that China was divided into colonies after 1895. It was not. The Qing state continued to govern the great majority of its territory and population, collected taxes, appointed officials, and conducted diplomacy. What changed was that its sovereignty became increasingly encumbered by treaties, leases, concessions, railway and mining rights, foreign loans secured on customs revenue, missionary privileges, and the strategic ambitions of competing powers. Understanding the difference between formal colonial rule, as in Taiwan after 1895, and constrained sovereignty, as in most of mainland China, is a central task of this chapter.

What continued through this period was equally important. The Qing court continued to rule from Beijing under the Guangxu Emperor, with the Empress Dowager Cixi retaining influence behind the scenes. Provincial governors-general such as Li Hongzhang, Zhang Zhidong, and Liu Kunyi continued to wield substantial regional power. Rural society continued to organize itself around families, lineages, villages, markets, and local religious institutions. And the habit of balancing powers against one another, which Qing diplomats had practiced for decades, continued, though with fewer options than before. What changed was the political atmosphere: from 1895 onward, Chinese scholars, officials, and ordinary readers increasingly spoke of national peril, partition (guafen, literally "carving up like a melon"), and the need for fundamental reform.

Historians approach these events through an unusually wide range of evidence: treaty texts and diplomatic correspondence in several languages, the memorials and telegrams of Li Hongzhang and other officials, Japanese military and naval records, accounts by foreign journalists and advisers, Korean court and local records, Donghak writings, Taiwanese local sources, missionary reports and mission-station archives, the records of the Imperial Maritime Customs, reform petitions and periodicals, and contemporary illustrations and cartoons. Each preserves a particular point of view, and this chapter teaches students to ask whose view that is.

The chapter also connects Chinese history to the wider history of Asia and the world. The war belongs to the history of Meiji Japan's rise, the transformation of Korea, the competition of European empires at the end of the nineteenth century, and the global scramble for naval bases, railways, and markets. The chapter ends on the threshold of the crisis that Chapter 56 will examine: the Boxer movement and the Qing court's catastrophic confrontation with the foreign powers in 1900. Chapter 55 explains the conditions that made that crisis possible, but it does not narrate it.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how Qing, Japanese, and Korean understandings of Korea's status differed before 1894 and why those differences made crisis possible.
  2. Trace the sequence of the First Sino-Japanese War from the Donghak crisis through Pyongyang, the Yalu, Port Arthur, and Weihaiwei.
  3. Analyze the military, financial, organizational, and political causes of Qing defeat without reducing them to a single factor such as technology.
  4. Evaluate competing historical explanations for the Qing defeat using the evidence presented in the Historical Debate feature.
  5. Interpret key provisions of the Treaty of Shimonoseki as a primary source, including the meaning of Korean "independence."
  6. Describe the Triple Intervention and explain how it altered the balance of power in East Asia.
  7. Assess the experiences of Korean, Taiwanese, and Chinese communities during and after the war, including the establishment of Japanese colonial rule in Taiwan.
  8. Distinguish spheres of influence, leased territories, treaty-port concessions, and formal colonies as different forms of foreign power.
  9. Analyze how railway concessions, mining rights, loans, and customs security constrained Qing sovereignty without eliminating it.
  10. Contextualize missionary conflict as the product of treaty privileges, property disputes, local politics, and religious difference rather than irrational hostility.
  11. Connect the sovereignty crisis to the Hundred Days Reform of 1898 and the conditions that preceded the Boxer crisis.
  12. Compare Chinese, Japanese, Korean, and Western perspectives on the war and the scramble for concessions.

Historical Context

To understand 1894, students must recall the long arc of Qing engagement with the world covered in Chapters 51 through 54. The Opium Wars had established the treaty-port system and extraterritoriality; the mid-century rebellions had devastated the Yangtze valley and the northwest and had shifted military and fiscal power toward regional officials; and the Self-Strengthening Movement had tried to rebuild Qing capacity through arsenals, shipyards, telegraph lines, a coal and steamship business, a foreign affairs bureau, and new schools. By the early 1890s the Qing was neither a collapsing state nor a static one. It had a large, fiscally stretched empire of several hundred million people, a customs service that generated reliable revenue, a growing treaty-port economy, and a handful of provincial leaders, above all Li Hongzhang, who controlled significant modern military assets.

The East Asian international environment was also changing. For centuries, relations between the Chinese courts and neighboring rulers in Korea, the Ryukyu Islands, Vietnam, and elsewhere had involved ritual hierarchy, investiture, and periodic embassies, combined with extensive practical diplomacy and trade. These relationships were not identical, and they were not a single rigid "tributary system"; they allowed neighboring rulers considerable autonomy in internal affairs, and in practice they were adapted to circumstances. Meiji Japan, after 1868, built a conscript army, a navy, a constitutional government (1889–1890), a general staff, and a fiscal system capable of financing war, and it entered the contest over Korea with a very different conception of international order, one derived from European treaty and sovereignty norms. By the 1880s, Qing and Japanese leaders were both attempting to define Korea's position in ways that served their own strategic interests.

Geography shaped the conflict. The Korean peninsula lies between Manchuria, the Qing dynasty's northeastern homeland, and the Japanese archipelago; Japanese strategists had long described it as a dagger pointed at Japan, and Qing officials regarded it as a protective buffer for the Manchurian homeland and for Beijing's sea approaches through the Bohai Gulf. The Liaodong peninsula, with the naval base of Port Arthur (Lüshun), and the Shandong peninsula, with Weihaiwei, guarded the entrance to the Bohai Gulf and the approach to Tianjin and Beijing. These locations became the main theaters of the war and, later, the principal targets of foreign leaseholds.

Finally, students should understand what "China" meant in this context. The Qing empire of 1894 was a multiethnic state governed by a Manchu dynasty, including the provinces of the Han Chinese heartland, Manchuria, Mongolia, Xinjiang, Tibet, and Taiwan (made a separate province in 1885). In the 1890s, the word "China" was beginning to take on the modern meaning of a nation among nations, but this was a contested and still-developing idea. Reformers, officials, and the court did not all use the same vocabulary or share the same sense of what was being lost in 1895. As the chapter proceeds, it examines how those ideas shifted under the pressure of defeat.

Section 55.1

China, Japan, Korea, and the East Asian Order Before 1894

For several centuries the Joseon dynasty of Korea (1392–1897) had maintained a relationship with the successive courts in Beijing that combined ritual deference, regular embassies, investiture of Korean kings, and substantial autonomy. Korean rulers received a calendar and a patent of investiture from the Chinese emperor; Korean envoys traveled to Beijing each year, and these missions were also occasions for trade and intellectual exchange. Yet Korea administered its own government, taxation, law, and foreign relations to a large extent, and the Joseon elite cultivated a strong sense of its own Confucian civilization. After the Manchu conquest of the Ming, many Korean scholars considered Joseon, rather than the Manchu court, to be the true custodian of Ming-style cultural orthodoxy, even as the court continued the practical relationship with the Qing. The relationship was therefore a layered one: ritually hierarchical, politically flexible, and interpreted differently by different actors.

Historical Vocabulary
Suzerainty, Investiture, and Sovereignty

Suzerainty is a Western diplomatic term for a relationship in which a dominant ruler holds ceremonial or limited political authority over a nominally autonomous state. Qing documents used a vocabulary of ritual relationship (including the language of the "small" serving the "great") rather than a single fixed term, and the practical content varied by place and period. Investiture was the ceremony by which a ruler's succession was recognized by the Chinese court. Sovereignty, in the sense used in nineteenth-century international law, meant exclusive authority over a defined territory and its foreign relations.

The crisis of 1894 arose in part because these vocabularies did not match. Qing officials increasingly sought to turn ritual ties into stronger, more measurable control, especially after 1882. Japanese leaders insisted on describing Korea as an independent state in treaty-law terms. Korean officials used both languages as circumstances required.

Meiji Japan, after 1868, approached the region differently. It proclaimed the end of the shogunate, built a conscript army (1873), sent a military expedition to Taiwan in 1874, and in 1879 annexed the Ryukyu Kingdom, which had maintained ties with both Japan's Satsuma domain and the Qing. In 1876 Japan used the threat of force, following a naval incident near Ganghwa Island, to impose the Treaty of Ganghwa on Korea, modeled on the unequal treaties that Western powers had imposed on Japan. Its first article described Korea as an independent state with rights equal to Japan's. Qing officials did not regard this language as ending the Qing-Korean relationship, and Korean officials, who had opened the first treaty ports under pressure, were divided over how far to cooperate with Japan.

After 1876 the Qing shifted from a hands-off approach toward more active engagement. Li Hongzhang encouraged Korea to sign treaties with the United States (1882) and other Western states as a way of limiting Japanese influence, while asserting in documents the dependent status of Korea. After a mutiny of Korean soldiers in Seoul in 1882 (the Imo Incident), in which Japanese legation staff were attacked, Qing troops were sent to restore order, and the Qing and Korea signed regulations granting Chinese merchants special privileges in Korea. In 1884, a coup by Korean reformers (the Gapsin Coup), backed by Japanese forces, was suppressed by Qing troops under Yuan Shikai's circle of commanders. The two empires nearly came to war, but in 1885 Li Hongzhang and Itō Hirobumi signed the Convention of Tianjin, committing both to withdraw their forces and to notify each other in advance before sending troops again. From 1885, Yuan Shikai served as the Qing's resident official in Seoul, with a wide-ranging role.

Korea itself was not a passive object. The court of King Gojong and Queen Min included conservative officials, reformers inspired by Japan or by Western learning, and ministers who looked to Russia, the United States, or China for protection, and many of them tried to play powers against one another. Korean peasants bore the cost of corrupt local officials, heavy tax collection, and the penetration of foreign merchants, particularly in rice and grain markets. Students should therefore avoid describing Korea only as a "buffer" or "prize"; Korean political actors had their own goals, divisions, and strategies, and the crisis of 1894 began with a Korean movement, not a Chinese or Japanese one.

Section 55.2

The Donghak Rebellion and the Crisis of 1894

The Donghak ("Eastern Learning") movement was founded by Choe Je-u in the 1860s as a religious tradition that drew on Confucian, Buddhist, Daoist, and folk elements and presented itself as an indigenous alternative to "Western Learning," the term then applied to Catholicism. Choe was executed by the Korean government in 1864, but the movement continued to spread among rural communities in the south, where it combined spiritual teaching with moral critique of corrupt officials and of social inequality. In the early 1890s Donghak followers petitioned for the rehabilitation of their founder and for the right to practice openly, and some groups, especially in the Jeolla provinces, began to organize more militantly.

In early 1894 a local official in Gobu, in the southwest, was accused of imposing excessive levies, including charges for a reservoir. A local Donghak leader, Jeon Bong-jun, led an armed uprising that spread across the province, defeated government forces, and in late May captured Jeonju, the provincial capital and a city of great symbolic importance as the ancestral home of the Joseon royal family. Fearing that its own forces could not suppress the rebels, the Korean court, influenced by the Min faction, asked the Qing for help in early June. The Qing, bound by the Convention of Tianjin, notified Japan, and sent roughly two thousand troops, landing them at Asan on the western coast. Japan, citing the same convention, dispatched a much larger force, eventually several thousand soldiers, to Incheon and Seoul.

Within days, the original rationale for the foreign deployments had disappeared. The Donghak forces and the Korean government agreed to a truce in mid-June, and the rebels dispersed. Korean officials asked both powers to withdraw. But Japan's cabinet, led by Prime Minister Itō Hirobumi and Foreign Minister Mutsu Munemitsu, chose to keep its troops in place and proposed a joint Sino-Japanese program of reform for Korea. The Qing responded that Korea's internal reform was a matter for Korea, and that both armies should leave. Japanese leaders, who had been struggling with a hostile Diet at home, judged that a confrontation might both secure Japanese influence in Korea and strengthen the government politically. Their diplomatic position was also improved by a new treaty with Britain, signed in July 1894, which promised to end British extraterritorial privileges in Japan.

On the Qing side, the confrontation reflected a mixture of confidence and miscalculation. Li Hongzhang expected that the great powers, especially Russia and Britain, would restrain Japan through diplomacy, and he was reluctant to risk the Beiyang forces he had spent decades building. A group of officials and scholars in Beijing, often called the "pure stream" faction, along with the Guangxu Emperor and his tutor Weng Tonghe, pressed for a firm response, and they believed that China's larger size would prevail. The result was a classic escalation: neither government wished a long war, but each judged that withdrawing first would carry costs in prestige and strategic position. The simultaneous deployments produced a confrontation, not an automatic war; the war began because both governments made choices, and Japan's choices, supported by prior planning, were the more deliberate.

Section 55.3

War Begins: Korea, Asan, and Pyongyang

On 23 July 1894, Japanese troops entered the royal palace in Seoul, seized King Gojong, and installed the king's father, the Heungseon Daewongun, as nominal head of a new government favorable to Japanese demands. This action, essentially a coup, gave Japan a Korean authority to "request" the expulsion of Qing forces. Two days later, off Pungdo Island, Japanese warships attacked Qing vessels, and the Japanese cruiser Naniwa sank the British-chartered transport Kowshing, which was carrying more than a thousand Qing soldiers; most of the troops aboard died. On 29 July Japanese forces defeated the Qing army at Seonghwan and then at Asan. Both governments formally declared war on 1 August 1894.

For ordinary Koreans, the war meant the arrival of two foreign armies on their soil, the requisitioning of food, porters, and animals, and the disruption of local administration. Japan imposed a series of reform measures through the Korean government, known as the Gabo Reforms, which abolished the traditional status hierarchy, restructured ministries, and changed the examination system and the fiscal structure; some Korean reformers welcomed these measures, though many Koreans resented their imposition under foreign military pressure. In late 1894, the Donghak movement rose again, this time against Japanese influence, and was suppressed with great loss of life by combined Japanese and Korean government forces; Jeon Bong-jun was captured and executed in 1895. Estimates of the dead vary and are uncertain, but they reach into the tens of thousands.

The next major engagement came at Pyongyang in mid-September 1894. Qing forces, perhaps fifteen thousand men, had concentrated in the old walled city in the north, but they did not have a single commander with authority over all units. Several generals, drawn from different regional armies, held separate commands, and their coordination was poor. Japanese forces converged on the city from several directions, and on 15 September launched a coordinated attack. After a day of fighting, in heavy rain, the Qing commander Ye Zhichao ordered a withdrawal, and the retreat, in the night, turned into a rout; one senior general, Zuo Baogui, was killed. Qing forces withdrew northward and recrossed the Yalu, leaving Korea under Japanese military control. The Battle of Pyongyang not only shattered the Qing position in Korea but also foreshadowed organizational issues that later battles would reveal: separate armies, separate supply systems, and unclear authority.

Section 55.4

The Naval War and the Battle of the Yalu

On 17 September 1894, two days after Pyongyang, the Beiyang Fleet, under Admiral Ding Ruchang, which had been escorting troop transports to the mouth of the Yalu, met the Japanese Combined Fleet under Vice Admiral Itō Sukeyuki near Haiyang Island. The Beiyang Fleet had been formally organized in 1888 and included two German-built battleships, the Dingyuan and Zhenyuan, armed with heavy 12-inch guns and thick armor, along with cruisers purchased from Britain and Germany and domestic vessels from Fuzhou. The Japanese fleet had fewer heavy guns but was faster on average, included newer cruisers such as the Yoshino, and had many more quick-firing guns, which allowed a much higher rate of fire against the lightly protected parts of Qing ships.

The battle lasted about five hours. The Qing fleet advanced in a roughly line-abreast formation, with its strongest ships in the center, a formation chosen to take advantage of the forward-firing heavy guns; the Japanese ships moved in a column and circled the Qing line, bringing concentrated fire on the weaker wings. Early in the action, a blast from the Dingyuan's own guns damaged its bridge and injured Ding Ruchang, disrupting signaling. Qing ships suffered severe losses, with five vessels sunk or destroyed, among them the cruiser Zhiyuan, whose captain, Deng Shichang, died in the battle, in accounts that later became part of Chinese patriotic memory. Several Japanese ships, including the flagship Matsushima, were badly damaged, but none was sunk. The surviving Qing ships withdrew to Port Arthur and eventually to Weihaiwei, and the Japanese fleet gained effective control of the sea approaches.

The battle is commonly read as a contest between modern and backward navies, but that reading is too simple. Both fleets used steam power, steel hulls, breech-loading guns, and torpedoes, and Qing ships had some advantages in armor and big guns. The key differences lay elsewhere. After 1888, the Qing had added very few ships, while Japan continued to expand and modernize its fleet and to equip it with large numbers of quick-firing guns. The Qing fleet's ammunition supply was uncertain; maintenance and coal supplies were uneven; and gunnery practice was limited. Training had been shaped by a patchwork of foreign advisers, and Ding Ruchang himself had begun his career in the army, not the navy, though he was an experienced administrator. Above all, the fleet was an instrument of Li Hongzhang's regional command rather than of a national navy: the southern fleets of Nanyang, Fujian, and Guangdong did not join the Beiyang forces, and Li, who feared losing the fleet on which his political position depended, ordered a cautious strategy after the Yalu. This cautious posture surrendered the initiative. Chapter 54's account of the Self-Strengthening achievements helps explain why the Qing possessed a modern fleet; the Yalu showed that a modern fleet was not the same as a modern naval system.

Section 55.5

The War Moves into Manchuria: Liaodong and Port Arthur

With naval control secured, Japan was able to invade the Qing homeland. In late October 1894, the Japanese First Army under Yamagata Aritomo crossed the Yalu River and took the Qing defensive positions at Jiuliancheng, and then advanced into southern Manchuria. At the same time, the Second Army under Ōyama Iwao landed on the Liaodong peninsula, near Huayuankou, without significant opposition. Jinzhou fell on 6 November, and Dalian, whose forts the Qing defenders had abandoned with their guns and maps still in place, fell the next day. Qing commanders, unprepared for an amphibious landing in a region many had thought secure, often retreated without a fight.

Port Arthur (Lüshun), the principal naval base and dockyard of the Beiyang forces, was a heavily fortified harbor built over more than a decade with foreign engineering expertise. Its defenses were formidable on paper, but many senior officers fled before the attack, the garrison lacked unified command, and the Japanese assault of 21 November overwhelmed the forts in a single day. In the following days, Japanese soldiers killed large numbers of people in the town, including civilians and prisoners. Foreign correspondents who were present, and military attachés and doctors, wrote accounts that were published in the United States and Europe and caused an international scandal. The exact number killed remains disputed. Contemporary foreign accounts and later reconstructions produced widely differing totals, often in the thousands, and historians disagree over how many victims were civilians, prisoners, disguised or retreating soldiers, and other combatants. What is not seriously in doubt is that Japanese troops killed prisoners and civilians after taking the city and that the episode produced an international scandal. Historians continue to examine Japanese military records, press accounts, and Chinese testimony to explain the causes, including battlefield anger, command failure, and the extent of any official tolerance of the killings. The Japanese government, which was then seeking revision of its unequal treaties with Western powers and wished to present itself as a civilized state, attempted to minimize and manage the controversy.

For the Qing, the fall of Port Arthur was a strategic disaster. It deprived the fleet of its principal repair facility, exposed Liaodong and Mukden (the old Manchu capital) to attack, and brought the war to the Manchu homeland. For civilians in southern Manchuria, the winter of 1894–1895 brought forced labor, requisitions, flight, and fear. The war showed that Japan could carry out sustained operations across the sea in difficult weather, supplied by a well-organized military administration, while the Qing forces, mostly regional armies drawn from different provinces, often fought without coordination and with little confidence in their commanders.

Section 55.6

Weihaiwei, the Beiyang Fleet, and Qing Defeat

The remaining ships of the Beiyang Fleet withdrew to Weihaiwei, a fortified harbor on the northern coast of the Shandong peninsula, protected by forts on the mainland and on Liugong Island at the harbor mouth. In January 1895 the Japanese Second Army, escorted by the Combined Fleet, landed at Yongcheng (Rongcheng) on the eastern tip of the peninsula, in bitter winter weather, and advanced overland to attack the forts from behind. By early February, the shore batteries were in Japanese hands, and captured guns were used against the Qing ships. Japanese torpedo boats attacked the harbor on the nights of 4–5 February, penetrating the defensive boom and sinking or crippling several vessels. The Dingyuan was badly damaged by a torpedo, grounded, and was subsequently scuttled by its crew to prevent capture.

Trapped, with ammunition running short and foreign advisers and some subordinate officers urging surrender, Ding Ruchang authorized surrender terms on 12 February. He and other senior officers took their own lives before the formal transfer, and surviving officers completed the surrender of the remaining ships, the Liugong Island garrison, and the installations, which was concluded on 17 February. The Japanese admiral Itō Sukeyuki, who had known Ding before the war, permitted the vessel Kangji to carry the bodies of the officers away. Historians have noted that Ding's decision has been interpreted very differently: as an honorable attempt to save the lives of the men under his command, or as a failure of will. What is clear is that the fall of Weihaiwei ended organized naval resistance and left the Qing without a fleet capable of contesting Japanese control of the Bohai Gulf.

In March, Japanese forces captured Yingkou and Niuzhuang in southern Manchuria, and later in the month seized the Pescadores (Penghu), the strategic island group in the Taiwan Strait, putting them in a position to demand Taiwan at the peace table. By then the Qing had begun seeking peace. A first mission of Zhang Yinhuan and Shao Youlian was rejected in early February because the envoys lacked full negotiating authority. The court then turned to Li Hongzhang, who had been temporarily stripped of some honors in the autumn for the military failures but who remained the only official able to negotiate with Itō and Mutsu. Li traveled to Japan in March 1895, a seventy-one-year-old official representing an empire in defeat.

Section 55.7

Why Did the Qing Lose?

The speed and scale of the defeat surprised contemporaries, and historians have offered many explanations. It is useful to separate several levels of analysis. At the level of command, the Qing had no unified command structure for the war. The Beiyang Army and Fleet were Li Hongzhang's personal responsibility, but other regional armies, including forces from Anhui, Hunan, Shandong, and Manchuria, were raised, paid, and led separately, and local commanders often preferred to preserve their units rather than risk them. At Pyongyang, as noted, no single general held authority; in the navy, coordination with the southern fleets was never achieved. Japan, by contrast, had a general staff, an Imperial Headquarters established in Hiroshima under the emperor's authority, and an integrated army-navy campaign plan.

At the level of logistics and finance, the Qing relied on provincial revenues and on a central treasury that was already burdened by the costs of suppressing rebellions, flood relief, frontier defense in Xinjiang, and the 1884–1885 Sino-French War. The Qing government raised money for the war through domestic loans, borrowing from foreign banks, and contributions from provinces and merchants, but the sums arrived slowly and unevenly. Japan, which had a smaller population but a more centralized tax system, floated domestic war bonds, mobilized its parliament behind the war, and financed operations at a speed the Qing could not match. Supplies of ammunition, coal, medical care, and winter equipment were also better organized on the Japanese side, though Japanese forces too suffered from disease and cold.

At the level of training and doctrine, Japan's conscript army, trained under European instructors and reformed after the 1870s, drilled in combined arms; its officers had attended staff schools. The Qing army was a mixture of units, some equipped with modern rifles and German artillery and others with older weapons, and training standards varied widely. At the level of politics, the Qing court itself was divided between those pressing for war and those seeking a negotiated settlement, and the war was conducted amid intrigue over responsibility and blame.

Popular accounts sometimes add the claim that Empress Dowager Cixi diverted the navy's funds to rebuild the Summer Palace for her sixtieth birthday, thereby causing the defeat. The evidence is more complicated. Some funds associated with naval administration were indeed used in the 1880s and 1890s for palace-related construction and for the court's expenses, and historians treat this as a symptom of fiscal strain and of the lack of a clear distinction between the court's and the state's resources. But the amount, the sources, and the effect on the navy's readiness are debated, and explaining the loss primarily by this diversion obscures the broader structural problems discussed above. Students should also avoid describing the war as a contest between a "reforming" Japan and a "static" China; Qing officials had led an extensive and in many ways successful modernization of military and industrial infrastructure, and the war exposed the limits of a reform strategy that left fiscal, administrative, and political structures largely unchanged.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Qing Lose the Sino-Japanese War?

The question: The Qing had a larger population, a modern fleet, and decades of military reform behind it. Why did it lose to Japan within nine months?

Interpretation A — Institutional and organizational weakness: Many historians argue that the decisive differences lay in state organization: the Qing relied on regionally financed and commanded forces, lacked unified command and a general staff, and had no integrated national fiscal system for war. Technology had been acquired, but the administrative and political structures needed to use it effectively, including coordination among regional armies and fleets, had not been built. On this view, Self-Strengthening was real but incomplete.
Interpretation B — Japanese capacity, technology, and contingency: Other scholars emphasize Japanese strengths and specific military factors: the number of quick-firing guns, ship speed, naval training, central war planning, and rapid mobilization, as well as contingent events such as the fighting at Pyongyang, the chance damage to the Dingyuan's bridge, and Li Hongzhang's decision to conserve the fleet. This view stresses that the outcome was not inevitable, and that some Qing units fought hard and some defensive positions were strong.

Evidence: Both interpretations use the same operational records, but they weigh them differently: the first draws heavily on Qing administrative and financial records and on the patterns of command failure; the second draws on naval gunnery data, Japanese staff records, and detailed battle narratives. Debates about court spending, ammunition quality, and the competence of particular officers remain unresolved and are affected by the incomplete state of the surviving records.

Why it matters: Explanations of defeat shaped Chinese reform strategies after 1895. If the cause was a technology gap, the solution was more purchases; if it was institutional weakness, the solution was political and administrative reform, the argument made by reformers in 1898 and again after 1900.

Student task: Which explanation better accounts for the failure at Pyongyang, and which better accounts for the Yalu? What additional evidence would help to decide between them?

Section 55.8

The Treaty of Shimonoseki, 1895

Negotiations opened in the Japanese port city of Shimonoseki on 20 March 1895, with Li Hongzhang facing Itō Hirobumi and Mutsu Munemitsu. On 24 March a Japanese assailant shot Li in the face as he was leaving a session; the wound was not fatal, but the incident embarrassed the Japanese government, which feared that foreign powers would sympathize with China and press for moderation. Japan agreed to an armistice covering the main theaters of the war (though not Taiwan and the Pescadores), and Li resumed negotiations after recovering. Japanese demands were then reduced somewhat, though they remained severe. The treaty was signed on 17 April 1895 and ratified at Yantai (Chefoo) on 8 May.

The treaty's provisions were wide-ranging. Article 1 recognized the "full and complete independence and autonomy" of Korea and ended tribute and ceremonies from Korea to China. Qing territory ceded to Japan included the southern part of Fengtian province (the Liaodong peninsula), the island of Taiwan with its dependent islands, and the Pescadores. The Qing agreed to pay an indemnity of 200 million Kuping taels, in installments over seven years, a sum that was more than twice the central government's annual revenue by commonly cited estimates. Residents of the ceded territories were given two years to sell their property and leave if they wished to retain Qing status; those who remained would be considered Japanese subjects. The treaty opened four new ports to Japanese trade (Shashi, Chongqing, Suzhou, and Hangzhou), granted Japanese ships navigation rights on the upper Yangtze and on the waterways to Suzhou and Hangzhou, and, of great long-term significance, allowed Japanese subjects to engage in manufacturing and to import machinery in the treaty ports. Because of the most-favored-nation clauses in earlier treaties, these privileges were soon shared by other powers. Weihaiwei was to be held by Japan as a guarantee until the indemnity was paid.

Primary Source Window

Treaty of Peace between China and Japan (Treaty of Shimonoseki), 17 April 1895

Creators: Plenipotentiaries Li Hongzhang (with Li Jingfang) for the Qing, Itō Hirobumi and Mutsu Munemitsu for Japan · Date: 17 April 1895 · Type: Bilateral peace treaty, published in Japanese, Chinese, and English-language versions

Context: The treaty concluded the war on terms dictated by the victor, while Japan was aware that the powers might intervene. The English text, used by both governments as the working diplomatic language, is in the public domain in standard collections of treaties.

Article 1 states that China recognizes the "full and complete independence and autonomy" of Korea, and that tribute and ceremonies by Korea to China in derogation of that independence shall cease. Other articles (paraphrased) cede Taiwan, the Pescadores, and southern Fengtian to Japan; fix the indemnity at 200 million Kuping taels; open four additional ports; and establish Japanese commercial and manufacturing rights.

Analysis: The treaty is the clearest textual evidence for how the war changed the East Asian order. Its language of "independence" belongs to international law, not to the vocabulary of ritual relations that had governed Qing-Korean contact, and it was imposed by a third party. Students should note that Korea did not participate in the negotiation and was not consulted, and that the treaty did not give Korean independence any practical guarantee. The treaty also shows who was not represented: the inhabitants of Taiwan and Liaodong, who were transferred without consultation. It does not reveal how the terms were experienced by those affected, nor the internal debates within the Qing court and the Japanese cabinet.

  1. What does this source say or show about the status of Korea?
  2. Who created the treaty, and what purposes did each side have?
  3. What can historians learn from the commercial and manufacturing provisions about Japanese goals?
  4. What can historians not learn from the treaty alone about the people it affected?

In China, the terms provoked outrage. In Beijing, where candidates for the metropolitan examination had gathered in the spring of 1895, hundreds signed petitions urging the court to reject the treaty, move the capital, and undertake wide reform; the best-known petition was drafted by Kang Youwei, who thereby became a national figure. Critics condemned Li Hongzhang as a traitor, though he had negotiated under conditions of military defeat and with limited choices. Yet the court ratified the treaty, because rejecting it meant risking an assault on Tianjin and Beijing. The war thus produced a political, intellectual, and fiscal crisis at the same time.

Section 55.9

The Triple Intervention and the New Balance of Power

Within a week of the treaty's signing, on 23 April 1895, the ministers of Russia, Germany, and France in Tokyo advised Japan to give up the Liaodong peninsula, arguing that its occupation would threaten Beijing and make Korean independence illusory. Each power had its own reasons. Russia, building the Trans-Siberian Railway and seeking an ice-free port on the Pacific, did not want a strong Japan in southern Manchuria. France, Russia's ally, supported it. Germany, under Kaiser Wilhelm II, wished to improve relations with Russia, to divert Russian attention from Europe, and to obtain a share of future compensation in Asia. Britain declined to join, and the United States stayed neutral.

Japan, exhausted by the campaign and aware that it could not fight three European powers, accepted on 5 May 1895 and, in a further convention in November, agreed to retrocede Liaodong in return for an additional indemnity of 30 million taels. The episode stung Japanese public opinion deeply, and the slogan of enduring hardship in order to prepare for revenge circulated widely; Japanese military spending rose sharply. For the Qing, the intervention appeared to confirm that great-power diplomacy could limit Japanese gains. Li Hongzhang and other officials pursued closer relations with Russia: in 1896, the Li-Lobanov agreement, signed in the context of Tsar Nicholas II's coronation, provided for a defensive arrangement against Japan and for the construction of the Chinese Eastern Railway across northern Manchuria, built and operated by a Russian-controlled company.

Cause and Effect

Why the War Transformed the Qing's International Position

Long-term causes

Competing Qing and Japanese claims over Korea; Meiji state-building; Qing reliance on regional armies and fleets; fiscal strain after rebellions and the 1884–1885 war with France; limited integration of Self-Strengthening projects.

Short-term causes

The Donghak uprising; dual troop deployments under the 1885 Tianjin Convention; Japan's decision to stay and press reform; the palace coup of 23 July; Qing misjudgment of great-power mediation.

Immediate effects

End of Qing standing in Korea; cession of Taiwan; a 200-million-tael indemnity; new treaty ports and manufacturing rights; heavy foreign borrowing; Japanese resentment after the Triple Intervention.

Long-term effects

Intensified imperial competition for leases and concessions; new Chinese reform movements and nationalism; Japanese power in Korea and Taiwan; reorientation of Qing diplomacy toward Russia.

Historical complexity: The defeat did not by itself cause partition fears or reform; the effects depended on the choices of foreign governments, Qing officials, and reformers. It also affected different groups differently, from Korean peasants to Taiwanese households to treaty-port merchants.

Section 55.10

Korea After Shimonoseki

The end of formal Qing standing in Korea did not produce secure independence. Under Japanese influence, the Gabo Reforms continued into 1895, while the Korean court, especially Queen Min and her allies, sought to counter Japanese pressure by drawing closer to Russia. The Triple Intervention, which showed Russian strength, encouraged this orientation. In October 1895, a group of Japanese agents and Korean collaborators, organized with the knowledge of Japan's minister in Seoul, Miura Gorō, entered the palace and killed Queen Min. The murder shocked Korean and international opinion; a Japanese court-martial and trial later acquitted the accused for lack of evidence.

In February 1896, King Gojong, fearing for his life, left the palace in secret and took refuge in the Russian legation, where he remained for about a year. The Korean government, now more aligned with Russia, canceled or modified some of the reforms; Russia and Japan agreed to share influence in Korea under a series of arrangements. In 1897 Gojong returned to a palace and proclaimed the Korean Empire, taking the title of emperor, a declaration that asserted equality with the Qing and Japanese emperors. Korean reformers, including those in the Independence Club (founded 1896), campaigned for sovereignty, press freedom, and constitutional reform, and the club symbolically built a new Independence Gate near the site where Korean kings had formerly received Qing envoys. The Korean experience after 1895 thus shows that the break in the Qing relationship was a transformation of Korea's vulnerability and options, not simply its liberation. Later developments, including Russo-Japanese rivalry and Japan's eventual annexation of Korea in 1910, lie beyond this chapter.

Section 55.11

Taiwan: Cession, the Republic of Formosa, and Japanese Conquest

Taiwan in 1895 was a multiethnic island of roughly two and a half million people. Han settlers from Fujian and Guangdong, including Hokkien-speaking and Hakka communities, lived mostly in the western plains and the northern basin; Indigenous peoples, many of them Austronesian-speaking, lived in the mountains and eastern regions, and some lived on the plains. The island had been incorporated into the Qing empire in 1683, was transformed during the nineteenth century by tea, camphor, and sugar exports and by the opening of treaty ports, and in 1885 was made a province under its first governor, Liu Mingchuan, who built railways, telegraphs, and coal-mining and defense installations. Taiwan was thus an economically dynamic and increasingly integrated part of the Qing, not an isolated frontier.

The cession of Taiwan under the treaty took place without consulting its inhabitants. Many Taiwanese officials and gentry opposed the transfer, and in May 1895 the governor, Tang Jingsong, and local leaders proclaimed the Republic of Formosa (Taiwan minzhuguo), with Tang as president, in the hope of attracting foreign support and of formally maintaining a link to the Qing emperor. Its proclamation, issued in the language of a temporary arrangement to resist Japanese rule, used a reign-title and mixed new republican vocabulary with loyalty to the Qing throne. It should not be read as the birth of a modern Taiwanese nation-state: it was a short-lived emergency regime, led by officials and gentry, that failed to win diplomatic recognition and had very limited military resources.

Japanese forces under Prince Kitashirakawa Yoshihisa landed on the northeastern coast in late May 1895, took Keelung in early June, and advanced on Taipei. Tang Jingsong fled to the mainland in early June, and the capital descended into disorder before Japanese troops, invited in by local notables, entered on 7 June. In the south, the former Black Flag commander Liu Yongfu continued to organize resistance from Tainan until he too left in October; Tainan fell on 21 October. Japanese forces faced determined resistance from local militias, including many Hakka and Hokkien communities, and suffered heavy losses from disease; Prince Kitashirakawa himself died on the island. Taiwanese residents who wished to remain Qing subjects had to leave by 1897, but only a small number did so. For most, the conquest brought not only a change of rulers but violence, disruption, and the loss of the political world in which they had lived.

Section 55.12

Early Japanese Colonial Rule and Resistance in Taiwan

The first Japanese Governor-General, Kabayama Sukenori, declared the island pacified in November 1895, but resistance continued for years in the form of local uprisings and guerrilla warfare, especially in the north, in Yunlin, and in the south. Japanese forces responded with punitive operations, including the destruction of villages; the Yunlin operations of 1896 caused many civilian deaths, and historians continue to debate the numbers. An important early legal measure was a 1896 law granting the Governor-General wide legislative powers, making the colonial government more executive than representative. The Government-General established a police system that penetrated local society and built on the traditional baojia mutual-responsibility system for local control, and a 1898 ordinance prescribed severe penalties for "bandits," a category that included armed resisters.

From 1898, under Governor-General Kodama Gentarō and his civil administrator Gotō Shinpei, Japanese policy turned toward a more systematic program of colonial administration. It included a land survey, which clarified landholding and tax liabilities and eventually reorganized rural property rights; a currency and banking reform; a gradual opium regime that licensed and taxed rather than immediately prohibited opium use; monopolies on camphor, salt, and later tobacco; and the construction of railways, ports, roads, and public-health and sanitation measures. Gotō's approach combined police power with investment in infrastructure and scientific surveys. These policies increased state revenue and economic output, particularly in sugar and rice for the Japanese market, but the benefits were distributed unequally and the colonial order rested on legal discrimination between Japanese and Taiwanese residents.

Experiences differed. Han landowners and merchants sometimes adapted to the new regime, taking advantage of new markets and credit, while others lost land or resisted. Hakka communities in the hills, the camphor-producing frontier, and Indigenous communities faced particularly intense pressure: the camphor monopoly and the expansion of the "guard line" into upland territories led to repeated conflict with Indigenous groups, who were often classified and governed under separate rules. This chapter stops with these early foundations; the long course of Japanese rule between 1895 and 1945 belongs to later chapters. The key point here is that the cession of Taiwan created a colonial relationship different in kind from the treaty-port arrangements elsewhere in China.

Section 55.13

The Scramble for Concessions and Spheres of Influence, 1897–1899

Japan's victory convinced many foreign governments that the Qing was weak and that its coast, railways, and mineral resources were open to bargaining. Between 1895 and 1899, foreign powers pressed the Qing for loans, railway contracts, mining rights, naval bases, and pledges not to cede particular regions to others. The period is sometimes called the "scramble for concessions," a term borrowed from the contemporary partition of Africa. The comparison is useful in one respect, because the powers did compete for strategic advantage, but it can mislead in another: unlike in Africa, the major powers did not formally partition the Chinese empire.

Historical Vocabulary
Sphere of Influence

A sphere of influence was a region in which one foreign power claimed a privileged position, usually through a combination of treaties with the Qing (for example, a pledge not to cede or lease territory in the area to any third power), railway and mining concessions, and diplomatic understandings with other powers. It was not a colony: the Qing continued to govern the region, collect taxes, and appoint officials. Spheres were claims on future opportunity, and their strength depended on the interests of the power in question, its rivals, and the response of the Chinese government and local society.

Examples include Russian claims in Manchuria, German claims in Shandong, British claims in the Yangtze valley, French claims in the southwest, and Japanese claims in Fujian. Sphere boundaries were often vague and overlapping, and they changed with diplomacy.

The scramble was driven by several motives. Strategic considerations were central: Russia wanted an ice-free port and a railway route across Manchuria; Germany wanted a naval base to support its growing Pacific squadron; Britain sought to protect its commercial dominance in the Yangtze valley and to counter Russian expansion; and France hoped to extend its position from Indochina into the southwest. Economic motives included investors' hopes of profits from railways and mines, and the search for markets for manufactured goods. Domestic politics also mattered: in Germany and Russia, naval and colonial lobbies saw China as an opportunity for prestige. And fear of rivals was itself a spur: each power worried that if it did not secure a position, another would.

Why did partition not occur? Several factors limited it. The powers were rivals and could not agree on a division; Britain, with the largest share of China's foreign trade, preferred an open market to formal partition; the cost and risk of governing hundreds of millions of people were obvious; the Qing, despite its weakness, retained the capacity to resist and to bargain; and Chinese local society could be expected to resist. Contemporary Chinese, however, saw partition as a real danger. Cartoons showing European powers and Japan carving up a melon or a cake circulated in the foreign and Chinese press, and the vocabulary of guafen became common in reform writing. Fear of partition, even though it did not occur, was thus a historical force in its own right.

Section 55.14

Leased Territories and Naval Bases

The leaseholds of 1897–1899 were the most visible expression of the scramble. The immediate trigger for the first was the killing of two German Catholic missionaries in Juye county, Shandong, in November 1897, discussed in Section 55.16. Germany, which had been seeking a naval base in China, used the incident as a pretext to seize Jiaozhou Bay on 14 November 1897. Under the convention of March 1898, the Qing leased the bay and surrounding territory to Germany for ninety-nine years and gave Germany railway and mining privileges in Shandong. Germany developed Qingdao as a planned port city with a harbor, brewery, and naval base.

Historical Vocabulary
Leased Territory (zūjièdì, 租借地)

A leased territory or leasehold (zūjièdì, 租借地) was a territory that the Qing transferred, by treaty, to a foreign power for a fixed term, typically twenty-five or ninety-nine years, with the foreign power exercising administrative, police, and military control. It differed from a treaty-port concession (zūjiè, 租界), which was a district within a Chinese city where foreigners lived under their own municipal administration, and from a sphere of influence, which involved no direct administration. The Qing continued to claim nominal sovereignty over leased territories, but in practice they were governed by foreign authorities.

The Chinese term zūjièdì emphasized the fact of "lending" rather than permanent cession, a distinction that mattered in Qing diplomacy, though foreign governments often treated leases as virtually permanent.

Russia, whose fleet had appeared at Port Arthur in December 1897, secured in March 1898 a twenty-five-year lease of Port Arthur and Dalian, together with a railway concession linking them to the Chinese Eastern Railway, thus turning the very territory it had helped to wrest from Japan into a Russian base. Britain, responding, obtained a lease of Weihaiwei in July 1898, to last as long as Russia held Port Arthur, and extended the territory of Hong Kong with a ninety-nine-year lease of the New Territories on the mainland, effective in July 1898. France obtained a ninety-nine-year lease of Guangzhouwan in 1899, adjoining its holdings in Indochina. Italy's demand for a base at Sanmen Bay in Zhejiang in 1899 was rejected by the Qing, a notable example of successful refusal. Japan, for its part, secured a Qing pledge not to cede Fujian to any other power, strengthening its interest across the strait from Taiwan.

The irony noted by many historians is that the three powers that had forced Japan to return Liaodong in the name of China's integrity each obtained a leasehold within a few years. For the Qing, the leases were severe losses of territory and prestige, but they were also negotiated: officials such as Li Hongzhang and Weng Tonghe sought to limit terms, bargained for the form of language, and tried to play powers against each other. For the local populations of leased territories, the experience involved land acquisition, labor recruitment, new ports and railways, and foreign policing.

Section 55.15

Railways, Mines, Loans, and Economic Imperialism

Many of the most important constraints on Qing sovereignty after 1895 were financial and contractual rather than territorial. The indemnity of 200 million taels, plus the additional 30 million for Liaodong, far exceeded the Qing's ability to pay from ordinary revenue. The government borrowed heavily from foreign banks: a Russian-French loan in 1895, and two large Anglo-German loans in 1896 and 1898, arranged by the Hongkong and Shanghai Banking Corporation and the Deutsch-Asiatische Bank. The 1895 Franco-Russian and 1896 Anglo-German loans relied heavily on revenue of the Imperial Maritime Customs, administered by Robert Hart, as security. By the 1898 Anglo-German loan, much of the available Customs revenue was already pledged, so specified likin and salt-likin revenues were also assigned to debt service. In each case, foreign creditors acquired an interest in how those revenues were collected and used. Servicing the debt consumed a large share of the government's funds and increased pressure on provincial budgets and on taxpayers.

Railways became the core of competition. Concessions for lines such as the Chinese Eastern Railway in Manchuria, the Shandong railways in the German sphere, the Beijing-Hankou line (financed by a Belgian-French syndicate), the Tianjin-Pukou line (British and German interests), and lines in the southwest under French influence gave foreign companies rights to build, operate, and in some cases police and mine along the route. Mining concessions, such as those of British interests in Shanxi and German interests in Shandong, added extractive rights. Foreign banks, merchants, and engineers saw these as investments; Chinese officials, who also wanted railways, often sought to limit foreign control through loan and operating terms, and a growing number of Chinese gentry and merchants began to argue that railways and mines should be owned by Chinese.

Students must understand the difference between formal territorial control and these other forms of imperialism. The Qing continued to rule, tax, legislate, and conduct diplomacy, and its officials sometimes succeeded in blocking or reshaping foreign demands. But treaty rights, extraterritoriality, customs security, concession contracts, and foreign naval presence created a layered system of constraint, in which foreign actors had legal and economic leverage inside Chinese territory. This was imperialism by contract and pressure, not simple conquest, and its effects varied by region: heavily felt in treaty ports, along railway lines, and in leased territories, and less visible in many inland villages.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Qing Sovereignty, 1842 to 1899

What Changed

  • Foreign rights expanded from a few treaty ports (1842) to dozens of ports, river navigation, mission rights, and leased territories
  • The Qing lost its ritual and diplomatic standing in Korea and territory in Taiwan
  • Foreign loans and customs security tied the central budget to international finance
  • Railway and mining concessions created foreign economic stakes inland
  • Educated Chinese began to speak of national danger and political reform

What Stayed the Same

  • The Qing court governed most of the empire's territory and population
  • Local government, lineages, markets, and village life continued largely as before in most regions
  • Provincial leaders held substantial power and bargained with foreign states
  • The dynasty and its examination system remained, until reforms in the following years

Who experienced change most directly: residents of treaty ports, leased territories, Taiwan, and railway and mission districts. Who experienced more continuity: many inland farmers, though taxation, indemnity burdens, and, in some regions, missionary conflict reached them as well.

Section 55.16

Missionaries, Christian Converts, and Local Conflict

After the treaties of 1858 and 1860, Catholic and Protestant missionaries were permitted to travel and reside in the interior, and the French government claimed a protectorate over Catholic missions. By the 1890s, hundreds of missionaries from many societies and nationalities were at work: French and German Catholics, Italian Franciscans, British, American, Scandinavian, and Canadian Protestants, working through churches, schools, orphanages, and dispensaries. They were a diverse group, with different theologies and attitudes; some were close to local communities and wrote sympathetically about Chinese culture, while others were more confrontational. Several hundred thousand Chinese had become Christians, the majority of them Catholics, in communities concentrated in North China, the lower Yangtze, and the southeast coast.

Conflict arose from specific causes. Mission stations acquired land and buildings, sometimes in places that villagers regarded as sacred or that affected geomancy (fengshui). Chinese Christians, who withdrew from village religious events and temple festivals that supported local public goods, could be seen as breaking communal obligations. Lawsuits and property disputes sometimes involved missionaries who intervened with officials on behalf of converts, drawing on treaty protection and, in the background, the threat of diplomatic pressure or gunboats. Some people joined churches for spiritual reasons; others hoped for protection in disputes; local elites, who had traditionally mediated disputes, resented the competing authority. Rumors about mission orphanages and medical practices, as well as economic pressures, drought, and flood, added tension. Chinese Christians themselves were not mere foreign agents: they were farmers, artisans, and teachers with their own motives and their own vulnerability to attack.

These local disputes, called jiao'an ("missionary cases"), repeatedly became diplomatic crises, as in the Tianjin violence of 1870 and many smaller incidents. The most consequential was the Juye incident of November 1897, in which a group of men attacked the mission residence of the German Society of the Divine Word in southwestern Shandong and killed two German priests, Franz Nies and Richard Henle. The killers were reportedly linked to the Big Sword Society, a local self-defense group. The German government used the killings as the pretext for the seizure of Jiaozhou Bay, and the Qing was obliged to punish officials, pay compensation, build churches, and grant the lease. The incident shows how a local conflict could be rapidly turned into a great-power crisis, and how the interests of the powers could use missionary protection as a lever. Students should avoid explaining the conflict as mere "xenophobia": it grew out of concrete issues of land, law, authority, religion, and the unequal legal position created by treaties.

Everyday Life and Social History

War, Indemnity, and Conflict in the Villages

For most people, the effects of the war and the scramble arrived indirectly. Soldiers from Anhui, Hunan, Shandong, and elsewhere marched north in winter, many of them peasants recruited by regional commanders, and families waited for pay that arrived late. In Manchuria and Shandong, villagers faced the requisitioning of carts, grain, and labor, the flight of families, and the loss of crops. After the treaty, the indemnity and loan payments were financed through additional taxes and surcharges, and through the pressure of provincial officials to raise revenue; the cost fell most heavily on peasants and small merchants. In the Shandong countryside, a region of dense population and recurrent Yellow River floods, harvest failure and drought in 1898–1899 added to hardship.

In treaty-port cities such as Shanghai and Tianjin, the new commercial and manufacturing rights, along with new railways, brought jobs in factories, docks, and railway construction, but also exposed workers to dangerous conditions and low pay. In Taiwan, families faced the decision of whether to stay under Japanese rule or sell property and leave; women, who managed households and, in many families, worked in tea sorting, textile work, and agriculture, faced new demands for registration, policing, and sanitation inspection. In Christian villages, families also experienced the choice of whether to join a community that could offer education, medical care, or legal protection, at the cost of tension with neighbors. The sources for ordinary experience are uneven, because most surviving documents were written by officials, missionaries, and foreign observers, so historians read them for indirect evidence and supplement them with local records and oral traditions.

Section 55.17

Defeat, Reform Pressure, and the Hundred Days Reform of 1898

The defeat of 1895 and the scramble of 1897–1898 transformed the political debate within the Qing elite. Before 1895, advocates of reform had mostly argued for the selective adoption of Western technology. After 1895, a growing group of scholars and officials argued that the Qing needed institutional change: reform of education and the examination system, new forms of public discussion, administrative modernization, and perhaps constitutional government. The most prominent was Kang Youwei, a scholar from Guangdong who drew on a reinterpretation of Confucian texts to argue that Confucius himself had been a reformer. Kang and his student Liang Qichao organized study societies, published journals such as Shiwu bao (The Chinese Progress), and helped to spread the language of national survival. In the interior, provincial officials such as Chen Baozhen in Hunan sponsored new schools and local reform institutions, showing that reform was not only a Beijing project.

In early 1898, as Germany, Russia, Britain, and France secured leases, the Guangxu Emperor, then in his twenties, came to support the reformers. From 11 June to 21 September 1898, a period of about a hundred days, he issued a series of edicts. They established an Imperial University in Beijing, called for the abolition of the "eight-legged essay" in the examinations, encouraged new schools, railways, mines, and agricultural and commercial bureaus, proposed reorganization of the military, and ordered abolition of redundant offices. Some officials resisted, some edicts could not be implemented in the provinces, and the program was uneven and rapid. The emperor also dismissed his former tutor Weng Tonghe, and began to appoint reformers to advisory positions.

Conservative officials and the Empress Dowager Cixi, who feared the removal of officials and the threat to her position, moved against the emperor. On 21 September 1898, Cixi resumed direct control of the government, confined the emperor in the palace, and ordered the arrest of the reformers. Kang Youwei and Liang Qichao escaped to Japan, with help from foreign diplomats; six reformers, including Tan Sitong, were executed on 28 September. Most of the reform edicts were rescinded, although the Imperial University survived. Historians debate the extent to which the Hundred Days was a practical program or a moment of political improvisation, the role of Yuan Shikai's information to the court, and the degree to which the reformers' ideas were distinct from later programs. The episode is best understood as a response to the sovereignty crisis, one that showed the depth of divisions within the elite. It should not be confused with the New Policies after 1901, covered in Chapter 56, which were carried out under Cixi's own direction and in a different political environment.

Section 55.18

The Open Door, Anti-Foreign Tension, and the Crisis of Qing Sovereignty

By 1899, the United States, which had just acquired the Philippines after the Spanish-American War and held a growing interest in the China market, but had no leasehold or sphere of its own, turned to diplomacy. In September 1899, Secretary of State John Hay sent notes to the major powers asking them to commit to equal commercial access in their spheres, to apply Chinese tariffs uniformly, and to avoid discrimination in port and railway charges. The replies were evasive, but Hay announced in 1900 that the powers had accepted. The Open Door policy did not stop the leasing of territory, and the American government had no means of enforcing it; but it expressed a position shared with Britain, that the Chinese market should remain open to all, and a second note in July 1900 asserted support for Chinese territorial and administrative integrity. For the Qing, such statements were welcome but not reliable protection.

Meanwhile, the political atmosphere in North China was deteriorating. Floods on the Yellow River in 1898 and drought in the following year had hit Shandong and neighboring provinces; the German presence at Qingdao and the construction of railways displaced villagers and disrupted livelihoods; indemnity-related taxes pressed on households; and missionary-convert disputes continued. Local martial arts and self-defense groups were active, and in Shandong and nearby areas, bands that would soon become known as the Boxers began to appear. The Qing court was uncertain whether to treat such groups as bandits, as potentially useful local militia, or as threats to diplomatic relations. At the same time, the court, after the failure of reform and the scramble for leases, was deeply suspicious of the foreign powers. This chapter ends here, on the threshold: the Boxer movement itself, its relationship to the Qing court, the siege of the foreign legations, and the intervention of foreign armies belong to Chapter 56.

A Beiyang naval officer: Trained in a mixture of Chinese and foreign methods and serving under regional command, he faced a fleet with limited ammunition and a strategic directive to avoid risk. A Korean peasant and Donghak participant: The original grievances were local: heavy taxes, corrupt officials, and the pressure of foreign trade. The uprising was crushed in the end by Japanese and Korean government forces, and Korean "independence" came at the price of foreign armies, forced reforms, and, for many, death and ruin.
A Taiwanese resident: A landowner, tenant, or merchant on an island that was transferred without consultation, he could resist, accept, or leave. The Republic of Formosa offered no secure alternative, and many local militias fought on after its leaders had left; for those who stayed, the early colonial period meant police control, land survey, and new economic opportunities for some and loss for others.
A Chinese Christian convert in Shandong: Whether motivated by faith, community, education, or protection, he lived within a church whose foreign leaders had treaty protection and a local society that was suspicious of its claims. After 1897, he was at greater risk of being seen as an ally of a foreign power, even though he was a member of a Chinese village.
A treaty-port merchant: Foreign banks, steamship lines, and new manufacturing rights brought opportunities in finance, shipping, and industry, but also competition and dependence on foreign institutions. Many merchants pressed for Chinese ownership of railways and mines, a demand that later shaped economic nationalism.

Why the perspectives are not equal in power: The Japanese army, foreign navies, and treaty rights gave some actors the ability to impose terms, while Korean peasants and Taiwanese residents had no voice in the treaty. Teaching historical complexity does not mean treating all positions as equally powerful.

Section 55.19

Chapter Review and Foreign Imperialism Checkpoint

The synthesis question for this chapter is: why did the Sino-Japanese War transform China's international position, and how did the foreign scramble after 1895 constrain Qing sovereignty without completely eliminating it? A strong answer should explain the war's causes and outcome, the terms of the treaty, the changing relationships with Korea and Taiwan, the Triple Intervention, the forms of foreign power that emerged (leases, spheres, concessions, loans), and the reasons why partition did not occur while sovereignty was nevertheless eroded.

Regional and Frontier Context

The war and the scramble affected regions very differently. Manchuria, the Qing dynasty's homeland and a frontier of Han settlement, saw Japanese invasion, then Russian railway construction, and the beginning of a long competition between Russia and Japan. Shandong combined dense peasant society, Yellow River hazards, and German and missionary presence. Taiwan, with Han, Hakka, and Indigenous populations, was transferred to Japanese colonial rule. The Yangtze valley became the core of British commercial interest, with treaty ports and steamship routes. The southwest and south coast faced French railway and territorial ambitions, and the Hong Kong hinterland was leased to Britain. Korea, on the frontier between empires, experienced the war on its own soil. Students should not treat the Beijing court's perspective as representative of any of these regions, nor assume that frontier populations were only objects of imperial competition: they negotiated, resisted, and adapted within their own political contexts.

China and the Wider World

The events of 1894–1900 connected Chinese history to several global processes: the rise of Japan as an industrial and imperial power; the extension of the Russian rail network across Eurasia; German naval and colonial ambition; British worries about the balance of power; French imperial expansion in Southeast Asia; the American acquisition of the Philippines and the articulation of an Open Door policy; and a global market for capital, in which European banks financed Chinese state debt. They also connected China to intellectual currents: reformers read translations of works on Western and Japanese politics, and after 1898 Japan became a major destination for Chinese students and exiles, such as Liang Qichao. Chinese actors were not merely recipients: Qing diplomats negotiated, provincial leaders raised loans and projects, merchants and officials reshaped foreign proposals, and, within a few years, Chinese communities, reformers, and activists began to shape new political movements in Japan, Southeast Asia, and North America.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the First Sino-Japanese War and the foreign scramble for concessions as a turning point in the late Qing's international position. The war grew out of a long and layered contest over Korea's status. The Qing, Japan, and Korea each used different vocabularies for the relationship, and Japan's interpretation, drawn from treaty law, collided with Qing efforts to strengthen practical control after 1882. The Donghak uprising, a Korean movement with Korean causes, led to simultaneous Qing and Japanese deployments, and Japan's decision to stay and to seize the Korean palace turned a crisis into a war.

The fighting at Pyongyang, the Yalu, Port Arthur, and Weihaiwei revealed that the Qing's achievements in Self-Strengthening, including warships, arsenals, and trained personnel, had not been integrated into a unified system of command, finance, logistics, and training. Historians continue to debate how much to attribute to institutional weakness, Japanese capacity, technology, and contingency, and the Historical Debate feature showed that explanations of defeat shaped later proposals for reform. Simple narratives of technological backwardness or palace corruption do not account for the evidence.

The Treaty of Shimonoseki ended the Qing's ritual and political standing in Korea, ceded Taiwan, the Pescadores, and Liaodong, imposed a vast indemnity, and opened new commercial and manufacturing rights. Korean independence, as defined in the treaty, did not translate into secure sovereignty: Japanese pressure, the murder of Queen Min, King Gojong's refuge in the Russian legation, and the creation of the Korean Empire showed how contested Korean autonomy remained. Taiwan's transfer without consultation produced armed resistance, the short-lived Republic of Formosa, and a Japanese conquest that created a colonial regime based on policing, surveys, monopolies, and infrastructure and on legal inequality between Japanese and Taiwanese.

The Triple Intervention showed that great-power rivalry could limit Japan, but it also showed that the intervening powers expected compensation. Within three years Germany, Russia, Britain, and France obtained leaseholds and a complex of railway, mining, and banking concessions. These constraints were different from colonial rule: spheres of influence and leases limited Qing sovereignty without eliminating the Qing state, which continued to govern most of its territory and to bargain with foreign powers. Foreign imperialism was competitive, not a single coordinated Western project, and the failure of partition reflected the rivalry of the powers, the interests of Britain and the United States in an open market, and the continuing capacities of the Qing and of Chinese local society.

Regional and social experiences varied. Manchuria, Shandong, Taiwan, the Yangtze valley, the southern coast, and Korea each encountered the war and the scramble differently. Peasants, soldiers, merchants, Christians, and local elites interpreted events through their own circumstances: indemnity-related taxes, requisitioning, new employment, conflicts over land and religion, and hopes or fears about foreign protection and reform.

Missionary conflicts illustrated how treaty privileges, local politics, property, religious difference, and great-power ambitions could interact. The Juye incident of 1897 produced the German seizure of Jiaozhou Bay, showing how a local event could become the pretext for a strategic move. Explaining these conflicts as mere xenophobia obscures the concrete legal and social issues that produced them.

The sovereignty crisis changed political debate. Kang Youwei, Liang Qichao, the Guangxu Emperor, and provincial officials argued that institutional reform was necessary for survival, and the Hundred Days Reform of 1898 attempted a rapid program that was terminated by a coup. The suppression of the reforms left the court deeply divided, and the experience of leases, indemnities, and failed reform contributed to an atmosphere of fear and resentment in North China.

The chapter ended on the threshold of the Boxer crisis, which Chapter 56 examines. The conditions that made that crisis possible, including missionary disputes, leases, economic hardship, floods and drought, and fears of partition, had been assembled by 1899, but the crisis itself was not inevitable. Students should resist reading backward from 1900 to explain the earlier period as simply a prelude.

In the larger history of Chinese civilization, the period from 1894 to 1899 marks the end of the Qing-centered East Asian order and the beginning of a new phase of national crisis, in which Chinese officials, reformers, merchants, and ordinary people tried to preserve or reconstruct political authority under intense external pressure. It also shows the interconnection between Chinese, Korean, Japanese, Russian, European, and American histories, and the importance of distinguishing sovereignty, colonial rule, and influence.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a regional crisis could change an entire international order. The war of 1894–1895 ended the old ritual and diplomatic arrangement in which the Qing court had been the senior partner for Korea, introduced Japan as a colonial power on the Chinese periphery, and exposed the limits of a reform strategy that had acquired new tools without fully reorganizing the institutions that would use them.

It also shaped later Chinese history. The indemnity and loans tied the Qing treasury to foreign finance; the leases and concessions created new areas of foreign presence; the failed Hundred Days Reform left unresolved the question of how to reform the state; and the atmosphere of fear and resentment in North China contributed to the conditions that Chapter 56 will examine. Many themes of the twentieth century, including nationalism, anti-imperialism, reform, and debates over sovereignty, took shape in these years, though none of them was predetermined.

The chapter reveals larger themes of this textbook: that "China" in the 1890s was a multiethnic empire with regionally varied experiences; that foreign interaction involved Chinese agency, bargaining, and resistance and not only pressure; that colonial rule, treaty privileges, and contractual constraints were different things; and that Korea and Taiwan had their own histories that cannot be reduced to episodes in Chinese or Japanese history. It also connects Chinese history to Japanese state-building, Russian railway expansion, European naval competition, and global finance.

Understanding this period helps students see Chinese history as diverse, interconnected, contested, adaptive, and unfinished. The Qing's defeat was real, and so were the sufferings of those who experienced war, conquest, and indemnity taxes; but so were the efforts of officials, reformers, merchants, villagers, and Christians to respond. The questions raised in these years, about sovereignty, reform, and the place of China in the world, were not settled in 1899, and they continue into the chapters that follow.
