Part VI — Song China and the Multi-State East Asian World
36

The Jin Dynasty and Southern Song

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

In the winter of 1126–1127, Kaifeng, the capital of the Northern Song and one of the largest and most commercially active cities in the world, was occupied by a Jurchen army. The city's markets, granaries, bureaus, and palaces had for more than a century organized the affairs of a state that ruled perhaps a hundred million people. Now the court itself was in the hands of its enemy. Within months, two emperors, their families, and thousands of officials, craftspeople, and entertainers would be marched north. Yet the emperor's ninth son, who happened to be elsewhere, would soon be proclaimed ruler of a new court; families across the Yellow River plain would be deciding whether to abandon fields, tombs, and ancestral halls; and a new conquest state would be deciding how to govern farmers who spoke a language it did not.

This chapter asks how the political world described in Chapter 35, in which the Song, the Khitan Liao, and the Tangut Western Xia coexisted through treaties, trade, and rivalry, could be violently reconfigured in scarcely more than a decade. The answer begins with the Jurchens, a people of the northeastern forests and river valleys who were subjects of the Liao until 1114–1115, when a revolt led by Wanyan Aguda produced the Jin state. Within ten years the Jin had destroyed the Liao empire; within twelve they had captured the Song capital. What emerged was not a political vacuum but a new arrangement: a Jin empire ruling the north and a Southern Song regime, headquartered in the south, that claimed the legacy of the Song and rebuilt its government in a different geography.

The chapter's central argument is that the twelfth century did not mark the collapse of "China" into disorder. It marked the creation of a new multi-state order in which two powerful successor regimes, Jin and Southern Song, competed, negotiated, traded, and shaped one another's institutions for roughly a century. The Jin governed the majority of the agrarian population of the north for more than a hundred years and became one of the principal states of the region; it should not be treated as a mere interruption of an otherwise continuous "Song China." The Southern Song, for its part, lost the historic heartland of the Yellow River plain but retained, and developed, some of the most productive and commercially dynamic regions of the entire Eurasian world. It should not be dismissed as a weakened remnant. Neither picture is adequate; both have to be held together.

The chapter also asks what conquest does to ordinary societies. Warfare devastated parts of the north, displaced large numbers of people, broke elite families, and redirected the movements of officials, soldiers, merchants, and monks. Jurchen military households were resettled on the North China plain, where they lived alongside Han Chinese farmers, Khitan communities, and others; refugees from the north crowded into Zhejiang, Jiangsu, Fujian, and Sichuan; and border regions along the Huai River became zones of smuggling, negotiation, fortification, and recurring warfare. The lives of soldiers, farmers, women, scholars, monks, merchants, and craftspeople all changed, but not in the same way or in the same direction.

Historians rely on an unusually varied body of evidence for this period. Song sources include court records, envoys' travel diaries, personal writings, and compilations of diplomatic documents. Jin sources, less abundant, include inscriptions in Chinese and in the Jurchen script, fragments of law and administrative practice, poetry and prose by scholars who served the Jin, and the later official History of the Jin, compiled under the Mongol-ruled Yuan dynasty. Archaeology, coinage, and printed books add further material. Almost all of these sources reflect particular vantage points. Song accounts frequently moralize about the causes of defeat; Jin accounts survive largely through the filter of later editors. Learning to read across them is a central skill of this chapter.

What continued through the period matters as much as what changed. Agriculture remained the base of both states. Confucian learning, Buddhist institutions, and popular religion persisted on both sides of the frontier. Printing, markets, and paper money spread on both sides, though not equally. Diplomacy remained institutionalized: after a generation of war, Jin and Southern Song built treaty relations with fixed borders, regular payments, ritual forms of address, and border markets, extending the interstate habits of the Song-Liao order. And the question of legitimacy, meaning who had the right to be regarded as an heir to earlier imperial traditions, remained open and contested, for both Jin and Southern Song.

By the end of the chapter, the arrival of a still more formidable power, the Mongols, will be visible on the northern horizon. The Jin fell in 1234 to a combination of Mongol conquest and internal exhaustion, and the Southern Song would survive for another generation. That story belongs to Chapter 38. This chapter closes the Jin arc, follows Southern Song through the period of coexistence, and leaves the detailed picture of the southern economy, Hangzhou, and the maritime world to Chapter 37.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe Jurchen society, its relations with the Liao, and the organizational basis of the Jin state.
  2. Trace the Jin conquest of the Liao and explain what the Jin inherited from the Liao empire.
  3. Explain why the Song-Jin alliance against the Liao formed and why it collapsed so quickly.
  4. Analyze the Jingkang Crisis and the fall of Kaifeng, distinguishing causes rooted in Song institutions from those rooted in Jin strategy and circumstance.
  5. Assess how Southern Song established legitimacy after the capture of two emperors, and why it should be understood as a new political configuration.
  6. Evaluate Jin governance in North China as institutional pluralism and adaptation rather than as simple "sinicization" or simple foreign domination.
  7. Compare Jin and Southern Song in government, military organization, economy, and cultural life.
  8. Interpret the treaties of 1141–1142, 1164–1165, and 1208 as evidence of an institutionalized interstate order.
  9. Analyze the migrations of the twelfth century and their consequences for North China and for the south.
  10. Explain how Mongol pressure destabilized Jin and ended the Jin-Southern Song order.
  11. Use Song, Jin, and later sources critically, identifying the perspective and limits of each.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did a people who were subjects of one empire organize themselves so quickly into a state capable of destroying two others?
  2. Why did the Song choose to ally with the Jin against the Liao, and what did that decision reveal about Song strategy and weakness?
  3. Why was the Northern Song unable to defend its capital, and how should historians weigh institutional, strategic, political, and contingent explanations?
  4. What does it mean for a state to survive after its ruling court has been captured, and what did Southern Song have to rebuild?
  5. How did the Jin rule a large agrarian population while preserving Jurchen identity and military institutions?
  6. How did war and diplomacy coexist in Song-Jin relations, and how did their treaties resemble and differ from those of the Song-Liao order?
  7. How did migration reshape both the north and the south?
  8. Why did the Jin state, apparently strong in the late twelfth century, prove vulnerable to the Mongols?

Historical Context

Chapter 35 introduced a political world in which no single state could claim the whole of the East Asian mainland. The Song ruled the agricultural core of the Yellow River plain, the Huai region, the Yangtze basin, Sichuan, and the southeast. The Liao, a Khitan-led empire, controlled Manchuria, the Mongolian plateau's eastern edge, and, since 938, the Sixteen Prefectures around modern Beijing and Datong, ruling both nomadic and agrarian populations through a dual administration. The Western Xia, founded by the Tanguts, held the Ordos and Gansu corridor. After the Treaty of Chanyuan in 1005 the Song and Liao maintained an institutionalized peace of unusual duration, sustained by annual payments, border markets, and shared diplomatic rituals. That peace also gave each state a strong interest in the other's continued existence.

Northern Song institutions, described in Chapter 31, emphasized civil administration over autonomous military rule. That emphasis was a deliberate response to the military governors who had dominated the late Tang and the Five Dynasties (Chapters 29 and 30). It produced a state with a large bureaucracy, a powerful fiscal apparatus, and a professional army paid from central revenue, but one that was often reluctant to allow generals continuous command. It also left the state paying large annual sums to buy peace on its frontiers, and it contributed to bitter factional struggles among officials over the New Policies of Wang Anshi and their reversal. By the early twelfth century, under the emperor Huizong (r. 1100–1126), the court was preoccupied with art, ritual, and Daoist patronage, and its policy on the northern frontier was shaped by a small group of favored officials.

The northeast, meanwhile, was a region of great ecological and cultural variety. Its river valleys, forests, and grasslands supported hunters, herders, fishers, and farmers, among whom the Jurchens, a Tungusic-speaking people, were prominent. Under the Liao some Jurchen groups had been drawn into the empire's administrative and economic networks, while others remained more autonomous. The Liao valued the region for its furs, horses, and other products, and the Jurchens' relationship with the empire was uneven, at times exploitative, and, in the last decades of the Liao, increasingly resented.

Students should be careful about the meaning of "China" in this chapter. In the twelfth century the term did not designate a single stable political entity. Both Song and Jin rulers presented themselves as heirs of earlier imperial traditions. The Song claimed continuity as the legitimate dynasty of the central plain; the Jin, after conquering the Central Plains, adopted a Chinese-style dynastic name, imperial titles, calendar, ritual practice, and, eventually, an examination system, while retaining Jurchen institutions and language. Historians of the period therefore increasingly speak of a Jin-Southern Song order rather than treating one state as "China" and the other as an external invader. Modern national narratives that assign the Jin to either the "Chinese" or the "foreign" category oversimplify how both states, and their subjects, understood their political situation.

Finally, this chapter should be read with attention to what belongs to neighboring chapters. Chapter 33 discussed the technologies, including gunpowder weapons, that both Song and Jin employed. Chapter 34 traced the intellectual and social life of Song Neo-Confucianism, which developed in the south during the period discussed here. Chapter 37 will examine Hangzhou, the southern economy, port cities, and maritime trade in detail. Chapter 38 will examine the Mongol conquest. The present chapter focuses on the political, military, institutional, and demographic transformation that connects them.

Section 36.1

The Jurchens and the Rise of Jin

The Jurchens should be introduced on their own terms, not simply as the people who attacked the Liao. Their ancestors had lived for centuries in the river valleys and forests of what is now northeastern China and adjacent regions, and earlier peoples associated with the same broad area, such as the Mohe, had sometimes established kingdoms, including the Bohai state that flourished before the Liao (see Chapters 27 and 35). The Jurchens spoke a Tungusic language, practiced a mixed economy of millet and other farming, hunting, fishing, pig-raising, and horse-keeping, and lived in villages and lineage groups whose leaders held authority through kinship, reputation, and success in warfare and trade. They traded furs, horses, ginseng, and other northeastern products, and they were tied by long-distance networks to Korea, the Liao, and, indirectly, the Song. Ecological and cultural variety within the northeast meant that "the Jurchens" were never one uniform community.

Under the Liao, Chinese and Khitan sources distinguished between "cooked" (shu) Jurchens, who lived within the empire's administrative reach in the Liaodong region and were registered as subjects, and "raw" (sheng) Jurchens, who lived farther north and east and were more autonomous. These labels reflect the perspective of the Liao state, not Jurchen self-understanding, and they should be treated as descriptions of degrees of political incorporation rather than of cultural development. The Wanyan clan, which lived along the Anchuhu River in the region of the Songhua drainage, belonged to the "raw" Jurchens. By the late eleventh century Wanyan leaders had used marriage alliances, warfare against rival groups, and skilled negotiation with the Liao to become the dominant figures in a confederation of Jurchen tribes.

The Liao imposed burdens that generated resentment. Liao officials demanded tribute in goods, including prized falcons, "Eastern pearls," and furs, and sent envoys whose demands and conduct were often bitterly remembered. The empire under the later emperor Tianzuo (r. 1101–1125) was also politically weakened by factional struggles at court and by the strains of governing dual populations. In 1114 Wanyan Aguda, leader of the confederation, raised an army of a few thousand at Ningjiangzhou and defeated a much larger Liao force. In 1115 he proclaimed himself emperor of a new state called Jin, "gold." Later Jin historical tradition connected the name "Gold" both with the Anchuhu River, whose name is associated with the Jurchen word for gold, and with an origin story in which Aguda contrasted enduring gold with iron, which the story associated symbolically with the Liao. This was a dynastic foundation narrative, not the literal meaning of the Chinese name Liao (遼), which is commonly linked to the Liao River and whose original Khitan meaning remains uncertain. Whatever the precise motives, the choice announced that the Jurchens were not merely rebels but the founders of a dynasty.

Historical Vocabulary
Meng'an and mouke (猛安謀克)

The basic Jurchen military-social units of the early Jin. A mouke was nominally a group of about three hundred households; ten mouke made up a meng'an. Their leaders held hereditary or semi-hereditary offices, and the households provided soldiers, horses, and supplies in wartime while farming, herding, and hunting in peacetime. The system fused kinship, settlement, taxation, and command. It matters historically because it explains why Jin could mobilize quickly and, later, why resettling meng'an-mouke households on the North China plain created both a garrison for the conquered regions and enduring friction with the Han farmers among whom the Jurchen households lived. Over time the units lost military efficiency as households became farmers, tenants, or landlords.

Jin state formation drew on these Jurchen institutions. Aguda organized his followers into meng'an and mouke units, and the units gave the new state a coherent structure: households and soldiers were the same people, and commanders were also administrators. Jurchen leaders continued to consult one another in councils of senior lineage heads, and early Jin politics often resembled a collective enterprise among the leading Wanyan and allied clans more than the autocracy of a single emperor. In 1119 the Jurchens began to create their own writing system, modeled in part on Khitan and Chinese scripts, with a large script presented by Wanyan Xiyin in 1119–1120, and a small script created and promulgated under the emperor Xizong in 1138 and first officially used in 1145. The aim was practical (records, edicts, translations) and symbolic (a distinct identity for a new state).

What is striking about the Jin is not that a small people "borrowed" civilization from its neighbors, but that its leaders repeatedly demonstrated administrative and organizational sophistication. They incorporated Khitan and Bohai experts and, increasingly, Han Chinese advisors and officials, using them to manage the growing population of conquered territories. They preserved core Jurchen practices, including the meng'an-mouke system and, for some time, clan-based deliberation, while adding the forms needed to govern farmers and towns. Students should avoid the older story that portrays the Jurchens as uncivilized and becoming sophisticated only after conquest. The Jin state was, from its foundation, a product of Jurchen political culture engaging with a region long shaped by Bohai, Liao, and Korean neighbors, and its early military success was a direct result of Jurchen organization.

Section 36.2

Jin Conquest of the Liao

The Liao empire of the early twelfth century was much larger than the Jin, but its size did not guarantee strength. The emperor Tianzuo was frequently absent from the affairs of government, court factions competed for influence, and regional commanders had to protect frontiers from Jurchen, Song, and Xia pressure. The Liao's dual system of administration, examined in Chapter 35, worked while the court could coordinate its nomadic and agrarian halves. When the court fell into disarray, the two halves increasingly pulled apart. The Jurchen revolt therefore struck an empire already weakened by internal divisions, and the Liao's initial counter-attacks failed.

Jin forces achieved a series of victories between 1115 and 1125. Aguda took Huanglongfu (Changchun region) and Liao towns in the Liaodong peninsula, and in the following years the Jin captured Liao's Supreme Capital, Shangjing, in 1120, the Central Capital in 1122, and other centers. Jin commanders offered generous terms to Liao officers and communities who surrendered, incorporated many of them into Jin service, and settled or moved populations to consolidate control. Many Khitan and Bohai commanders joined the Jin, and some Han and Khitan officers from the Liao's southern regions became prominent Jin officials. The conquest was thus not a simple story of one people replacing another; it was a process of absorbing Liao personnel and practices into a new imperial structure.

Liao resistance did not end at once. In the south, around the Liao's Southern Capital (Yanjing, modern Beijing), a Northern Liao regime was briefly proclaimed in 1122 under a Liao noble. The last Liao emperor, Tianzuo, fled westward and was captured by Jin forces in 1125, which is conventionally treated as the end of the Liao. Yelü Dashi, a Khitan prince of the imperial line, escaped westward with a following of Khitans and other supporters, and in the 1130s he established a new state in Central Asia, known in Chinese sources as the Western Liao and in Persian sources as the Qara Khitai. The Khitan political tradition therefore continued, in an altered form and in a new region, for nearly another century. Other Khitan communities remained in the Jin realm, at times serving in Jin armies and at times rising in revolt, as they did in the 1150s and 1160s.

The conquest should be read for continuity as well as rupture. The Jin inherited territories, populations, and administrative problems that the Liao had managed: ruling both pastoral and agrarian communities; managing the frontier with Song and Xia; and holding cities in the Yan region whose population was overwhelmingly Han Chinese. They also inherited the diplomatic apparatus of the Song-Liao relationship, including expectations about border markets, embassies, and treaty language, which the Jin would reshape in their own favor. The Jin adopted some Liao institutions directly, including aspects of the five-capital system, routes of communication, and several categories of officials, and employed many Liao-trained scribes and administrators. At the same time, Jin rule was more oriented to the agrarian south than the Liao had been, a change with lasting consequences once the Jin took the Central Plains.

In the process, the Jin also inherited the Liao's strategic position. The Liao's buffer between the Song and the northeastern peoples had disappeared. For the first time since the tenth century, the Song had a new and unfamiliar neighbor with an army energized by rapid victory, a state still developing formal institutions, and every reason to test the strength of a wealthy and militarily awkward southern neighbor. The alliance discussed in the next section made that testing possible.

Section 36.3

Alliance and Conflict With Northern Song

The Song court had long resented the Liao's control of the Sixteen Prefectures, the strategic region around modern Beijing and Datong that had passed to the Liao in 938, and had never fully accepted the loss. Earlier campaigns to recover it in the tenth century had failed, and the peace of 1005 (Chapter 35) had brought stability at the price of annual payments. When news of the Jurchen victories reached the Song court, Huizong and his advisors saw an opportunity. The court dispatched envoys by sea, since the overland route through Liao territory was closed, to negotiate with the Jurchens. The resulting agreement, known as the Alliance Conducted at Sea (Haishang zhi meng), negotiated in stages between roughly 1120 and 1123, provided that the Jin would attack the Liao's Central Capital while the Song would attack Yan (modern Beijing); the Song would recover the Sixteen Prefectures, and would transfer to the Jin the annual payments it had made to the Liao.

The alliance was a strategic gamble that reflected both ambition and calculation. Song statesmen argued that the Jurchens were a distant, small power and that using them to weaken the Liao would allow the Song to regain lost territory cheaply. Critics at court warned that removing the Liao would bring a more dangerous neighbor to the Song's northern frontier and that Song had no means of enforcing agreements. Their warnings proved accurate. Song forces under the eunuch commander Tong Guan attacked Yan in 1122 and were defeated by the Liao remnants, exposing serious weaknesses in Song military organization. The Jin then took Yan themselves. In the settlement of 1123 the Jin returned the city and a limited number of nearby prefectures to the Song, but stripped the area of much of its population and wealth beforehand and demanded substantial compensation in payments and grain. The Song regained a territory that was smaller and poorer than the one it had sought, while its army's failure was plain to the Jin.

Several further episodes eroded trust. In 1123 a Liao general named Zhang Jue defected to the Song with a territory around Pingzhou. The Jin demanded him back as a violation of the agreement; the Song, unwilling to fight, eventually killed Zhang Jue and sent his head to the Jin, an act that discredited Song promises to defectors. Meanwhile other Liao officials with knowledge of Song weakness moved to the Jin, where they became advisors and commanders. Jin leaders learned from these officers about the Song's defenses, its reliance on the Yellow River plain and the capital, and its tendency to negotiate under pressure.

Behind these incidents lay a deeper problem. The alliance rested on incompatible expectations. The Song court hoped to recover a traditional frontier and a status equal to or higher than the Jurchens; the Jin, whose military successes far exceeded expectations, increasingly regarded the Song as a wealthy state that had failed to earn what it sought. The old expectations of Song-Liao diplomacy, which had rested on mutual acknowledgment of parity, did not carry over. Jin negotiators bargained from strength, and Song officials sometimes believed that terms already agreed could be re-negotiated. The result was a rapid transition from partnership to confrontation.

The lesson of the alliance is a general one about the risks of temporary strategic partnerships. Both parties entered the alliance for immediate goals, but the alliance itself altered the balance of power on which each depended. The fall of the Northern Song was not an isolated surprise. It emerged from a rapidly changing three-state struggle in which the Song underestimated the consequences of removing a buffer state and overestimated its ability to manage a new and stronger neighbor.

Section 36.4

The Jingkang Crisis and Fall of Kaifeng

Historical Vocabulary
The Jingkang Crisis (靖康之變)

The name given to the Jin capture of Kaifeng and the removal of the Song imperial family and court, named for the reign period Jingkang (1126–1127) of the emperor Qinzong. Song and later writers described it with words for "disaster" or "shame," and the phrase became a lasting reference point for arguments about loyalty, war, and peace. Historians use "crisis" or "incident" to describe a process that included two invasions, two sieges, failed negotiations, and a final occupation, rather than a single day. It matters because it ended the Northern Song, because the captivity of two emperors created a legitimacy problem for the successor regime, and because it shaped later Song and Chinese historical memory.

In the autumn of 1125 a Jin invasion began in two principal columns: a western army under Wanyan Zonghan advancing from the Shanxi region and an eastern army under Wanyan Zongwang moving through the Yan region and Hebei toward the Yellow River. The Song court, whose frontier forces had been badly handled in the Liao campaign, was unprepared. In early 1126 Huizong, facing the Jin advance, abdicated in favor of his son, who reigned as Qinzong and adopted the reign title Jingkang. The transition, made in the middle of a crisis, left unclear who exercised power: Huizong moved away from the capital, and his advisors, several of them later blamed for the disaster, were dismissed or fled.

The Jin reached Kaifeng in the first weeks of 1126 and besieged it. Defenders, led by officials such as Li Gang, held the walls, while court officials debated whether to fight, to pay, or to move the capital. The Jin demanded an enormous indemnity in precious metals, silk, horses, and cattle, cession of important prefectures including Taiyuan, Zhongshan, and Hejian, and hostages, including a Song prince. Sums in the millions of taels of gold and silver appear in Song sources and were far beyond what the court could easily raise. The Song agreed to terms, and Jin forces withdrew, in part because their commanders were reluctant to remain during the spring and because relief armies were converging.

That withdrawal turned out to be a pause. Song leaders divided between those who wanted to honor the agreement and those who wanted to resist. Relief armies moving toward the north were defeated, and the court repeatedly reversed its policy: it dismissed its most determined defenders, then recalled them; it delayed paying the indemnity, then attempted to raise it by confiscating the wealth of the capital's residents. Song accounts describe desperate measures, including reliance on a self-proclaimed magician whose "six-jia" soldiers were expected to defeat the Jin by supernatural means. These accounts must be read with caution, since they were often written by writers eager to explain a stunning collapse by pointing to folly, but they suggest a court that no longer had confidence in its military.

In the autumn of 1126 the Jin returned. Zonghan's army took Taiyuan and moved down the Fen valley, while the eastern army crossed the Yellow River. The Song lacked large mobile forces in the region, and many of its best troops had been stationed elsewhere, particularly in the northwest and in the Yan region. Kaifeng, located on an open plain with limited natural defenses and dependent on canal-borne grain, was difficult to hold without field armies to relieve it. The city fell around the turn of the year 1126–1127. In the following months the Jin plundered the palaces and treasuries, took away books, instruments, ritual objects, and craftspeople, and deposed the Song emperors. Huizong and Qinzong, together with many members of the imperial clan, senior officials, and thousands of others, were taken north in 1127. Huizong died in captivity in 1135 and Qinzong in 1161.

The Jin did not at first attempt to rule the Song heartland directly. In 1127 they installed a former Song official, Zhang Bangchang, as emperor of a state called Chu, intending it to function as a buffer regime; it collapsed within months when Zhang, lacking support, stepped aside. Later Jin created another buffer state, Qi, under Liu Yu (1130–1137), which governed parts of the Central Plains. These experiments show that Jin leaders initially hoped to manage the plain indirectly, through Chinese officials, rather than annex it. They also show how difficult it was to build political legitimacy on the strength of foreign backing alone.

Three questions organize historians' analysis of the crisis. First, why was the Northern Song unable to defend its capital? The answers include the location of Kaifeng, the distribution of Song armies, the failure of the Liao campaign, and the sudden loss of a functioning chain of command after the abdication. Second, how did court factionalism, military weakness, diplomacy, and Jin strategy interact? The Song court's alternation between appeasement and resistance made both policies less effective, while Jin commanders exploited Song hesitation and divisions. Third, what happens to a state when its ruling court is physically captured? The Northern Song, as an institution, ended. But the capture did not extinguish the dynasty's claim, since other members of the imperial family remained free, and the state's administrative traditions, personnel, and ideological resources existed in many regions. These conditions made a restoration possible, though far from certain.

The war also had an environmental consequence. In 1128 a Song commander, Du Chong, ordered the breaching of Yellow River dikes near Kaifeng in an attempt to block Jin advances. The river's course shifted, flooding wide areas of the plain, and later flooding shifted the main channel more permanently to the south, toward the Huai. The measure illustrates how warfare and environmental management interacted in the plains, and how military decisions had long-term consequences for the region's agriculture and population.

Section 36.5

The Founding of Southern Song

Historical Vocabulary
Xingzai (行在) and Lin'an (臨安)

Xingzai means "the place where the emperor is currently staying," a "temporary residence." In 1129 the Southern Song court named Hangzhou Lin'an, a name often interpreted as "overlooking peace" or "temporary peace," and from the 1130s the city served as the emperor's xingzai. The Southern Song never formally declared Hangzhou its permanent capital, because doing so would have implicitly abandoned the claim to recover Kaifeng. The terminology therefore reveals the political stakes: the name of a city expressed a claim about legitimacy and about the temporary nature of the loss of the north.

The imperial family's fate left a gap in Song authority. Zhao Gou, a son of Huizong, was not in Kaifeng when the city fell. He had earlier been sent to the Jin as part of negotiations in 1126, then returned, and was in Hebei when the city was captured; accounts differ on the details. Whatever the precise sequence, he was in a position to take advantage of the void left by the fall of the capital. In 1127 he was enthroned as emperor, Gaozong, at Yingtianfu (modern Shangqiu, Henan), a city with dynastic significance since it had been the Song founder's early base. Legitimacy was reinforced by the empress dowager Meng, who had been deposed from the palace long before and thus was not in the captured city; her endorsement of Zhao Gou's succession helped bridge the gap created by the removal of Qinzong.

The new court faced overwhelming difficulties. Jin armies continued to advance, and the court moved, first to Yangzhou, then across the Yangtze to Jiankang (Nanjing), and then to Hangzhou and other places. In 1129 a mutiny by officers in Hangzhou briefly forced the emperor to abdicate in favor of an infant son before loyal commanders restored him. In late 1129 and early 1130, a Jin army under Wanyan Zongbi (Wuzhu) crossed the Yangtze, sacked Hangzhou and other cities, and pursued Gaozong to the coast, where the court took to boats. A Jin force was checked at the Yangtze by the commander Han Shizhong's blockade at Huangtiandang in 1130 and then retreated. The Jin did not conquer the south, in part because of logistics, terrain, disease, waterways, and the difficulty of holding cities distant from Jin bases; the Song, however, had barely survived.

Southern Song survival depended on rebuilding the state's fiscal and military foundations. The Song had lost the plain that had produced much of its revenue and the capital that had organized its bureaucracy. What remained was a region of great productive potential, especially the lower Yangtze delta, the middle Yangtze, Fujian, and Sichuan, but one that had not previously been the center of state administration. The court created special revenue agencies and regional supply arrangements to provision armies; expanded existing taxes and levies, which fell heavily on southern farmers; and increased reliance on paper currency, monopoly revenues, and commercial taxation, subjects discussed in Chapter 32 and again in Chapter 37. It reorganized its armies, which in the 1130s were often regional forces under powerful commanders. Generals such as Yue Fei, Han Shizhong, Zhang Jun, and Liu Guangshi commanded large forces in the Yangtze and Huai regions, partly recruited from northern refugees and volunteers, and often functioned with a degree of autonomy that reminded some officials of the late Tang.

Legitimacy was another problem. Gaozong reigned without the traditional insignia, without the ancestral temples of the capital, and with the reigning emperor and his predecessor in Jin hands. The court emphasized continuity by keeping the name and institutions of the Song, by treating Kaifeng as its rightful capital, by honoring the ancestral cult, and by calling for the recovery of the north. Yet Gaozong also had reasons to avoid wholehearted commitment to reconquest. A successful campaign that brought back Qinzong could raise awkward questions about who should rule; powerful generals could become political rivals; and war placed heavy burdens on the fiscal system. These concerns, together with genuine military weakness, shaped the peace policies of the 1130s and 1140s discussed in the next section.

Students should understand Southern Song as a new political configuration, not merely Northern Song transplanted southward. The state was smaller, faced a powerful northern rival, and depended on southern resources; its court was located in a great commercial and administrative city with a different social geography; and its officials, many of them northern refugees or their sons, had to come to terms with southern elites, local families, and regional interests. It preserved the Song's civil bureaucracy and examination system, and drew on the intellectual and cultural traditions of the Northern Song, but it did so in a different setting and under different pressures. The Confucian Kong family illustrates the shift: while the main line remained at Qufu under Jin rule, a branch moved south with the court and eventually settled at Quzhou, and both branches claimed the hereditary title of Duke Yansheng, a small but telling example of competing claims to inherit cultural authority.

Section 36.6

Jin Government and Society in North China

By the mid-twelfth century the Jin ruled a huge agrarian population, on the order of tens of millions according to household registers of the late twelfth century (the figures are approximate and vary from one census to another), alongside its pastoral and forest peoples in the northeast. This section matters because it prevents the Jin from existing only as an invading army. Rather than a "conquest dynasty" that simply imposed foreign rule, or a dynasty that gradually became "Chinese," the Jin is better understood as a state of institutional pluralism and adaptation: its rulers selectively adopted inherited Liao and Chinese administrative forms while maintaining Jurchen identity and Jurchen military institutions.

Early Jin government was closely tied to the meng'an-mouke system and to councils of Jurchen leaders. After the conquests, the Jin added Chinese-style administration, initially in an improvised fashion, employing former Liao and Song officials, and using puppet regimes in the Central Plains. Under the emperor Xizong (r. 1135–1149) the court adopted Chinese-style ministries and a central bureaucracy, replacing much of the older council system, and the reforms culminated under Wanyan Liang (r. 1149–1161), who assassinated Xizong, consolidated the central government in a single department of state affairs supported by a privy council and censorate, and in 1153 moved the principal capital from the northeastern Shangjing to Zhongdu, on the site of the former Liao Southern Capital. Jin retained five capitals, following the Liao example, which allowed the court to maintain a presence in multiple regions. Wanyan Liang also eliminated potential rivals in the Wanyan clan and prepared the invasion of the south. His autocratic style, and the costs of his campaigns, caused deep resentment and led to his assassination in 1161.

Local administration relied on circuits, prefectures, and counties, staffed by a mixture of Jurchen, Khitan, and Han officials, with the Jurchen and Khitan often prominent in military and supervisory posts and Han officials filling many civil positions. The Jin promulgated a Chinese-language legal code, the Taihe Code (1201), which drew heavily on the Tang code and Song legal practice, while Jurchen customary law and imperial edicts continued to matter in practice. The Jin also developed an examination system. Han Chinese candidates took examinations in the Chinese classics and poetry, and from 1173 the Jin instituted an examination in the Jurchen language and script, alongside translations of Chinese classics and histories into Jurchen. The Jurchen exams and the state school system show that Jin rulers saw education both as a way to recruit officials and as a way to preserve Jurchen language and identity, a goal they came to regard as increasingly urgent as Jurchen households mixed with their neighbors.

Land and military settlement posed a persistent problem. After the conquest the Jin moved large numbers of Jurchen households from the northeast onto lands in Hebei, Shandong, Henan, and elsewhere in the north, settling them in meng'an-mouke groups, often in villages adjacent to Han farming communities. The Jurchen received land grants, some of it confiscated from former Song or Liao holders. Over time, many Jurchen households, unaccustomed to intensive plow farming or preferring an easier life, leased their land to Han tenants, fell into debt, or sold property. The state repeatedly intervened, surveying lands, redistributing holdings, and warning against the abandonment of martial skills. The emperor Shizong (r. 1161–1189), whose reign later generations remembered as a period of stability and good order, worked to preserve Jurchen language, dress, riding, and archery, and to prevent Jurchen households from losing their land. Yet the underlying pressures persisted: as Jurchen households became farmers among farmers, the military system that had powered the conquest weakened.

Relations among peoples varied by place, class, and time. Many Han Chinese elites served the Jin as officials, tutors, physicians, and local gentry, and their families often accepted Jin rule while defending Chinese cultural traditions. Others resisted, joined loyalist militias in the early years, or crossed the frontier to the south. The Khitans, who had ruled the previous empire, occupied a distinctive position: many held office, some intermarried with Jurchen elites, and others rebelled. Jurchen elites themselves ranged from mouke families living on rural lands to court aristocrats participating in Chinese-style ritual and literary culture. The Jin emperors ordered Han men in conquered areas to adopt Jurchen hairstyles and dress in the early years, provoking resistance; scholars note that enforcement was uneven and that such regulations did not become a lasting feature of Jin rule. In addition, the Jurchen elite acquired many captives as dependent laborers during and after the conquests, and authorities periodically sought to regulate or redeem them, a reminder that conquest included coercion and servitude as well as accommodation.

The Jin economy was based on agriculture, with important contributions from crafts, mining, salt, iron, and trade. The state issued paper currency (jiaochao) from the 1150s, a practice adapted from the Song's earlier experiments (Chapter 32), and struggled thereafter with inflation and the depreciation of notes, especially when military expenses rose. Coin supplies were limited, and Song coin often flowed north while Jin officials worried about outflows of silver and copper in exchange for southern tea and other goods. Printing thrived in Jin territories, particularly in Shanxi, where the town of Pingyang was a center of commercial and government printing. A striking example of religious and social history is the Buddhist canon carved in Shanxi under the initiative of a woman, Cui Fazhen, who is recorded as having vowed, in the mid-twelfth century, to raise funds for carving the woodblocks of the entire canon; the resulting work, completed in the 1170s, is known as the Jin canon and survives partly in the Zhaocheng region.

Religious and intellectual life in Jin territories was vigorous. Buddhist monasteries retained wealth and influence; Chan and Huayan teachers were prominent; and new Daoist movements emerged. The most important was Quanzhen Daoism, founded in Shandong by Wang Zhe (1113–1170), which emphasized inner cultivation, monastic discipline, and the harmony of Confucian, Buddhist, and Daoist teachings, and which would become one of the major Daoist lineages (Chapter 39). Jin literati produced poetry, prose, and drama, including narrative medley performances and early forms of theater that would flourish under the Yuan. Scholars such as Yuan Haowen, who lived through the Jin's fall, were conscious of preserving the memory of Jin culture. The wider point is that Jin territories were not a cultural wasteland awaiting Mongol or Song rescue; they were a region of creative institutions and communities, some of them in tension with the state.

Section 36.7

Song-Jin Warfare and Diplomacy

After the 1120s the Song-Jin relationship passed from conquest to a longer contest in which war and diplomacy proceeded together. During the 1130s the Southern Song, at first desperate, gradually stabilized its position. Defensive campaigns on the Yangtze and Huai, the recovery of some regions in the middle Yangtze by commanders such as Yue Fei, and successes in the Sichuan-Shaanxi frontier under Wu Jie and others demonstrated that the Jin could not easily conquer the south. But neither could the Song recover the north. Jin, for its part, found the deployment of large armies in the south difficult and, after its early experiment with puppet states failed, began to think in terms of stable frontiers.

Within the Southern Song court, debates over war and peace were bitter. Officials are often described retrospectively as favoring "war" or "peace," but these were overlapping policy positions and personal networks rather than two stable political parties, and individuals shifted positions as circumstances changed. Advocates of resistance, including officials and generals who had lost homes in the north, argued that concession would dishonor the dynasty and encourage further Jin aggression. Advocates of peace argued that the state lacked the resources for reconquest, that the risk of defeat was too high, and that a permanent war footing would strengthen generals at the expense of civilian government. The emperor Gaozong, who ultimately decided policy, and his chief councilor Qin Gui, who served for most of 1138–1155, favored peace. In 1138–1139 they negotiated an agreement by which the Jin would return the Henan and Shaanxi regions to the Song. A Jin coup in 1139 brought Jin leaders favoring renewed war to power; the agreement was repudiated, and war resumed in 1140. Yue Fei's northern campaign that year won important victories, but the court ordered his forces to withdraw. In 1141 Gaozong and Qin Gui removed the leading generals from their commands by promoting them to civilian positions, thereby placing the armies under closer central control. Yue Fei was arrested and, in early 1142, executed.

The resulting settlement, usually called the Treaty of Shaoxing (concluded in 1141–1142), established the Huai River and the Dasan Pass as the frontier, thereby confirming Jin possession of the Central Plains; provided that the Song would send annual payments of 250,000 taels of silver and 250,000 bolts of silk to the Jin; and required the Song emperor to accept the status of a subordinate (chen, "subject") in relation to the Jin emperor. In return, the Jin returned the remains of Huizong and released Gaozong's mother. For Southern Song, the terms were humiliating; for Gaozong and his supporters, they secured a stable frontier and the survival of the state. Later Song writers, especially those who idealized resistance, treated Qin Gui as the archetypal traitor, and Yue Fei as a loyal hero. Historians today are more cautious. They emphasize that Gaozong, not Qin Gui alone, made the decisive choices; that the court had legitimate concerns about the autonomy of powerful commanders; that Southern Song lacked the means to reconquer the north; and that Yue Fei's execution nevertheless reflected a ruthless political decision that many contemporaries found deeply troubling. Yue Fei was rehabilitated in 1162, and later dynasties, and especially modern nationalism, transformed him into a symbol of patriotic loyalty, a reading that draws on, but goes beyond, the twelfth-century evidence.

The peace did not last unbroken. In 1161 the Jin emperor Wanyan Liang, seeking to complete the conquest of the south, launched an invasion with a large, hastily assembled army. The Song, under a commander named Yu Yunwen, defeated Jin river forces at Caishi on the Yangtze; and within weeks, amid rebellions in the north (including a Khitan uprising) and dissatisfaction with his rule, Wanyan Liang was assassinated by his own officers. The new Jin emperor, Shizong, withdrew. Gaozong abdicated in 1162 in favor of Xiaozong, who was more inclined to resist. In 1163 a Song northern expedition under Zhang Jun initially captured places but was then defeated at Fuli. The two courts then negotiated the Treaty of Longxing (1164–1165), which changed the relationship from subject-and-lord to that of "nephew" and "uncle," reduced the annual payments (now "annual coins" rather than tribute), and adjusted the frontier. The change in diplomatic language, though limited, mattered enormously to Song officials, for whom protocol was a way of expressing status.

Another round came in 1206–1208. Han Tuozhou, a powerful Song chief councilor, promoted a northern expedition (the Kaixi expedition) in 1206, hoping to exploit Jin's growing difficulties with the Mongols and with internal problems. The campaign failed. In the Treaty of Jiading (1208), the Song, whose court had by then executed or removed Han Tuozhou, agreed to higher payments and an indemnity, while the Jin abandoned the campaign. The treaty maintained the uncle-nephew terminology, and the relationship stabilized again.

Border relations included much more than treaties and campaigns. Envoys traveled regularly between the two courts, carrying gifts, letters, and information, and their accounts (including those of Song envoys such as Fan Chengda, who traveled north in 1170) offer valuable evidence about conditions in the Jin realm. Border markets on the Huai frontier regulated trade in tea, silk, salt, horses, and other goods, though smuggling was common and both governments periodically restricted certain exports. The diplomacy involved rituals and forms of address, fixed schedules of exchanges, careful rules about titles, and formal negotiation of terms; in short, it evolved into an institutionalized interstate order, like the Song-Liao order that preceded it, but under different terms and with continuing contest over legitimacy and territory. The important lesson is that warfare did not prevent diplomacy, and diplomacy did not end rivalry.

Section 36.8

Migration and Regional Change

The Jin conquest and the years of war that followed caused one of the largest migrations of the premodern period. The precise numbers are unknown, and estimates range from hundreds of thousands to millions of people moving south across the Yangtze and Huai in the 1120s and 1130s. Those who moved included officials and their families, members of the imperial clan, scholars, soldiers and militia leaders, artisans, merchants, monks and nuns, physicians, actors, and ordinary farming households. Some traveled in organized groups with their kin and dependents; others were scattered individuals attempting to reach relatives. Movements were not only southward: the Jin also moved populations, including captured artisans and officials, northward, and Jurchen households moved south to settle the plains.

North China experienced conquest, demographic disruption, and new political structures. Warfare, famine, and the breaching of the Yellow River dikes devastated parts of Henan, Hebei, and Shandong, and the population declined in areas of heaviest fighting. Local loyalist militias fought Jin forces, and bandits and irregular armies preyed on rural communities during the transition. Once the Jin consolidated its rule, however, the northern countryside recovered. Meng'an-mouke settlements introduced new households, land registration and taxation were reorganized, and towns and markets revived. Kaifeng, though it never regained its earlier scale, remained a significant city and became the Jin's Southern Capital. Historians caution that the north's decline has sometimes been exaggerated by southern sources and by modern narratives centered on the Song; Jin territories remained densely populated and productive.

The south absorbed migrants, capital, skills, and administrative personnel. Many northern officials entered the Southern Song bureaucracy, and northern families of scholarly background competed for positions with southern families, creating tensions that surfaced in examination competition, patronage, and factional politics. Refugee elites brought books, family records, and cultural practices; commercial families brought capital and networks; and skilled artisans, including printers, weavers, and cooks, brought techniques. In cities such as Hangzhou, the arrival of northerners contributed to a rapid growth in population and demand, and northern culinary, theatrical, and religious traditions became part of urban culture (a subject taken up in Chapter 37). Rural regions in Zhejiang, Jiangxi, Fujian, and elsewhere also absorbed migrants, sometimes through land reclamation, expansion of rice cultivation in hills and marshes, and the extension of tenancy relations. Fujian, Sichuan, and the Lower Yangtze had their own regional identities and interests, and the migrants' relations with local populations were sometimes cooperative and sometimes strained.

Migration should also be tied to the long-term demographic shift from north to south. The process had begun during the Age of Division (Chapters 20 and 21), when northern elites moved south after the collapse of the Western Jin, and had continued after the An Lushan Rebellion (Chapter 29), and again during the Five Dynasties (Chapter 30). The twelfth-century migration reinforced a pattern already present: by the early twelfth century, southern regions had already gained in population and productivity through rice agriculture and commerce, and the movements of the 1120s accelerated the concentration of people, wealth, and cultural production in the Yangtze basin and the southeast. The Southern Song can thus be understood as the political expression of a transformation that had been underway for centuries.

The consequences for the north were complex. Northern intellectual life continued, though with different institutional ties: Jin scholars maintained Confucian schools, temples, and academies, and in some respects preserved Tang and Northern Song practices more conservatively than the south, which experienced rapid change in intellectual movements such as the school of the Learning of the Way (Chapter 34). Regional differences in dialect, cuisine, dress, and custom widened between the two states, and northerners and southerners increasingly saw one another as different, though both sides used the shared vocabulary of Chinese literary culture. Yet family, commercial, and religious connections persisted across the frontier, in the form of trade, pilgrimage, and the movement of texts.

Section 36.9

Mongol Pressure and the End of Jin

In the late twelfth century the Jin state faced growing internal and external difficulties. The meng'an-mouke system had weakened, and Jin armies increasingly depended on Han and Khitan levies; paper currency depreciated; the Yellow River's shifting course, notably around 1194, caused floods and dislocation in Henan and Shandong; and the court was troubled by succession disputes and factionalism. The Jin also devoted immense resources to defending their northern frontier against steppe raiders, including the construction of long lines of ditches and walls, sometimes called the Jin frontier fortifications, and to periodic expeditions against steppe groups. Meanwhile, on the Mongolian plateau, a new power was forming.

In 1206, after years of warfare among steppe groups, Temüjin was acknowledged as Chinggis Khan of a unified Mongol confederation (see Chapter 38). The Jin at first regarded the Mongols as one of many frontier peoples to be managed through alliances and payments. But the Mongols combined striking cavalry mobility, a flexible command structure, and a capacity to absorb technical experts and allies. Several Jin frontier peoples, including the Ongut and parts of the Khitan, defected to the Mongols. In 1211 a Mongol army defeated a large Jin force in the northwest and swept into the North China plain. The Jin court, weakened by the assassination of an emperor in 1213 and by disputes among its officials, could not organize a coherent defense. In 1214 the Jin agreed to a settlement and, fearing further attacks, moved its capital from Zhongdu south to Kaifeng. The Mongols, regarding this as a breach, resumed the war and captured Zhongdu in 1215.

Territorial contraction followed. The Jin lost the northeast, Hebei, and much of Shandong and Shanxi. In some regions Khitan and Han commanders proclaimed independent regimes or submitted to the Mongols; in Shandong the "Red Coat" rebels and local leaders created semi-independent power bases. Restricted to Henan and parts of Shaanxi, the Jin attempted in 1217 to compensate by invading the Southern Song, which had stopped payments after 1214. The campaign lasted until 1224, drained Jin resources, and alienated the Song. Meanwhile the Mongols were also conquering the Tangut Western Xia (Chapter 35).

In the early 1230s Mongol armies pressed into Henan, and the Jin court, after losing Kaifeng, retreated to Caizhou. The Song, whose leaders remembered the humiliations of 1127 and hoped to recover the Central Plains, agreed in 1233 to cooperate with the Mongols against the Jin, supplying grain and troops. Caizhou fell in early 1234 and the last Jin ruler died, ending the Jin. Song attempts to occupy Kaifeng and Luoyang soon afterward were driven back, opening a direct Song-Mongol conflict. The campaigns themselves, including their commanders, tactics, and weapons, are examined in Chapter 38.

Historians offer several explanations for the Jin's collapse. Some emphasize the Mongols' extraordinary military and organizational capacity, which no state of the region could easily match. Others stress internal weaknesses in Jin: the decline of the Jurchen military class, fiscal stress and currency problems, ethnic tensions, and the difficulty of defending open plains and long frontiers. Still others emphasize contingency: the timing of the Mongol rise, the 1213 assassination, and the diversion of resources to the southern war. All of these factors, in different combinations, mattered. The end of the Jin closed the political arc begun in 1115: the state that had destroyed the Liao and Northern Song was destroyed in turn by a still larger steppe empire. The Jurchens themselves did not disappear; many were absorbed into Mongol and Han communities, while Jurchen populations of the northeast continued to exist as distinct communities. Their later descendants were an important historical antecedent of the Manchu community whose identity was consolidated in the seventeenth century (Chapter 45), a process of construction, not the simple renaming of an unchanged people.

Section 36.10

Chapter Review and Jin-Southern Song Checkpoint

This chapter has traced how one multi-state order replaced another. The Liao-Song-Xia arrangement of Chapter 35 gave way, in the space of a generation, to a Jin-Southern Song order. Jurchen conquest destroyed the Liao and Northern Song, displaced populations and political centers, and produced two regimes with different geographies and institutions. The Jin governed the north through institutional pluralism and adaptation; the Southern Song rebuilt an imperial court in a different setting and survived by combining defensive warfare, fiscal reinvention, and diplomacy. Migration linked and transformed both regions. Growing Mongol power eventually destabilized the order they had created.

Students preparing for the checkpoint should be able to compare Jin and Southern Song in government, military organization, economy, and culture; explain why the Song-Jin alliance failed; discuss the causes and consequences of the Jingkang Crisis; explain the terms and significance of the treaties of 1141–1142, 1164–1165, and 1208; describe how migration changed North China and the south; and explain why the Jin fell. The features that follow ask students to work with these questions through timelines, maps, documents, and debate.

The documents concerning the settlement of 1141–1142 record that the Song ruler would address the Jin ruler in the language of a subject; that the boundary would follow the Huai River and a line to the west; that the Song would send fixed yearly quantities of silver and silk; and that the Jin would return the remains of the deceased emperor Huizong and the emperor's mother.

Analysis: The compilation reveals the formal structure of interstate relations: fixed terms, ritual language, and clear rules about payment and boundaries. It shows the perspective of a Song writer, who preserved documents, arranged them, and sometimes added commentary that reflects Song attitudes; Jin voices appear mainly through documents addressed to the Song. It does not tell us how Jin officials understood the treaties, how the payments were collected, how border communities experienced them, or how the terms were debated privately within each court.

Questions for students:

  1. What does this source say or show?
  2. Who created it, and what might their purpose have been?
  3. What can historians learn from it?
  4. What can historians not learn from it alone?
Historical Debate

Why Did the Northern Song Fall So Quickly?

The question: The Northern Song was one of the wealthiest and most populous states of its time. Why did it lose its capital and its court within roughly two years of the first Jin invasion?

Interpretation A — Institutional and military weakness: Some scholars stress structural features: a system that distrusted senior commanders, divided authority in ways that made rapid command difficult, and had stationed its best armies on other frontiers, leaving the capital region under-defended. Dependence on payments and a tradition of buying peace may have blunted military readiness.
Interpretation B — Strategic miscalculation and Jin effectiveness: Others emphasize that the alliance with the Jin removed the Liao buffer, that Song planners misjudged the Jurchens, and that Jin commanders were exceptionally effective: highly mobile, well-informed by defectors, and quick to adapt. On this view, the Song's weaknesses mattered because of the opponent it faced.
Interpretation C — Court politics and policy instability: A third view stresses factional conflict, the influence of a small circle around Huizong, and the abrupt abdication in the midst of war, which produced hesitation and reversals. Later Song and Yuan-era historians often adopted moral explanations of this kind, blaming named officials and the emperor's tastes.

Evidence: All three draw on Song court records, memorials, and diaries; on Jin and Yuan-era histories; and on the geography of the campaigns. The sources are problematic: Song writers had reasons to explain defeat in moral terms and to assign blame to particular officials, while Jin sources are sparse.

Why it matters: How historians explain the fall shapes how they see the Song state overall: as fundamentally fragile, as capable but unlucky, or as damaged by particular decisions. It also bears on how they interpret the "dynastic cycle," which is not an adequate explanation for events that depended on fiscal, military, diplomatic, and political specifics.

Student task: Using evidence from Sections 36.3 and 36.4, decide which interpretation, or which combination, is best supported, and identify what additional evidence would help.

Multiple Perspectives

Living Through the Conquests and the New Order

Note: these are analytical syntheses of patterns visible in the sources, not quotations from individuals.

A Jurchen military household (mouke): Conquest brought land, status, and obligations. Households moved south to unfamiliar plains, provided soldiers and horses, and were expected to remain warriors while farming among Han neighbors. Some prospered; others, as land was leased or sold, found their circumstances eroded.
A North China farming family under Jin rule: After the years of fighting, the priority was survival: rebuilding fields, paying taxes to a new government, and adjusting to Jurchen neighbors and officials. Some families suffered dispossession or enslavement; many experienced Jin rule mostly through tax collectors and local magistrates, often Han themselves.
A Southern Song court official: Officials debated how to balance the duty to recover the north with the realities of military weakness and fiscal strain. Many refugee officials were personally invested in war; others feared the power of generals and the costs of conflict.
A refugee family moving south: Leaving ancestral land and tombs was wrenching and often violent. Families faced hunger, disease, and separation, and in the south they encountered both opportunities and resentment from local populations who feared competition for land and office.
A border merchant: Trade across the Huai was profitable and risky. Official markets and smuggling both flourished, and merchants moved information, tea, silk, salt, and coin, with authorities on each side trying to tax, restrict, or profit from the traffic.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From the Liao-Song-Xia Order to the Jin-Southern Song Order

What Changed

  • The Song-Liao relationship, based on parity as "brother" courts, gave way to relationships of subordination (1142) and then uncle-nephew status (1165, 1208)
  • The north was governed by a Jurchen-led state controlling far more of the agrarian population than the Liao had
  • The Song lost the Central Plains and the historic capital and relocated to the south
  • The balance between the two states depended more on the Huai-Yangtze zone than on the old northern frontier

What Stayed the Same

  • Interstate relations continued to rely on treaties, payments, envoys, and border markets
  • Agriculture remained the fiscal foundation, and Buddhism, Daoism, and Confucian learning persisted on both sides
  • Rival states still claimed to be legitimate heirs of earlier imperial traditions
  • Steppe power continued to reshape the region, first through the Jurchens and then the Mongols

Who Experienced Change Most Directly?

Northern refugees who left the plain, communities in the war zones of the Central Plains, Khitan and Han populations newly brought under Jin rule, relocated Jurchen military households, and frontier populations along the Huai and in the northeast experienced the most direct change in residence, status, and obligation.

Who Experienced Continuity?

Agricultural households away from the main campaigns, surviving local elites who served new governments, Buddhist and Daoist institutions, and the bureaucratic and literary traditions carried by officials on both sides experienced considerable continuity in daily work and belief.

Why the pattern matters: The new order reconfigured rather than abandoned earlier diplomatic practices; those practices persisted until Mongol expansion overturned the balance on which the Jin-Southern Song interstate system depended.

Cause and Effect

The Jingkang Crisis and the Southward Shift of the Song State

Causes

Long-term: a civil-dominated military system; dependence on payments and diplomacy; factional conflict; best armies stationed elsewhere; a capital with weak natural defenses on an open plain. Short-term: the collapse of the Liao and the failed Yan campaign; the alliance that removed the buffer; Jin defectors' intelligence; Huizong's wartime abdication; inconsistent policy toward negotiation and resistance.

Event or transformation

The Jingkang collapse: two Jin invasions, the fall of Kaifeng, and the capture of two emperors, followed by the southward reconstruction of the Song state under Gaozong, with a new capital region, new revenue arrangements, and a new frontier.

Effects

Immediate: Jin control of the north; buffer regimes in the plain; environmental damage from the breached dikes; the survival of Zhao Gou. Long-term: a Southern Song state in the Yangtze region; a Jin state governing the north; a century of institutionalized rivalry; accelerated southern development; a lasting cultural memory of the loss.

Historical complexity

The crisis cannot be reduced to one cause, such as "corruption" or "weakness." It emerged from the interaction of institutions, strategy, geography, contingency, and the decisions of both Song and Jin leaders.

Note: Most current historians favor multi-causal explanations that combine fiscal, military, political, and geographic factors.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Displaced Household and a Mixed Frontier Community

Historians reconstruct ordinary experience from fragments: anecdotes in writers such as Hong Mai, poems and records of officials and travelers, legal cases, local gazetteers, and the personal writings of a few refugees. One well-known example is Li Qingzhao (1084–1155), a poet from a scholarly family who fled south after Kaifeng fell and whose postscript to her husband's catalog of inscriptions describes losing the collection the couple had spent years assembling. Her case is exceptional in the record, since few displaced people left writings, but it shows the loss of books, property, and family that many experienced.

A displaced household of modest means would have faced the practical problems of the road: obtaining grain, avoiding soldiers and bandits, finding a place to settle, and, in the south, securing land, work, or patronage. Women often bore heavy burdens, managing households after husbands were killed or conscripted, and some were taken captive. Children born in the south grew up in families that still spoke of a northern home.

Along the frontier, mixed communities lived with both risk and opportunity. In the north, Jurchen, Khitan, and Han households shared villages, markets, and temples. Language and marriage patterns varied, and food, dress, and religion mixed in practice even when governments tried to regulate them. Border towns on the Huai had merchants, soldiers, smugglers, interpreters, and officials. Students should remember that these lives varied by place and class and that surviving sources generally favor elites, so any picture is incomplete.

Historical Evidence

The principal Song sources for this chapter include the Sanchao beimeng huibian discussed above; the records and memorials of court officials; the writings of envoys, including Hong Hao, who was detained in the Jin realm for about fifteen years and wrote of his experiences, and Fan Chengda, whose 1170 mission north produced a travel record and a series of poems on what he saw; and miscellaneous writings such as the anecdotal notebooks of Hong Mai. The Song histories compiled later, under the Mongol-ruled Yuan, form the standard narrative but carry the perspective of later editors and the moral concerns of Song historiography.

Jin sources are scarcer. The History of the Jin (Jin shi), compiled in the 1340s, draws on Jin veritable records now largely lost. Inscriptions in Chinese and in the Jurchen script, including stele erected for officials, temples, and imperial ceremonies, provide contemporary evidence. Jin law, poetry, and scholarly writing, such as that of Yuan Haowen, and religious materials such as the Buddhist canon carved in Shanxi and the writings of the Quanzhen movement, add further evidence. Archaeology, including excavations of Jin tombs, towns, and printing sites, and studies of coinage and paper currency, allow historians to check written accounts against material remains.

Students should always ask who wrote a source, for whom, and with what purpose. Song writers tended to explain the fall of the north in moral terms and to treat the Jin with either fear or contempt; Jin-era writers who served the state sometimes emphasized loyalty or cultural accomplishment; and Yuan compilers had their own reasons for how they portrayed the Jin and the Song. Reading against the grain, and comparing documents from both sides, is essential.

Regional and Frontier Context

The chapter's developments were experienced very differently across regions. The northeast, home of the Jurchens, remained the political and military heartland of the early Jin but declined in importance once the capital moved to Zhongdu; it retained ties to forest and river peoples and would later be one of the regions from which the Manchu community of the seventeenth century emerged. The Yan region around modern Beijing, previously the Liao's Southern Capital, became the Jin's political center. The Central Plains, including Kaifeng and Luoyang, were devastated in the war years but recovered as part of the Jin realm. Shandong, home to Wang Zhe's Quanzhen movement and to local militias, became a region of both cultural creativity and political turbulence. Shanxi and Shaanxi were frontier zones between Jin, Song, and Xia, with distinct military and commercial roles.

In the south, Zhejiang and Jiangsu, with Lin'an at the center, became the political and economic heart of the Southern Song; the Huai region was a militarized frontier; Sichuan, with its own resources and relative separation, was governed partly as a semi-autonomous military zone in the early years; and Fujian, Jiangxi, and the southeast coast received migrants and developed rapidly (Chapter 37). Regional differences among these areas influenced how the Song state functioned, since central authorities had to negotiate with local elites, military commanders, and merchants. Frontier peoples on both sides, including Khitan communities in Jin lands, Tangut groups in the northwest, and non-Han populations in the southwest, were participants in this world, not merely subjects of central policy.

China and the Wider World

The Jin-Southern Song order was embedded in a wider Eurasian setting. The Jurchens' relations with Korea (Goryeo), which had long dealt with both Liao and Jin, shaped the northeastern frontier; Goryeo eventually accepted Jin overlordship in the 1120s while maintaining its own independent state and culture. The Khitan Western Liao created a Central Asian power connected to Islamic and Inner Asian worlds, and its existence, along with that of the Xia, complicated Jin and Song strategy. Southern Song increasingly looked outward by sea, developing the maritime connections examined in Chapter 37, partly because the loss of overland routes and northern revenue increased the value of southern ports. And the rise of the Mongols, described in Chapter 38, was a pan-Eurasian event that connected steppe politics to Central Asia, Persia, and eventually Europe. The Song and Jin were active participants in these networks through diplomacy, commerce, warfare, and technology, including the exchange and development of gunpowder weapons, and were not merely passive objects of external forces.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the rise of the Jin did not produce a simple political vacuum in East Asia. It created a new interstate system in which Jin ruled the north and the Southern Song rebuilt imperial government in the south. The system replaced the Liao-Song-Xia order of Chapter 35, but it inherited many of its features: treaties, payments, envoys, and border markets, and the continuing contest over legitimacy.

The Jurchens, a people of the northeastern forests and river valleys, formed the Jin state in 1115 under Wanyan Aguda, drawing on kinship-based military and social institutions, notably the meng'an and mouke. The Jin were not an unsophisticated people who became civilized after conquest; they were skillful organizers who incorporated Khitan, Bohai, and Han expertise while preserving Jurchen identity and institutions.

The Jin destroyed the Liao by 1125, absorbing much of its personnel and territory, while a Khitan remnant established the Western Liao in Central Asia. The Song, hoping to recover the Sixteen Prefectures, allied with the Jin, but the alliance exposed Song military weaknesses, removed a buffer, and quickly broke down. The result was the Jingkang Crisis: the sieges and fall of Kaifeng and the capture of Huizong and Qinzong, which ended the Northern Song.

Historians explain the fall by combining institutional weakness, strategic miscalculation, factional politics, Jin effectiveness, and geography, and by attending to the limits of Song and later sources. The event also had an environmental dimension in the breaching of the Yellow River dikes.

Southern Song began in 1127 with Gaozong, and survived a difficult decade. It was a new political configuration: smaller, dependent on southern resources, headquartered in Lin'an as a temporary residence, and shaped by problems of legitimacy, military autonomy, and finance. Its peace policy under Gaozong and Qin Gui produced the Treaty of Shaoxing and the execution of Yue Fei, events remembered in very different ways by later generations.

The Jin governed a large agrarian population through institutional pluralism and adaptation, combining Jurchen military settlement and identity with Chinese-style ministries, law, examinations, and taxation. They moved the capital to Zhongdu, issued paper money, sponsored printing, and presided over a religiously and culturally lively society that included the Quanzhen movement and the Jin Buddhist canon. Tensions over land, identity, and the decline of the meng'an-mouke system persisted.

Song-Jin relations combined war and diplomacy. The treaties of 1141–1142, 1164–1165, and 1208 changed the terms of hierarchy and payment while confirming a stable frontier, and warfare, when it occurred, did not end diplomacy. Migration linked the regions: northerners moved south in large numbers, shaping southern cities, elites, and economies, while the north experienced devastation, resettlement, and recovery.

In the early thirteenth century the Jin faced the Mongols, whose military and organizational strength, combined with Jin internal weaknesses, led to the loss of the north, the move to Kaifeng, and, after a joint Mongol-Song campaign, the end of the Jin in 1234. The Song then confronted the Mongols directly.

Taken as a whole, the chapter shows how conquest, adaptation, diplomacy, and migration together produced a new political geography. It also shows that neither state can be reduced to a simple label, whether "foreign conquerors" or "weakened remnant," and prepares students for Chapter 37's study of the maritime and commercial south and Chapter 38's study of the Mongol conquest.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a political order can be reshaped quickly by conquest while institutions, economies, and cultures continue to develop. The Jin and the Southern Song were rivals, but they also shared habits of diplomacy and administration, and they drew on overlapping intellectual and religious traditions. Understanding them together prevents the simple story in which "China" is a single state that suffered a foreign invasion.

The period shaped later Chinese history in lasting ways. It strengthened the south's demographic and economic weight, deepened the practice of institutionalized interstate diplomacy, and demonstrated that non-Han-led states could govern large agrarian populations for long periods through adaptive institutions. It also produced powerful memories, including the ideals of loyalty and resistance associated with Yue Fei, that later generations reinterpreted in new political circumstances.

The chapter also connects China to wider Eurasian history. The Jurchens' rise, the Khitan migration to Central Asia, the Song's turn toward maritime connections, and the Mongol expansion all illustrate that Chinese history cannot be separated from developments on the steppe, in Korea, in Central Asia, and across the seas.

As students move to Chapter 37, they should carry forward the picture of a Southern Song state that was militarily constrained but economically vibrant, and, in Chapter 38, the picture of a Mongol power that would overturn the order described here. Chinese history in this period appears as diverse, interconnected, creative, contested, adaptive, regionally varied, politically changing, and unfinished.
