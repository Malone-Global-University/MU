Part I — China, Geography, and the Deep Past
6

Early States, Erlitou, and the Xia Question

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

In a bend of the Yi-Luo river system in modern Henan province, beneath fields that farmers have plowed for centuries without knowing what lay beneath them, archaeologists in 1959 began to uncover the remains of an enormous settlement — one that, in its final and most developed phase, covered several square kilometers, contained planned avenues wide enough for wheeled vehicles, enclosed a palace-like compound built on a raised rammed-earth platform larger than a modern city block, and housed workshops where specialists cast bronze vessels and worked turquoise into intricate inlaid ornaments. The site came to be known, from the modern village nearby, as Erlitou. No inscription found there names a king. No contemporary document identifies the polity that built it. And yet the scale of its architecture, the concentration of its wealth, and the organization evident in its layout leave little doubt that Erlitou was the product of a society with genuinely centralized political authority — a society, in other words, that many archaeologists now describe as an early state.

Centuries after Erlitou had been built, occupied, and eventually abandoned, Chinese writers living under the Zhou, and later under the Han, composed detailed historical accounts describing a dynasty called the Xia — a royal house said to have ruled before the Shang, founded by a sage-king named Yu who earned the right to rule by taming a catastrophic flood, and eventually overthrown by a corrupt final ruler whose failures justified the transfer of political legitimacy to a new line of kings. These accounts, compiled and elaborated over many centuries, became a fixed and richly detailed part of later Chinese historical memory, appearing in classical texts, court chronicles, philosophical writings, and eventually in the great official histories that later dynasties used to organize their own understanding of the past.

This chapter is about the relationship — and the gap — between these two bodies of evidence: what archaeologists have excavated at Erlitou and related sites, and what later texts say about a dynasty called Xia. It would be convenient, and it is tempting, simply to declare that Erlitou was the capital of the Xia dynasty, closing the gap between archaeology and tradition in a single confident sentence. Many popular accounts of Chinese history do exactly this. But the actual evidence does not permit that sentence to be stated with certainty, and one of the central purposes of this chapter is to explain why, carefully and without either dismissing the possibility or overstating what can be proven.

Chapter 5 traced the emergence of increasingly complex, increasingly hierarchical regional societies across late Neolithic China — Longshan-horizon communities in the Yellow River region, Liangzhu in the Yangtze delta, Hongshan in the northeast — while cautioning students against assuming that these regional cultures developed along a single, inevitable path toward the state. This chapter continues that story into the early second millennium BCE, when archaeological evidence from the Central Plains region of the middle Yellow River shows a further, more pronounced concentration of political, economic, and ritual authority at Erlitou. At the same time, this chapter introduces, for the first time in this textbook, the problem of using later written sources to reconstruct a period that itself left no contemporary writing — a problem that will recur, in different forms, throughout the study of early and even later Chinese history.

Students should understand from the outset that this chapter is not designed to answer the question "did the Xia dynasty exist?" with a simple yes or no. Historians and archaeologists do not currently possess evidence that would allow such a question to be answered definitively, and a textbook that pretended otherwise would be teaching students to accept confident-sounding claims rather than to evaluate evidence. Instead, this chapter is designed to teach a skill that will serve students throughout their study of history: the ability to hold two things in mind simultaneously — a body of textual tradition, with its own history, purposes, and value, and a body of archaeological evidence, with its own capacities and limitations — without collapsing one into the other prematurely.

The stakes of getting this right extend well beyond one archaeological debate. How historians handle the Xia question shapes how they think about a much larger set of problems: how later societies remember, reconstruct, and sometimes invent their own early history; how archaeological cultures relate, or fail to relate, to named political communities; and how national historical traditions can shape, and sometimes distort, scholarly interpretation of ambiguous evidence. Modern Chinese historical and national identity has, at various points, invested considerable meaning in the question of Xia's existence and its connection to Erlitou, a fact this chapter will note without allowing it to substitute for careful evaluation of evidence.

By the end of this chapter, students should understand what the archaeological record at Erlitou actually documents, what the traditional textual accounts of Xia actually claim, why these two bodies of evidence cannot currently be joined with confidence, and how the developments examined here — regardless of whether they are ultimately labeled "Xia" — mark a genuine and historically significant threshold: a distinctive further concentration of political, economic, and social authority within the Central Plains developmental sequence, setting the stage for the far more securely documented Shang civilization examined in Chapter 7.

Historical Context

Chapter 5 established that late Neolithic China was a landscape of multiple, regionally distinct cultures — Yangshao and Longshan in the Yellow River region, Hemudu and Liangzhu in the Yangtze delta, Hongshan in the northeast — each developing increasing social complexity, craft specialization, and, in some cases, pronounced social hierarchy through its own local pathway. That chapter closed by noting that several of these societies, including the remarkably developed Liangzhu culture, ultimately declined or transformed rather than developing in an unbroken line toward the early states examined in this chapter. Students should carry that lesson forward: the emergence of Erlitou in the Central Plains region was not the inevitable culmination of a single line of development running through all of the cultures examined in Chapter 5, but rather one outcome among several possible regional trajectories, occurring specifically in the middle Yellow River region during the transition from the Longshan horizon into the early second millennium BCE.

The geographic setting matters considerably. The Central Plains region — the area along the middle and lower reaches of the Yellow River encompassing parts of modern Henan, southern Shanxi, and neighboring areas — had, by the later third millennium BCE, already developed the walled Longshan-culture towns examined in Chapter 5, along with increasing evidence of interregional conflict, craft specialization, and social stratification. This chapter picks up the story in this same broad region during the final Longshan phases and the subsequent centuries, roughly corresponding to the early second millennium BCE, when archaeological evidence shows a further intensification of settlement scale, architectural monumentality, and craft specialization at a small number of sites, most prominently Erlitou.

At the same time, an entirely separate historical process was unfolding many centuries later and in a different medium altogether: the composition, transmission, and elaboration of written texts describing a period their authors could access only through inherited oral tradition, earlier written fragments no longer extant, ritual memory, and, in some cases, invention or reconstruction shaped by the political and moral concerns of the authors' own much later era. The core narrative traditions concerning the Xia dynasty as most students encounter them today derive substantially from texts compiled during the Zhou and Han periods — many centuries after the Erlitou-period Central Plains societies this chapter examines archaeologically had already risen, transformed, and been succeeded by the Shang and Zhou states covered in Chapters 7 and 8. Understanding this chronological gap between the events supposedly described and the texts describing them is essential background for everything that follows in this chapter.

Students should also recall, from Chapter 1's discussion of historical method, the general distinction between contemporary evidence — produced at or near the time of the events it describes — and later tradition, produced well after the fact and shaped by the interests, assumptions, and literary conventions of its own time. Chapter 1 introduced this distinction in the abstract; this chapter applies it directly and in detail to one of the most consequential and contested cases in all of Chinese historiography. The stakes of the distinction are high: later Chinese historical writing treated the Xia dynasty as the first of three ancient dynasties (Xia, Shang, and Zhou) whose succession illustrated foundational political and moral principles, including the concept of political legitimacy and its loss — ideas examined further in Chapter 8's discussion of the Mandate of Heaven. Whether or not Xia existed as a specific, identifiable political community, the idea of Xia became deeply important to later Chinese political thought, and this chapter asks students to take that cultural and intellectual significance seriously even while remaining appropriately cautious about historical fact.

Finally, students should understand that the archaeological study of Erlitou did not begin with the goal of testing later texts against material evidence in a neutral, disinterested way. Chinese archaeology in the twentieth century developed partly in direct response to the same textual traditions this chapter examines, and early excavators at Erlitou and related sites were often explicitly searching for physical confirmation of the Xia dynasty described in ancient texts. This search has continued, with considerable scholarly sophistication, into the present day, and this chapter presents both the archaeological findings themselves and the ongoing scholarly debate about how to interpret them, while urging students to distinguish between what the evidence shows and what any particular researcher, past or present, may have hoped it would show.

Section 6.1

The Traditional Xia Narrative

According to later Chinese historical tradition, the Xia dynasty was the first of the "Three Dynasties" (Xia, Shang, and Zhou) that together formed the foundation of ancient Chinese political history. In this account, the Xia was founded by Yu the Great, who succeeded the legendary sage-kings Yao and Shun not through hereditary succession but through recognition of his personal virtue and, above all, his success in controlling a catastrophic flood that had devastated the land, examined further in Section 6.2. Yu's son, Qi, is said to have broken with the earlier pattern of rule by recognized virtue and established hereditary succession within his own family, founding the Xia as a proper dynasty in the sense of a ruling house that passed power from father to son across multiple generations.

The traditional narrative lists a sequence of Xia rulers, describes periods of strength and weakness within the dynasty, and culminates in the reign of a final ruler, traditionally named Jie, portrayed in later texts as a tyrant whose cruelty, extravagance, and neglect of proper government caused the Xia to lose its right to rule. According to this narrative, a rising power under a ruler named Tang overthrew Jie and established the Shang dynasty, examined in detail in Chapter 7. This succession story — virtuous founder, generations of rule, moral decline, and eventual overthrow by a new, more virtuous power — became a template that later Chinese historical writing applied, with variations, to the transitions between many subsequent dynasties, closely connected to the concept of the Mandate of Heaven examined in Chapter 8.

Students should notice several features of this narrative that matter for historical analysis. First, it is highly patterned: the moral logic of virtuous founder and corrupt final ruler recurs so consistently across different dynastic transitions in later Chinese historiography that historians must ask whether this pattern reflects genuine historical memory or a literary and political convention imposed retrospectively onto earlier periods to explain and justify later dynastic change. Second, the narrative assigns specific personal names, reign lengths, and genealogical relationships to Xia rulers with a level of textual detail and confidence that stands in some tension with the near-total absence of contemporary written evidence from the period in question, a tension examined directly in Section 6.3. Third, the narrative was not fixed at a single moment but developed and was elaborated over centuries of transmission, with later texts often adding details, moral commentary, and narrative color absent from earlier surviving fragments.

None of this means that the traditional Xia narrative should be dismissed as pure invention. Later Chinese historians who compiled these accounts, including Sima Qian, worked from earlier written and oral traditions that had circulated for generations before being fixed in the texts that survive today, and it remains entirely possible that some genuine historical memory of an early political community, or of specific rulers, events, or places, survived within this transmitted tradition even as other elements were reshaped, moralized, or invented along the way. The historian's task, examined throughout this chapter, is to identify what, if anything, in this transmitted tradition can be corroborated by independent evidence, rather than either accepting the narrative uncritically or rejecting it outright.

Section 6.2

Yu the Great and Flood Traditions

At the center of the traditional Xia narrative stands Yu the Great, remembered above all for his role in taming a great flood that, according to later texts, had submerged much of the land and threatened the survival of settled communities. Where Yu's father, Gun, is said to have failed in his own flood-control efforts by attempting to block the floodwaters with dikes and embankments, Yu is credited with a different and ultimately successful method: dredging channels to guide the floodwaters to the sea, working tirelessly for years, and, according to the most famous version of the story, passing by his own house three times without entering because of his devotion to the task. This story of disciplined, selfless labor in the service of the community became one of the most celebrated moral exemplars in later Chinese political and ethical thought, invoked for millennia afterward as a model of virtuous, hardworking, self-sacrificing leadership.

Historians and geologists have long been intrigued by the possibility that this flood tradition preserves a genuine memory of major flooding events in the ancient Yellow River basin, an area long subject to catastrophic flooding due to the river's heavy sediment load and tendency to change course. Recent geological research has identified evidence for a massive flood event in the upper Yellow River region, associated with an outburst from a landslide-dammed lake, dated by some researchers to a period roughly consistent with the traditional dating of Yu and the founding of Xia. Some scholars have proposed that this specific flood event, or a comparable one, may lie behind the flood traditions associated with Yu.

Students must exercise particular caution here. Even if geological evidence confirms that a major flood occurred at approximately the right time and place, this would demonstrate only that ancient flooding of the kind described in later tradition was geologically possible and perhaps did occur — it would not, by itself, demonstrate that a historical individual named Yu organized flood-control efforts, that a dynasty called Xia was founded as a direct result, or that any specific king list transmitted in later texts accurately preserves the names and sequence of early rulers. The chain of reasoning from "a major flood occurred" to "Yu therefore existed and founded Xia" involves several separate, unproven steps, each of which would require its own independent evidence. This chapter treats the correspondence between geological flood evidence and the Yu tradition as an intriguing and legitimate subject of ongoing research, not as settled proof of any specific historical narrative.

The deeper historical significance of the Yu tradition, in any case, extends beyond the question of whether a literal flood or a literal individual named Yu can be verified. The story encodes an enduring set of ideas about political legitimacy in Chinese political thought: that rulership is earned through service to the community rather than simply inherited or seized by force; that mastery over water and land — an enduring practical concern in a civilization built substantially around river-based agriculture, as introduced in Chapter 2 — is a fundamental test and demonstration of a ruler's fitness to govern; and that extraordinary personal sacrifice for the collective good is the mark of ideal leadership. These ideas shaped Chinese political culture for millennia, regardless of the specific historical facts underlying the story of Yu himself, and students should understand the Yu tradition both as a possible, if unverifiable, echo of real historical events and as a foundational myth whose content mattered enormously to how later Chinese thinkers understood the nature and purpose of political authority.

Section 6.3

The Problem of Later Written Sources

This section addresses directly one of the central methodological problems of this chapter, and one of the most important lessons in the entire textbook: the difference between a contemporary written source, produced at or near the time of the events it describes, and a later written tradition, produced many generations or even centuries after the events it purports to describe. This distinction is not a minor technicality. It fundamentally shapes how much confidence historians can place in specific claims, and it explains why this textbook treats the traditional Xia narrative with considerably more caution than it treats, for example, the Shang royal lineage examined in Chapter 7, which is independently corroborated by contemporary oracle-bone inscriptions.

No text describing the Xia dynasty survives from the period the dynasty is traditionally said to have existed, roughly the early second millennium BCE. The earliest surviving references to a Xia dynasty and to Yu appear in texts associated with the Western Zhou and, more substantially, the Eastern Zhou period — meaning that even the earliest surviving mentions of Xia postdate the traditional Xia period by many centuries. The most complete and systematic account of the Xia royal succession, including a full list of rulers and reign details, appears in Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian, compiled during the Han dynasty, more than a thousand years after the Xia is traditionally said to have ended. Between the traditional Xia period and Sima Qian's own time lies an immense span during which the Xia narrative could have been transmitted orally, transmitted through written texts that no longer survive, elaborated, moralized, reshaped to fit the political needs of later periods, or, in whole or in part, constructed retrospectively by later writers seeking to explain the deep past in terms that made sense to their own political and philosophical concerns.

This does not mean that texts written centuries after the events they describe are worthless as historical evidence. Later texts can and sometimes do preserve genuine historical memory, transmitted carefully across generations through oral tradition, ritual practice, or now-lost written sources that once existed closer in time to the events themselves. The important historiographical point is not that later texts are automatically false, but that they require a different, more cautious standard of evaluation than contemporary sources, and that historians should actively look for independent corroboration — archaeological, epigraphic, or otherwise — before treating specific details in a later text as established historical fact.

Historical Vocabulary: Protohistory and the Textual Threshold

Historians sometimes use the term protohistory to describe a period that lies at the edge of written historical documentation — a period for which no contemporary written sources survive from within the society itself, but for which later societies, possessing writing, produced texts describing or claiming to describe that earlier period. Protohistory is distinct from prehistory, which refers to periods with no writing anywhere connected to them at all, and from history proper, in the strict sense used by some historians, which refers to periods documented by contemporary written sources produced by people who directly witnessed or closely followed the events described. The Erlitou period examined in this chapter sits precisely at this protohistorical threshold: the society that built Erlitou almost certainly had no fully developed writing system of the kind later used by the Shang, and yet later societies, writing many centuries afterward, produced detailed accounts of a dynasty many scholars associate, tentatively and with real uncertainty, with this same general period and region. Understanding the concept of protohistory helps students see why this chapter must proceed with unusual methodological care, drawing a firm line between what archaeology can establish about Erlitou directly and what later tradition claims about a dynasty called Xia.

A comparison with the Shang dynasty, examined fully in Chapter 7, illustrates the stakes of this distinction with unusual clarity. Before the discovery and decipherment of oracle-bone inscriptions beginning in 1899, some historians doubted whether the Shang dynasty, known previously only from later texts much like those describing Xia, had existed at all as a specific historical dynasty rather than as legendary tradition. The oracle bones, however, are contemporary written documents, produced by the Shang state itself during the period of Shang rule, and they independently confirmed the existence of a royal lineage whose names correspond closely to the list of Shang kings recorded in later texts such as Sima Qian's history. This discovery transformed Shang from a subject of doubt into one of the most securely documented ancient dynasties in world history, and it demonstrated that later Chinese historical texts, whatever their limitations, could in at least one major case preserve substantially accurate historical memory across many centuries. This success naturally raised hopes that a comparable discovery — a contemporary written source produced by the Xia state itself, however that state might eventually be defined — might someday confirm the Xia dynasty in the same way. No such discovery has yet been made, and this chapter's central methodological caution follows directly from that continuing absence.

Section 6.4

Erlitou Archaeology

Having examined the textual tradition on its own terms in Sections 6.1 through 6.3, this chapter now turns decisively to a different body of evidence: the physical remains excavated at Erlitou and related sites in the Central Plains region since systematic archaeological work began there in 1959. Students should approach this section, deliberately, as an independent line of evidence, evaluated first on its own terms, before Section 6.8 asks how, if at all, this archaeological evidence relates to the textual tradition examined earlier in the chapter.

Erlitou is located near the confluence of the Yi and Luo rivers in modern Henan province, within the broader Central Plains region introduced in Section 6.0's historical context and long associated, in later tradition, with the heartland of early Chinese civilization. Erlitou flourished during the early second millennium BCE; its absolute chronology remains debated among specialists, with commonly cited ranges extending approximately from the nineteenth or eighteenth century BCE to the sixteenth or fifteenth century BCE, depending on the dating methods and calibration used. In its earliest phases, Erlitou appears to have been a large but not yet architecturally monumental settlement; by its later phases, it had grown into what many archaeologists describe as one of the largest and most complex known political centers in East Asia during its period, covering an estimated area of roughly three square kilometers at its height. Students should note that other large, complex settlements existed elsewhere in China both before and during Erlitou's rise, including the massive walled site of Shimao in the north, discussed further in the Regional and Frontier Context feature later in this chapter; Erlitou's importance lies in its specific role within the Central Plains sequence traced from Chapter 5, not in any claim that it was without rival anywhere in East Asia.

The site's layout in its mature phases shows clear evidence of deliberate planning. Archaeologists have identified a network of wide, intersecting roads, apparently laid out according to a coherent plan and wide enough to have accommodated wheeled vehicles, dividing the settlement into distinct zones. At the center of this planned layout stood a large enclosed compound, bounded by rammed-earth walls, containing multiple large rammed-earth platform foundations that once supported substantial timber-frame buildings — structures generally interpreted by archaeologists as palace-like halls used for elite residence, ritual activity, or governmental functions, examined further in Section 6.5. Surrounding this central compound, archaeologists have identified distinct areas dedicated to specialized craft production, including a workshop area associated with bronze casting and another associated with the working of turquoise into finished ornaments, along with residential areas and burial grounds showing considerable variation in the wealth and elaboration of grave goods.

Erlitou's material culture includes finely made ceramic vessels, jade objects continuing traditions of craft skill traceable in modified form back to the late Neolithic cultures examined in Chapter 5, and — most significantly for understanding the site's historical importance — some of the earliest known bronze ritual vessels produced in China using sophisticated piece-mold casting technology, examined in detail in Section 6.6. Excavators have also recovered a small number of turquoise-inlaid bronze plaques of exceptional craftsmanship, along with turquoise beads and ornaments, indicating specialized access to and working of a material that, like jade in the Neolithic cultures of Chapter 5, appears to have carried significant status value.

A note on terminology: This chapter refers to the site and its associated material culture as "Erlitou" or "the Erlitou culture" throughout Section 6.4 and Section 6.5, deliberately avoiding the term "Xia capital" or "Xia dynasty" until Section 6.8 examines directly, and critically, the question of whether such an identification is warranted. Students should adopt this same discipline in their own writing: describe what the archaeology shows using archaeological terminology, and treat any connection to the textual Xia tradition as a separate interpretive claim requiring its own justification.

Beyond the site of Erlitou itself, archaeologists have identified a network of related settlements across the surrounding region sharing similar material culture, generally referred to collectively as the Erlitou culture, spanning a substantial area of the Central Plains and, in its later phases, showing evidence of contact and influence extending into neighboring regions well beyond the immediate vicinity of the type-site. This regional network, examined further in Section 6.7, provides important evidence for assessing the political reach and organizational capacity of whatever authority controlled or was centered at Erlitou.

Section 6.5

Palaces, Workshops, and Elite Authority

This section asks a more interpretive question than Section 6.4: what do the archaeological patterns documented at Erlitou actually tell historians about the nature of political authority and social organization in this society? The answer requires moving carefully from physical evidence to historical inference, a process this textbook has practiced since Chapter 5's discussion of social hierarchy in late Neolithic burials, and one that becomes especially important here because, unlike at Liangzhu, Erlitou's evidence must support arguments not merely about social stratification in general but about the specific question of whether a genuine early state, as opposed to a large chiefdom, had emerged.

The scale and organization of the large rammed-earth enclosure at the center of Erlitou provides some of the strongest evidence for concentrated authority. Constructing and maintaining walls and platforms of this scale required the sustained, coordinated labor of a substantial workforce over an extended period — labor that, as with the Liangzhu hydraulic system examined in Chapter 5, could not plausibly have been organized through purely voluntary, informal, or small-scale household cooperation. Some form of authority capable of directing, feeding, and coordinating this labor over time must have existed, whether that authority rested in a single paramount ruler, a small ruling elite, or some more complex combination of political, religious, and lineage-based leadership roles that the archaeological evidence alone cannot fully specify.

The internal organization of the central compound reinforces this picture. Multiple substantial buildings, arranged within a walled precinct and evidently rebuilt and modified across different phases of the site's occupation, suggest a location used consistently, across multiple generations, for functions requiring formal, bounded, elite space — whether residence, administration, ritual, or, most likely, some combination of all three functions that a modern observer might be tempted to separate but that Bronze Age elites likely did not experience as entirely distinct categories. The clear spatial separation between this elite compound and the surrounding residential and workshop areas of the settlement further suggests a formalized distinction between elite and non-elite space that goes beyond the more modest spatial organization documented at earlier Yangshao village sites such as Banpo, examined in Chapter 5.

Burial evidence at Erlitou, while less dramatically documented than the spectacular elite tombs of Liangzhu, similarly shows meaningful variation: a small number of burials contain bronze vessels, jade objects, turquoise ornaments, and lacquerware, while the majority of burials at the site contain few or no comparable goods. This pattern is consistent with, though it does not by itself prove, a society organized around a hereditary or at least strongly institutionalized elite whose status was marked, and perhaps reinforced, through control of and access to prestige materials and specialized craft production, extending into the afterlife through burial practice much as it apparently did in life.

Specialized craft production, examined further in Section 6.6 with specific reference to bronze, provides a further important line of evidence. The workshops identified at Erlitou for bronze casting and turquoise working required skilled, presumably at least partially full-time specialists, supported by some mechanism — elite patronage, communal support, or exchange — that freed them from full-time agricultural labor, extending the pattern of craft specialization already documented for Longshan and Liangzhu societies in Chapter 5 to a new level of technical sophistication and, quite plausibly, closer integration with formal political authority, given that bronze production in particular required not only skilled labor but organized access to metal ores that, in many cases, had to be transported from considerable distances.

Taken together, this evidence — monumental, planned architecture; formalized elite space; unequal burial; and specialized craft production closely associated with political authority — leads most archaeologists to conclude that Erlitou represents a society with genuinely centralized, institutionalized political authority extending beyond a single ruler's lifetime or a single generation, a threshold many scholars consider sufficient to describe Erlitou as an early state rather than merely an unusually large or wealthy chiefdom. As Chapter 5's discussion of the chiefdom-state distinction emphasized, however, this classification depends significantly on the specific definition of "state" a given scholar adopts, and some researchers continue to describe Erlitou using more cautious terminology, such as "complex polity" or "proto-state," reserving the term "state" for societies with more securely documented administrative institutions of the kind that become clearly visible only with the securely dated, contemporary written records of the Shang period examined in Chapter 7.

Section 6.6

Bronze Production and Social Hierarchy

Among all the evidence recovered at Erlitou, the site's early bronze industry has attracted particular scholarly attention, both because of its technological sophistication and because of what it reveals about the relationship between craft production, resource control, and political power in the formative stages of Chinese Bronze Age civilization. This section examines bronze not primarily as an artistic achievement, though the objects themselves are frequently of considerable aesthetic refinement, but as a technology with profound political and economic consequences.

Bronze — an alloy typically combining copper with tin and, in varying proportions, lead — requires a chain of specialized knowledge and organized labor considerably more demanding than the ceramic and jade production examined in Chapter 5's discussion of Neolithic craft specialization. Producing bronze objects requires locating and mining copper and tin ores, often from sources located considerable distances from the point of manufacture; smelting these ores to extract usable metal; alloying the metals in controlled proportions; and, in the case of the sophisticated ritual vessels associated with Erlitou and later Chinese Bronze Age civilization, constructing multi-part ceramic molds through the piece-mold casting technique, in which a clay model of the desired vessel is used to create separate mold sections that are then reassembled around a core, allowing molten bronze to be poured into the resulting cavity to produce a finished, and often intricately decorated, vessel.

Historical Vocabulary: Erlitou versus Xia

Erlitou refers to a specific archaeological site and, by extension, to the broader archaeological culture identified through material remains — pottery styles, architectural forms, bronze and turquoise objects, and settlement patterns — shared across a network of related sites in the Central Plains region during a particular, archaeologically dated span of the early second millennium BCE, debated in its precise absolute limits. Xia refers to a dynasty described in later written texts, composed centuries after this period, with a specific king list, founding narrative, and moral framework attached to it. These are two fundamentally different kinds of historical category: one defined by physical, datable, excavated evidence, and the other defined by a transmitted literary and historiographical tradition. It is entirely possible that Erlitou and Xia refer, at least in part, to the same underlying historical reality — but this must be demonstrated through evidence, not assumed through convenient terminology. This chapter deliberately avoids using "Erlitou" and "Xia" as interchangeable terms, and students should adopt the same care in their own writing and analysis.

This technological chain has significant political implications. Unlike jade, which, as Chapter 5 discussed, moved through exchange networks connecting distant Neolithic communities without necessarily requiring centralized political control, the specific combination of resources, technical knowledge, and organized labor required for bronze production at the scale documented at Erlitou is consistent with, and may have reinforced, an increasingly centralized coordination of raw-material acquisition, skilled labor, and finished-object distribution. Some researchers interpret this prestige-goods production as effectively state-managed; others caution that the precise organizational mechanism — direct administrative control, concentrated elite patronage, or some looser combination of the two — remains difficult to establish from the archaeological evidence alone. One plausible, though not directly documented, function of such control may have been to allow a central authority at Erlitou to build and maintain political relationships with elites at other settlements within its network, examined further in Section 6.7, by granting or withholding access to finished bronze objects — a mechanism that becomes far more clearly and directly documented for the Shang period examined in Chapter 7, where bronze vessels served explicitly as markers of rank, political favor, and ritual authority within a documented state hierarchy.

The ritual dimension of early Chinese bronze production deserves particular emphasis. The earliest Erlitou bronze vessels, and the far more elaborate bronze ritual vessels of the subsequent Shang and Zhou periods examined in Chapters 7 and 8, appear to have been used primarily not for everyday practical purposes but for elite ritual and ceremonial practices, possibly including rites directed toward ancestors — a religious and social practice with much deeper roots, as Chapter 5 noted, in Neolithic mortuary and ritual traditions. Students should note that this ritual interpretation, while widely shared among specialists, rests on the form and burial context of the vessels themselves rather than on any inscribed or textual statement of their purpose from the Erlitou period; the specific theology, the identity of any ritual recipients, and the precise content of the ceremonies involved cannot be reconstructed with the same confidence that becomes possible for the securely documented ancestor cult of the Shang royal house, examined in Chapter 7 on the basis of contemporary oracle-bone inscriptions. The fact that some of the most technologically sophisticated and resource-intensive production in Erlitou society was directed toward ritual rather than purely utilitarian ends underscores a theme already introduced in Chapter 5's discussion of Liangzhu jade: in many early complex societies, ritual authority and political authority were closely intertwined, and the capacity to sponsor, control, and display elaborate ritual objects was itself a central component, rather than a mere decoration, of political power.

Students should also recognize the limits of what bronze evidence alone can establish. The presence of sophisticated bronze production at Erlitou demonstrates considerable political, economic, and organizational capacity; it does not, by itself, identify the name, title, or ethnic or dynastic affiliation of the authority that controlled this production. No inscribed bronze vessel from the Erlitou period has been found bearing a ruler's name or a dynastic title, in sharp contrast to the securely dated and extensively inscribed bronzes of the subsequent Western Zhou period examined in Chapter 8. This absence of textual confirmation on the objects themselves is a central reason why the identification of Erlitou with Xia, examined directly in Section 6.8, remains a matter of interpretation rather than settled fact.

Section 6.7

Regional Networks and Political Consolidation

Chapter 5 introduced the concept of "peer polity interaction" to describe how late Neolithic regional cultures developed in dialogue, exchange, and competition with one another rather than in isolation. This section applies a related but distinct framework to Erlitou: rather than examining relationships among roughly equal, independent regional cultures, this section examines evidence for an emerging settlement hierarchy and possible political dominance extending outward from a single, unusually large and complex center.

Archaeological survey across the Central Plains region has identified numerous smaller settlements sharing Erlitou-style pottery, architectural techniques, and other material culture markers, distributed across a substantial geographic area surrounding the primary site. The distribution of these related settlements, and the apparent decline in relative importance of some Longshan-era regional centers examined in Chapter 5 during the period of Erlitou's rise, has led many archaeologists to argue that Erlitou functioned as the dominant political and economic center of a genuine regional settlement hierarchy — a network of subordinate or allied communities linked to Erlitou through political authority, economic exchange, ritual connection, or some combination of these relationships.

The degree and nature of Erlitou's control over this wider network remains, however, a matter of ongoing research and some disagreement. Some archaeologists emphasize evidence suggesting relatively direct political control, including apparent Erlitou-style administrative or ritual outposts established at some distance from the primary site, interpreted by some researchers as evidence of deliberate territorial expansion or colonization directed from Erlitou itself. Other researchers favor a more cautious interpretation, emphasizing that shared material culture across a region can result from trade, cultural emulation, and social prestige-seeking by local elites eager to adopt the styles and symbols of a powerful and impressive neighbor, without necessarily implying direct political subordination or centralized administrative control in the way a modern reader might imagine from later, more thoroughly documented Chinese empires.

Evidence for long-distance material acquisition further supports the picture of a wide-reaching network of contacts, whatever its precise political character. The copper and, in some interpretations, tin used in Erlitou bronze production likely derived from sources located well outside the immediate Central Plains region, in some cases potentially extending toward areas of the middle Yangtze region or further afield, though the precise sourcing of specific metal artifacts remains an active area of scientific research using techniques such as lead-isotope analysis. Similarly, some scholars have proposed connections, whether through direct contact, intermediary exchange, or independent technological development, between Erlitou-period bronze technology and bronze-working traditions further west across Eurasia, though this remains a genuinely open and actively debated question rather than an established conclusion, and students should treat claims of specific long-distance technological transmission with the same evidentiary caution urged throughout this chapter.

Whatever the precise mechanisms of Erlitou's regional influence, this section's central lesson connects directly back to Chapter 5's caution against assuming any single, simple developmental trajectory. Erlitou did not simply replace all other regional societies with a single, homogeneous political and cultural system. Other regional traditions, including societies in the Yangtze region, the southeast, and elsewhere, continued to develop according to their own distinctive local patterns during this same period, some in contact with the Central Plains network and others largely independent of it, a diversity that this textbook will continue to trace throughout the early historical chapters that follow.

Section 6.8

Archaeology and the Xia Debate

This section brings together the two evidence streams examined separately throughout this chapter — the traditional textual narrative of Xia examined in Sections 6.1 through 6.3, and the archaeological evidence from Erlitou examined in Sections 6.4 through 6.7 — to address directly the central historiographical question of this chapter: can Erlitou be identified with the Xia dynasty described in later texts?

The case for identification rests on several converging lines of reasoning. First, chronological correspondence: the radiocarbon-dated span of Erlitou's occupation falls within a range broadly consistent with traditional and modern chronological reconstructions of when the Xia dynasty is supposed to have existed, particularly when scholars use later textual chronologies, combined with astronomical calculations and other methods, to estimate Xia dates. Second, geographic correspondence: later texts consistently place the Xia dynasty's core territory within the same general Central Plains region where Erlitou is located, and some later texts even associate specific place names with locations in reasonably close proximity to the Erlitou site itself. Third, developmental correspondence: the scale, organization, and apparent political centralization documented archaeologically at Erlitou, examined in Sections 6.4 and 6.5, corresponds to the kind of substantial, multi-generational political authority that the traditional Xia narrative describes, marking a further concentration of authority beyond the walled Longshan-era Yellow River settlements examined in Chapter 5. Chinese archaeologists associated with the long-running Xia-Shang-Zhou Chronology Project and related research initiatives have generally favored some version of this identification, treating Erlitou, particularly in its later phases, as either the Xia dynasty's capital or capitals, or as a strong archaeological candidate for that identification.

Historical Debate

Was Erlitou the Xia Dynasty?

The question: Can the archaeological site and culture known as Erlitou be securely identified with the dynasty called Xia in later Chinese historical texts?

Interpretation A — Erlitou should be identified, at least provisionally, with the Xia dynasty: Scholars favoring this view point to the convergence of chronology, geography, and developmental scale described above. They argue that later textual traditions, while composed centuries after the events they describe, plausibly preserve genuine historical memory transmitted across generations, much as the Shang king list transmitted in later texts was substantially confirmed by the independent discovery of oracle-bone inscriptions. They note that dismissing any connection between Erlitou and Xia risks treating an entire, long-transmitted textual tradition as pure invention without sufficient justification, and that a large, politically centralized society clearly existed in the right place at roughly the right time — a society that must have had some name, even if that name cannot currently be confirmed.
Interpretation B — The identification of Erlitou with Xia cannot currently be confirmed and should be treated as unproven: Scholars favoring this view emphasize that no contemporary written evidence from the Erlitou period itself names the polity, its rulers, or a dynasty called Xia. They argue that archaeological cultures, defined by shared material remains, cannot automatically be equated with named political or dynastic entities known only from texts composed many centuries later, since material culture and political identity do not necessarily correspond in any simple, one-to-one way — a lesson already introduced in Chapter 5's discussion of the difficulty of assigning ethnic or political labels to Neolithic archaeological cultures. They further note that the Shang case, often cited as encouraging precedent, actually illustrates the specific evidentiary standard required for confident identification — a contemporary inscribed source naming the ruling lineage — and that no comparable source has yet been found for Erlitou. Without such a source, they argue, "Xia" remains a hypothesis applied to Erlitou by scholars, not a conclusion demonstrated by the site itself.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of archaeological and textual evidence described in this chapter; they differ chiefly in how much evidentiary weight to place on later textual tradition in the absence of independent contemporary confirmation, and in how strictly to apply the evidentiary standard set by the Shang oracle-bone precedent.

Why it matters: This debate is not merely academic. It shapes how confidently historians can describe the deepest roots of Chinese political history, how museums and heritage institutions in China and elsewhere present Erlitou to the public, and how national historical narratives incorporate — or overstate — the certainty of archaeological findings. It also serves as an important case study for how scholars should handle the broader, recurring historical problem of connecting archaeological cultures to named political communities known only from later texts, a problem that recurs, in different forms, in many other historical traditions around the world.

Student task: Based on the evidence presented in Sections 6.1 through 6.7, identify what specific new discovery — textual, archaeological, or scientific — would most strengthen the case for identifying Erlitou with Xia, and explain what discovery would most strengthen the case against such an identification. Is the current evidence sufficient to declare the question settled in either direction? Explain your reasoning.

This textbook adopts neither position as settled fact. Instead, it presents the following calibrated conclusion, which students should adopt as a model for handling comparable evidentiary problems elsewhere in their study of history: the archaeological evidence securely demonstrates that a large, politically centralized, socially stratified society existed at Erlitou in the Central Plains region during the period traditionally associated with Xia; later texts describe a dynasty called Xia occupying roughly the same time and place; but no currently available evidence allows historians to confirm, with the same confidence achieved for the Shang dynasty, that these two things refer to the same specific historical entity. Students encountering confident claims in either direction — whether flatly declaring "Erlitou was the Xia capital" or flatly declaring "the Xia dynasty is a myth" — should recognize both claims as overstating what the evidence currently supports.

Historical Evidence

Reading the Bronze Age Without Writing

Historians studying Erlitou must rely almost entirely on archaeological evidence, since no securely attested writing system has been confirmed for the site itself. This evidence includes excavated architectural remains (rammed-earth platforms, walls, and road networks), burial data (the quantity, quality, and distribution of grave goods), craft-production evidence (workshop remains, tools, unfinished objects, and finished bronze, jade, and turquoise artifacts), radiocarbon dating of organic material to establish chronology, settlement-pattern survey across the wider region to assess regional hierarchy, and increasingly, scientific techniques such as lead-isotope analysis to trace the geographic origin of metal ores. Each of these evidence types has real value and real limitations: architectural remains reveal scale and planning but not the specific titles or names of those who commissioned them; burial data reveals relative status but not the specific criteria — kinship, achievement, ritual role — by which that status was assigned; and craft-production evidence reveals organizational capacity but not the political structure that organized it. Historians must combine multiple, independent lines of evidence and remain explicit about what each type of evidence can and cannot support, resisting the temptation to fill evidentiary gaps with confident narrative detail borrowed uncritically from later textual tradition.

Section 6.9

From Early States to the Shang World

Regardless of how the Xia identification question examined in Section 6.8 is ultimately resolved, the archaeological record shows a clear and significant transition beginning around the sixteenth century BCE: Erlitou's importance as the dominant regional center of the Central Plains declined, and a new archaeological culture, known as Erligang, rose to prominence, marked by rapid territorial expansion, the construction of a massive walled city at the site of modern Zhengzhou, and continued, increasingly sophisticated bronze production building directly on Erlitou-period technological foundations. The Erligang culture is widely associated by scholars with early Shang civilization, examined in full in Chapter 7, though students should note that this association rests on archaeological and geographic reasoning broadly similar in kind, if generally considered stronger, to the reasoning applied to Erlitou and Xia in Section 6.8. Contemporary written confirmation of the Shang royal lineage arrives only later, with the securely documented Anyang phase of Shang civilization, where the oracle-bone inscriptions discussed in Sections 6.3 and Chapter 7 were eventually discovered. Students should therefore avoid treating "Erligang equals early Shang" as though it carried the same evidentiary certainty as the Anyang-phase Shang identification, even though most specialists consider the Erligang-Shang association considerably better supported than the Erlitou-Xia association.

Students should resist describing this transition as simply "Erlitou became Shang," as though a single continuous political entity gradually changed its name. The relationship between the end of Erlitou's dominance and the rise of Erligang involves real archaeological questions about continuity and change — some material culture traditions, including piece-mold bronze casting technology and certain ceramic and architectural forms, continued and were further developed from the Erlitou period into the Erligang period, suggesting genuine cultural and possibly political continuity, while other changes, including the sudden appearance of a much larger fortified city at Zhengzhou and evidence of rapid territorial expansion well beyond the Erlitou culture's apparent reach, suggest a significant transformation in political organization, scale, and possibly ruling authority, whether through internal political change, conquest, or some combination of the two that current evidence cannot fully resolve.

What can be said with confidence is that the trajectory of increasing political centralization, craft specialization, and social hierarchy traced from the late Neolithic cultures of Chapter 5 through the Erlitou-period developments of this chapter culminates, by the time of the Erligang and subsequent Anyang-phase Shang civilization examined in Chapter 7, in a society whose scale, organizational sophistication, and — crucially — surviving contemporary written evidence place it on unmistakably firmer historical ground than anything examined in this chapter. The oracle-bone inscriptions of the Shang period name specific kings, record specific historical and ritual events, and can be checked against later textual king lists with remarkable, independently verified accuracy. This represents a genuine transformation in what historians can know, not merely a continuation of the same kind of evidence at greater volume.

This chapter's placement between Chapter 5's Neolithic regional cultures and Chapter 7's securely documented Shang civilization is therefore not accidental. Chapter 6 occupies the historical and evidentiary threshold between prehistory and protohistory on one side and documented history on the other — a threshold at which archaeological evidence, growing steadily richer and more suggestive of centralized political authority, still falls short of the kind of confirmation that only contemporary written sources can provide. Students moving into Chapter 7 should carry forward a clear sense of what changes at that threshold: not simply "more evidence," but a different, more secure category of evidence altogether.

Section 6.10

Chapter Review and Early State Formation Checkpoint

This chapter has examined the emergence of early state-level political organization in the Central Plains region of the middle Yellow River during the late third and early second millennia BCE, focusing on the archaeological site and culture of Erlitou, while separately and carefully examining the later textual tradition describing a Xia dynasty said to have ruled during roughly the same period.

The core insights of this chapter are as follows. The traditional Xia narrative, transmitted and elaborated across many centuries and most fully recorded in Han-period texts such as Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian, describes a founding sage-king, Yu the Great, celebrated for his role in taming a catastrophic flood, followed by a dynastic succession of rulers ending in a tyrannical final king overthrown by the founder of the Shang. This narrative should be understood as a later written tradition, not a contemporary source, and its historical claims require independent corroboration before they can be treated as established fact. Archaeological evidence from Erlitou, by contrast, provides direct, physical, radiocarbon-dated evidence for a large, planned, socially stratified settlement with monumental rammed-earth architecture, specialized craft production including sophisticated early bronze piece-mold casting, and apparent regional political influence extending across a network of related settlements — evidence that most archaeologists interpret as demonstrating a genuine early state, though some prefer more cautious classifications. The identification of Erlitou with the Xia dynasty described in later texts remains a matter of ongoing scholarly debate rather than settled historical fact, resting on suggestive chronological and geographic correspondence but lacking the kind of contemporary written confirmation that secured the historicity of the Shang dynasty through the discovery of oracle-bone inscriptions.

The chapter has also modeled an essential historical thinking skill: distinguishing between what archaeology demonstrates directly, what later texts claim, and what remains a matter of scholarly interpretation connecting the two. This skill matters well beyond the specific case of Erlitou and Xia, and students should expect to apply it again when this textbook examines other cases in which later tradition and material evidence do not align neatly.

Multiple Perspectives

Living Near an Early Political Center

From the perspective of a member of the Erlitou elite: Residence within the walled central compound, access to bronze ritual vessels and turquoise ornaments, and participation in whatever ceremonies took place within the palace-style halls likely marked and reinforced a family's status, distinguishing it sharply from the great majority of the surrounding population, much as elite burial goods at Liangzhu had done for an earlier generation of archaeologists to study.

From the perspective of a farming household in a smaller settlement within the wider Erlitou-culture network: Adopting Erlitou-style pottery forms or occasionally acquiring a prestige object associated with the larger center may have offered social or economic advantages, whether through genuine political subordination, voluntary trade relationships, or simple cultural emulation of a nearby society whose wealth and organization were difficult to ignore.
From the perspective of a modern archaeologist evaluating the Xia question: Distinguishing between what the Erlitou evidence demonstrates directly and what later national and cultural narratives hope it will demonstrate requires constant methodological discipline, since the desire to confirm a cherished historical tradition can subtly shape how ambiguous evidence gets interpreted, even among careful and well-trained researchers.
Continuity and Change Over Time

From Ritual Authority to Political Authority: Chapter 5 to Chapter 6

What Changed

  • Settlement scale and architectural monumentality substantially increased beyond even the largest Longshan-era walled towns examined in Chapter 5, developing a distinctive form of planned, palace-centered organization not directly paralleled at Liangzhu
  • Craft specialization extended to bronze metallurgy, a technology requiring more extensive resource control and organized labor than Neolithic pottery or jade working
  • Evidence for a formalized settlement hierarchy, with Erlitou apparently exercising influence over a network of smaller related communities, became more pronounced
  • The scale of elite architectural and ritual investment, concentrated within a walled central compound, suggests a further institutionalization of political authority beyond what most Chapter 5 societies documented

What Stayed the Same

  • Agriculture remained the fundamental economic foundation of Erlitou society, as it had for all Chapter 5 societies
  • Prestige goods, particularly jade and now also bronze and turquoise, continued to serve as markers of status and, quite likely, mechanisms for reinforcing social hierarchy
  • Ritual practice, closely bound up with elite authority, continued as an important organizing feature of society, extending the pattern already documented at Liangzhu and Hongshan
  • No securely attested system of writing has been confirmed for this period, continuing the evidentiary challenge already faced throughout Chapter 5

Why the pattern matters: Recognizing both the genuine escalation in scale and organization at Erlitou and the underlying continuities with Chapter 5's Neolithic societies helps students understand early state formation as an intensification of existing social patterns, achieved through new technologies like bronze casting, rather than as a sudden, unprecedented invention of political authority from nothing.

Cause and Effect

The Emergence of Early State Institutions

Economic causes

Continued agricultural productivity across the Central Plains supported growing populations and surplus, while control over long-distance metal-ore acquisition created new forms of concentrated, politically significant wealth beyond what jade or fine pottery alone had provided.

Organizational and technological causes

Bronze production required a longer, more demanding chain of specialized knowledge and coordinated labor than earlier craft traditions, likely reinforcing the advantage of whatever authority could organize and control that chain from ore acquisition through finished ritual vessel.

Social and ideological causes

Ritual and ceremonial practices, possibly including rites directed toward ancestors and building on traditions traceable to the Neolithic societies of Chapter 5, plausibly provided a framework within which control of bronze ritual vessels could translate into religious and political legitimacy.

Effects

The cumulative result was a settlement of unprecedented scale and organizational complexity in the Central Plains, exercising apparent influence over a wider regional network, and establishing technological and institutional precedents — especially in bronze production and ritual practice — that the Shang civilization examined in Chapter 7 would develop much further.

Historical complexity: No single cause fully explains the rise of Erlitou, and historians should avoid reducing early state formation to any one factor, whether economic surplus, technological innovation, or ideological legitimation. Most current researchers favor explanations that combine these factors, recognizing that they likely reinforced one another over time.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Community Around Erlitou

Archaeological evidence allows historians to offer a cautious reconstruction of daily life within and around Erlitou during its period of greatest development. Most residents likely continued to live in ways not radically different from earlier Longshan-era farming communities examined in Chapter 5, cultivating millet and other crops, raising domesticated animals, and producing much of their own basic pottery and tools, but now within a settlement whose center contained monumental architecture, specialized workshops, and a small elite population whose lifestyle, access to prestige goods, and ritual role differed dramatically from that of the surrounding agricultural population.

Specialized bronze casters and turquoise workers likely occupied a distinct, probably relatively small, social position within Erlitou society, their labor and livelihood closely tied to elite patronage rather than to independent agricultural production. Workers involved in the construction of the settlement's rammed-earth platforms, walls, and road network likely represented a broader cross-section of the population, mobilized periodically for large communal building projects in a pattern with roots traceable to the walled Longshan towns and the Liangzhu hydraulic system examined in Chapter 5.

Family and lineage organization at Erlitou cannot be reconstructed in specific detail from the available evidence, but the association of certain neighborhoods and burial areas with particular levels of wealth and access to prestige goods suggests that hereditary or lineage-based distinctions likely shaped daily life and social position for most residents, much as burial evidence at Liangzhu had suggested for an earlier late Neolithic society. Students should recognize that, as with every society examined so far in this textbook, the dramatic evidence of elite architecture and craft production documented by archaeologists represents only a small fraction of the population, and most people at Erlitou likely experienced daily life shaped primarily by agricultural labor, household organization, and local community ties, even as the wider political and economic structure of their society was becoming considerably more centralized around them.

Regional and Frontier Context

This chapter has concentrated on the Central Plains region because this is where the archaeological record for early state formation in this period is most fully documented and where later textual tradition locates the Xia dynasty. Students should not conclude, however, that the Central Plains stood alone as the site of significant social and political development in this era. Other regions of China continued to develop along their own trajectories during the same centuries: societies in the middle and lower Yangtze region, following the decline of Liangzhu examined in Chapter 5, developed new regional cultures with their own patterns of settlement and craft production; communities in the southeast, southwest, and northeast maintained distinctive local traditions, some in contact with Central Plains developments and others largely independent. Of particular note is the massive walled site of Shimao, in the northern loess highlands along the Yellow River's great northward bend, which reached an extent of roughly four hundred hectares — larger than Erlitou's mature-phase footprint — during a period overlapping the beginning of the Erlitou sequence, complete with monumental stone-faced walls, elaborate gate structures, and evidence of specialized craft production and long-distance exchange. Shimao is a valuable reminder that Erlitou's importance rests on its specific place within the Central Plains developmental sequence leading toward Shang and Zhou civilization, not on any claim that it was uniquely large or advanced among all contemporary Chinese societies. The relative archaeological prominence of Erlitou in current scholarship reflects, in part, the historical accident of where later Chinese textual tradition directed early archaeological search, a pattern students should bear in mind as a caution against assuming that the best-documented region was necessarily the most historically significant region in every respect.

China and the Wider World

Although Erlitou-period society developed substantially through indigenous regional processes traceable to the Neolithic cultures of Chapter 5, some scholars have raised questions about possible connections between early Chinese bronze metallurgy and bronze-working traditions elsewhere across Eurasia, given that bronze technology appears somewhat earlier in parts of Western and Central Asia. Whether Chinese bronze casting developed through independent invention, through the diffusion of basic metallurgical concepts later adapted into the distinctively Chinese piece-mold casting technique, or through some combination of local innovation and external influence, remains an active and genuinely unresolved area of archaeological and scientific research, discussed briefly in Section 6.7. What can be said with more confidence is that the piece-mold casting technique itself, and the specific artistic and ritual traditions associated with early Chinese bronze vessels, represent a distinctively regional development that would go on to shape East Asian material culture for millennia, regardless of the ultimate origins of the underlying metallurgical knowledge.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the threshold between the regionally diverse Neolithic societies traced in Chapter 5 and the securely documented Bronze Age civilization of Shang China examined in Chapter 7, focusing on the archaeological site and culture of Erlitou and the later textual tradition describing a Xia dynasty said to have ruled during roughly the same period and region.

Students have learned that these two bodies of evidence — archaeological remains from Erlitou and written texts about Xia composed many centuries later — must be evaluated separately before any connection between them can be responsibly drawn. The traditional Xia narrative, most fully preserved in Han-period texts such as Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian, describes Yu the Great's flood-taming achievement, the founding of hereditary dynastic rule, and an eventual transfer of legitimacy to the Shang following the misrule of a final tyrant. This narrative carries deep significance for later Chinese political thought, particularly regarding ideas of virtuous rulership and the loss of legitimacy, even though its specific historical claims cannot currently be verified through contemporary evidence.

The chapter examined in detail what the archaeological record at Erlitou actually documents: a large, deliberately planned settlement with monumental rammed-earth architecture, a walled elite compound, dedicated craft workshops producing sophisticated bronze ritual vessels through piece-mold casting technology and finely worked turquoise ornaments, unequal burial patterns suggesting hereditary or strongly institutionalized social hierarchy, and evidence of a wider regional settlement network showing Erlitou's apparent political and cultural influence. This evidence, considered on its own terms, supports the conclusion that Erlitou represented a genuine early state, or at minimum a highly complex, centralized polity, marking a distinctive concentration of political authority, specialized production, and settlement hierarchy in the Central Plains region. Students should recall from Chapter 5 that Liangzhu and other late Neolithic centers were themselves large and organizationally sophisticated societies; Erlitou's significance lies not in simply exceeding every earlier center in scale, but in the specific developmental sequence it represents within the Central Plains region that would give rise to Shang and, later, Zhou civilization.

The chapter then examined directly the central historiographical question of whether Erlitou can be identified with the Xia dynasty, presenting the strongest arguments on both sides of this ongoing scholarly debate. Students learned that chronological and geographic correspondence, along with the scale of political organization documented at Erlitou, provide suggestive but not conclusive grounds for identification, and that the absence of any contemporary written source naming a ruler, dynasty, or polity at Erlitou — in sharp contrast to the oracle-bone inscriptions that independently confirmed the Shang king list — means that the identification remains a matter of scholarly interpretation rather than settled historical fact.

This chapter placed particular emphasis on historical method: the distinction between contemporary sources and later textual tradition, the concept of protohistory as a threshold period between prehistory and fully documented history, and the importance of evaluating archaeological and textual evidence independently before drawing connections between them. These methodological lessons extend well beyond the specific case of Erlitou and Xia, preparing students to handle similar evidentiary problems throughout their study of history.

Finally, the chapter traced the transition from Erlitou to the Erligang culture and, eventually, to the fully documented Shang civilization of Chapter 7, emphasizing that this transition represents a genuine change in the kind of historical knowledge available to historians — from suggestive but inconclusive archaeological inference to the far more secure combination of archaeology and contemporary written evidence that characterizes the study of Shang China. Armed with a clear understanding of both the achievements and the limits of the Erlitou evidence, and a disciplined approach to the relationship between archaeology and later textual tradition, students are now prepared to move into Chapter 7's examination of a civilization whose kings, for the first time in this textbook's narrative, can be named with confidence.

Key Terms

Early state
A political community with institutionalized authority that persists beyond a single ruler's lifetime, formal territorial organization, and the capacity for sustained labor and resource mobilization.
Protohistory
A period at the edge of written documentation, lacking contemporary written sources but described in later texts composed by a subsequent, literate society.
Traditional narrative
An account of the past transmitted through later texts rather than contemporary written sources, requiring independent corroboration before being treated as established fact.
Contemporary source
A written or material source produced at or near the time of the events it describes, generally considered stronger evidence than later tradition.
Archaeological culture
A recurring assemblage of artifacts, architectural forms, and settlement patterns identified through excavation, used to define a distinct prehistoric or protohistoric society.
Identification problem
The challenge of connecting an archaeological culture, defined by material remains, with a specific named political community known from later texts.
Piece-mold casting
A bronze-casting technique using multiple separately made ceramic mold sections assembled around a core, refined during the Erlitou period.
Palace-style compound
A large, walled, architecturally formal precinct containing rammed-earth platform buildings, interpreted as elite residential, ritual, or administrative space.
Rammed earth (hangtu)
A construction technique compacting earth in layers within a frame, used for walls and platform foundations at Erlitou and earlier Longshan sites.
Elite compound
A spatially distinct, architecturally formal area of a settlement associated with a ruling or high-status group.
Craft workshop
A dedicated production area associated with specialized manufacture, such as bronze casting or turquoise working, identified through production debris and tools.
Turquoise inlay
A decorative technique setting small pieces of turquoise into a bronze or other surface, documented in exceptional Erlitou-period plaques.
Settlement hierarchy
A pattern in which some settlements are notably larger and more complex than surrounding sites, indicating regional political or economic organization.
Political center
A settlement exercising disproportionate political, economic, or ritual influence over a surrounding region.
Peer polity interaction
A framework describing how neighboring, independent societies influence one another's development through exchange and competition, introduced in Chapter 5.
Mandate narrative
A pattern in later Chinese historiography linking dynastic rise and fall to the moral virtue or corruption of rulers, examined further in Chapter 8.
Dynastic succession
The transfer of hereditary rulership from one generation to the next within a ruling family or lineage.
Historiography
The study of how history has been written, including the methods, sources, and biases of historians and earlier chroniclers.
Radiocarbon dating
A scientific dating method measuring the decay of carbon-14 in organic material to estimate its age.
Erlitou culture
The archaeological culture, spanning the early second millennium BCE (absolute dating debated), associated with the type-site of Erlitou and related settlements in the Central Plains.
Erligang culture
The archaeological culture succeeding Erlitou, widely associated by scholars with early Shang civilization, examined in Chapter 7.
Xia-Shang transition
The traditional dynastic transition described in later texts, tentatively associated by some scholars with the archaeological transition from Erlitou to Erligang.
Xia dynasty
The first of the traditional Three Dynasties described in later Chinese historical writing, said to precede the Shang.
Yu the Great
A legendary sage-king credited with taming a great flood and founding the Xia dynasty according to later tradition.
Oracle-bone inscriptions
Shang-period divinatory inscriptions on bone and shell, examined fully in Chapter 7, whose discovery confirmed the historicity of the Shang royal lineage.
Lead-isotope analysis
A scientific technique used to trace the geographic origin of metal artifacts by analyzing isotopic ratios in the metal.
Chiefdom
An analytical category for societies with hereditary leadership and labor mobilization capacity, intermediate between egalitarian villages and states, introduced in Chapter 5.
Central Plains (Zhongyuan)
The region along the middle and lower Yellow River associated with Longshan, Erlitou, Erligang, and early Shang developments.
Sima Qian
Han-period historian whose Records of the Grand Historian preserves the fullest traditional account of the Xia royal succession.
Xia-Shang-Zhou Chronology Project
A modern Chinese research initiative that sought to establish absolute dates for the Xia, Shang, and Zhou dynasties using combined archaeological, textual, and scientific methods.
Ancestor veneration
Ritual practices honoring deceased forebears. Such rites are securely documented for the Shang royal house through oracle-bone inscriptions; some scholars interpret Erlitou elite ritual and bronze use as possibly involving ancestral rites, though no contemporary Erlitou-period text confirms the ritual recipients or theology involved.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter has examined one of the most historiographically delicate moments in the entire span of Chinese history: the point at which archaeological evidence for genuine early state formation grows compelling, while contemporary written confirmation of the specific political community that formed it remains absent. Understanding this chapter matters not because it resolves the Xia question — it deliberately does not — but because it teaches students how to think when evidence is suggestive but incomplete, a condition historians encounter far more often than tidy, fully resolved cases.

The developments examined in this chapter — monumental architecture, specialized bronze production, unequal burial, and regional political influence — did not disappear with Erlitou's decline. They were continued, adapted, and transformed during the Erligang period and, ultimately, reappeared and developed further in the archaeological sequence associated with the securely documented Shang civilization examined in Chapter 7, where oracle-bone inscriptions finally allow historians to write history in the fullest sense: naming rulers, dating events, and testing later textual tradition against contemporary evidence. The threshold crossed in Chapter 7 depended on the political, economic, and technological foundations laid, however anonymously, at Erlitou.

This chapter has also modeled an important historical thinking skill with implications far beyond early China: resisting the pressure, often reinforced by popular narrative, national pride, or simple storytelling convenience, to declare a debated question settled before the evidence actually settles it. Appreciating the difference between "Erlitou shows evidence of a genuine early state" and "Erlitou was definitely the Xia dynasty" prepares students to evaluate similarly contested claims throughout the rest of this textbook and in their broader study of history.

As students move forward into Chapter 7's examination of the Shang dynasty, they should carry with them a clear sense of what has changed and what has not: the underlying processes of political centralization, craft specialization, and ritual authority traced from Chapter 5 through this chapter reappear and develop further in the archaeological sequence associated with Shang civilization, but the historian's evidentiary toolkit expands dramatically, for the first time in this textbook, to include a genuine contemporary written record — a change that will make Chapter 7 a very different kind of chapter to write, and to read, than the one just completed.
