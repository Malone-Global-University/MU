Part III — The Han Empire
15

Han Government, Economy, and Society

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

Picture a county office in the autumn of a year near the turn of the era. Clerks sit at low tables with bundles of wooden tablets and strips of bamboo, cutting, binding, and sealing records. Households from the surrounding villages have been counted: how many people, of what ages, with how much land, how many oxen, what taxable property. Each figure will be copied, summed, and forwarded to the commandery, and from there to the capital, where officials will add it to the year's accounts. The emperor will never see the tablets that record one farming family's fields. Yet the empire's capacity to feed its court, pay its officials, and field its soldiers depends on thousands of such records, and on thousands of people willing, or compelled, to make them true.

This chapter opens the door of the Han state and looks inside. Chapter 13 described how Qin created the first centralized empire in the Warring States world, and Chapter 14 explained how the Han dynasty, after the collapse of Qin, made that imperial form durable through political compromise, fiscal restraint, and the consolidation of central authority. Chapter 15 asks a different kind of question. It asks what made a durable empire function from day to day, and who actually carried its burdens and received its benefits. The scene above is a reconstruction, built from the kinds of administrative documents that archaeologists have recovered from Han-period offices, garrisons, and tombs, but it captures the central subject of the chapter: an empire of perhaps sixty million people governed largely through written records on wood and bamboo, seals, and the cooperation of households.

The chapter's central argument is that the Han Empire was sustained not by the emperor alone but by an interconnected system of officials, local communities, registered households, agricultural production, taxation, markets, families, and social hierarchies. Imperial power depended on extracting resources from society, but it also depended on cooperation with local elites and on keeping households productive enough to meet their obligations. Over time, wealth, education, landholding, and government service became increasingly intertwined, creating opportunities for social advancement and tensions that later Han governments struggled to control. The empire was powerful because it could organize society, but its power ultimately depended on the households, officials, producers, merchants, and local elites it sought to organize.

For that reason, this chapter deliberately avoids treating government, economy, and society as separate subjects. A county official needed household registers because households supplied taxes and labor. The state favored agricultural producers because they sustained its revenues. Wealthy families could convert economic resources into education, patronage, and office. Merchants could grow rich enough to worry officials, and the state responded by taxing, restricting, and, at times, taking over branches of commerce. Families organized the labor on which both household survival and taxation rested. Bureaucracy existed inside the economy and society, not above them. Students should therefore read each section of this chapter as one view of a single interconnected system.

Much of what follows depends on unusually rich evidence. Beginning in the twentieth century, excavations at Han-period garrison sites in the northwest, in tombs in the Yangtze region and elsewhere, and in the ruins of administrative offices have recovered thousands of wooden and bamboo documents: statutes, registers, accounts, letters, and reports. These sit alongside the great transmitted histories, above all Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian and Ban Gu's Book of Han, and alongside memorials, debates, and agricultural writings. Together they let historians see not only how officials and thinkers described the empire but also, at moments, how its clerks and accountants actually worked. They also carry limits: the transmitted histories were written by educated men close to the court, the excavated documents are fragments that survived by accident, and neither gives ordinary farmers, women, or laborers a full voice.

What changed during the four centuries of Han rule (traditionally 202 BCE to 220 CE, divided into the Western Han, the interregnum of Wang Mang's Xin dynasty, and the Eastern Han)? The state moved from the cautious, low-tax policies of its early decades to a much more interventionist fiscal regime under Emperor Wu, and then to a more varied pattern in which local elite families gained influence. Recruitment into office broadened, and a growing connection between classical learning and official service began to reshape local elites. Commerce and private wealth expanded despite ideological suspicion of merchants. What continued was the basic administrative architecture inherited from Qin, the reliance on registered households as the unit of taxation and service, and the overwhelming importance of agriculture.

Who was affected? Everyone living within the empire's registered territories, but unequally. Officials, clerks, rank-holders, landowners, tenants, smallholders, merchants, artisans, women who wove cloth and managed households, and people held as enslaved or dependent laborers experienced the same institutions from very different positions. This chapter places them side by side. It also connects to the wider story of the book: Chapter 16 will show the Han state projecting its power outward toward Inner Asia, financed by the systems described here; Chapter 17 will explore the intellectual and everyday cultural life of Han people; and Chapter 18 will examine how stresses within this system, including the concentration of wealth and local power, contributed to the empire's crisis and fall. Comparisons across Eurasia are also instructive, since the Han Empire's contemporary, the Roman Empire, faced similar problems of governing large agrarian populations through local intermediaries, and Chapter 15 raises those comparisons where they illuminate rather than distract.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the structure of Han central, commandery, county, and local administration, and explain how written records made a large empire governable.
  2. Explain how recruitment into Han office worked, and distinguish recommendation and other Han routes from the later imperial examination system.
  3. Analyze the role of clerks, local leaders, and elite families in county-level government and why imperial rule depended on local knowledge.
  4. Trace the components of the Han fiscal system (land tax, household and person taxes, labor and military service, commercial revenue) and compare early Han restraint with the more interventionist policies of Emperor Wu.
  5. Explain the connection among agriculture, household survival, taxation, and state revenue, and compare agricultural conditions in different regions of the empire.
  6. Assess the relationship between landholding, wealth, and political influence, and explain why the structural tension between smallholders and wealthy families should not be read as an inevitable decline.
  7. Evaluate the place of merchants and markets in Han society, and explain the gap between ideological suspicion of commerce and its practical importance.
  8. Distinguish among legal rank, occupation, wealth, and political power, and explain why Han society cannot be reduced to a four-class pyramid.
  9. Interpret the household as an economic, legal, and administrative unit, and explain the varied roles of women in Han family economies.
  10. Evaluate competing interpretations of the reach and limits of Han state power in local society, using both transmitted and excavated evidence.
  11. Connect government, economy, household organization, and social hierarchy as one interconnected system rather than as separate topics.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did an empire of tens of millions of people, without modern communications, keep track of its population, land, and resources?
  2. Why did imperial government depend so heavily on local knowledge, clerks, and locally connected families?
  3. What did the Han state need from its population, and how did it obtain it?
  4. How did the fiscal needs of the state shape its attitude toward agriculture, merchants, coinage, and private wealth?
  5. Why could the state benefit from economically secure farming households while wealth accumulated in ways that made those households more vulnerable?
  6. How should historians describe social status in an empire where rank, office, wealth, occupation, and lineage overlapped rather than aligned?
  7. In what ways was the household both a family and a unit of government, and how did that shape women's and men's lives?
  8. Was Han society controlled by the state, or did the state depend on society, and what evidence bears on the question?

Historical Context

The Han inherited its administrative habits from Qin, and Qin drew on the reforming states of the Warring States period examined in Chapter 10. Those states had experimented with household registration, standardized law, ranked titles awarded for service, direct rule through appointed officials rather than hereditary lords, and the taxation of peasant households to support mass armies. Chapter 12 introduced the Legalist political thought associated with such experiments, and Chapter 13 described how Qin extended them across a conquered empire. The Han founder and his successors did not abandon the Qin administrative framework. They kept its commanderies and counties, much of its law, and its emphasis on registered households, while softening its harshness, restoring a degree of regional self-government through kingdoms and marquisates, and cutting the burdens of taxation and labor, as Chapter 14 explained.

Geographically, the Han Empire at its height encompassed the Yellow River valley and the North China Plain, the Wei River valley and the Guanzhong region around the capital at Chang'an, the Huai River region, the middle and lower Yangtze basin, the Sichuan Basin, parts of the southeast and Lingnan, and a chain of frontier commanderies extending northwest through the Hexi Corridor. As Chapter 2 emphasized, these regions differed sharply in climate, crops, population density, and social organization. The empire's political map was not the map of modern China. It excluded much of the steppe, the Tibetan plateau, and large parts of the southwest, and its southern and southwestern zones included communities whose relationship to the imperial administration was thin or contested. Registered subjects, that is, people the state could count and tax, were concentrated in the great river plains and basins.

The Han economy was overwhelmingly agrarian. The surviving population figures, drawn from a census recorded in the Book of Han for the year 2 CE, give roughly 12 million households and nearly 60 million registered people. These figures are among the most remarkable statistical survivals of the ancient world, but they are also state records with all the limitations of such records: they reflect who the government managed to register and who evaded registration, and they may have been shaped by administrative reporting practices. Later Han figures, for example from the mid-second century CE, are lower for the total population, and historians debate how much of the difference reflects real demographic change, dislocation, regional shifts, or weakened registration. The important point is that the state saw its population through a system of registered households, and that system structured much of what follows.

Socially and intellectually, the Han period saw a gradual change in how the state and elite families thought about government. In the early decades, the court leaned on figures trained in a range of traditions, including Huang-Lao learning, Legalist legal expertise, and various forms of classical scholarship. Under Emperor Wu (r. 141–87 BCE), the court increasingly patronized classical learning, founded an Imperial University in 124 BCE, and encouraged officials who could combine legal-administrative skills with classical education. Chapter 17 will examine Han Confucian learning in its own right. In this chapter, the relevant point is administrative and social: education, family connections, and officeholding began to reinforce one another, and local elites increasingly saw literacy in the classics as a route to standing.

The historical stakes are high. The Han Empire, and the model of governance and social order that later generations associated with it, shaped the meaning of "imperial" government in East Asia for two millennia. Later dynasties looked back to the Han as a source of institutions, legitimacy, and even names: the majority population of later China still calls itself "Han" people, though, as Chapter 1 stressed, that identity has itself changed across time. Yet students should resist projecting later imperial practice backward. Han government was not the Tang or the Ming in miniature. It had its own logic, its own recruitment methods, its own tax system, and its own social hierarchies, and this chapter tries to explain them on their own terms.

Section 15.1

Imperial Government and Administration

At the summit of the Han system stood the emperor, whose person, edicts, and ritual functions gave the government its legitimacy. But the Han empire existed administratively through thousands of people who counted households, recorded land, collected revenue, judged disputes, transmitted orders, and kept records. The emperor occupied the summit of the system, yet he could not personally govern a territory that stretched across thousands of kilometers. The story of Han government is therefore a story of institutions: how authority was divided, delegated, documented, and checked.

Han central government inherited a pattern from Qin in which a small group of senior officials stood beneath the emperor. In the Western Han, the highest central offices are commonly grouped as the "Three Excellencies": the Chancellor (or Counselor-in-Chief), the Imperial Counselor (who supervised officials and oversaw documents and censorial functions), and the Grand Commandant, an office associated with military affairs whose functions and continuity varied. The arrangement reflects a deliberate separation of governmental, military, and supervisory functions. Beneath them stood the "Nine Ministers," heads of specialized bureaus responsible for matters such as imperial ritual, palace guards, the imperial stables and transport, the judiciary, foreign guests and dependent rulers, the imperial clan, the state's agricultural revenues, and the emperor's private income. Some of these portfolios overlapped by design. Han government deliberately divided, and sometimes duplicated, responsibilities among officials, partly to prevent excessive concentration of power, and the arrangement changed over time. The precise titles and functions of the Three Excellencies were reorganized several times, and under Eastern Han the Secretariat, a body originally attached to the palace, came to handle much of the flow of documents.

Emperor Wu's long reign illustrates how the balance shifted. To bypass the established ministers, he relied increasingly on personal advisers and palace secretaries, a group that historians sometimes describe as an "inner court" distinguished from the "outer court" of formal ministers. Such arrangements gave emperors more flexibility, but they also created tension between the formal bureaucratic hierarchy and the emperor's favorites, relatives, and household staff. Chapter 14 introduced the court politics of empresses and imperial in-laws; here the point is administrative: the structure of central government was not fixed but was repeatedly adapted to the needs and personalities of rulers.

Below the court, the empire was divided into commanderies (jun) and counties (xian), the same basic system created by Qin. In the early Han, as Chapter 14 explained, large regions were assigned to kings from the imperial family and to marquises, whose territories were governed by their own appointed officials under imperial supervision. After the suppression of the great regional rebellion of 154 BCE and later measures, the kingdoms were reduced and brought more closely under central control. By the census of 2 CE, the empire comprised roughly one hundred commanderies and kingdoms and around fifteen hundred county-level units. Counties were the state's essential contact point with the population. Each commandery was headed by a grand administrator, assisted by a military commandant and a staff of clerks, and each county by a magistrate, or by a chief in smaller units. In the later first century BCE and afterward, regional inspectors, and eventually governors, added a supervisory layer over groups of commanderies, an institution whose evolution shows the central government's constant worry about unchecked local power.

Bureaucratic hierarchy was expressed in ranks measured in grain. Officials were categorized by the nominal annual grain equivalent of their stipend, with the highest posts described as "ten thousand bushels" and commandery and county heads in categories such as "two thousand" or "six hundred" bushels. In practice, salaries were paid in a combination of grain and cash, and the ranking system worked as a way to arrange offices in a legible hierarchy. Ranks in bushels should not be confused with the twenty ranks of honor discussed in Section 15.7, a separate system of social status.

What made this apparatus governable was writing. Han offices ran on documents: statutes and ordinances, edicts, memorials, reports, registers, accounts, letters, and lists, written on strips of bamboo or wood tied into rolls, on silk in more prestigious cases, and, in later centuries, on paper (Chapter 17). Orders passed down through officials and post stations, sealed with clay impressions to guarantee authenticity, and reports flowed upward. At year's end, commanderies and kingdoms sent accounting officers to the capital with annual reports on population, land, revenues, criminal cases, and other matters. The great administrative documents recovered at frontier garrison sites, such as those at Juyan on the northwestern frontier, show the practices of daily recordkeeping in a military-agricultural context, including inventories, duty rosters, and correspondence, and demonstrate that even a remote garrison lived by written records. Excavated legal texts, including the statutes from Zhangjiashan in Hubei, show that many aspects of law and administration were regulated with considerable detail.

Students should be careful not to imagine Han government as perfectly centralized. Orders could be delayed, misunderstood, or ignored. Officials could falsify reports or cooperate with local interests. The capital's understanding of the countryside was mediated by documents prepared by local clerks. And imperial policies were adapted, sometimes greatly, by the officials who implemented them. The strength of the Han system lay in its combination of record-based procedures, hierarchical supervision, legal norms, and repeated reform, not in the emperor's ability to control every detail.

Section 15.2

Officials and Local Government

The bureaucracy reached the population at the county. A county magistrate was appointed by the central government and, in accordance with a general principle in Han practice, was normally not a native of the commandery he served, a measure intended to reduce local entanglement. But the magistrate governed with a staff drawn largely from the local area. These clerks, together with subordinate officers, formed the operational core of county government. They kept the registers, prepared documents, investigated crimes, assessed taxes, organized labor service, and interacted daily with villagers. A magistrate might serve a few years and move on; clerks, and the families connected to them, often stayed. This asymmetry of knowledge is one of the most important facts about Han local government: imperial government depended on local knowledge.

Below the county office, administration reached down through sub-county units. Townships (xiang) had their own officers, including a "tax and judicial" officer and an elder who advised on moral and community matters; smaller units such as pavilions (ting) served as police and courier stations; and villages were organized in wards (li) of roughly a hundred households, with groups of five and ten households linked by mutual responsibility. These arrangements, whose exact shape varied by place and period, worked as a means of registration, surveillance, and collective liability. They also meant that the state's lowest tier consisted of people who were both government agents and villagers embedded in local kinship and neighborhood networks.

Legal administration was a major part of county work. Magistrates investigated crimes, heard disputes, and referred serious cases upward. The Zhangjiashan collection also contains the Book of Submitted Doubtful Cases, a collection that illuminates early imperial legal reasoning, investigation, and judicial procedure, including how difficult cases were referred to higher officials. It should not be treated as a direct courtroom docket: scholarship identifies literary shaping and a mixture of material based on actual cases with constructed narratives. Law, as Chapter 13 explained for Qin, remained a central instrument of governance in Han times, although Han rulers and officials reformed penalties and increasingly incorporated moral arguments drawn from classical learning.

Historical Vocabulary
Recommendation (chaju) and the "Han examination" misconception

Recommendation was the practice by which officials nominated candidates for office. From the reign of Emperor Wu, commanderies and kingdoms were expected to recommend men regarded as "filial and incorrupt" (xiaolian), and other categories, such as "abundant talent" and "worthy and upright," were also used at various times. Candidates were then appointed, sometimes after being questioned about policy or classical learning by the court.

This is not the later imperial examination system, which is examined in Chapters 26 and 31. Han recruitment was diverse: it included recommendation, imperial summons of individual scholars, promotion of clerks, privileges that allowed some sons of high officials to enter service, purchase of office in some periods, and study at the Imperial University. Some forms of testing existed, but there was no standardized, competitive, regularly held examination that governed entry into the bureaucracy as a whole. Students who read that the Han "had civil service examinations" are compressing very different institutions. Understanding this difference matters because it shows that Han elites gained office through reputation, connections, and patronage as much as through demonstrated learning.

Recruitment into office reveals how closely government and local society were bound together. Recommendation depended on the judgment of officials and local notables about who counted as filial, virtuous, and capable. It rewarded reputation, and reputation was built within families, communities, and networks of patronage. Candidates from families with the resources to educate their sons and cultivate connections had a decisive advantage, though the system also provided some routes of advancement for talented men from less prominent families. Clerks, who did much of the actual administrative work, could in some circumstances rise to become officials, but the career of a clerk and the career of a high official were often different tracks. The founding of the Imperial University in 124 BCE, with students who studied classical texts and could be examined and appointed, created another route that connected learning to office. Its growth over the following centuries, especially under the Eastern Han, gave classical education increasing significance for official careers, though it never displaced other paths.

The government also tried to supervise its own officials. Regional inspectors, established in 106 BCE, were charged with examining the conduct of commandery officials and local powerful families; according to later quotations of their charge, one of their principal tasks was to watch for powerful families who seized land and abused their neighbors. This detail is revealing: as early as the reign of Emperor Wu, the government identified locally powerful families as both a source of potential disorder and, implicitly, a group that officials could not easily control. Officials, meanwhile, were subject to evaluation, promotion, demotion, and punishment, and had to submit annual reports. The evidence suggests a system with real supervisory mechanisms, but also one whose effectiveness depended on the honesty and skill of individual officials and on the willingness of local societies to cooperate.

By the Eastern Han, many of the men who staffed the upper administration came from families in which office, landholding, and classical education had become mutually reinforcing. Modern scholarship emphasizes both the sophistication of the Han bureaucracy and its growing dependence on cooperation with local elites. This point should be understood carefully. It does not mean that the bureaucracy was corrupt or hollow, or that the empire was governed by a hereditary aristocracy. It means that the officials who ran the state were tied, through their families, networks, and local standing, to the societies they governed, and that the state often found it easiest to govern by working through those ties.

Section 15.3

Taxation and State Revenue

What did the Han state need from its population, and how did it obtain it? The state needed grain to feed officials, soldiers, and dependents; cash to pay salaries, purchase supplies, and maintain the court; labor to build and repair roads, canals, walls, and palaces; military manpower; and, increasingly, funds for an expanding array of programs. It obtained these through a combination of taxes on land, taxes on people, obligations of labor and military service, revenues from trade and state-controlled production, and the mobilization of goods through the administrative system. Taxation was a system, not simply a percentage. A household had to be identified, registered, assessed, connected to officials, able to produce resources, and made accountable for payment or service.

Historical Vocabulary
Registered household (hu) and corvée (yao)

The registered household (hu) was the fundamental unit of Han administration. Registers recorded the household's members, their ages and sexes, and, in many cases, property such as land, houses, and livestock, along with status information such as rank. Registered households were the source of most taxes and the pool from which labor and military service were drawn.

Corvée refers to unpaid labor service owed to the state. Han men in the relevant age range were liable for periods of service, for example on construction, transport, or guard duties, and for military service, though the details changed over time and some obligations could be commuted by payment. The English word is borrowed from medieval Europe and must be used carefully: Han labor obligations formed part of a state system built on registration and law, with its own categories, exemptions, and rules.

The land tax was levied on agricultural produce, a share of the harvest. According to the transmitted tradition, the early Han rate was one-fifteenth of production. Emperor Wen reduced it to one-thirtieth in 168 BCE and remitted the land tax altogether in 167 BCE. Collection was later restored under Emperor Jing at the one-thirtieth rate, which became an important Han benchmark. These low rates, which reflect the priorities of early Han policy examined in Chapter 14, do not mean that the burden on households was light in practice. The land tax was only one of several claims, and the total burden depended on the size and productivity of the household's land and on the other obligations it faced.

The other major regular taxes were levied on persons. Adults within specified ages paid an annual poll tax in cash, while children paid a smaller tax, and Emperor Wu later increased the burden on younger children. Because these taxes were levied in coin, households needed access to markets in which they could sell grain, cloth, or other goods to obtain cash, a fact that links taxation directly to commerce and to the vulnerability of poor households to price changes and debt. Alongside cash payments, the state required labor: adult men owed a period of service, often described as a month per year in the household's home area, and military service including a period of training and duty in the capital or on the frontier. Officials could convert some of these obligations into payments, allowing wealthier households to buy their way out while poorer ones bore the burden in person. Households also paid taxes on commercial property, on vehicles and boats, and, at various times, on other items.

The fiscal history of the Han is best understood in phases. The early Han, as Chapter 14 described, was a period of restraint: the state was weakened by civil war and dependent on a recovering population, and its policies aimed to restore agricultural output and reduce burdens. Under Emperor Wu, the state sought far larger revenues to finance its expanding activities, including military campaigns that Chapter 16 treats. The government responded by introducing new levies on merchants' and artisans' property, establishing monopolies over salt and iron production and, for a time, liquor, standardizing coinage through central minting of the wuzhu coin, and creating trading and transport agencies, sometimes called "equalization and standard delivery" programs, to move goods and stabilize prices while earning revenue. Modern economic histories stress both the continuity from Qin in the basic fiscal system and the substantial increase in state economic regulation under Emperor Wu.

These policies had consequences that are hard to summarize in a single verdict. The transmitted sources reveal both support and severe criticism, some of it from officials who thought the state was competing unjustly with its subjects and burdening the poor, and some from those who believed the measures were necessary and just. Section 15.6 and the Primary Source Window in this chapter examine the great debate of 81 BCE over these measures. Later Han governments varied in their use of state monopolies; the details are complicated and, for some periods, debated. Students should avoid presenting a single tax rate or a single set of policies as "the Han system" for four centuries. The fiscal system changed with political circumstances, with the size and condition of the population, and with the state's aims.

Finally, the administrative infrastructure required to collect revenue deserves emphasis. Assessing and collecting taxes required accurate registers, clerks who understood the local population, storehouses, transport systems, and mechanisms for auditing. That is why the discussion of taxation is inseparable from the discussion of local government in Section 15.2. The state's fiscal capacity was a function of its administrative capacity, and its administrative capacity depended on its relationship with local society.

Section 15.4

Agriculture and Rural Society

Most Han people were farmers, and the government depended overwhelmingly on registered rural households. The chain of dependency was direct: agriculture supported household survival; surviving, productive households paid taxes and provided labor; and those resources supported the army, the court, and the administration. Farming was therefore integral to imperial history rather than a sidebar about everyday life.

Students should avoid the phrase "the Chinese peasant" as though a single figure could represent so large and varied a population. In the northern plains and the Guanzhong basin, the principal crops were millet and, increasingly, wheat, grown in dry fields with ox-drawn iron plows and a routine of seasonal labor. In the Huai region and farther south, wet-rice cultivation was more prominent, dependent on irrigation and standing water. In the Sichuan Basin, irrigation works inherited from Qin, such as the Dujiangyan system, supported high productivity. Farther south and southwest, in areas that Sima Qian described as thinly populated and lightly farmed, agricultural practice differed again, and many local communities were not yet fully integrated into the imperial tax system. Han agricultural technology included iron tools, deep plowing, the use of draft animals, improved seeding methods, crop rotation in some regions, and a wide range of irrigation and water-lifting techniques. Agricultural treatises and seasonal calendars written in the Han period, some surviving only in fragments, show how officials and writers sought to promote productive practices.

A household's farming year followed the seasons: plowing, planting, weeding, harvesting, threshing, storing, and, in the slack season, repairing tools, spinning, weaving, and engaging in other tasks. Farming families also raised animals, tended vegetable plots and orchards, gathered fuel, and produced cloth from hemp, ramie, or silk. Because a family's labor was limited by its size and composition, illness, death, or the calling of an adult male to service could disrupt the household economy. The vulnerability of households is vividly described in a memorial attributed to Chao Cuo in the time of Emperor Wen. Paraphrasing, he portrays a typical household of five people, with two adults able to work about a hundred mu of land, who labor through every season, pay taxes, provide labor, and suffer floods and droughts. When they must pay taxes on short notice, they sell goods at a loss or borrow at high interest, and some end by selling their land and houses, or even their children, to repay debts. Chao Cuo was arguing a political case in favor of policies that rewarded grain producers, so his portrait cannot be treated as neutral statistics, but it identifies the mechanisms through which small farmers could fall into hardship: taxes payable in cash, price volatility, debt, and natural disaster.

The government recognized this vulnerability, in part because stable, independent farming households were taxable and recruitable. Han rulers made agriculture a matter of ideological priority, honoring farming in ritual and rhetoric, remitting taxes after disasters, distributing seed or grain, and, in some circumstances, resettling people onto land. The Han state also invested in irrigation and water-control projects, and its granaries, in principle, provided stores for relief and stabilization. But these efforts varied greatly in scale and effect. Students should distinguish between the ideal of the independent smallholder and lived conditions. Rural society included smallholders working their own land, tenants who farmed land owned by others and paid a share of the crop, hired workers, laborers attached to estates, and wealthy landowners who employed or lodged many dependents. A memorial associated with Dong Zhongshu complained that poor farmers worked the land of the rich and paid half of their crop, an expression of concern about how much of the produce of the poor was being taken by others.

Regional variation also affected the state's relationship to rural society. In densely populated core regions, counties were more thoroughly organized, registers more complete, and tax collection more routine. In peripheral areas, the state's reach was thinner, and its officials often worked through local leaders. Over the course of the Han dynasty, and especially the Eastern Han, the population distribution shifted, with a gradual increase in the population of the Yangtze region and the south, a movement that later chapters will trace and that would ultimately transform the empire's economic geography.

Section 15.5

Landholding and Wealth

This section introduces one of the great structural tensions of Han society. The state benefited from economically secure households, yet wealth could accumulate in ways that weakened those households and increased elite power. The tension was structural rather than inevitable: it arose from the way taxes, debt, inheritance, and political connections interacted, and its consequences varied with time and place. Chapter 18 will ask what happened when concentrations of wealth, land, dependents, and local power contributed to imperial weakness. This chapter establishes the structure without prejudging that later story.

Land in Han China could be bought, sold, mortgaged, and inherited. Excavated legal texts and later documents show that land and houses were treated as registered property, and that the law regulated inheritance and transfers. Under the early Han, the government also granted land as part of the rank system, with statutes specifying entitlements of land and houses for holders of different ranks, though it is uncertain how consistently these were carried out. Most rural households held small parcels, typically a few dozen mu, the Han mu being considerably smaller than the modern measure. Larger holdings belonged to marquises and other nobles, to officials, to prosperous farming and commercial families, and to local magnates. Some estates included tenants, hired workers, and dependent laborers, and some magnates also controlled workshops, storehouses, and stores of grain.

How did wealth accumulate? Several paths overlapped. Successful farmers could buy land from neighbors in difficulty. Merchants and workshop owners could invest profits in land. Officials could combine salaries, gifts, and opportunities with landholding, though the extent of official corruption is debated. Inheritance practices, which in many cases divided family property among sons, tended to fragment holdings over generations, so families that sustained wealth needed to keep acquiring assets or to manage inheritance carefully. Debt was a critical mechanism: households that borrowed at high interest, especially after a bad harvest or a demand for cash taxes, could lose land to creditors. Chao Cuo's memorial, discussed in Section 15.4, describes just this process.

Wealth also translated into influence. Wealthy families could support the education of sons, maintain networks of clients and dependents, host guests, and cultivate relationships with officials, all resources that mattered in a recruitment system based on reputation and recommendation. Scholarship on early imperial China stresses that elite status rested not simply on office but on combinations of wealth, landholding, family connections, education, patronage, and access to political power. Office, in turn, could bring gifts, protection, opportunities for land purchase, and prestige. The consequence was a set of local families whose economic, social, and political standing reinforced one another. Han sources call them "powerful clans" or "great families," and the Han government's uneasy relationship with such families is visible from the inspectors' charge onward.

Contemporaries noticed and debated these developments. Dong Zhongshu urged limits on the amount of land families could hold and lighter burdens on the poor. In the last decades of the Western Han, officials proposed setting limits on landholding and on the number of dependents, though the plan was not effectively implemented; and the reforms of Wang Mang after 9 CE included an ambitious, and unsuccessful, attempt to change land and slave-holding arrangements. The very existence of these proposals shows that some Han observers saw land concentration and rural inequality as problems. It does not prove that all small farmers inevitably lost their land to giant estates. The evidence on landholding is limited and uneven, and historians differ about how far concentration proceeded and how it varied by region. Students should avoid a teleological claim in which the story of the Han is reduced to the steady loss of independent smallholdings; the more defensible claim is that smallholders and wealthier families coexisted, that wealth could purchase advantage, and that the state's interests were bound up in both groups.

Section 15.6

Merchants, Markets, and Commerce

Han China was not an exclusively agrarian world hostile to commerce. Local markets, urban trading quarters, workshops, transport networks, and long-distance internal trade were integral to the economy, and the state itself relied on markets to convert grain and cloth into cash and to supply its needs. The paradox that students should keep in view is that merchants could be politically disparaged while becoming economically powerful.

Markets were regulated spaces. Cities and towns had designated market areas, supervised by officials who inspected weights and measures, collected fees, and recorded traders. Merchants were sometimes listed in separate "market registers," which subjected them to particular obligations and restrictions. The transmitted histories report that early Han rulers restricted merchants from wearing silk and riding in carriages and imposed heavier taxes on them, and that merchants and their families were barred at various times from holding office. These restrictions expressed a widespread ideological view, shared by many officials and writers, that agriculture was the foundation of society and that traders profited from the labor of others. But such restrictions did not mean commerce was unimportant or that merchants were uniformly poor or powerless.

Sima Qian's chapter on the accumulation of wealth in the Records of the Grand Historian is a key source for the commercial world of the early Han. He describes regional economies with distinct products and specialties, and lists individuals and families who became wealthy through iron production, salt, herding, trade, moneylending, and other ventures. Some of the great iron-making families, for example in the Sichuan region and the Central Plains, employed large numbers of workers and controlled substantial resources. Sima Qian's view of wealth is neither simply admiring nor simply condemning, and he treats economic motives as a normal aspect of human behavior. Archaeological finds of iron workshops, salt-production sites, lacquerware, textiles, and coinage confirm the scale and variety of Han production.

Coinage was a central element of the economy and of state policy. Coins circulated widely, taxes were partly payable in cash, and the state's control of minting was contested in the early Han, when private minting was permitted for periods. Under Emperor Wu, the government reformed the currency, introducing the wuzhu coin, which remained a standard for centuries, and moved to centralize the minting of coin. The reforms, together with the new taxes on commercial property, the salt and iron monopolies, and price-stabilization measures, represented a major expansion of state involvement in the economy. They should be understood in terms of political economy: the government's need for revenue and its wish to limit private accumulation of wealth in commerce shaped a program of regulation and state enterprise. The wars that drove much of the spending are treated in Chapter 16.

The consequences of these policies were debated at the time and remain debated now. Salt and iron monopolies gave the government access to revenue, but they also reduced or redirected the activities of private producers, and critics complained about poor-quality iron tools and inconvenience to farmers. Supporters argued that state control prevented private magnates from profiting at the expense of the public and financed necessary expenditures. The debate over these policies, held at court in 81 BCE, is the subject of this chapter's Primary Source Window. Its significance is that it shows Han intellectuals and officials arguing, in surprisingly modern terms, about the proper relationship between state and market.

Han merchants were a varied group. Some were small shopkeepers or peddlers, some were wealthy long-distance traders, some were workshop owners, and some were moneylenders. Powerful merchant families could become landowners, cultivate connections, and, in some periods, see family members enter office despite formal restrictions. The evidence suggests a gradual blurring of boundaries between landholding, commerce, and officeholding in local elite families, a pattern that will be relevant again in the discussion of status in Section 15.7. Long-distance trade beyond the empire, including the Silk Roads, is treated in Chapter 16; here the focus is on the internal economy that made such networks possible.

Section 15.7

Social Classes and Status

It is tempting to present Han society as a neat pyramid, with the emperor at the top, then officials, farmers, artisans, and merchants. That picture, drawn from ideological formulations of the "four occupations," captures how some writers ranked the moral value of different kinds of work, but it does not accurately describe how people lived. Han society had overlapping forms of status. Students must distinguish legal status, occupation, wealth, and political power, because these did not always coincide.

Historical Vocabulary
Rank, office, and status

Rank in the Han context often refers to the twenty ranks of honor (ershi jue), a system of titles inherited from Qin that could be conferred on men (and, in some circumstances, families) for military service, for service to the state, or as general awards. Rank brought legal privileges, such as reduced penalties, exemptions from certain obligations, and, according to statutes, entitlements to land and houses. The lowest ranks were widely held by ordinary free men, while the highest, including marquis, were the preserve of the political elite.

Office refers to a position in the administration, ranked by grain stipend. Status is a broader term for social standing, which could depend on rank, office, wealth, family, education, reputation, occupation, and legal condition. A person could be high in one dimension and low in another: a low-ranking official might wield authority exceeding his wealth, a wealthy merchant might be barred from certain privileges, and a poor scholar might command respect despite little property.

At the top stood the imperial family, including kings and their households, followed by marquises and other titled nobles, some of whom held estates and income from designated households, though after the early Han their political power was limited. Officials formed another category, ranging from senior ministers to county clerks, whose standing depended on rank, position, and family. Alongside officials were holders of the twenty ranks of honor, a large group extending well below the political elite. Scholarship describes Han society as strongly structured by legally recognized ranks that brought differentiated privileges, while wealth and hereditary advantage increasingly complicated the formal structure. Statutes recovered from tombs show that rank affected the treatment of offenders, the size of the land and housing to which a person was entitled, and the household's obligations.

Below the titled and the ranked were the free commoners, the great majority of the population, including farming households of very different means, artisans, laborers, peddlers, and merchants. Farmers ranged from prosperous households with substantial land to poor tenants and hired workers. Artisans worked in state workshops, private workshops, and households; some were skilled specialists in iron, lacquer, textiles, or building. Merchants, as noted, could be very wealthy or very modest. Beneath or at the margins of this order were people of dependent or unfree status. The Han government held bonded servants and convicts, some of whom performed labor for the state, and private households, including wealthy ones, held enslaved people. Their numbers, though uncertain, were probably small in proportion to the total population, but they were significant in particular contexts. Han rulers and reformers on occasion attempted to restrict slave trading or to release certain categories of people, most notably in the early years of the Eastern Han and under Wang Mang, which shows that enslavement was considered a public issue.

Gender, region, and ethnicity also shaped status. Women's standing often depended on the household to which they belonged, while non-Han communities incorporated within the empire, especially in frontier and southern zones, were subject to different arrangements, discussed in the Regional and Frontier Context section. Educated families increasingly distinguished themselves through knowledge of the classics and participation in literary and ritual culture, a form of prestige that overlapped with, but was not identical to, wealth or office. Historians of the Eastern Han often point to the growth of locally powerful families whose standing combined land, dependents, office, and learning.

Consider three examples. A wealthy iron-workshop owner in the Central Plains might command greater resources than most officials, yet might face legal disabilities and social disdain. A county clerk, technically of modest rank and stipend, might hold real authority over local villagers, since he controlled the records that determined their obligations. A member of a long-established local lineage might combine land, education, and connections and see sons enter office while remaining outside the formal aristocracy. In each case, the simple ladder of "scholar, farmer, artisan, merchant" fails to describe the person's position. The moral ranking of occupations was a real ideological force, and it influenced law and policy, but it coexisted with a social order in which power flowed through multiple channels.

Section 15.8

Family, Gender, and Household Organization

The household was both a social institution and a unit of government. The state did not encounter isolated individuals; it governed people largely through households. Registers listed household members under a household head, taxes were assessed at the household level, and obligations of labor and military service were drawn from its adult men. The state therefore had a direct interest in the composition, stability, and productivity of households, and this interest shaped law on marriage, inheritance, division of property, and household headship.

The typical registered household was small. The 2 CE census implies an average of around five people per household, which suggests that the most common household consisted of a married couple and their children, sometimes with an elderly parent or other relatives. Larger, multi-generational households existed, especially among elites, but many families divided their property and established separate households, a practice encouraged by state policies inherited from the Qin era that taxed households as units. Household division could be ritualized and recorded, and its patterns of inheritance, in which sons usually received shares of the family property, shaped the fortunes of families across generations. Legal texts show that succession to the position of household head, and the disposition of property, were regulated by statute, with provisions for cases in which a household had no adult son, in which women could in some circumstances stand as heads or heirs.

Marriage was a family arrangement that created alliances, transferred labor and property, and provided heirs. Elite families used marriage to build connections among lineages, while poorer households used it to secure labor and stability. Widowhood, remarriage, and divorce occurred, and the law and social custom regulated them, although practices varied by class and place. Students should not assume that a later, codified form of Confucian patriarchy was a timeless and uniform Han reality. The Han period saw the emergence of ideals of female virtue and household hierarchy in didactic writing, notably in works by educated authors, but actual practice depended on wealth, status, region, and circumstance. Chapter 17 will treat these cultural and intellectual dimensions of family life more fully. Here the concern is the family as an economic, legal, social, and administrative unit.

Women were central to the household economy. Textile production, especially spinning and weaving of hemp, ramie, and silk, was regarded as women's work in both rhetoric and practice, and its products were vital for clothing, for household exchange, and for payments and gifts. The state itself operated textile workshops, and cloth was a form of value in the Han economy, used as currency in some settings and as a component of rewards and tribute. Women also worked in fields, tended animals and silkworms, prepared food, and managed household resources. The moralizing language of official rhetoric, which praised "men plowing, women weaving," idealized this division of labor, but the reality was flexible, with women engaged in agricultural work when needed and men in crafts and trade.

Elite women could hold property, manage estates, and influence family strategies, and some exercised political power, especially empresses and empress dowagers, as Chapter 14 explained. Some women achieved learning; the historian and moral writer Ban Zhao of the first-second century CE, discussed further in Chapter 17, is the best-known example. But experiences differed dramatically by status. A poor tenant's wife or an enslaved woman faced constraints, hardships, and vulnerabilities different from those of a wealthy widow with property or a palace consort. Even within a single household, age and seniority could matter as much as gender: a mother-in-law might hold substantial authority over younger women and men.

The registered household was also a channel of state influence and a unit of collective responsibility. Neighbors were grouped into mutual-responsibility units in which members could be held accountable for one another's conduct, and the state rewarded households and individuals it regarded as exemplary, including "filial sons," "obedient grandsons," and "chaste widows," which linked family morality with public recognition. This official interest in family virtue created incentives for households to conform to state-approved ideals, especially where reputation could open avenues to office. The relationship between family, morality, and politics, in short, ran in both directions: the state regulated families, and families used the language and practices approved by the state to build standing.

Section 15.9

State and Society in Han China

This section synthesizes the chapter around the relationship between imperial reach and social cooperation. The Han state possessed impressive bureaucratic capacities, but it could not govern hundreds of communities by direct intervention from Chang'an. It depended on officials, clerks, registered households, local elites, families, landowners, producers, merchants, and networks of recommendation and education. That dependence raises the chapter's deepest question: was Han society controlled by the state, or did the state depend on society?

The best answer is deliberately reciprocal. The Han state organized, taxed, ranked, regulated, and mobilized society, but its ability to do so depended on cooperation from social institutions and local people that it did not completely control. On one side, the evidence for state capacity is considerable: population registers, standardized statutes, hierarchical reporting, tax systems, inspectors, monopolies, and the ability to mobilize labor and armies on a massive scale. On the other side, the evidence for dependence is equally strong: clerks with local roots, officials whose appointment and reputation depended on local networks, powerful families whose influence rivaled that of officials, and the repeated efforts of the state to restrain its own agents and local magnates. Neither picture alone is adequate.

The relationship was also dynamic. Early Han restraint reflected a state that relied on recovery from below. Emperor Wu's activism strengthened the state's fiscal and administrative reach but also produced resistance and criticism, and after his reign later governments varied in how far they pursued state economic intervention. During the Eastern Han, the state's approach to local society changed again, with more reliance on local elites, on classical learning as a mark of qualification, and on gentry-like families whose social standing extended across generations. These shifts did not follow a single trajectory, and they varied by region.

A significant aspect of this relationship was the growing influence of educated officials. As Han administrators became increasingly associated with classical learning, local elites increasingly connected education with official service, especially as the dynasty developed. Officials educated in the classics wrote memorials citing ancient models, staffed the Imperial University, and drew on the classics to argue about policy, as in the 81 BCE debate. But it would be a mistake to conclude that a later-style scholar-official class or examination meritocracy already existed. Han officials came from varied backgrounds, with legal, clerical, military, and classical training all represented, and recruitment continued to depend on recommendation, patronage, and privilege. The educated official of the Han is best understood as an emerging figure: a sign of change in how office and learning were linked, and an important step toward later developments, but not yet the scholar-official of Tang, Song, or Ming times. Chapter 17 will examine Han classical education and intellectual culture; the relevant point here is how learning began to interact with the social structure described in this chapter.

The reciprocal relationship helps explain both the durability and the vulnerabilities of Han rule. It was durable because it combined a strong administrative framework with practical cooperation from local communities and elites. It was vulnerable because the same cooperation could dilute central control, especially when powerful families accumulated land, dependents, and influence, and when fiscal pressures burdened smallholders. The Han did not fall because of a mechanical cycle. As Chapter 18 will show, the crisis of the later Han arose from specific political, fiscal, social, environmental, and military conditions. This chapter provides the structural background for understanding those conditions.

Section 15.10

Chapter Review and Han Society Checkpoint

This chapter has examined how the Han Empire governed and sustained a large population, through administration, taxation, agriculture, landholding, commerce, social hierarchy, and family organization. Students should leave the chapter understanding that these were parts of one interconnected system, and the review below asks them to trace the connections rather than memorize each institution separately.

Three Pathways Through the Han System

Pathway 1: the fiscal chain
Registered land → farming household → agricultural production → taxation and labor → county administration → imperial revenue → support for court, officials, and army. Break any link (through disaster, debt, evasion, or administrative failure) and the chain weakens.
Pathway 2: the elite chain
Wealth → land and property → education and connections → recommendation → office → greater local influence → more wealth. This pathway helps explain how local families and the state became so intertwined.
Pathway 3: the commercial chain
Commerce → private wealth → political suspicion and ideological criticism → state regulation, taxation, and intervention → new arguments about the proper role of the state. The salt and iron debate belongs here.

The core insights of this chapter are as follows. The emperor sat at the summit, but the empire existed administratively through many people who counted, recorded, taxed, judged, and communicated. Recruitment into office was diverse and did not follow a later-style examination system. Taxation was a system linking registration, production, and accountability, and it changed significantly between the early Han and the reign of Emperor Wu. Agriculture was the economic foundation and varied by region. Landholding and wealth created a structural tension between household security and elite accumulation, without predetermining later crisis. Merchants were economically important even while politically disparaged. Social status was multidimensional rather than pyramidal. Households were the essential unit through which state and society met, and women's labor and roles were basic to their operation. And the state and society were mutually dependent.

Historical Evidence

Historians of Han government and society draw on two broad kinds of source, transmitted and excavated, and each offers different strengths.

Transmitted histories and texts. The Records of the Grand Historian (Sima Qian) and the Book of Han (Ban Gu and collaborators) provide narrative, biographical, and treatise material, including the Book of Han's treatises on food and money and on geography, which record the census of 2 CE. The Book of the Later Han, compiled later, covers the Eastern Han. These works reflect the viewpoints of court-connected authors, are shaped by literary and political purposes, and sometimes compress or idealize administrative realities. Memorials, such as those of Chao Cuo and Dong Zhongshu, are arguments addressed to rulers, not neutral descriptions. The Discourses on Salt and Iron is a literary compilation of a court debate.

Excavated documents. Wooden and bamboo documents recovered from garrison sites in the northwest, such as Juyan and Dunhuang, reveal the routine record-keeping of frontier administration. Legal manuscripts from tombs, notably at Zhangjiashan, are of two kinds: the statutes preserve contemporary legal rules, while the Book of Submitted Doubtful Cases illuminates legal reasoning and professional knowledge but is not an official docket. Neither should be treated as a county-office archive recording routine administration in the way documents from administrative sites do. Tomb documents from various regions include household and tax-related lists, and registers from some tombs record households and property. These sources allow historians to see administrative practice at a level of detail that transmitted histories rarely provide, but they survive by chance, often from particular regions, and may not represent the empire as a whole.

Archaeology and material culture. Excavated farm tools, iron workshops, salt-production sites, coins, granaries, tomb models of farmsteads, and pictorial reliefs depicting farming, markets, and salt-well operations illuminate the material conditions of production and exchange. Tomb burial goods and tomb architecture show how status was displayed. Their interpretation requires caution, since tombs represent the burial practices of those who could afford them and the ideals of the living.

Statistical records. The census figures of 2 CE and of the Eastern Han are among the earliest large-scale population statistics in the world. They are invaluable but should be read as administrative products. Historians debate what they exclude, how they were compiled, and what changes over time mean.

Using these sources together requires attention to perspective. Almost all sources were produced by literate men associated with the state or with elite families. The experiences of tenants, laborers, enslaved people, and most women must often be inferred from indirect evidence, from the assumptions and complaints of elites, and from archaeological traces. Students should ask, for every source: who made it, for whom, and what could it not tell us?

Map Study: Governing and Feeding the Han Empire

On a historical map of the Han Empire, students should locate and consider:

Primary Source Window

Primary Source Window: The Salt and Iron Debates (81 BCE) and the Discourses on Salt and Iron

Compiler: Huan Kuan (fl. 1st century BCE) · Date represented: 81 BCE, compiled some decades later · Type: Literary compilation of a court debate on state economic policy

Context: After the death of Emperor Wu in 87 BCE, the court under the regency of Huo Guang summoned a group of men recommended from the commanderies, often described as literati or "worthies," to debate with senior officials, notably the Imperial Counselor Sang Hongyang, on the policies of the previous reign, including the state monopolies on salt and iron, the liquor monopoly, and the price-stabilization programs. Huan Kuan's Discourses on Salt and Iron presents the exchange as a dialogue, but it is a later literary version and not a stenographic transcript.

Paraphrase of the arguments (not a translated quotation):

The government spokesmen argue that the state's enterprises produce revenue needed for defense and public needs, that they prevent wealthy private interests from monopolizing resources, and that an active state can strengthen the realm. The literati reply that the state should rely on moral example and light burdens, that monopolies burden farmers with poor tools and interfere with honest livelihoods, and that officials should not compete with the people for profit.

Analysis: The text shows that Han elites debated the role of the state in the economy, connecting fiscal policy to moral and political principle. It reveals concerns about frontier spending, merchants, private wealth, and the burdens on farmers, and it displays the tension between administrative and classical-moral approaches to government. As a source, it reflects the perspectives of officials and recommended scholars, not those of farmers or merchants themselves. As a literary compilation, it may have shaped or dramatized the arguments, and the extent to which the recorded speeches match what was said in 81 BCE cannot be verified. Following the debate, the liquor monopoly was ended, while other measures, including the salt and iron monopolies, continued for a time.

Questions for students:

  1. What does this source say about the aims of state economic policy and the objections to it?
  2. Who created it, and under what circumstances?
  3. What purposes may have shaped Huan Kuan's presentation of the debate as a dialogue?
  4. What can historians learn from it about Han fiscal policy and about elite ideas of government?
  5. What can historians not learn from it alone, for example about the actual effects of monopolies on farmers and merchants?
Historical Debate

Historical Debate: How Powerful Was the Han State in Local Society?

The question: Historians agree that the Han produced an impressive bureaucratic apparatus. They differ on how deeply that apparatus penetrated local society and how far it could impose its will against local interests.

Interpretation A — A state with substantial administrative reach: Emphasizing excavated statutes, registers, and administrative documents, some historians argue that the Han state maintained a sophisticated system of registration, taxation, law, reporting, and supervision that reached down to households. The scale of the 2 CE census, the detail of legal texts, and the routine documentation of remote garrisons show a state capable of extensive record-keeping and mobilization.
Interpretation B — A state dependent on local elites and intermediaries: Other historians emphasize the limits of that reach, noting the dependence on locally recruited clerks, the importance of powerful families, the state's worry about them, and the possibility that registers reflected what local officials and households chose to report. On this view, the empire functioned through negotiated cooperation as much as through command.

Points of disagreement: The disagreement is mainly one of scale and of how to interpret administrative evidence: how much weight to give documents that record procedure and ideal, and how far reports, registers, and complaints reveal actual practice. It is not primarily a dispute about whether the documents exist or are authentic.

Evidence: Both views draw on the same categories of evidence, read for different emphases. Administrative documents show procedure and ideal; they cannot always show how far practice matched it. Complaints in memorials about powerful families and evasion suggest limits, but they also show the state's ongoing efforts to respond. Many historians treat these as complementary dimensions of Han governance rather than mutually exclusive claims.

Why it matters: How historians assess state power shapes how they interpret dynastic durability and crisis, and how they compare the Han with other early empires.

Student task: Using evidence from this chapter, decide which interpretation is better supported, or explain what additional evidence, such as more complete local records from different regions, would help resolve the question.

Multiple Perspectives

Multiple Perspectives: One Tax Season

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns found in Han sources, not quotations from named individuals.

A county magistrate: He must deliver the county's accounts, keep the peace, and fill quotas, while depending on clerks who know the villages. His career depends on reports, reputation, and avoiding disorder; he has strong incentives to collect revenue and few means of verifying every household's report.
A smallholding farming household: After the harvest, the household must find cash for poll taxes, contribute labor or pay a substitute, and provide for its own food. A bad year or a family illness may force borrowing at high interest. Its members experience the state through the clerk and village leader as much as through any distant emperor.
A wealthy landowner and local notable: Better able to pay commuted obligations and to lend to neighbors, he cultivates relationships with officials and educates his sons. He may serve as an informal mediator, supplying goods and credit, and benefits from a system in which local knowledge and connections matter, although inspectors and legal rules constrain him.
A merchant or workshop owner: Subject to additional taxes, market registration, and in some sectors state monopoly, he may nonetheless grow rich. He faces legal and social disdain while supplying goods on which officials and farmers depend, and he may invest in land to secure standing.
A woman managing household production: She weaves cloth for the family and for exchange, manages food and household needs, and may take over the farm labor when men are called away. Her work underpins the household's ability to meet its obligations, but she appears in registers only as a member of a household headed by someone else, in most cases.

These perspectives should not be treated as equally powerful. The magistrate and landowner had access to information, influence, and legal resources that the farmer and weaver lacked, and the state's demands fell most heavily on those with the least margin.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change Over Time: From Qin Administration to the Mature Han State and Society

What Changed

  • Harsh Qin burdens gave way to early Han restraint, followed by a more interventionist fiscal state under Emperor Wu.
  • Recruitment broadened to include recommendation and a growing role for classical learning.
  • Local elite families gained influence, and the link between education, wealth, and office strengthened.
  • Population and economic weight gradually shifted toward the Yangtze region and the south.

What Stayed the Same

  • The commandery-county framework and the reliance on written records.
  • Registered households as the basis of taxation and service.
  • The overwhelming importance of agriculture and of household labor.
  • The dependence of imperial officials on local clerks and networks.

When Did Change Occur, and What Was Its Pace?

Change came in distinct phases: the shift from Qin burdens to early Han restraint in the decades after 202 BCE; a marked acceleration in fiscal and educational institutions under Emperor Wu (roughly 134–106 BCE); partial adjustment after the 81 BCE debate; and a slower shift toward local-elite influence and classical learning across the Eastern Han. Change was gradual in recruitment and elite formation, abrupt in the fiscal measures of the later second century BCE, uneven across regions, and contested, as the salt and iron debate shows.

Who experienced change most directly? Merchants and workshop owners experienced the sharpest changes as state policy shifted; educated families experienced new opportunities; farming households experienced greater continuity in their reliance on household labor and in the basic pattern of registration, taxation, and service, even as the size and form of demands shifted. County clerks and local officials likewise experienced continuity in their daily work of records and assessment. Why it matters: The pattern shows that "Han government" was not a fixed model, and that continuity in structure could coexist with major shifts in policy and social influence.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: How Did Fiscal Needs Reshape the Relationship Between State and Economy?

Long-term causes

A Qin-derived system of household registration and taxation; an overwhelmingly agrarian economy dependent on smallholder production; a persistent ideological suspicion of private commercial wealth; and a growing merchant and workshop economy.

Short-term causes

Recovery of population and stores under early Han restraint, which gave the state a larger base; and rising expenditures under Emperor Wu, including military and administrative programs (Chapter 16).

Immediate trigger and context

Fiscal pressure in the reign of Emperor Wu, combined with officials such as Sang Hongyang and others who proposed drawing revenue from commerce and production, and with concern about private accumulation of wealth.

Event or transformation

Taxes on commercial property, salt and iron monopolies, coinage reform, and price-stabilization agencies (roughly 119–110 BCE) expanded state participation in production, trade, and finance.

Immediate effects

Increased revenue, new officials and enterprises, and criticism from those who felt burdened, including complaints about tools and burdens on farmers.

Long-term consequences

An enduring debate about state and market; adjustments after 81 BCE (the liquor monopoly ended); and a lasting model of state involvement in resources such as salt and iron, with later governments varying in their use of it.

Historical complexity

The changes cannot be reduced to one cause. Fiscal need, ideas about government, the interests of officials, the wealth of merchants, and the condition of farmers all interacted, and effects varied by region and over time.

Note: Explaining the expansion of state economic activity as "corruption" or "greed" would miss its fiscal, institutional, and ideological dimensions.

Everyday Life and Social History

Everyday Life and Social History: A Registered Han Household

Consider a farming household in the northern plains, typical of many described in sources like Chao Cuo's memorial (a reconstruction, not a portrait of a particular family). Five people live in a modest dwelling: a married couple, two or three children, and possibly a grandparent. They farm perhaps fifty to a hundred mu, plowing with an iron-tipped plow, sometimes with a borrowed or shared ox, growing millet and wheat, and cultivating vegetables. They pay part of their harvest as land tax and cash poll taxes for adults and children.

The husband may be called for corvée labor for a month or for military service, leaving the wife, children, and elders to manage the fields. The wife spins and weaves cloth, part of which the family uses and part of which may be sold to raise cash. In the autumn, a clerk and village officers visit to verify the household's members and property. A poor harvest, illness, or a delayed tax demand may force the family to borrow from a wealthier neighbor at high interest.

The family belongs to a ward of about a hundred households and is grouped with neighbors in mutual-responsibility units. Local festivals, ancestor offerings, and markets provide social life and exchange. The family may hope that a son will study, or gain rank through service, but for most such households, advancement is uncertain. This life differed from that of a tenant, a household in the south with less thorough registration, a wealthy family with estates, or a merchant's household in a town. Students should avoid imagining that this household represents everyone.

Regional and Frontier Context

Chang'an and Luoyang were not the empire. The Yellow River plains and Guanzhong were densely settled and administratively thorough, with populations concentrated in the areas from which most of the state's revenue came. The Huai region and the Yangtze basin grew in importance, and the Sichuan Basin combined irrigated agriculture with major iron and salt production. In the far south, in Lingnan and in the southwest, imperial commanderies were established, but many local communities lived with different livelihoods, languages, and forms of authority, and the extent of registration and taxation was thinner. Officials often governed through local leaders, and peoples in these regions experienced Han administration as newcomers imposing new obligations, as trading partners, or, in some cases, as a political option among several.

In the northwest and north, frontier commanderies were staffed with garrison troops and settlers, supplied partly through the interior's grain and labor, as Chapter 16 will show. Here, the Han state's fiscal and administrative capacity was tested and stretched. Frontier communities included Han settlers, soldiers, and people of Inner Asian origin, and their experiences differed from those of interior farmers. The perspective of frontier peoples is largely missing from Han sources, which were written from the standpoint of the state, and historians must infer it from archaeology and from occasional remarks in documents.

China and the Wider World

The Han Empire's internal economy was tied to the wider world, even though the main foreign relationships are treated in Chapter 16. The state's need for horses, military supplies, and frontier garrisons shaped fiscal policy, and Han commerce, including silk, moved beyond the empire's borders. The Han was one of several large agrarian empires of its era, and comparison with the Roman Empire is instructive: both used registration, taxation, legal codes, and local elites to govern large populations, both relied on road and communication networks, and both faced questions about the balance between central power and local wealth. Yet they differed in institutions, in the role of written administrative records, and in the nature of elite recruitment, so comparison should highlight difference as well as similarity. In East Asia, the Han administrative model later influenced neighboring states such as Korean and Vietnamese polities, whose relationships with Han commanderies varied from direct rule to selective adoption, and these processes belong to later chapters.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the Han Empire governed and sustained a large population. Its central argument is that the empire was sustained not by the emperor alone but by an interconnected system of officials, local communities, registered households, agricultural production, taxation, markets, families, and social hierarchies. Imperial power depended on extracting resources from society, but also on cooperation with local elites and on keeping households productive.

The chapter began with the machinery of government. The emperor and court stood at the summit, with senior ministers, divided responsibilities, and evolving institutions, but the empire existed administratively through commanderies, counties, and the many people who counted households, recorded land, collected revenue, judged disputes, and transmitted orders. Writing on wood and bamboo, seals, reports, and statutes made the empire governable, though not perfectly centralized.

At the county level, imperial officials depended on local clerks and on local knowledge. Recruitment into office relied on recommendation, imperial summons, clerical advancement, privilege, and study at the Imperial University; it was not a competitive examination system of the later imperial type. Officials were supervised by inspectors and evaluation, and the state's persistent concern with powerful families reveals both its aims and its limits.

The fiscal system connected registered households to state revenue through land taxes, poll taxes, labor and military service, commercial levies, and coinage. Early Han restraint gave way, under Emperor Wu, to a far more interventionist approach, with monopolies, new taxes, and price-stabilization programs. The 81 BCE debate shows that contemporaries argued vigorously about the state's economic role.

Agriculture underlay the entire system. Farming households in different regions produced the grain and cloth that supported taxes and labor, and their vulnerability to disaster and debt was a constant concern. Landholding and wealth created a structural tension: the state benefited from secure smallholders while wealthy families could accumulate land and influence, and that wealth could be converted into education, connections, and office.

Commerce was more important than official rhetoric suggested. Merchants were legally and ideologically disparaged, yet some became very wealthy, and the state both taxed and, in some sectors, took over production. Han society, correspondingly, cannot be reduced to a four-class pyramid; rank, office, wealth, occupation, and lineage overlapped, and people of dependent status occupied the margins.

The household was the crucial unit where state and society met. Registers, taxation, and service obligations were organized around households, whose members included women whose textile and agricultural labor was fundamental. Women's experiences varied greatly by status, and the codified patriarchy of later periods should not be projected backward.

The chapter's deepest question was whether the state controlled society or depended on it. The answer proposed here is reciprocal: the state organized, taxed, ranked, and mobilized society, but its ability to do so depended on officials, clerks, households, local elites, and producers whom it did not fully control. The growing influence of educated officials marked an early step in the link between learning and office, without yet producing a later-style scholar-official class.

Regional variation qualifies every generalization. Core regions were densely registered, while southern, southwestern, and frontier zones were governed more lightly and through local intermediaries. Comparison with other empires reveals both shared problems and distinctive Han solutions.

Chapter 15 thus supplies the structural foundation for what follows. Chapter 16 will show how the Han state projected power outward, financed and supplied by the systems described here. Chapter 17 will turn to the intellectual and everyday cultural life of Han people. Chapter 18 will examine how the tensions within this system, including the concentration of wealth and local power, contributed to crisis and collapse.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 15 matters because it shows how an empire actually worked. Dynasties and emperors provide a chronological frame, but it was households, clerks, farmers, weavers, merchants, and local families who made imperial government possible. Understanding these people and institutions helps students see the Han not as a distant court but as a society organized through registration, taxation, exchange, and family life.

The patterns identified here shaped later Chinese history. Later dynasties inherited the idea of governing through registered households and county administration, debated the same questions of state and market, and dealt with similar tensions between smallholders, wealthy families, and officials. But later institutions, including the examination system, differed from those of the Han, and this chapter has urged caution about projecting later practice backward.

The chapter also reveals larger themes of this textbook: the interdependence of state and society, the regional variety within any empire, the importance of ordinary people's labor, and the need to read sources critically. Chinese civilization here appears as diverse, adaptive, and contested, with tensions built into its institutions. The Han Empire's experience also invites comparison with other agrarian empires, connecting Chinese history to wider Eurasian patterns.

As students move to Chapter 16, they should carry with them the understanding that Han expansion and long-distance exchange were supported by the fiscal and social system examined here, and that Chapter 18's crisis emerged from stresses within it. The Han Empire was powerful because it could organize society, but its power ultimately depended on the households, officials, producers, merchants, and local elites it sought to organize.
