Part XII — Reform and Contemporary China

The 1989 Protest Movement and Its Aftermath

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

In the days after former Communist Party General Secretary Hu Yaobang died on April 15, 1989, wreaths, poems, and handwritten couplets began to appear at the Monument to the People’s Heroes in Tiananmen Square and on the walls of Beijing’s university campuses. Seven weeks later, tanks and infantry were moving along the capital’s main avenues, residents lay wounded in improvised carts on the way to hospitals, and the leadership of the Party was permanently changed. This chapter explains how one led to the other.

Chapter 73 studies the largest and most consequential political protest movement in the history of the People’s Republic of China. It began as mourning for a deposed reformer, became a campaign by university students for dialogue with the government and for a changed relationship between the state and society, and attracted participation from workers, journalists, teachers, officials, and ordinary residents in Beijing and in hundreds of other cities. It ended on the night of June 3–4, 1989, when units of the People’s Liberation Army, acting under orders of the Party leadership, moved into central Beijing and used lethal force against civilians. The number of people killed has never been established with certainty. That the army fired on unarmed civilians and killed many of them is, however, firmly documented.

The topic matters because 1989 was a hinge in the history of the reform era. The decade covered in Chapter 72 had produced rapid growth, new opportunities, and a more open intellectual life, but also price instability, official profiteering, and deep disagreement about how far reform should extend beyond the economy. The crisis exposed those disagreements within the leadership itself. Its outcome removed one General Secretary, elevated another, reshaped the Party’s approach to ideology and security, and altered China’s relations with the United States, Europe, Japan, Hong Kong, Taiwan, and the Chinese diaspora. It also created a lasting struggle over memory: families seeking to name the dead, exiles keeping records abroad, and a state that restricts public discussion of the events.

The chapter asks what changed and what continued. What changed was the political leadership, the language of official politics, the structure of the international relationships on which the reform economy depended, and the lives of thousands of people who were killed, wounded, imprisoned, dismissed, or forced into exile. What continued was the Communist Party’s monopoly on political authority, the policy of “reform and opening” as a framework for economic development, and the central place of the military in the Party’s own conception of its security. How reform would continue after June 1989 is the subject of Chapter 74; this chapter stops at the immediate political and economic aftermath and at the long argument over what the events meant.

The people affected were far more varied than the shorthand “students in the Square” suggests. University and secondary students were the most visible organizers, but their organizations were divided and changed their tactics repeatedly. Journalists marched to demand press freedom. Factory workers formed autonomous federations with grievances about wages, prices, and privilege. Teachers, scientists, and officials joined marches; some Party members and soldiers were uncertain about their orders. Residents of Beijing blocked army convoys, brought food and water to the Square, and rescued the wounded. Senior leaders disagreed with one another, and the decisions that produced martial law and the military operation were taken by a small group of men whose motives and calculations remain partly hidden.

Historians reconstruct these events from a distinctive set of sources: contemporary newspapers and broadcasts, student leaflets and petitions, photographs and film footage, diplomatic cables and intelligence reports declassified decades later, official Chinese government statements, hospital and eyewitness testimony, memoirs by participants and officials, and the documentation assembled by families of the dead. Each type has strengths and limits, and one purported archive of leadership discussions, The Tiananmen Papers, remains the subject of a scholarly dispute over authenticity. This chapter treats source criticism as part of the history itself.

The events also connect China to the wider world in 1989, a year of transformation in the Soviet bloc. Mikhail Gorbachev’s state visit to Beijing, the first Sino-Soviet summit in three decades, brought hundreds of foreign journalists into the capital just as the movement reached its peak. Elections in Poland and political change elsewhere in Eastern Europe formed the international backdrop against which Chinese leaders judged the stakes of the confrontation. At the same time, the Chinese movement had its own domestic roots and its own vocabulary, drawing on a century of student patriotism associated with the May Fourth Movement of 1919 (Chapter 60) and on reform-era debates described in Chapters 71 and 72.

The chapter is organized in eight divisions. The first examines the reform-era contradictions that preceded the crisis. The second and third follow the movement from Hu Yaobang’s death through the April demonstrations and the May mobilization. The fourth analyzes the leadership crisis and the imposition of martial law. The fifth reconstructs the military operation of June 3–4 with attention to where and how violence occurred. The sixth examines the repression, leadership change, and international reaction that followed. The seventh turns to memory, censorship, and evidence, and the eighth is a review checkpoint. Throughout, the chapter distinguishes established facts from contested interpretations and official claims from independently verified evidence.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain how inflation, official profiteering, restrictions on expression, and disagreements over political reform created the background to the 1989 protest movement.
  2. Trace the chronology of the movement from Hu Yaobang’s death on April 15 through the aftermath of June 1989, distinguishing April, May, and June as different phases.
  3. Describe the varied demands and organizations of students, workers, journalists, intellectuals, and residents, and explain why the movement was not unified.
  4. Analyze the significance of the April 26 People’s Daily editorial and the language of “turmoil” in hardening the confrontation.
  5. Compare the positions of Deng Xiaoping, Zhao Ziyang, Li Peng, and other senior leaders, and explain how institutional authority shaped decision-making.
  6. Distinguish the decision to impose martial law (May 17–20) from the military operation of June 3–4 and from Zhao Ziyang’s formal removal on June 23–24.
  7. Reconstruct the geography of the June 3–4 violence, distinguishing events on the roads approaching Tiananmen Square from events in the Square itself.
  8. Evaluate the evidence for casualty estimates, including official Chinese figures, independent investigations, and diplomatic reports, without presenting an uncertain total as exact.
  9. Assess regional variation in protest and repression, including Shanghai, Chengdu, and other cities.
  10. Explain the domestic political, economic, and international consequences of the crackdown, including sanctions and reactions in Hong Kong and Taiwan.
  11. Interpret historical memory, censorship, and the work of bereaved families, exiles, and archivists as part of the history of 1989.
  12. Apply source criticism to official reports, declassified cables, memoirs, photographs, and The Tiananmen Papers, and identify what remains unresolved.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did the death of a politician who had been removed from office two years earlier become the occasion for a nationwide movement?
  2. How did economic reform contribute to political grievances, and why should the movement not be reduced to a single cause such as inflation or a demand for Western-style democracy?
  3. How did the language used by the Party and by student organizations (“turmoil,” “patriotic,” “counterrevolutionary”) shape the options available to both sides?
  4. Why did leaders facing the same crisis reach different conclusions about whether to negotiate or use force?
  5. How did participation broaden from students to workers, journalists, and residents, and how did these groups’ aims differ?
  6. What does a geographic reconstruction of June 3–4 reveal that a summary focused on the Square alone would obscure?
  7. How do historians establish what happened when official records are closed and eyewitness accounts conflict?
  8. Why does the precise number of dead remain unknown, and what can be said with confidence despite that uncertainty?
  9. How did the events of 1989 affect China’s domestic politics and its international position?
  10. How have memory, censorship, and commemoration shaped the history of 1989 as much as the events themselves?

Historical Context

The movement of 1989 must be placed in the decade that preceded it. After the death of Mao Zedong in 1976 and the rise of Deng Xiaoping (Chapter 71), the Party leadership replaced class struggle with economic modernization as its central task. The Household Responsibility System dismantled the people’s communes and raised rural output; Special Economic Zones and foreign trade opened coastal China to investment; township and village enterprises and urban private trade created new income sources (Chapter 72). Universities reopened, hundreds of thousands of Chinese studied abroad, and publishers, film makers, and journalists tested the limits of acceptable expression. For many urban Chinese, the 1980s were a time of rising expectations.

The reforms, however, did not apply evenly. Prices in China’s planned economy had long been set administratively. From the mid-1980s many goods were sold under a “dual-track” system in which a fixed state price coexisted with a market price. This created opportunities for people with access to allocated goods to buy at the low price and resell at the high one. The practice, known as guandao (official profiteering), became a symbol of corruption and unfairness, especially because the beneficiaries were often officials or their relatives. At the same time, urban employees on fixed salaries, including teachers and researchers, saw their relative incomes decline compared with private traders. Rural migrants, workers in state factories, and students facing uncertain job assignments each experienced the reform era differently.

The political environment was also contradictory. Deng Xiaoping and other leaders endorsed economic experimentation and some administrative reform, but they insisted on the Four Cardinal Principles adopted in 1979, which upheld socialism, the leadership of the Communist Party, the people’s democratic dictatorship, and Marxism–Leninism–Mao Zedong Thought as limits on political debate. Periodic campaigns, including the campaign against “spiritual pollution” in 1983–84 and the campaign against “bourgeois liberalization” that followed the student demonstrations of December 1986, reminded intellectuals and students of those limits. The January 1987 removal of Hu Yaobang as General Secretary, discussed in Chapter 72, showed that even a top Party leader could be dismissed for being judged too tolerant of liberalizing tendencies.

Hu was succeeded by Zhao Ziyang, who had overseen rural reform in Sichuan and later served as Premier. At the Thirteenth Party Congress in October 1987, Zhao presented the thesis that China was in the “primary stage of socialism” and proposed a program of limited political reform, including greater separation between Party and government functions and more consultation with the public. Li Peng, a more cautious figure with a background in electric power and ties to senior planners, became Premier. A decision of 1988 to accelerate price reform led to panic buying, bank runs, and a surge in inflation, after which the leadership shifted to austerity. By early 1989 economic management had become a source of division among top officials and of anxiety among ordinary urban residents.

Geographically, the central events took place in Beijing, whose political geography matters for understanding the movement. Tiananmen Square lies at the center of the capital, bordered by the Gate of Heavenly Peace to the north, the Great Hall of the People to the west, the museums of Chinese history and revolution to the east, and the Mao Zedong Mausoleum to the south, with the Monument to the People’s Heroes at its center. Chang’an Avenue, the major east–west thoroughfare, runs north of the Square past Xinhuamen (the gate to the Zhongnanhai leadership compound) and through districts such as Xidan and Muxidi to the west. The principal universities, including Peking University, Tsinghua University, and Beijing Normal University, lie in Haidian District to the northwest, giving the movement a spatial link between campuses and the Square.

The Chinese state of 1989 was the People’s Republic established in 1949, a single-party system in which the Communist Party controlled the government, the army, and the media. The People’s Liberation Army was formally a Party army, and the Central Military Commission, chaired by Deng Xiaoping, directed it. Deng held no state title as head of government or of the Party, but he exercised ultimate authority, and a 1987 understanding within the leadership affirmed that major issues would still be referred to him. This is essential context for the crisis: formal institutions such as the Politburo Standing Committee, the State Council, and the National People’s Congress existed alongside a more informal circle of senior Party “elders” who retained great influence.

The year 1989 was also a symbolic year. It was the seventieth anniversary of the May Fourth Movement of 1919 (Chapter 60), the fortieth anniversary of the founding of the People’s Republic, and the bicentennial of the French Revolution, and many Chinese intellectuals planned commemorations or petitions. Internationally, the Soviet Union under Gorbachev was loosening political controls through glasnost and perestroika; Poland and Hungary were moving toward negotiated political change. Unrest in Lhasa in early 1989 had led the Chinese government to declare martial law in Tibet’s capital in March, giving the leadership recent experience with the use of that measure (Chapter 76). These developments shaped how both protesters and leaders understood their moment.

Finally, students should bear in mind the limits of the chapter’s scope. Chapter 72 covered the economic reforms of the 1980s and the earlier demonstrations of 1986–87; Chapter 73 begins its sustained chronology in April 1989. Chapter 74 covers the Southern Tour of 1992 and the later acceleration of market reform. The meaning of “China” in this chapter is the People’s Republic of China as it existed in 1989, with its government in Beijing; it does not include the Republic of China on Taiwan, which is discussed here only in terms of its reactions.

Section 73.1

Political Reform and Intellectual Debate in the Late 1980s

Discussion of political reform in the 1980s did not begin with students in 1989. In August 1980 Deng Xiaoping himself criticized the over-concentration of power in individual officials and in the Party, and called for clearer divisions of responsibility. The experience of the Cultural Revolution (Chapter 70) convinced many senior leaders that institutional safeguards against arbitrary rule were needed. During the following decade the Party restored a regular system of retirement, reconstituted the legal system, reopened universities, and encouraged technocratic expertise. These changes created real expectations that political life would become more predictable and more open.

The most developed statement of official political reform came at the Thirteenth Party Congress in October 1987. Zhao Ziyang proposed to separate Party and government functions, to reduce the Party’s direct management of enterprises and administrative bodies, to strengthen the legal system, to increase transparency of decision-making, and to establish channels for consultation and dialogue with social groups. Zhao also rejected a Western-style separation of powers and a multiparty system. The proposals were therefore significant in the context of Party politics but limited in comparison with the demands later raised by some students and intellectuals. Many provisions remained general, and implementation was slow.

Intellectual debate outside official channels was lively. Scholars and journalists discussed the sources of China’s backwardness, the relationship between markets and political institutions, and the meaning of modernization. Fang Lizhi, an astrophysicist and university vice-president, gave widely circulated lectures arguing for intellectual freedom and human rights, and was dismissed from his post after the 1986–87 demonstrations. In 1988 a television documentary series, River Elegy (Heshang), argued that China’s inward-looking agrarian culture had hindered modernization and urged openness to the maritime and industrial world; it provoked intense public discussion and criticism by more conservative figures. In 1988–89 a debate over “neo-authoritarianism” asked whether a strong reforming leader might guide economic modernization before democratic institutions developed, while critics argued that unchecked power was the source of existing problems.

These debates were conducted among a small educated minority, but their language spread widely through magazines, university lecture halls, and informal salons. Students and teachers were not uniformly in favor of democratization, and intellectuals disagreed about whether political liberalization should precede or follow economic restructuring. What many shared was a sense that reform of the economy had outrun reform of the political system, and that officials were insufficiently accountable. This sense of a “gap” between economic opening and political constraint is essential to understanding why a demand for dialogue and accountability resonated in 1989.

Section 73.2

Inflation, Corruption, and Social Discontent

The late 1980s were a period of economic dynamism and strain. Gross output grew rapidly, rural industry expanded, and urban consumers could buy goods that had been scarce a decade earlier. But the pricing system created tensions. Under the dual-track system, state-allocated goods such as steel, fertilizer, and coal were sold at fixed prices while above-plan output could be sold at market prices. People with connections to allocation authorities could buy at the low price and sell at the high one. This activity, known as guandao, became in the public mind a symbol of unearned privilege.

In the summer of 1988 the leadership, meeting at the seaside resort of Beidaihe, resolved to move more quickly toward price reform. The announcement, together with rumors that prices would rise sharply, produced panic buying of consumer goods and runs on bank savings in August. Official statistics later recorded retail price increases of roughly 18 percent for 1988, with greater increases in many cities. In September the Party Central Committee shifted course, adopting a program of “improving the economic environment and rectifying economic order,” which restricted credit and investment. The reversal damaged confidence in the leadership’s competence and was identified with Premier Li Peng’s emphasis on stabilization, while Zhao Ziyang, associated with faster reform, found his position weakened.

Urban residents on fixed salaries felt the squeeze directly. A widely discussed contrast was that of professors, researchers, and teachers earning less than street vendors or taxi drivers, an inversion that critics called a reversal of “brain work” and “manual work.” Workers in state enterprises worried about wages and job security as managers gained authority and factories were told to seek profit. Rural residents experienced different pressures: costs of inputs rose, and in some places farmers received promissory notes in place of cash for state grain purchases. Not all groups were losers, as many entrepreneurs and officials prospered, which sharpened perceptions of unfairness.

Corruption concerns focused on both specific cases and perceived structure. Rumors circulated about the commercial activities of children and relatives of senior leaders, whom people called “princelings,” and about companies run by Party and government agencies. Historians caution that rumors were not always accurate and that the scale of corruption was difficult to measure, but the perception was itself politically important. The demands later presented by students, including publication of officials’ incomes and property, reflected this concern. The social discontent of 1988–89 was thus not simply a matter of economic hardship; it was a concern about fairness, transparency, and who benefited from reform.

Historical Evidence

Historical Evidence — Inflation, Wages, and Public Discontent, 1986–1989

Historians use several kinds of evidence to measure economic conditions before 1989. Official statistics from the State Statistical Bureau provide retail price indices, wage data, and output figures; they are valuable but compiled by a government with interests in how conditions appear, and they average widely varying regional and sectoral experiences. Contemporary newspapers and magazines reported on shortages, panic buying, and official profiteering, though press content was shaped by editors’ reading of the political situation. Student leaflets, petitions, and posters recorded demands and complaints in participants’ own words. Surveys by Chinese researchers and later oral-history interviews allow historians to examine how ordinary people perceived price changes.

None of these sources alone shows why people protested. Statistics show that inflation was high but not who blamed whom; posters show popular grievances but not their prevalence; recollections after the event may reflect later knowledge. Historians therefore combine them, treating price data, public rumor, and political demand as separate layers of evidence. The key point for students is that discontent was real and varied, but that no single measure explains participation. Many participants were not economically deprived, and some who were deprived did not participate.

Section 73.3

Students, Universities, Journalism, and the Reform-Era Public Sphere

By 1989 China’s universities were crowded, politicized, and anxious. Students in elite institutions such as Peking University and Tsinghua University were among the best-educated people in the country, but they had grievances: poor dormitory conditions, small stipends, uncertainty about government job assignment, and the sense that diplomas offered fewer rewards than commercial activity. Many felt a responsibility to speak on national affairs, a tradition reaching back to the 1919 May Fourth Movement and to Confucian ideals of scholar responsibility (Chapters 11 and 60). Student demonstrations had occurred in 1985, 1986, and 1988, so protest had precedent.

Students and young teachers formed discussion groups and informal “salons.” At Peking University, a self-described Democracy Salon invited scholars to speak about political ideas, and student newspapers and wall posters circulated opinions. Students visited sympathetic intellectuals and borrowed ideas from foreign publications that became available during the opening. Not all students were politically active; many were primarily concerned with study, emigration, or careers. But a core of activists formed networks across campuses, with experience in organizing meetings and circulating leaflets, and these networks proved crucial when the movement began.

Journalism offered another channel. Under the reform, newspapers and magazines enjoyed more latitude than before, particularly those with commercial pressures to attract readers. The Shanghai weekly World Economic Herald, under editor Qin Benli, published candid discussions of political reform and would become a flashpoint in April 1989. Journalists in official outlets such as the People’s Daily and Xinhua News Agency also pressed for more open reporting. However, all media remained under Party supervision, and editors were liable to dismissal if they crossed political lines. This created a contradictory situation: a more open press that could not freely criticize senior leaders.

In the winter of 1989, intellectuals took public steps. In January, Fang Lizhi addressed an open letter to Deng Xiaoping urging an amnesty for political prisoners, including Wei Jingsheng, who had been imprisoned since 1979 for his Democracy Wall writings. Other petitions followed in February, signed by dozens of established writers and scientists. The authorities did not meet these petitions with concessions. In February, when the American president visited Beijing, Fang Lizhi was prevented from attending a banquet to which he had been invited. Students and intellectuals thus entered the spring of 1989 with an established tradition of petitioning and an expectation that the authorities would not respond.

Historical Vocabulary
Political reform (zhengzhi tizhi gaige)

In Chinese Party language, “political reform” meant changes in administration and institutions to support economic modernization, such as separating Party and government functions, rather than competitive elections or independent institutions. Different groups used the term to mean very different things in 1989, which is why historians must ask who was using it and for what purpose.

Corruption (fubai) and guandao

In 1989, “corruption” referred both to bribery and to the use of official position for private gain, including the resale of price-controlled goods (guandao). Protesters often used it to criticize privilege and lack of transparency rather than a specific legal offense.

Turmoil (dongluan)

An official term meaning disorder that threatens the political system. When applied to the protests it carried a serious political meaning, since the Party had historically associated “turmoil” with the Cultural Revolution. Its use was a decision about how to classify the movement, not a neutral description.

Martial law (jieyan)

A legal measure that gives military and security forces authority to restrict movement, assembly, and communications in a designated area. In 1989 it was applied to parts of Beijing from May 20; it is a political decision, distinct from the later military operation carried out under it.

Section 73.4

The Communist Party Leadership and Conflicting Political Priorities

To understand the decisions of April–June 1989, students must understand who held power and how. The Politburo Standing Committee in spring 1989 had five members: Zhao Ziyang (General Secretary), Li Peng (Premier), Qiao Shi (responsible for security and legal affairs), Hu Qili (responsible for ideology and media), and Yao Yilin (Vice Premier). Below them sat the Politburo and the Central Committee. Above and alongside this structure stood Deng Xiaoping, who held the chairmanship of the Central Military Commission and was the leader whose judgment others deferred to on major decisions, and a circle of revolutionary-era seniors including Chen Yun, Li Xiannian, Peng Zhen, Bo Yibo, and Wang Zhen. Yang Shangkun, President of the PRC, was Deng’s close associate within the military leadership.

These leaders shared a commitment to Party rule and to economic development, but differed in emphasis. Zhao Ziyang favored faster market reform, more reliance on consultation, and a relatively flexible approach to dissent. Li Peng and more conservative figures, including Chen Yun, were wary of rapid price reform and market forces and emphasized planning, stability, and ideological discipline. Deng supported market reform strongly but was committed to preserving Party control, and had approved the campaign that ended Hu Yaobang’s leadership in 1987. All leaders had lived through the Cultural Revolution and the Red Guard period, and many viewed street protest as a danger to be stopped early.

Institutional rules were ambiguous in ways that mattered. A confidential understanding at the Thirteenth Congress reportedly gave Deng the last word on major questions even though he held no formal Party office beyond his military post. Zhao disclosed this arrangement to Mikhail Gorbachev during the May 16 meeting, a statement that Deng’s circle regarded as an attempt to shift responsibility to him. Other important institutional questions concerned who could lawfully impose martial law and how, a matter taken up in Section 73.20.

Beijing’s municipal leadership also mattered. Beijing Party Secretary Li Ximing and Mayor Chen Xitong were responsible for city security and played a major part in reports to Deng and to the Standing Committee that interpreted the protests as aimed at the Party’s rule. Historians disagree about how much local reporting influenced national decisions, and Chen Xitong later said he had played a smaller role than commonly assumed. What the evidence does show is that the leadership was operating under pressure, with imperfect information and rival interpretations of what the students wanted.

Section 73.5

Hu Yaobang’s Death and the Beginning of the 1989 Movement

Hu Yaobang was born in Hunan in 1915 and joined the Communist movement as a teenager, serving on the Long March and later leading the Communist Youth League. After the Cultural Revolution he directed the rehabilitation of many officials and intellectuals who had been persecuted, became Party chairman and then General Secretary, and was known for informality, openness to young people, and tolerance of debate. Intellectuals and students admired him; some senior leaders criticized him for permitting what they called bourgeois liberalization. In January 1987, after student demonstrations in several cities, he was forced to resign as General Secretary after a Party meeting at which he was criticized, though he retained a seat on the Politburo.

On April 8, 1989, Hu suffered a heart attack during a Politburo meeting on education and was hospitalized. He died on April 15. The official announcement praised his career, and the Party announced that a state funeral would be held. For many people the news brought together mixed feelings: genuine regret for a leader associated with openness, sympathy for a man who had been removed, and anger that others at the top seemed uninterested in continuing his approach. Mourning offered a legitimate vehicle for expression that was difficult for authorities to prohibit without appearing disrespectful to a man the Party had honored.

Within hours, posters appeared at Peking University and other campuses, mourning the death and often making critical comments about contemporary leaders. Wreaths and elegiac couplets were laid at the Monument to the People’s Heroes in Tiananmen Square. On April 17, students from Beijing’s University of Political Science and Law and others marched to the Square; by April 18, thousands were gathering and some began to circulate demands. The movement of 1989 can thus be dated to the mourning gatherings of April 15–18. Its early participants were mostly students and teachers, and their immediate aims were to honor Hu, to reassess his record, and to press the authorities for changes in policy.

Historians emphasize that nothing about these gatherings made a nationwide crisis inevitable. Funeral-related gatherings had occurred before, notably in April 1976 after the death of Zhou Enlai (Chapter 70), when mourning at the Square developed into a protest that authorities suppressed. This precedent was in the minds of both students, who referred to 1976, and leaders, who remembered its political consequences. What turned mourning into something larger was the sequence of events over the following weeks, to which the next division turns.

Division II — Mourning, Student Organization, and the April DemonstrationsApril 15 – 30
Section 73.6

The First Mourning Gatherings and Student Petitions

During April 16–19, mourning gatherings grew in Tiananmen Square and spread to universities across Beijing. Students formed marching contingents, carried banners and wreaths, and chanted slogans that moved from praise of Hu Yaobang to criticism of officials. Many marchers called for democracy and for an end to corruption; others emphasized remembrance. The crowds were generally peaceful and attracted onlookers, and Chinese media coverage was initially restrained but not hostile.

Students began drafting petitions. The best-known list, which circulated in several versions, called on the government to reassess Hu Yaobang’s record, to repudiate the campaigns against “spiritual pollution” and “bourgeois liberalization,” to publish information on leaders’ incomes and property, to permit privately run newspapers and freedom of the press, to increase education funding and raise intellectuals’ pay, to lift restrictions on demonstrations in Beijing, and to report objectively on the students’ activities. Students sought a hearing for these demands through a formal petition and by seeking dialogue with senior officials. The list reflects how the movement combined specific, practical complaints with broad political ones.

On the night of April 17–18, several thousand students staged a sit-in outside the Great Hall of the People, while others went to Xinhuamen, the entrance to Zhongnanhai, seat of the Party and State Council leadership, and demanded to speak with leaders. Officials did not meet them. On April 19–20, security forces cleared protesters from Xinhuamen with force; some students were struck and reports of injuries, which were disputed by the authorities, spread rapidly. This incident deepened student anger and persuaded many that the leadership would not respond through normal channels.

The early petitions illustrate something important about the movement: its first demands were not for the overthrow of the Party. They were requests that the authorities listen, correct official errors, and allow limited freedoms. Whether the authorities could have defused the situation by accepting dialogue at this stage is a central question for historians, and one that is addressed in the Historical Debate in Section 73.19. What is clear is that the lack of any acceptable channel for conversation was an important source of escalation.

Section 73.7

Hu Yaobang’s State Funeral and the April 22 Demonstrations

The state funeral of Hu Yaobang was held on the morning of April 22 in the Great Hall of the People. Zhao Ziyang delivered the eulogy, and the ceremony followed standard Party protocol. Outside, roughly 100,000 students and others had gathered in Tiananmen Square overnight and in the early morning, many having marched from universities in the dark. The Square was closed to the public for the ceremony, but the students had arrived before the cordon was complete. They listened to the funeral broadcast through loudspeakers, observed moments of silence, and chanted slogans.

At the end of the ceremony, three students knelt on the steps of the Great Hall holding a petition and asked to deliver it to Premier Li Peng. They were Guo Haifeng, Zhang Zhiyong, and Zhou Yongjun, who knelt according to traditional gestures of petition. No official came out to receive them. The students, and many witnesses, interpreted this as a refusal to listen and as an insult to the idea that citizens could address the state. The image of the kneeling petitioners, with its echoes of imperial-era supplication, became a powerful symbol.

The consequences were immediate. Students began calling for a boycott of classes across Beijing, and other cities saw large demonstrations. In Xi’an and Changsha on April 22, crowds attacked and burned vehicles and looted shops in violent disturbances that students’ groups did not organize. Government spokespeople and media later cited these events as evidence that the protests had been captured by hooligans or criminals, while student leaders argued that the violence was not theirs. The difference between peaceful protest and urban riot is therefore one of the critical distinctions in the evidence.

The April 22 events show how a ceremony designed to end mourning instead became the beginning of organized protest. The state’s ritual was successful in staging the funeral, but it had no way of acknowledging the students’ demand for a hearing. Evidence of the day includes eyewitness accounts, photographs, and contemporary reports; the number in the Square is estimated, not counted, and varies by source. The petition delivered or not delivered that morning has since been analyzed in many accounts, including those based on student recollections and the notes of observers.

Section 73.8

Autonomous Student Organizations and Their Political Demands

On April 23, students from Beijing universities formed the Beijing Students’ Autonomous Federation (Beijing gaoxiao xuesheng zizhi lianhehui), an organization independent of the official student unions controlled by the Party-affiliated All-China Students’ Federation. Zhou Yongjun, one of the kneeling petitioners, was elected its first chairman. Other early figures included Wang Dan of Peking University and Wuer Kaixi of Beijing Normal University; leadership roles shifted frequently, and individuals who became prominent in one week might be sidelined in the next. The Federation organized the class boycott and coordinated marches.

The creation of an autonomous organization was itself political. The Party’s system depended on organizations that it controlled; independent student groups, even if they declared loyalty to the Party and the constitution, challenged that system by claiming the right to represent students without authorization. Students insisted that the Federation was patriotic and legal, and many leaders stressed they were asking the government to fulfill its own promises. Authorities regarded unauthorized organizations as illegal and, as the movement grew, as potential instruments of anti-Party forces.

Representing a large, shifting group proved difficult. Students at different universities had different priorities, traditions, and views of risk. Peking University students tended to emphasize political discussion, while students at institutions with less prestige often concentrated on conditions and fairness. Leaders were selected through meetings rather than formal elections, and the large number of participants made unified decisions hard. Communication relied on leaflets, loudspeakers, bicycle messengers, and (for the first time in a Chinese movement) fax machines and international telephone links to overseas supporters.

The student organizations also developed internal tensions between moderates, who sought negotiation and incremental concessions, and radicals, who wanted an uncompromising stance. Historians have emphasized that these divisions were not simply between “good” and “bad” leaders; they reflected different judgments about what the government would accept and what the movement could sustain. As the movement continued through May, the problem of leadership and representation grew, and many scholars regard it as one of the factors that made compromise difficult.

Section 73.9

The April 26 People’s Daily Editorial and the Definition of Political Turmoil

The Party leadership’s first major collective response came while Zhao Ziyang was abroad. Zhao left Beijing on April 23 for a state visit to North Korea. On April 24, Premier Li Peng chaired a meeting of the Standing Committee at which Beijing officials reported that student organizations were challenging Party leadership, and on April 25 Li Peng and Yang Shangkun briefed Deng Xiaoping, who agreed with the characterization of the protests as a serious political threat and said firmly that a clear stand against “turmoil” was needed. The text of his remarks was circulated and became the basis for a front-page People’s Daily editorial published on April 26.

The editorial, titled “Take a Clear-Cut Stand Against Disturbances,” described the protests as a carefully planned conspiracy by a small number of people who wished to negate the leadership of the Party and the socialist system. It distinguished between sincere students mourning Hu Yaobang, who should be treated with patience, and unnamed “extremely small number” of persons with ulterior motives, but it condemned the class boycott and demonstrations. Its language revived terms used during the Cultural Revolution, and its audience was both the public and the Party apparatus, to whom it signaled the line to follow.

Zhao’s role is a subject of historical discussion. According to his later recollections, he was informed while in Pyongyang and expressed general support for the Standing Committee’s determination to oppose disorder, but he did not see the editorial’s wording in advance and came to believe it had made the situation much worse. When he returned on April 30, he tried to adjust the line. Because Zhao’s account was given later and under house arrest, it must be read with attention to its context; the editorial’s text itself, however, is firm evidence of the government’s public position.

Student reaction was one of anger, and the editorial affected the nature of the movement. Many students felt that a patriotic effort to support reform had been labeled as a hostile conspiracy. The demand that the editorial be retracted, and that the movement be described as patriotic and democratic, became central. Ordinary people who had previously been uninterested in politics reacted to what they saw as a heavy-handed official response. For the leadership, however, backing down would risk appearing to concede that the Party’s judgment could be overturned by street demonstrations.

Section 73.10

The April 27 Demonstration and the Expansion of Public Support

The students’ response to the editorial was a large demonstration on April 27. Several tens of thousands of students, by some estimates well over 100,000, marched from the university district toward the center of Beijing, defying a ban on demonstrations. Authorities placed police cordons across major roads, intending to block the march. In many places the cordons were overwhelmed or opened without violence. The marchers kept discipline, carried banners proclaiming support for the Party and constitution, and used slogans that distinguished their patriotism from the editorial’s charges.

What made April 27 historically important was the reaction of onlookers. Along the route, thousands of Beijing residents applauded the marchers, handed out food and water, and in some cases joined. Many ordinary people who had not participated before expressed approval of the students’ stand. Some police and officials showed visible sympathy. This public response indicated that the movement’s concerns, and the sense that the editorial was unjust, extended beyond the campuses. Journalists who observed these scenes reported the support to editors.

For the leadership, the April 27 march was ambiguous. On one hand, the march ended peacefully, with no major confrontation. On the other, the failure of police cordons demonstrated the limits of the leadership’s ability to prevent demonstrations without using force, and the public sympathy suggested that the editorial had not discouraged participation. Zhao Ziyang, who returned from North Korea on April 30, argued that the movement’s peaceful behavior and wide support showed that the editorial’s characterization should be softened. Others interpreted the strength of the demonstration as evidence of organized opposition.

The April 27 march also expanded the movement’s social geography. Workers, residents, and employees of state agencies watched, and some joined in later marches. Students from Beijing’s universities were joined by contingents from other cities. The cumulative effect was to move the movement from a campus-based protest to a broader public movement within the capital, setting the stage for the May mobilization.

Section 73.11

Dialogue, Negotiation, and Disagreements Over Protest Strategy

On April 29, government officials held a televised meeting with student representatives, the first official dialogue. State Council spokesman Yuan Mu and other officials, including Beijing’s Party Secretary Li Ximing, met students chosen largely from official student bodies rather than the Autonomous Federation. The government regarded the event as a gesture of openness and a means of explaining its position; many students regarded the participants as unrepresentative and the discussion as lacking real concessions. The Federation declined to recognize the meeting as legitimate dialogue.

When Zhao Ziyang returned on April 30, he made conciliatory statements. On May 3, in a speech marking the anniversary of the May Fourth Movement, he praised the students’ patriotism and said that the movement was not aimed at the system itself. His speech reflected his belief that the protests could be resolved through democracy and law, that the government should respond to demands for anticorruption measures and transparency, and that “turmoil” was not an accurate description. But his position at the top of the Party was not secure, and he could not simply reverse the editorial without Deng Xiaoping’s consent.

Within the student movement, disagreement continued. Some leaders argued that the students had made their point and should return to classes while continuing to press for dialogue; others insisted that withdrawal would be defeat, because the government had not retracted the editorial and no concrete reforms had been promised. The class boycott had been under way since April 23, and its length created exhaustion and doubts. Preparations for the seventieth anniversary of the May Fourth Movement forced a decision about whether to hold a large march, which was widely supported, and what to do afterward.

These debates illustrate that the movement in late April was not a unified body with a single strategy. Students operated with imperfect information about the leadership’s internal discussions and without effective institutions to negotiate. The leadership, similarly, had no mechanism to engage with a movement that had no recognized representative. The lack of channels for compromise, rather than any single decision, became one of the structural conditions for later escalation.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate — What Were the Movement’s Principal Demands?

The question: Scholars and participants disagree about what the movement wanted. Was it chiefly a movement for democracy and political freedom, a movement against corruption and privilege, a patriotic movement for dialogue with the Party, or something more complex?

Interpretation A: The movement was fundamentally about democracy and political rights. Proponents point to slogans calling for democracy and freedom of the press, to the creation of autonomous organizations that challenged Party control, to the later erection of the Goddess of Democracy statue, and to speeches by some leaders advocating political pluralism. They argue that anti-corruption demands cannot be separated from demands for accountability.
Interpretation B: The movement was a loyal, reformist protest focused on dialogue, transparency, and anti-corruption, and many participants sought improvement within the existing system. Proponents point to the petitions’ requests, banners affirming support for the Party and constitution, the emphasis on patriotic language and the legacy of May Fourth, and workers’ and residents’ concerns about prices and privilege. They argue that democracy meant different things to different participants.

Evidence: Evidence includes petitions, leaflets, speeches, slogans, interviews, and documentary film; each captures only part of a diverse movement. Statements by organizers changed over time, and different sources emphasize different groups. Official documents represent the demands in ways that served political purposes. Historians such as Craig Calhoun and Jeremy Brown have stressed the movement’s variety and the interplay of demands among students, workers, and others.

Why it matters: The answer affects how historians explain both the movement and the government’s response, and whether the leadership’s judgment that the movement threatened the Party was an accurate reading or an interpretation with consequences. It also affects how the events are remembered.

Division III — The May Movement and Nationwide MobilizationMay 4 – 19
Section 73.12

The May Fourth Anniversary and the Renewal of Student Protest

May 4, 1989, was the seventieth anniversary of the demonstrations of 1919 in which Beijing students protested the decisions of the Paris Peace Conference concerning Shandong (Chapter 60). In official memory the May Fourth Movement was a patriotic and progressive event that prepared the way for Communist politics, and each year the Party commemorated it, usually through ceremonies for youth. In 1989 students in Beijing seized the anniversary as a symbolic opportunity to claim that they were the heirs of the movement, and the government found itself in the awkward position of honoring a tradition of student activism while trying to contain a present one.

On May 4 students from dozens of universities, in numbers estimated at around 100,000, marched through Beijing to Tiananmen Square, some carrying banners with May Fourth slogans on science and democracy and calling for a free press. Contingents of journalists, who had asked for permission to participate, also marched, and on May 9 about a thousand journalists petitioned the official journalists’ association for press freedom. Students at the Square read statements and then, after discussion, the Autonomous Federation announced that most students would return to classes on May 5, while stating that the movement would continue through other means.

On the same day Zhao Ziyang told delegates of the Asian Development Bank, who were meeting in Beijing, that the situation was calm and that China would not experience major upheaval. He repeated that the students’ demands for fighting corruption and promoting democracy were consistent with the Party’s own goals. His remarks, reported in the press, were widely interpreted as indicating a softer approach than the editorial of April 26, and they were later criticized by Deng Xiaoping and others as contradicting the Party’s position.

May Fourth also shows how nationalism and reform were intertwined. Students described their actions as patriotic, appealed to a tradition of national salvation, and used the symbols of the Party’s own history. This linking of patriotism and protest made it difficult for the government to portray the movement as simply hostile to China, and it made students’ demands appear legitimate to many observers. At the same time, it did not remove the fundamental disagreement over who had the authority to define patriotism.

Section 73.13

Divisions Within the Student Movement

After May 4, the student movement faced the problem of how to proceed. The class boycott had lasted for weeks, momentum was declining at some campuses, and the government had not retracted the editorial. Students were divided between those who wanted to wind down the boycott and pursue dialogue and those who thought the movement needed a dramatic step. A new group formed, the Dialogue Delegation, whose representatives sought talks with officials, while leaders of the Autonomous Federation continued to coordinate marches. Conflicts developed over who spoke for students.

The group of students responsible for the next step were largely graduate students and other young activists who had been less prominent earlier. Among them was Chai Ling, a graduate student at Beijing Normal University, and others, who proposed a hunger strike to dramatize the demand for dialogue and for retraction of the April 26 editorial, particularly in advance of Gorbachev’s visit. Wang Dan and some other leaders had reservations about the strategy because of the risk to participants and the reduction of room for negotiation, but they ultimately supported students who proceeded.

Scholars have noted several recurring divisions. One concerned means: whether to continue public demonstrations or negotiate quietly. Another concerned ends: whether to seek limited reform or a more thorough change in the political system. A third concerned organization: whether leaders should be chosen by consensus, by campus delegations, or by those who held positions in the Square. A fourth concerned the movement’s relation to other social groups, whom some students wanted to welcome and others feared would bring chaos or disrupt discipline.

Evidence for these divisions comes from interviews, memoirs, and documentary films produced after the events; some of these sources were created in exile or were influenced by the participants’ later disputes. Historians therefore treat accounts of motives and quarrels with caution. A key point is that the movement’s decisions were often improvised, and that the choice of tactics sometimes narrowed the space for compromise on both sides.

Section 73.14

The May 13 Hunger Strike and the Transformation of the Movement

On May 13, about 300 students began a hunger strike in Tiananmen Square. They announced two principal demands: that the government hold a dialogue on equal terms with student representatives, with live broadcast, and that the government recognize the movement as patriotic and democratic, thereby repudiating the label of “turmoil.” The hunger strikers wore headbands, sat in the Square, and accepted only water. Within days their numbers grew into the thousands as additional students joined.

The hunger strike changed the character of the movement. The students’ willingness to risk their health attracted sympathy from people who had not supported demonstrations. Ambulances and medical staff arrived, and large numbers of students fainted in the heat and were taken to hospitals. Residents brought food, water, and umbrellas, collected donations, and organized aid. The movement acquired a moral force that was harder for the authorities to dismiss, and some officials urged the leadership to respond. Yan Mingfu, head of the Party’s United Front Work Department, met with intellectuals and student representatives on May 14 and urged a compromise, but the students insisted on conditions the leadership was unwilling to meet.

The hunger strike also created pressure within the movement. Students who had begun the strike felt committed to continue, and those who wished to negotiate worried about the risk of deaths. Logistics (shelter, sanitation, medical care, and security) required organization, and the movement’s management became more centralized in the hands of those in the Square. Chinese authorities struggled to respond: forcible removal would be dangerous, but allowing the strike to continue as Gorbachev arrived was embarrassing.

Historians are cautious about numbers. Estimates of hunger strikers range from hundreds to several thousand, depending on the day and the definition of participation, and reports of those hospitalized vary. The documents of the time, including the declaration below, show the students’ emotional appeals, which emphasized their patriotism and their wish for dialogue. They also show how the hunger strike used moral language to describe the relationship between students and government.

Section 73.15

Gorbachev’s Visit and the Internationalization of the Crisis

Mikhail Gorbachev, General Secretary of the Soviet Communist Party, arrived in Beijing on May 15 for a state visit, the first by a Soviet leader since the Sino-Soviet split of the late 1950s and early 1960s (Chapter 69). The visit was intended to mark the normalization of relations between the two Communist powers. Beijing had planned a formal welcome ceremony in Tiananmen Square, but the occupation by students forced the authorities to move it to the airport. Subsequent official events had to be rearranged, and Gorbachev was brought into the Great Hall of the People by side entrances.

Gorbachev met Deng Xiaoping on May 16, in a meeting that formally ended three decades of estrangement. Zhao Ziyang met Gorbachev the same day and, in a statement that was broadcast, told him that major questions in China were still referred to Deng, a remark intended to inform the visitor of the formal position but which Deng’s circle regarded as an attempt to blame him for the crisis. The visit concluded on May 18, and Gorbachev left China after traveling to Shanghai.

More than a thousand foreign journalists had come to cover the summit, and many stayed to report on the demonstrations. Satellite broadcasts carried images of the hunger strikers and the crowds to audiences abroad, and Chinese people learned of events from foreign radio broadcasts such as the Voice of America and the BBC. The movement became a global media event, which affected how students behaved (many were conscious of the international audience) and how the leadership perceived its situation, as the visit was both a diplomatic success and a source of embarrassment.

The visit also gave the movement symbolic resources. Gorbachev was an advocate of political opening in the Soviet Union, and some students and onlookers welcomed him as a figure of reform. Chinese leaders, in contrast, viewed Soviet political changes with concern. The coincidence of the movement and the visit illustrates how international and domestic politics intersected: the world was watching, and that visibility sharpened the leadership’s sense that the crisis was a test of its authority.

Section 73.16

Workers, Journalists, Residents, and the Broadening of Participation

Between May 13 and May 18 participation in the movement widened sharply. On May 17 and again on May 18, demonstrations in Beijing involved perhaps a million people at their peak, according to widely cited estimates, including students, workers, teachers, government employees, and residents. Marchers carried banners identifying their work units: universities, factories, research institutes, hospitals, and offices. Staff of the People’s Daily, Xinhua, Central Television, and other media, who had been told to report in line with the editorial, marched in support of the hunger strikers and press freedom.

Workers joined in distinctive ways. Some marched as members of their enterprises, and in mid-to-late May workers established the Beijing Workers’ Autonomous Federation, whose organizers included young workers such as the railway electrician Han Dongfang. Their demands overlapped with students’ in some respects, including opposition to official profiteering and calls for press freedom, but also included issues of wages, prices, job security, and the right to form independent unions. The government was acutely concerned by the idea that students and workers might unite, in part because of the experience of the Polish union Solidarity.

Residents played an extraordinary role. They supplied the Square with food and water, provided transportation, and organized volunteer groups. Motorcyclists, called “Flying Tigers,” carried messages and supplies. Neighborhood residents formed brigades to maintain order and to protect students. Many ordinary Beijingers, who had no prior involvement in politics, were moved by the hunger strike and by their sense of grievance about prices and privilege. Their participation changed the movement from one of students to one of the city.

Not all participants shared the same aims. Journalists focused on freedom of the press; workers on livelihood and representation; residents on fairness and sympathy; officials who marched on a limited basis sometimes sought a way to express reservations. Understanding these differences prevents the error of treating the movement as a single bloc. It also helps to explain the later difficulty of repressing it: many people had participated, and the authorities had to decide how to treat different groups.

Section 73.17

Demonstrations in Shanghai, Nanjing, Chengdu, and Other Cities

The movement was not limited to Beijing. Demonstrations occurred in Shanghai, Nanjing, Wuhan, Xi’an, Chengdu, Guangzhou, Hangzhou, Changsha, Harbin, Lanzhou, and in hundreds of other cities and towns, according to scholarly counts. In many cities students marched to local government buildings, organized sit-ins, and sent representatives to Beijing. On May 17–19, the largest demonstrations in provincial capitals brought out hundreds of thousands, with banners expressing support for the hunger strikers in Beijing.

Local circumstances shaped the movement. In Shanghai, where students and residents had a long tradition of political activity, local authorities under Party Secretary Jiang Zemin dismissed Qin Benli, editor of the World Economic Herald, in late April and early May after the paper tried to publish material about Hu Yaobang; the dismissal was seen as an attack on press freedom. In Xi’an and Changsha, violent disturbances in April had given authorities a basis to link protest with disorder. In Chengdu, large crowds assembled in the central square and other districts; in Wuhan, protesters blocked the Yangtze River bridge and rail traffic. Cities had their own grievances about wages, prices, and municipal officials.

Many provincial leaders adopted cautious or conciliatory approaches, seeking to avoid confrontations while awaiting directions from Beijing. Others took measures to limit demonstrations. Because local media were controlled differently, news of events in Beijing reached residents through a mixture of official reports, foreign broadcasts, telephone calls, and word of mouth. The speed with which a student movement in the capital produced echoes across the country shows how extensively information traveled, and how widely the grievances were shared.

The national scope matters because it undermines the idea that 1989 was merely a Beijing event or a student quarrel. It also shaped the leadership’s perception: the spread of protests to many cities increased fears that the Party’s authority was at risk nationwide. Conversely, the unevenness of protest, which was stronger in large cities with universities than in rural areas, reminds us that the movement was an urban phenomenon and that most rural people had limited involvement.

Regional and Frontier Context

Beyond the Capital: Provincial Capitals and Tibet in 1989

Regional variation shaped both protest and repression. Large coastal and inland cities such as Shanghai, Guangzhou, Wuhan, Xi’an, and Chengdu, with universities and substantial urban workforces, saw the largest demonstrations. Smaller towns and rural counties saw few. Local officials had varying degrees of autonomy in responding, a variation that would become important after June 4.

Tibet was in a different situation. Unrest in Lhasa during early March 1989, connected to the thirtieth anniversary of the 1959 uprising and long-standing grievances over religion and governance, led authorities to declare martial law in the city on March 8; it remained in force until May 1990. This was a separate episode with its own causes, but it gave the leadership recent experience with martial law and shaped perceptions of its use. Section 73.18

Failed Negotiations and Zhao Ziyang’s Final Appeal

As the hunger strike continued, senior officials arranged meetings with student representatives. On May 18 Premier Li Peng, with other officials, met a delegation of student leaders at the Great Hall of the People in a session broadcast on television. Wuer Kaixi, who had been taken to a hospital after fasting, attended wearing hospital clothing and interrupted Li Peng, saying that the discussion should address the question of whether the students would end their strike only with concrete assurances. The televised meeting made evident the distance between the two sides: Li Peng spoke of the need for order and for the students to leave the Square, while the students pressed for recognition of the movement as patriotic and for dialogue on equal terms.

Within the leadership, the question had been decided by the time of this meeting. On May 17, in a meeting at Deng Xiaoping’s home, Deng supported the use of martial law, and Zhao Ziyang, according to his later account, said he could not carry out such a policy. Zhao’s position was now isolated. At around 5 a.m. on May 19 he went to Tiananmen Square, accompanied by Wen Jiabao, then head of the Party Central Committee’s General Office. Using a megaphone, he spoke briefly to students, said that he and others had come too late, and urged them to end the hunger strike and preserve their health, adding that the questions raised would be resolved over time. It was his final public appearance as General Secretary.

Some students and others were moved by Zhao’s visit. Later that day, student leaders announced an end to the hunger strike, while continuing the occupation of the Square, and many hunger strikers were evacuated to hospitals. But the visit had no effect on the decision already reached. On the night of May 19, Li Peng and Yang Shangkun addressed a meeting of Party, government, and army cadres in the Great Hall; Zhao did not appear, and the speeches announced that decisive measures against the “turmoil” were forthcoming.

Historians treat Zhao’s final appeal as a pivotal moment because it shows both the existence of a leadership alternative and its political weakness. It remains disputed whether a different approach by either side might have avoided confrontation, a question discussed in Section 73.19. What is not disputed is that after May 19 the leadership prepared to use force, and that Zhao was removed from decision-making.

Division IV — Leadership Divisions, Martial Law, and Political DeadlockMay 17 – June 3
Section 73.19

Deng Xiaoping, Zhao Ziyang, Li Peng, and the Leadership Crisis

The decision to use force was made by a very small group, and the evidence about their discussions is incomplete. The most important sources are Zhao Ziyang’s recollections (published as Prisoner of the State in 2009, from recordings he made while under house arrest), the diary-based memoir attributed to Li Peng, official statements, accounts by officials who later left China, and the contested Tiananmen Papers (Section 73.40). Each has a viewpoint and a purpose, and none permits a fully verified reconstruction. What can be said with confidence is the outline: Zhao opposed martial law, Li Peng and several senior figures supported it, and Deng Xiaoping made the decisive judgment.

The leaders’ reasoning reflected different readings of the movement’s significance. Zhao regarded the protests as an expression of grievances that could be addressed through dialogue, anticorruption measures, and a moderate retraction of the editorial. He believed that force would damage the Party and the reforms. Li Peng and others believed that the movement’s organization, its challenge to the Party’s authority, and its mass character threatened social order and the Party’s survival. Deng, who remembered the Cultural Revolution and valued stability for economic development, concluded that tolerating the occupation of the capital would invite further disorder, and that the Party could not tolerate groups claiming independent legitimacy.

Party elders played a significant role. Senior figures such as Chen Yun, Li Xiannian, Peng Zhen, and Wang Zhen pressed for firm action, and some had urged action earlier. The institutions through which they exercised influence were informal, which is one reason the process is difficult to document. Meetings were held at Deng’s residence rather than in formal Politburo sessions, and their legitimacy rested on seniority and revolutionary credentials. This pattern reveals the limits of institutionalization in the political system: formal offices did not always determine real authority.

Other leaders took positions that mattered. Qiao Shi and Hu Qili, according to Zhao’s recollection, opposed martial law within the Standing Committee, though their later influence was limited; Hu Qili was removed in June, while Qiao Shi retained his position. Yao Yilin supported Li Peng. Military leaders, including Yang Shangkun and Defense Minister Qin Jiwei, implemented the decision, although some retired generals reportedly sent letters urging that the army not be used against the people. The complexity of these positions warns against describing the leadership as a unified bloc or against attributing the crackdown to a single individual.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate — Political Alternatives Before Martial Law

The question: Could the confrontation have been resolved without the use of military force? Scholars disagree about whether realistic alternatives existed in late April or May 1989, and about who bore the main responsibility for their absence.

Interpretation A: A negotiated settlement was possible and was forestalled by hardliners and by Deng’s decisions. Proponents point to Zhao Ziyang’s attempts to modify the editorial’s language, to the students’ stated willingness to talk, to the breadth of public support, and to the possibility of concessions on press freedom or anticorruption measures. They argue that the April 26 editorial and the refusal of dialogue closed options and that the leadership chose confrontation.
Interpretation B: The conflict was structurally difficult to resolve, and opportunities for compromise were limited on both sides. Proponents note that the leadership viewed independent organizations as a threat to Party rule, that the students’ demands for recognition and equal-footed dialogue were difficult to accept, that divisions among student leaders prevented binding agreements, and that the movement’s growth made negotiation harder. Some also point to the influence of events elsewhere in the communist world.

Evidence: Evidence includes the leadership’s public documents, memoirs and recollections, diplomatic reports, and student leaflets and interviews. Because internal Party records are unavailable, many claims about intentions rest on later testimony. Counterfactual claims cannot be proven, only judged for plausibility.

Why it matters: The question affects how historians assign responsibility and how they interpret the nature of the political system. It shows that “what could have happened” depends on assumptions about institutions, individuals, and the information available at the time.

Section 73.20

The Decision to Impose Martial Law, May 17–20

On May 17, after a day in which perhaps a million people marched in Beijing, Deng Xiaoping convened the Standing Committee members and senior figures at his home. According to Zhao’s later account, Deng determined that martial law should be imposed and troops brought to Beijing, and that Zhao’s dissent did not change the outcome. The Standing Committee was divided, with Zhao and, by his recollection, Hu Qili and Qiao Shi opposed. On May 18 and 19, Li Peng and others took steps to prepare, and on the night of May 19 Li Peng and Yang Shangkun gave speeches announcing that the situation required firm measures.

On May 20, at 10 a.m., martial law took effect in designated urban and suburban districts of Beijing under an order issued by Premier Li Peng in the name of the State Council. The order cited the “turmoil” that had disrupted social order and prohibited demonstrations, strikes, and class boycotts in the affected area; authorized the use of measures by security forces; and restricted foreign journalists’ activities. Chinese state television satellite feeds that had carried foreign broadcasts were cut off. Martial law troops were assigned to enforce the order; they included units from the Beijing Military Region and, later, other regions.

The legality of the decision was disputed. Under the 1982 constitution, the State Council could declare martial law in parts of provinces, autonomous regions, and municipalities, while the NPC Standing Committee could decide on martial law in whole provinces and autonomous regions. Critics argued that the declaration in the capital did not follow proper procedure, and some members of the NPC Standing Committee, including Hu Jiwei, attempted to call an emergency session to examine the matter. The effort failed. Supporters of the measure said that the State Council had power to impose martial law in specific districts. These disputes show that legal arguments were an arena of political conflict.

It is important to distinguish martial law from the military operation of June 3–4. Martial law was an administrative and political measure announced on May 20; the use of lethal force on the night of June 3–4 was a subsequent decision about how to enforce it. Between those dates, two weeks passed in which the army was blocked by residents, the leadership debated its options, and troops were reorganized. Also distinct is Zhao Ziyang’s formal dismissal, which occurred only at the Fourth Plenum on June 23–24; between May 19 and then he was politically sidelined but formally still General Secretary.

Section 73.21

Beijing Residents and the First Efforts to Block Military Entry

The troops that began moving toward the center of Beijing on May 19–20 did not reach it. Within hours, Beijing residents learned of the order through radio and word of mouth, and tens of thousands poured into the streets. At intersections on the outskirts, residents formed human barriers and used buses and trucks to block roads. Convoys of army trucks, carrying soldiers who in many cases had not been told their destination, were stopped. In some places soldiers were surrounded by crowds who talked, offered food and water, and urged them not to fire.

Accounts describe scenes of confusion. Soldiers, many of them young conscripts from other provinces, sat in vehicles for hours while civilians explained the movement and appealed to their sense of loyalty. Some soldiers wept; some officers refused to answer; some units turned back. Reports indicate that many troops were ordered not to use weapons and in some cases had no ammunition, although accounts vary. Students and residents emphasized that they loved the army, a phrase echoing the Communist tradition of the army as the people’s army, which contributed to the troops’ hesitancy.

The standoff lasted several days. By around May 22–24, army units had been unable to advance into the center, and many pulled back to bases on the outskirts. The temporary success of the roadblocks gave participants a sense of victory and demonstrated popular opposition to martial law. It also had a consequence not anticipated by many: it persuaded some leaders that the army’s authority was under challenge and that a stronger operation would be necessary, with troops carrying live ammunition and clear orders.

The role of residents in this phase also shows the scale of urban participation. Elderly people, workers, shopkeepers, and children took part, and communities built networks of information. These events have been recalled in memoirs and oral histories as moments of civic solidarity. Historians also note that the movement’s success in blocking the army created illusions about the possibility of avoiding a more forceful response, and that those who took part had little understanding of the military preparations under way.

Section 73.22

The Continuing Occupation of Tiananmen Square

After martial law was announced, students continued to occupy the Square, but their numbers fluctuated. Many hunger strikers went home or to hospitals, and students from other cities arrived in large numbers, often by train, bringing new energy and, according to some accounts, different views. The Square developed a distinctive social order, with tents, loudspeakers, food supplies, first-aid stations, and banners. Donations, including funds from Hong Kong supporters, financed tents and equipment.

Internal disagreement intensified. A group of student leaders organized the “Defend Tiananmen Square” headquarters, with Chai Ling as commander, which tended to favor continued occupation until demands were met. Other leaders, including Wang Dan and Wuer Kaixi, argued at various points that students should withdraw while they could claim a moral victory. On May 27, leaders agreed to leave the Square on May 30, but the decision was reversed amid disputes, and the occupation continued. Accounts differ as to who was responsible and whether the reversal reflected democratic debate or the pressure of the most determined participants.

The Workers’ Autonomous Federation, which maintained its own presence near the Monument to the People’s Heroes, called for strikes and worker support, though with limited effect. On May 30 art students from the Central Academy of Fine Arts erected a ten-meter statue, the Goddess of Democracy, facing the portrait of Mao on the Gate of Heavenly Peace. The statue, of plaster and foam, was both an artwork and a symbol that provoked official anger; Chinese state media denounced it as an insult. On June 2, four intellectuals, Liu Xiaobo, Hou Dejian, Zhou Duo, and Gao Xin, began a short hunger strike and offered to mediate and to encourage students to avoid violence.

By June 2, the number of students in the Square had declined, but the possibility of a confrontation had grown. Students feared that the army would return; some leaders appealed for nonviolence while others urged resistance. The state, meanwhile, was assembling forces. The final days of the occupation illustrate the difficulty of managing a decentralized movement in a context where a rapid, forceful response by the state was probable, and where participants had limited information about what the army would do.

Section 73.23

Military Preparations, Command Authority, and the Crisis of Obedience

The People’s Liberation Army in 1989 was a professional but politically controlled force. It reported to the Party’s Central Military Commission, chaired by Deng Xiaoping, with Yang Shangkun as vice-chair and Qin Jiwei and Chi Haotian in senior military posts. Its basic organization consisted of group armies assigned to military regions. Because the army’s role in domestic politics was ambiguous, with its history in the Party’s revolution and its relationship to the people emphasized in doctrine, orders to use force against civilians raised questions of loyalty and professional ethics.

The documented signs of reluctance are significant. Xu Qinxian, commander of the 38th Group Army, a unit stationed near Beijing, refused to carry out orders, reportedly on grounds that he could not lead troops against civilians without a written order from the Central Military Commission and a clear legal basis. He was removed, court-martialed, and imprisoned. A letter signed by several retired senior military figures, including former defense minister Zhang Aiping, urged the leadership not to send the army against the people, according to reports. Evidence of unwillingness among lower ranks is less certain and comes largely from anecdotal accounts.

To ensure compliance, the leadership brought units from several military regions, some of which had not been heavily exposed to the demonstrations. Estimates of the total number of troops assembled around Beijing vary widely, with many sources suggesting well over 100,000 soldiers and some indicating around 200,000. Troops were housed in public buildings and tunnels in and around the capital in the days before the operation, and received political education emphasizing that they were facing a counterrevolutionary rebellion. The government’s changing plans, from an initial attempt to enter the city peacefully to the use of live ammunition and armored vehicles, reflected the failure of earlier efforts.

The “crisis of obedience” should be interpreted with care. The evidence shows that there was individual and institutional hesitation, but not that the army as a whole refused orders. In the end, the leadership found units willing to carry out the operation. This outcome says something about the strength of the Party’s control over the army and the role of discipline, ideology, and the isolation of troops from civilian sentiment. It also illustrates a general point about the relationship between state coercion and popular support: the failure of one effort did not prevent a more forceful second effort.

Cause and Effect

Military Intervention and Civilian Resistance

Causes

Long-term: The Party’s control of the army; leaders’ memories of the Cultural Revolution and of 1976; the absence of legal channels for dissent; economic grievances.

Short-term: The April 26 editorial; the hunger strike during Gorbachev’s visit; Zhao’s isolation; the failure of the first martial law deployment.

Immediate: The loss of civilian life on June 3–4, occupation of central Beijing by the army, mass arrests.

Long-term: Reassertion of Party control; changes in leadership; sanctions; lasting debates over responsibility and memory.

The use of force was not the automatic outcome of a conflict between “reformers” and “conservatives.” It resulted from decisions by particular leaders, perceptions of threat, institutional structures, the dynamics of mass protest, and the failure of communication. Section 73.24

The Final Days Before the Crackdown

In the last days of May and the first days of June, the situation on the ground appeared to be at a stalemate. Troops had gathered on the outskirts of the city, while students and workers remained in the Square. The Standing Committee, reorganized without Zhao, held meetings with senior figures, and Deng Xiaoping met military leaders and the martial law command. Public statements from officials emphasized that order would be restored, and warnings were broadcast through the media. On May 30, the Goddess of Democracy statue was installed; and on June 1–2, plans for the clearing of the Square were reportedly finalized.

A series of incidents on June 1–3 raised tension. On June 1, a People’s Armed Police vehicle struck civilians and killed three cyclists, according to a later U.S. Army historical reconstruction. On June 2, an unarmed military column attempted to enter the city near the scene of the collision but was stopped by crowds, including mourners for those killed; other unarmed or lightly equipped personnel encountered resistance elsewhere. These confrontations increased official determination and fed the sense of imminent military action. On June 3, police used tear gas near Xinhuamen against crowds, and in the late afternoon and evening state media broadcast urgent notices. The collision and subsequent troop movements must not be conflated into an incident occurring entirely on June 2.

At around 6:30 p.m. on June 3, radio and television carried an “emergency notice” from the martial law headquarters and Beijing municipal authorities, warning citizens to stay indoors and keep away from the Square and areas where troops were operating, and stating that the army would carry out its duties. Residents who heard the broadcast responded in different ways: some went home, others gathered in the streets to try to stop the army. The warnings were also heard by students in the Square, many of whom debated whether to leave.

The evidence concerning final orders is limited. The decision to clear the Square by force and the rules governing the use of weapons have been discussed in memoirs and in contested texts, but official Chinese records remain closed. Historians can establish that senior leaders were aware of the plans and responsible for approving the operation, and that army units entered the city on the evening of June 3 with weapons and armored vehicles. How specific orders were framed, what rules of engagement were provided, and how commanders interpreted them are matters on which the evidence is incomplete.

Division V — The Military Crackdown of June 3–4, 1989The night of June 3 – June 5
Section 73.25

Military Mobilization and the Movement into Beijing, June 3

The operation of June 3–4 was a multi-directional military advance toward the center of the capital. Units that had been assembled in the suburbs and in compounds within the city moved toward Tiananmen Square along several routes. The largest and most deadly axis was the broad east–west boulevard known as Chang’an Avenue, along which troops advanced from the west through Muxidi, Fuxingmen, Xidan, and the area around the Great Hall of the People. Other columns approached from the north, the south, and the east, including through Jianguomen, in order to encircle the Square. The forces included infantry in trucks and on foot, armored personnel carriers, and tanks.

Historians use diplomatic reports, eyewitness accounts, photographs, and film footage to reconstruct the chronology. Reports place the first widespread lethal shooting on the western approach in the late evening, around 10 p.m. to 11 p.m., as armored columns moved eastward and confronted crowds trying to block them. Over the next several hours troops reached Xidan and Liubukou, a few hundred meters from the Square, and by around 1 to 2 a.m. on June 4 the Square was substantially encircled. Because there were many separate engagements across a large city at night, no single account captures the entire operation, and times in witness accounts are approximate.

Accounts identify units from several group armies, including elements from outside the Beijing Military Region; reports differ on which units were involved in specific incidents, and historians are cautious about attributing particular shootings to named units without corroboration. What is established is that troops carried live ammunition, that they used assault rifles and machine guns as well as armored vehicles, and that they fired at crowds, including at unarmed civilians, as they advanced. The use of tanks and armored vehicles in an urban setting was extraordinary and indicates a decision to prioritize speed of occupation over avoiding casualties.

The June 3 notice broadcast to the public, warning residents to stay indoors, indicates that the authorities expected resistance. In the evening, many residents came out in response to rumors of troops’ movement and the sound of gunfire, seeking to block vehicles, help the wounded, or see what was happening. This meant that a large number of unarmed civilians were on the roads along the advance. The combination of an armored advance, an armed force with orders to proceed, and crowds in the streets produced the casualties of the night.

Section 73.26

The Use of Lethal Force on Beijing’s Western Approaches

The shootings in the western part of the city are among the most thoroughly documented parts of the night. Around Muxidi, a major intersection on Chang’an Avenue where residential compounds for officials and professionals stand along the road, crowds confronted an advancing column of armored vehicles and infantry. Residents tried to block the road with buses and barriers, shouted at soldiers, and in some cases threw objects. Eyewitnesses describe the troops opening fire on people on the avenue and on the sidewalks, including people standing at a distance. Bullets struck civilians in the street and, according to residents, also hit apartment buildings.

Among those killed was a seventeen-year-old high school student, Jiang Jielian, who, according to Amnesty International’s investigation, was shot around 11 p.m. near Muxidi. His mother, the philosophy professor Ding Zilin, would later become a leading figure in documenting the dead (Section 73.37). Other victims included workers, students, bystanders, and residents who came out to observe. Doctors and medical staff who treated the wounded reported gunshot injuries consistent with automatic weapons. The sequence of events, with deaths occurring before troops reached the Square, is central to understanding why the focus on the Square alone is misleading.

The troops did not advance uniformly. In some places, crowds persuaded soldiers to stop or forced vehicles to halt, and there were moments of confrontation in which soldiers and civilians shouted at each other. In others, the column moved quickly, firing as it went. The army’s approach from the west, with soldiers shooting at those in front of them, was not consistent with a controlled response to violent resistance, according to many witnesses, although the army’s own account emphasizes attacks on soldiers and vehicles (Section 73.29). Careful historians treat both bodies of evidence and note that they are not equivalent in scale.

Further east, troops continued toward Xidan, where there were additional clashes, and on to Liubukou and the area near the Great Hall of the People. Because the troops moved through densely populated areas at night, many residents who witnessed the events from windows or balconies later gave accounts, some of which were collected by journalists, diplomats, and later researchers. The overall pattern shows that the lethal force was applied across a wide geographic area and over many hours, not in a single concentrated engagement.

Section 73.27

Hospitals, Emergency Aid, and the Human Consequences of the Shooting

The injured were taken to hospitals through improvised means. Ambulances were scarce and sometimes blocked or fired on; rickshaw drivers, cyclists, and passers-by used tricycle carts and flatbed carriers to carry the wounded to nearby hospitals. Hospitals near the western approach, including Fuxing Hospital, and those in the center, such as Beijing Union Medical College Hospital and other major facilities, received large numbers of casualties during the night and the following day. Medical staff worked through the night, treating gunshot injuries and performing emergency operations, in conditions of shortage and confusion.

Medical personnel are among the most important witnesses, though they were put under pressure. Authorities sought to control information; hospitals were reportedly instructed not to release casualty figures or records and to prioritize military casualties. Some physicians kept notes or counts, and several later spoke or wrote about what they had seen. Jiang Yanyong, a military surgeon at a Beijing hospital who treated wounded, wrote a letter in 2004 calling for the government to reassess its verdict on June 4, and was detained for a period. The experiences of staff, who also faced personal danger and moral stress, form an important part of the history.

Counting the dead was difficult. In the chaos, many bodies were taken to hospitals or morgues, some were collected by relatives, and others were removed by authorities. Families often found it hard to locate relatives, and some bodies were cremated quickly. Medical and morgue evidence is partial, and published numbers by hospital staff and others reflect only the facilities where counts were available. These conditions help to explain why the death toll remains uncertain, a subject examined in Section 73.30.

The human consequences extended beyond the dead. Thousands of people were wounded, some with permanent disabilities, and families faced medical expenses, social pressures, and surveillance. Victims who survived were often afraid to seek treatment from official hospitals because of fear of arrest. In the weeks that followed, the authorities encouraged the public to report “rioters,” and those who had been in the streets were at risk. The long-term impact on victims and their families was profound and is part of the history of the period.

Everyday Life and Social History

Hospital Staff and Civilian Emergency Responders

Note: the account below is an illustrative reconstruction synthesizing patterns documented in interviews, memoirs, and scholarship. It does not describe a single named individual.

Imagine a nurse on night duty at a hospital near the western approach on June 3. Within an hour of the first shots, the emergency department had more patients than beds. Neighbors and strangers brought the injured on flatbed carts; many had gunshot wounds to the chest, abdomen, and limbs. Staff improvised, using corridors and waiting rooms, and sought blood and supplies. The nurse’s duties included recording names where patients could give them, though many were unconscious or unidentified.

Outside, cart drivers and cyclists continued to bring in the wounded, sometimes in danger. Residents offered to donate blood. Over the following days, staff faced questions from relatives searching for family members, and instructions from supervisors on what information could be shared. Some kept private notes. The experience was one of professional duty, fear, and, for many, lasting trauma, a reminder that the history of June 4 includes people who treated the injured, as well as those who were shot.

Section 73.28

The Clearing of Tiananmen Square Before Dawn on June 4

By the early hours of June 4, thousands of people remained in the Square, many of them students gathered around the Monument to the People’s Heroes. Troops had taken positions on the edges of the Square, and some soldiers were visible on the steps of the Great Hall and the Museum buildings. Loudspeakers broadcast an order to evacuate. Student leaders and others debated what to do, and in the early hours the four hunger-striking intellectuals, particularly Hou Dejian and Zhou Duo, sought to negotiate with military officers to arrange a peaceful withdrawal.

Negotiations with officers produced an agreement that students would be allowed to leave by the southeast corner of the Square. At the Monument, students held a vote by acclamation about whether to withdraw; many voted to leave, though some accounts say the result was contested, and some students wanted to remain. At around 4 a.m. the lights in the Square went out, and in the next half hour or so they came back on as troops advanced to the Monument. Shortly afterward, between roughly 5 and 6 a.m., the remaining students formed a column and left the Square under the observation of soldiers. The occupying troops then cleared the Square of tents and materials.

The most contested question is what happened inside the Square during the clearing. Some eyewitnesses, including Hou Dejian, later said that they had not seen mass shooting of students in the Square itself and that the final evacuation involved negotiation and movement. Other accounts describe beatings, shots fired, tanks crushing tents, and deaths at or near the Monument. Evidence is conflicting, and the existence of both types of testimony does not indicate that all testimony is equally reliable. What is clear is that violence had already occurred on the approaches, that troops cleared the Square by force of arms and presence, and that students were forced out.

This distinction affects how historians describe the event. The phrase “Tiananmen Square massacre,” widely used in English, has been criticized for suggesting that the principal killing occurred in the Square. The geographic evidence, discussed in the June 22 diplomatic cable (Section 73.40) and in later scholarship, indicates that many of the deaths occurred on roads leading to the Square. The distinction is not meant to diminish the killings; it is a matter of accuracy about where and how they occurred, and about the evidence available.

Historical Evidence

Historical Evidence — Eyewitness and Diplomatic Accounts of June 3–4

Several categories of evidence shed light on the night. Journalists who were in the Square, including foreign reporters and film crews, produced contemporary reports and footage. Diplomats and military attachés in Beijing, whose compounds overlooked parts of Chang’an Avenue and the diplomatic quarter at Jianguomenwai, wrote cables and reports. Participants, including students, residents, medical workers, and soldiers, gave later interviews and memoirs. Hospital records and photographs supplement these accounts.

Each has limits. Journalists could see only part of the city, and their ability to report was restricted by the martial law order. Diplomats relied partly on second-hand information and rumor. Participants’ memories are affected by trauma, time, and later political debates, and some accounts were shaped by the circumstances in which they were given (for example, interviews in exile or under official supervision). Historians therefore compare accounts across categories and look for convergence on specific points, such as the location of shootings, which emerge reliably in multiple independent sources.

Section 73.29

Street Confrontations, Civilian Resistance, and Military Casualties

The violence of the night was not one-sided in the sense that some civilians resisted and attacked soldiers and vehicles. In several locations, crowds threw bricks and bottles at advancing troops, set fire to buses and military vehicles used as barricades, and attacked soldiers who were separated from their units. Photographs and video footage, including images broadcast by Chinese state television, show burning armored personnel carriers and the bodies of soldiers. Some soldiers were killed, and others were seriously injured, and some were beaten to death by crowds enraged by the shootings.

The official account emphasized these incidents. Government statements asserted that “rioters” had attacked soldiers, burned vehicles, and seized weapons, and that the army had acted with restraint after taking heavy casualties. Official figures and lists were reported at different times: a July 3 People’s Daily account named ten soldiers posthumously honored, while Beijing Mayor Chen Xitong’s June 30 report asserted that several dozen soldiers and police had been killed and more than 6,000 wounded. These are different categories and cannot be treated as a single consistent count. Independent researchers have been unable to verify a complete military and police death toll; the deaths of some soldiers are nevertheless documented.

Historians treat this evidence carefully. The violence against soldiers is real and should be recorded; it also has context. Much of it occurred after troops had begun to fire on civilians, in the confusion and anger of the night. The existence of violence against soldiers does not establish that civilians had initiated lethal violence before the army’s advance, and it does not alter the scale or institutional character of the army’s use of lethal force. The army was a state organization acting under orders, and the soldiers’ deaths do not equate to the killing of unarmed civilians by troops.

Further confrontations occurred on June 4, including at Jianguomen on the east side, where troops fired on crowds, and near the diplomatic compounds on Jianguomenwai Avenue, where bullets struck residential buildings used by foreign diplomats and journalists. Barricades appeared in various streets, and residents continued to shout at troops. Their courage and anger are recorded, as are the pain and fear of conscript soldiers, some of whom were injured or killed. Presenting these facts without erasing the distinction between victims of state violence and members of the armed forces is a central task of responsible history.

Section 73.30

The Dead, the Missing, and the Problem of Casualty Estimates

The number of people killed on and after June 3, 1989 has never been established. Estimates come from several sources and are not directly comparable, because they refer to different areas (the Square, central Beijing, the whole city, the whole country), different time periods, and different categories (civilians, students, soldiers). The Chinese government, in a statement by State Council spokesman Yuan Mu on June 6, referred to a total of nearly 300 dead, including soldiers. On June 30, Beijing Mayor Chen Xitong told the NPC Standing Committee that more than 200 civilians, including 36 university students, had been killed and more than 3,000 civilians wounded; he also asserted that more than 6,000 soldiers and police were wounded and several dozen killed. These are figures from an official report whose methods and completeness cannot be independently verified.

Independent estimates are higher. Amnesty International, after investigating in 1989, concluded that the available evidence suggested at least 1,000 people had been killed. Other estimates, drawn from hospital sources, journalists, and diplomatic reports, range into the several thousands, and a cable attributed to the then British ambassador, declassified in 2017, reported a figure of about 10,000 on the basis of a secondhand source; historians treat that figure with great caution because of its provenance. A figure of 2,600 attributed to the Chinese Red Cross circulated early but was reportedly withdrawn, and its origin is disputed. The group Tiananmen Mothers has compiled a list of more than 200 victims identified by name and circumstances, which is a minimum, not an estimate of the total.

The reasons for uncertainty are structural. The state restricted reporting and did not conduct or publish an independent investigation; hospitals and morgues were pressured to withhold information; families often could not or would not come forward; bodies were cremated; and some casualties occurred in places where no one counted. Counting the missing is also difficult, as some people fled or hid for fear of arrest. Under these conditions, the evidence supports a range of estimates, and a single precise number cannot be responsibly asserted.

Students should keep two statements distinct. First, the total number of deaths is uncertain, and different estimates reflect different methods and definitions. Second, the use of lethal force by the army against unarmed civilians, causing deaths and injuries, is firmly documented by multiple independent sources, including the government’s own acknowledgment of more than 200 civilian deaths. Uncertainty about the total does not create uncertainty about whether soldiers fired on and killed civilians.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate — Reconstructing the Location and Scale of the Violence

The question: Where did most of the killing occur, and how many people died? Historians disagree about methods and about how to weigh different kinds of evidence.

Interpretation A: The death toll was likely in the high hundreds to low thousands. Supporters cite Amnesty International’s investigation, hospital and eyewitness testimony, lists of victims, and the pattern of shooting along multiple routes. They argue that official figures minimized deaths and that the lack of systematic official data does not justify very low estimates.
Interpretation B: The death toll cannot be responsibly narrowed beyond a wide range, and some very high estimates rest on weak or second-hand evidence. Supporters emphasize the provenance problems of figures such as 10,000 or 2,600, the difficulty of verifying rumors in a closed information environment, and the need to rely on documented lists and corroborated reports. Some also stress that official figures were issued under political pressure and cannot be treated as complete.

Evidence: Sources include the official statements of June 6 and June 30, Amnesty International’s report, a diplomatic cable of June 22, 1989 describing the military operation and the location of deaths, Tiananmen Mothers’ documentation, hospital testimony, and journalists’ reports. The sources differ in coverage and reliability. Geographic evidence suggests that many, and possibly most, deaths occurred on streets and approaches around the Square rather than in the Square itself.

Why it matters: The estimate affects public memory, political accountability, and the credibility of different narratives. It also illustrates how scholars deal with uncertainty: by distinguishing documented minimums, plausible ranges, and unverified claims.

Student task: Make a table listing three casualty figures, who produced each, the geographic and time scope, and the evidence on which it rests. Then write a paragraph explaining which claims about casualties can be asserted with confidence.

Section 73.31

June 4–5: Military Control, Continuing Violence, and the Tank Man Image

By the morning of June 4, the army had established control of Tiananmen Square and the central avenues. Troops and armored vehicles stood in the Square, and tanks and other vehicles patrolled Chang’an Avenue. The city was tense. During the day crowds gathered in various places, shouting at soldiers, and there were further shootings, including near diplomatic areas in the east. Many residents stayed indoors; others went to hospitals or searched for missing relatives. State radio and television announced that the army had put down a counterrevolutionary rebellion and restored order.

On June 5, a day after the main operation, a column of tanks moved east on Chang’an Avenue near the Beijing Hotel. A man in a white shirt, carrying what appeared to be shopping bags, stepped into the road in front of the lead tank. The tank attempted to go around him; he moved to block it again, and at one point climbed onto the turret to speak with someone inside. After a short time, people from the crowd pulled him away. Several photographers and a video crew recorded the scene from hotel balconies, and the photographs became among the most widely circulated images of the twentieth century.

The man’s identity and fate are unknown. Various names have been proposed, but none has been verified, and Chinese official responses to questions about him have been evasive or dismissive. The image is powerful, and historians treat it with a distinction: it is a documented event, recorded from several angles, but it does not by itself reveal what occurred on the night of June 3–4. The tanks were in the street on June 5, after the main violence; the image can be interpreted in different ways, including as an example of the military’s restraint at that moment, an interpretation that has been advanced by some officials and that other observers reject as unrepresentative of the earlier events.

The photograph’s fame illustrates how a single image can shape historical memory. For international audiences, it became a symbol of the confrontation; for many in China, particularly younger people, it has been less familiar because of censorship (Section 73.39). Students should learn to use photographs as evidence while recognizing their limits: they show a moment, from a vantage point, selected and circulated for reasons. They need to be corroborated by other sources.

Division VI — Political Repression, Leadership Changes, and International ConsequencesJune 1989 – 1991
Section 73.32

Arrests, Trials, Official Investigations, and the State’s Interpretation

In the days after June 4, the authorities began a nationwide campaign of investigation and arrest. State media broadcast images of burned vehicles and injured soldiers and denounced the “counterrevolutionary rebellion.” On June 13 the government issued a wanted list of twenty-one student leaders, headed by Wang Dan and Wuer Kaixi. Many students and workers were detained, and some, including Wang Dan (arrested in July), were later tried. Workers who had joined independent federations were treated with particular severity, and Han Dongfang was held for many months. Intellectuals associated with the movement, including Liu Xiaobo, were arrested.

Courts and security organs applied charges such as counterrevolutionary propaganda and incitement, arson, and disrupting traffic. Reports document that several people were executed in June 1989, including in Shanghai, where three men were sentenced to death for setting fire to a train that had struck protesters on June 6, and in other cities. Many others received long prison sentences. Official work units conducted investigations among employees, and Party members and officials were required to reflect on their conduct. Journalists who had supported the movement were disciplined, and publications such as the World Economic Herald were closed.

Meanwhile, some activists escaped abroad. Informal networks, including one that later became known in Hong Kong as “Operation Yellowbird,” helped student leaders and others leave China; Fang Lizhi, who had sought refuge in the American embassy on June 5, remained there until June 1990, when he was allowed to leave. The exile experience shaped later politics and memory (Section 73.38). The scale of repression is difficult to measure because the authorities did not publish comprehensive figures; estimates of those detained vary widely.

Students should distinguish official claims from established facts. The official narrative portrayed the movement as the work of a small group of conspirators supported by foreign forces, and described the army’s action as a necessary response to a counterrevolutionary rebellion. Independent evidence does not support the claim that the movement as a whole was a conspiracy; it shows a diverse movement with unplanned elements. At the same time, the government’s documents are primary evidence of its policies and rationales, and are essential to understand its decisions and its subsequent actions.

Primary Source Window

Paraphrase, not a quotation: The report described the spring’s events as organized turmoil culminating in a counterrevolutionary rebellion, led by a small number of people seeking to overthrow the Party and socialist system. It said troops had shown great restraint and been forced to act after heavy attacks, listed damaged vehicles and injured soldiers and police, reported that more than 200 civilians, including 36 students, had died and over 3,000 civilians been injured, and praised the martial law forces.

Analysis: The report is the principal primary source for the government’s position, its characterization of the movement, and its public casualty figures. It shows, for instance, that the government acknowledged more than 200 civilian deaths and a substantial number of injuries. Its limits are strong: it does not explain how the figures were derived, it attributes the violence to rioters while describing the army’s actions in terms of restraint, and it omits students’ and residents’ own accounts. It should be read alongside independent evidence, as the next sections do.

Section 73.33

Zhao Ziyang’s Removal and the Reorganization of Party Leadership

Zhao Ziyang’s removal was a formal Party procedure that took place weeks after the military operation. A Central Committee plenum, the Fourth Plenum of the Thirteenth Central Committee, met on June 23–24, 1989. It accepted a report charging Zhao with supporting turmoil and splitting the Party, relieved him of his posts as General Secretary and as member of the Standing Committee and Politburo, and removed Hu Qili from the Standing Committee. It appointed Jiang Zemin, the Party Secretary of Shanghai, as General Secretary, and added Song Ping and Li Ruihuan to the Standing Committee.

The choice of Jiang Zemin reflected a compromise acceptable to the elders. In Shanghai he had managed protests without a mass bloodbath while acting against the World Economic Herald, and he had a record of economic management. He lacked a power base in Beijing and depended on the support of Deng Xiaoping and senior figures. Deng, who resigned from the chairmanship of the Central Military Commission in November 1989, handed that position to Jiang, helping to secure the new leader’s authority over the army; Deng retained substantial informal influence for several years.

Zhao himself was kept under house arrest in Beijing, in a courtyard house, until his death in January 2005. He was never tried, and for years his name was largely absent from official media. In the 2000s he secretly recorded memories, which were published abroad after his death as Prisoner of the State. In them he defended his position and described what he regarded as the decisions of the leaders. As a source, the memoir is an account by a participant with obvious interests, and its recordings were made under constraint; it is nonetheless a major source for historians and is generally treated as authentic, though its details cannot always be verified.

The leadership change had consequences beyond personnel. It marked the end of the group of senior reformers who had emphasized political reform, and it brought into prominence leaders who prioritized stability, Party discipline, and ideological control. At the same time, the leadership did not abandon economic reform in principle. How it would proceed became a subject of dispute in the following two years, a story taken up in Chapter 74. Students should avoid assuming that the repression of June implied the end of the reform policy, or that it guaranteed its later resumption.

Section 73.34

Repression and Political Responses Beyond Beijing

Responses outside the capital varied. In several cities, news of the shootings in Beijing led to protests, strikes, and marches in the first days of June. Local governments responded in different ways, depending on the preferences of leaders and local conditions. The result was a patchwork of experiences, with some cities seeing deadly violence and others primarily detentions and investigations.

In Shanghai, students and residents mounted large demonstrations after June 4, and crowds blocked roads and railway tracks. Under Jiang Zemin and Mayor Zhu Rongji, the city leadership relied on persuasion, negotiation, and gradual clearing of barricades rather than massive force. On June 6, a train crashed into a crowd that had blocked the track at the Guangxin Road crossing, killing six people; angry crowds burned the train and attacked its crew, after which the authorities arrested those they identified, and three were later executed. Local officials emphasized the burning of the train as evidence that the city had been disrupted by criminals.

In Chengdu, according to research by Jeremy Brown, police and security forces violently dispersed demonstrators on June 4 and 5, and people died; the incident included clashes around the central square and subsequent searches of hotels and other places. Figures for deaths are disputed, but there is evidence of killing and serious injuries. Chengdu’s experience shows that violence was not confined to Beijing and that it could be carried out by police forces as well as the army. In other cities, including Xi’an, Nanjing, Wuhan, Guangzhou, and Lanzhou, protests ended through a mix of arrests, pressure on work units, and the departure of students.

These regional differences matter because they show that repression was not a single event but a set of local decisions within a national campaign. Leaders at the provincial and municipal levels made choices about methods, while the central authorities sent signals about how severely to treat activists. The variety also complicates attempts to estimate the human cost, since local records are inconsistent, and demonstrates the importance of local archives and testimony, many of which remain inaccessible.

Regional and Frontier Context

Many Cities, Different Experiences

Because the movement and its repression took place in hundreds of cities, generalizations drawn from Beijing alone are misleading. Coastal and inland provincial capitals had different political climates, and small cities experienced the events mainly through marches and rumors. Official responses ranged from negotiation to violence to quiet detention. Historians increasingly use local archives, newspapers, and memoirs to reconstruct these varied experiences, while recognizing that access remains limited.

Section 73.35

Economic Retrenchment and the Domestic Political Aftermath

The political crisis coincided with an economic downturn. The austerity program begun in the autumn of 1988 continued under Li Peng, with controls on credit and investment, restrictions on prices, and the closure or merger of some enterprises. Official data show that economic growth slowed sharply in 1989 and 1990, to roughly 4 percent in each year, far below earlier rates, and that urban unemployment and unsold inventories worried planners. Foreign investment and tourism declined for a time after June. Rural industry, which had been a source of employment, was constrained by credit limits.

Politically, the leadership emphasized unity, discipline, and ideological education. Universities introduced military training for new students, and political study sessions were required. Campaigns criticized “bourgeois liberalization,” and publications and individuals associated with the reform wing were disciplined. Officials who had sympathized with the movement faced investigation. The Party also announced anti-corruption measures, including limits on the commercial activities of leaders’ relatives, an acknowledgment of the importance of that grievance, though the effect of these measures was disputed.

Martial law remained in place in Beijing until January 11, 1990, when Premier Li Peng announced its end. The troops were withdrawn gradually and the leadership sought to normalize the city. The events in Eastern Europe in late 1989, including the opening of the Berlin Wall on November 9 and the fall of Communist governments, strengthened the conviction of many Chinese leaders that political relaxation risked the collapse of Party rule, and they cited these developments in arguing for vigilance. These lessons shaped policy in the 1990s.

It is important not to move too quickly into the next chapter’s story. The immediate aftermath was one of uncertainty about the direction of reform, with conservative officials emphasizing planning and some questioning the value of special economic zones and foreign capital. The Party’s final course would be determined by later developments, including Deng Xiaoping’s 1992 Southern Tour (Chapter 74). Students should also resist reading the 1990s back into 1989: the conditions that allowed renewed growth were not predetermined.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Official Political Institutions Before and After June 1989

What Changed

  • The top leadership was reorganized, with Jiang Zemin replacing Zhao Ziyang and the elders’ influence made visible
  • Political reform in the sense of separating Party and government was largely set aside, and ideological and security organs gained emphasis
  • Media, universities, and civil society organizations faced stricter supervision
  • The army’s role as an internal security force was reasserted and its political training intensified
  • Foreign relations were affected by sanctions and new diplomatic constraints

What Stayed the Same

  • Party rule, the Four Cardinal Principles, and the leading role of Deng Xiaoping as ultimate authority
  • The State Council, NPC, and army as formal institutions, with the Party directing them
  • The commitment to economic development and “reform and opening” as policy, even amid debate
  • The central place of stability as an objective for the leadership

Who experienced change most directly, and who continuity: Participants and supporters of the movement, and officials associated with reform, experienced change most directly, through arrest, dismissal, or exile. For many ordinary people, daily economic life continued with limited institutional change, though political discussion became more restricted.

Why the pattern matters: The pattern shows that a crisis can alter personnel and priorities without changing the basic structure of the political system, and that continuity in institutions can coexist with significant change in the conditions for political expression.

Section 73.36

International Reactions, Sanctions, Hong Kong, Taiwan, and the Chinese Diaspora

Foreign governments reacted quickly. On June 5, the United States announced the suspension of government-to-government sales and commercial exports of weapons to China and of military visits, and later suspended high-level official exchanges. The administration of President George H. W. Bush also gave protection to Fang Lizhi in the American embassy and extended the stay of Chinese students in the United States. It simultaneously sought to preserve the relationship: in July and again in December 1989, Bush sent national security adviser Brent Scowcroft and Deputy Secretary of State Lawrence Eagleburger to Beijing in secret and later public missions. Congress and the public debated whether to maintain trade and the policy of engagement.

Other states imposed different measures. The European Community, at the Madrid summit in late June, adopted an embargo on arms sales to China and suspended some high-level contacts; this embargo has remained a subject of debate. Leaders of the major industrial democracies, meeting in Paris in July, condemned the use of force and called for restrictions on international financial lending, and the World Bank and some other agencies postponed new loans. Japan, an important aid donor, suspended some yen loans but resumed them over the next year or two. Many governments, particularly in the developing world and in the Soviet bloc, were reserved or supportive of Beijing. The Soviet leadership, concerned by its own situation, made restrained statements.

In Hong Kong, then a British colony scheduled to return to Chinese sovereignty in 1997, the events had profound effect. Large rallies in May had drawn hundreds of thousands, and organizers estimated around a million people at the largest on May 21. After June 4 there was mourning, anger, and anxiety about the future. A new organization, the Hong Kong Alliance in Support of Patriotic Democratic Movements in China, organized annual vigils beginning in 1990 in Victoria Park. The crisis weakened confidence in the transfer of sovereignty, led to emigration by some residents, and influenced debates over political arrangements in Hong Kong (Chapter 76).

In Taiwan, where the Republic of China government was beginning a transition away from authoritarian rule, the events drew sympathy and large demonstrations. The government of the Republic of China condemned the crackdown, and the events contributed to debates about relations across the Taiwan Strait. Overseas Chinese communities, including students in North America, Europe, and Australia, organized protests and fundraising and formed new organizations. Some exiled activists later became prominent in diaspora politics. The movement thus produced a network of connections across the Chinese-speaking world.

China and the Wider World

International Diplomatic Responses

The reactions to June 1989 show that China’s international relations had changed during the reform decade. Because the reform economy depended on trade, investment, technology, and educational exchange with the United States, Japan, and Western Europe, sanctions mattered, and the leadership took them seriously. At the same time, many governments weighed these costs against strategic interests and commercial opportunity, and the practical effect of sanctions was limited and uneven.

Chinese diplomats also acted: they sought to explain the government’s position, to restore contacts, and to mobilize support from countries that had not criticized the action. These efforts reflect an active foreign policy, not simply passive reception of international pressure. Students should compare the distinct decisions of the United States, the European Community, Japan, and the Soviet Union rather than speaking of a single “international community,” and should note that diplomatic and economic relations continued in modified form.

Division VII — Historical Memory, Censorship, Evidence, and InterpretationFrom 1989 to the present
Section 73.37

Families of Victims, the Tiananmen Mothers, and the Search for Accountability

Among the most important participants in the long history of 1989 are the relatives of those who died. Ding Zilin, a professor of philosophy at People’s University, lost her seventeen-year-old son, Jiang Jielian, on the night of June 3. In the early 1990s she began to seek out other families, to record details of those who had died, and to collect testimony. She and other relatives, including Zhang Xianling, formed a network that became known as the Tiananmen Mothers, which compiled a list of victims with names, ages, occupations, and the circumstances of death where known.

The group’s work included open letters to the Chinese authorities calling for a full and public investigation, for the release of information, for compensation, and for those responsible to be held accountable. They also sought to publish the names of the dead and to gather evidence of the effect on families. Their documentation has identified more than two hundred people killed, and they have emphasized that the list is incomplete. The authorities responded with surveillance, restrictions on movement, and pressure on members; permissions to mourn publicly, particularly around the anniversary in early June, were refused or limited.

The Mothers’ work shows how memory and evidence intertwine. Their lists are among the few sources that name victims, and they have been used by researchers; they also reflect the limits of what families can document under restrictions. Many bereaved relatives faced stigma, loss of employment, and fear. Some accepted limited “humanitarian” financial assistance without an acknowledgment of responsibility, and others refused. Their demands, summarized as truth, compensation, and accountability, highlight questions about the relationship between the state and citizens in situations of mass violence.

For historians, the Mothers’ archive is both a source and a subject. It illustrates the problem of reconstructing a history when the main body of official evidence is unavailable, and it illustrates the role of ordinary people as historians. Their persistence over decades has been recognized internationally, though it is little known in mainland China. The account of 1989 would be incomplete without the families’ perspective, which places the personal losses at the center of a narrative often told in terms of politics and strategy.

Section 73.38

Exile, Overseas Commemoration, and the Geography of Historical Memory

The aftermath of 1989 created a diaspora of activists. Student leaders, intellectuals, and workers who left China, voluntarily or under pressure, settled in the United States, France, Britain, Taiwan, Hong Kong, and elsewhere. Some, such as Wuer Kaixi, Chai Ling, Wang Dan (released in 1998 and allowed to go to the United States), and Shen Tong, became public figures, while many lived quietly, working in universities, businesses, and nongovernmental organizations. Rowena Xiaoqing He, in Tiananmen Exiles, examines how these people dealt with exile, with disagreements about the movement, and with their effort to continue political work.

Exile politics were marked by division as well as solidarity. Activists disagreed about responsibility for decisions made in the Square, about strategy, and about relations with the Chinese government. Some wished to return and were refused entry, while others felt guilt about those left behind. Overseas Chinese communities also differed in their responses: some regarded the movement as a defining moment of political awakening, others as an episode they preferred to leave behind or considered distant from their lives.

Commemoration took place in several locations. In Hong Kong, annual vigils attracted tens of thousands, and organizers reported that the 2019 gathering drew a very large number; a museum dedicated to the events opened in 2012. After the introduction of the national security law in 2020, authorities banned the vigils on public-safety grounds, and in 2021 the Alliance disbanded and the museum closed, which means that Hong Kong’s role as a site of mass commemoration has been sharply reduced (Chapter 76). In Taiwan, North America, and Europe, memorials and exhibitions continue, and digital archives hold photographs, documents, and oral histories.

The geography of memory highlights a central feature of the history: events inside China are remembered most openly outside it. This asymmetry means that the available documentation depends on the work of exiles, overseas institutions, and international media. It also raises questions for historians about perspective, since diaspora narratives may differ from the memories of people who remained in China. Students should treat overseas commemoration as an essential source and as a historical phenomenon with its own contexts.

Section 73.39

Official Narratives, Censorship, and the Politics of Historical Silence

The Chinese government’s position has evolved in language but not in basic judgment. In 1989, official statements described a “counterrevolutionary rebellion”; later documents have used softer terms such as the “political disturbance” (zhengzhi fengbo) of the spring and summer of 1989. Officials have maintained that the decision to act was necessary to preserve stability and to enable the economic growth that followed. They have not conducted a public investigation and have declined to release archives. The government’s view is that the matter has been settled and that discussion is unnecessary.

Censorship operates in several ways. Mainland Chinese media do not discuss the events, textbooks give them little or no space, and internet platforms block or delete references. Terms such as “June Fourth” and the date “6/4” are sensitive; users have devised indirect expressions, which are in turn censored. Archives relating to the period remain closed, and researchers who study the topic inside China face constraints. Public commemorations are not permitted, and relatives and activists who attempt them risk detention.

The effect is a significant generational difference in knowledge. Journalist Louisa Lim, in The People’s Republic of Amnesia, reported that when she showed the photograph of the man before the tanks to students at several Beijing universities, most did not recognize it. Others know of the events through family, foreign travel, or tools that circumvent controls, and some regard official accounts as sufficient or are reluctant to discuss them. It would be a mistake to assume uniform ignorance or uniform belief: individuals within China have varied knowledge and attitudes, and historians have only limited data about them.

Historical silence is itself a topic of study. Scholars examine how states manage memory, how people respond to enforced silence, and how traces survive. In this case the contrast between the volume of evidence outside China and the restrictions inside it shapes the research. Students should read official statements as evidence of the state’s position, not as accounts of what happened, and should treat claims that the events are “well known” or “forgotten” skeptically unless supported by evidence.

Section 73.40

Sources, Archives, Eyewitnesses, and Conflicting Historical Accounts

Historians of 1989 use many kinds of sources. Contemporary newspapers and broadcasts, both Chinese and foreign, record public statements and events. Student leaflets, posters, and petitions provide participants’ voices; some are collected in volumes such as Han Minzhu’s Cries for Democracy. Official documents, such as the April 26 editorial, the martial law order, and the June 30 report, record the state’s positions. Diplomatic cables and intelligence reports, many declassified, such as those held in the National Security Archive, provide outside observers’ views. Memoirs by participants, including Zhao Ziyang and, from the opposing side, Li Peng, and oral histories by researchers such as Rowena He supply personal perspectives. Photographs, film, and documentaries, such as The Gate of Heavenly Peace (1995), preserve images, while medical evidence and victim lists document casualties.

Each category has its own limits. Contemporary journalism was constrained by martial law, and foreign correspondents could see only portions of events. Memoirs are retrospective and written with purposes. Declassified cables record what diplomats believed at the time, often based on hearsay. Official Chinese documents are propaganda as well as record. The absence of Chinese archives is the largest gap: the minutes of Politburo and Standing Committee meetings, military orders, and security files have not been released. This means that many claims about leadership discussions rest on memoirs and secondary evidence.

The scholarly works used by students illustrate different approaches. Timothy Brook’s Quelling the People examines the military operation and the experience of soldiers and civilians. Craig Calhoun’s Neither Gods nor Emperors analyzes the students’ movement in the context of Chinese intellectual traditions and of social movement theory. Jeremy Brown’s June Fourth shows the movement and its repression across Beijing and other cities, including the experiences of workers and local officials. Louisa Lim’s book addresses memory and censorship, and Rowena He’s study addresses exile. Reading them together shows how the choice of sources and questions shapes interpretations.

Students should therefore learn to ask of any source: who created it, when, for whom, what could the creator know, and what interests did they have? They should compare accounts to identify points of convergence and divergence, to separate what is established from what is claimed, and to identify what evidence is missing. This is not a counsel of skepticism that treats all claims as equal; it is a method for building confidence where evidence converges, and for acknowledging uncertainty where it does not.

Historical Debate

Historical Interpretation — The Tiananmen Papers and Documentary Authenticity

The question: In 2001 a book titled The Tiananmen Papers, compiled by a pseudonymous “Zhang Liang” and edited by scholars Andrew Nathan and Perry Link, presented what it described as Chinese leadership documents on the decision to use force. Is it reliable evidence of confidential discussions?

Interpretation A: The documents are likely authentic in substantial part. Supporters, including the editors, argued that the material was detailed, internally consistent, and consistent with other evidence, and that it provided insight into leaders’ deliberations that was otherwise unavailable. They note that the original materials could not be released to protect sources.
Interpretation B: The authenticity is unproven, and some portions appear reconstructed. Critics, including Alfred Chan in The China Quarterly (2004), argued that parts relied on public or semi-public sources, that the compiler’s commentary and style resembled popular political narratives, and that there were discrepancies between versions. Bao Pu, publisher of Zhao Ziyang’s memoir, also raised questions about authenticity.

Evidence: Because the editors never possessed the original documents and the compiler remained anonymous, independent verification is not possible, and the dispute has persisted. The Chinese government has denounced the book as fabricated.

Why it matters: The book has influenced popular understanding of the leadership’s decisions; if its content is treated as verified transcripts, mistakes could enter historical accounts. Using it responsibly is a test of source criticism.

Student task: Write a brief note explaining how you would use a passage from The Tiananmen Papers in an essay: what you would claim, what corroboration you would seek, and how you would qualify your statement.

Section 73.41

Historical Interpretations and the Long-Term Significance of 1989

Scholars interpret 1989 in several ways. One approach emphasizes elite politics: the crisis resulted from decisions by a few leaders, especially Deng Xiaoping, and from divisions among them. Another emphasizes social movement dynamics: the movement’s organization, leadership disputes, and its relationship to a changing society explain how mobilization developed. A third emphasizes social history: workers, residents, and local officials, rather than students alone, shaped the movement and its repression. A fourth emphasizes the army and state violence, asking how the military was organized and how its commanders and soldiers experienced the operation. These approaches are complementary, and each draws on different evidence.

Debates concern the movement’s aims and strategy. Some scholars argue that student leaders’ insistence on uncompromising demands and on occupying the Square reduced room for settlement; others reply that the leadership’s hostility and refusal to engage meant that any strategy would have been rejected, and that the expectation of compromise from protesters facing a regime with a record of repression is unrealistic. Disagreements also exist about the extent to which protesters sought systemic change and about the weight of nationalism and the legacy of May Fourth in the movement’s language.

The long-term significance of 1989 is also disputed. Some argue that the suppression of the movement and the economic growth that followed formed a kind of implicit bargain in which political participation was limited in exchange for prosperity; others contend that this description oversimplifies a complex period and underestimates continuing protest, local activism, and state adaptation. Some historians emphasize that the leadership learned from Eastern Europe and the Soviet Union and developed new methods of control, including patriotic education, a more sophisticated media apparatus, and attention to social unrest. Others emphasize the continuing importance of memory as a source of political and moral claims.

Students should be cautious about retrospective interpretation. It is tempting to treat 1989 as the beginning of a predetermined path, but the actual consequences depended on later decisions, including the 1992 Southern Tour and the market reforms of the 1990s (Chapter 74). Events in 1989 do not by themselves explain later developments, and counterfactual claims about what might have happened if the movement had succeeded cannot be tested. The task of the historian is to explain what happened, why, with what evidence, and with what uncertainties, and to present the interpretations fairly.

Section 73.42

Chapter Review and 1989 Crisis Checkpoint

This chapter has reconstructed the protest movement of 1989 as a sequence of connected phases rather than a single event. It began with grievances arising from the reform decade: inflation and official profiteering, disputes about political reform, and the restrictions on expression that coexisted with greater openness. Hu Yaobang’s death on April 15 turned mourning into protest; the funeral of April 22, the formation of autonomous student organizations, and the April 26 editorial created a confrontation about who had the right to define the movement. In May, the hunger strike, Gorbachev’s visit, and the participation of workers, journalists, and residents transformed a campus protest into a mass movement across the country, while the leadership moved toward martial law. In the first days of June, the army advanced into Beijing and used lethal force against civilians, principally on the roads leading to Tiananmen Square. The consequences included repression, leadership change, sanctions, economic retrenchment, and a continuing struggle over memory.

Students should be able to explain the relationships among these developments, to distinguish martial law (May 20), the military operation (June 3–4), and Zhao Ziyang’s removal (June 23–24), and to state clearly what is established, what is estimated, and what is unknown. Established: the movement’s chronology, its diverse participation, the leadership’s decision to impose martial law, the army’s use of lethal force against civilians, the leadership changes of June, and international sanctions. Estimated: the number of participants at each stage and the number of dead, for which only ranges and minimums are supportable. Unknown: the full content of leadership deliberations, the precise orders given to units, and the identity of the man who stood before the tanks.

Chapter Summary

The protest movement of 1989 arose from the contradictions of the first decade of reform and opening. Economic growth and a more open intellectual life coexisted with inflation, official profiteering, and a political system that offered few channels for expressing grievance. Disagreements within the leadership over the pace of market reform and the limits of political change added to the tension.

Hu Yaobang’s death on April 15, 1989 turned private regret into public gatherings. Students petitioned the government, formed autonomous organizations, and boycotted classes after the apparent rejection of their petition on April 22. The People’s Daily editorial of April 26, which characterized the movement as turmoil, shifted the dispute from the specific demands of students to the question of the movement’s legitimacy and drew new participants.

In May the movement widened. The anniversary of May Fourth, the hunger strike that began on May 13, and Gorbachev’s visit brought public sympathy and international attention. Workers formed their own organizations with their own demands; journalists, officials, teachers, and residents joined marches that included perhaps a million people in Beijing; and demonstrations occurred in hundreds of cities. The movement was diverse and its leaders were divided over strategy.

The leadership was also divided. Zhao Ziyang favored dialogue and a conciliatory interpretation; Li Peng and others, and ultimately Deng Xiaoping, concluded that the movement threatened the Party’s authority. The decision of May 17–20 to impose martial law reflected the influence of senior leaders operating through informal channels. The first deployment of troops failed when residents blocked convoys, and the army withdrew from the center.

On the night of June 3–4, troops advanced on Beijing from several directions with live ammunition and armored vehicles. They fired on civilians, especially on Chang’an Avenue and its western approaches, killing and wounding many, and later cleared Tiananmen Square after negotiations led to a withdrawal of students. Some soldiers were also killed by crowds. The death toll is uncertain, with estimates ranging from official claims of more than 200 civilian dead to independent estimates of at least 1,000 or more.

The consequences were wide. Arrests and trials followed; Zhao Ziyang was dismissed and kept under house arrest; Jiang Zemin became General Secretary; the economy went through retrenchment; and the Party strengthened ideological and security controls. Regional experiences differed, with deadly violence in Chengdu and a different approach in Shanghai.

Internationally, the United States, the European Community, and other governments imposed various sanctions while keeping relations in modified form. Hong Kong, Taiwan, and diaspora communities mobilized in support of the movement, and exile networks formed. The reactions show the interdependence between China’s reform and its external relationships.

The history of 1989 is also a history of memory. The Chinese government has maintained its judgment, limited discussion, and kept archives closed. Families of victims, led by the Tiananmen Mothers, have documented the dead and demanded accountability. Overseas communities have sustained commemoration, though Hong Kong’s vigils were banned after 2020.

Historians use a wide range of sources, with the absence of internal Chinese archives the largest limitation. Source criticism is necessary for official reports, memoirs, diplomatic cables, photographs, and contested works such as The Tiananmen Papers. Interpretations differ on the movement’s aims, the avoidability of the crackdown, and its significance.

Chapter 73 thus contributes to the history of Chinese civilization by showing how a society negotiating rapid economic change confronted questions of political voice, state authority, and historical truth. It does not determine the later course of reform, which Chapter 74 examines, but it shaped the political and international conditions in which that course developed.

Chapter Reflection

Chapter 73 matters because it shows how the reform era’s successes and tensions came to a decisive test. The same decade that raised living standards and widened intellectual life also produced inequality, uncertainty, and demands for accountability. The events of 1989 revealed how far the political system would go to preserve its structure, and they shaped the leadership’s approach to stability, ideology, and economic policy.

The chapter also connects to earlier themes of the textbook: the relationship between rulers and educated elites (Chapters 11 and 41), student patriotism from the May Fourth Movement (Chapter 60), the role of the army in politics (Chapters 61–65), and the cycle of mobilization and campaign in the Mao era (Chapters 66–70). It does not show a repetition of the past, but it illustrates how historical memory and institutional experience shaped choices.

Internationally, the chapter shows China as an actor within a changing world: the Soviet summit, the revolutions in Eastern Europe, the sanctions of Western governments, and the networks of Hong Kong, Taiwan, and the diaspora. China’s reform economy depended on global connections, and those connections shaped both the crisis and the response.

Finally, the chapter helps students see Chinese history as diverse, contested, and unfinished. The events of 1989 are remembered differently by participants, officials, families, exiles, and people who were not yet born, and the sources available to historians remain incomplete. Understanding this history requires attention to evidence and its limits, respect for those who suffered, and care to distinguish what is known from what is claimed.

Contemporary documents and archival records

April 26, 1989 People’s Daily editorial
English translation, based on Foreign Broadcast Information Service reporting: It Is Necessary to Take a Clear-Cut Stand Against Disturbances. The original document's accusation of turmoil is a political claim, not an independent description of the demonstrations.
May 13, 1989 student Hunger Strike Declaration
Columbia University, Asia for Educators, The May 13 Hunger Strike Declaration. The English document can be used to check the movement's explicitly stated demands and rhetoric.
Zhao Ziyang's May 19, 1989 appeal
Chinese contemporaneous press reproduction, People’s Daily, May 19, 1989, page one. The chapter's paraphrase should be compared with this published Chinese transcript or a clearly identified translation.
May 20, 1989 martial law order
State Council order signed by Li Peng, and the distinct Beijing municipal enforcement order, reproduced in People’s Daily, May 20, 1989, page one. The municipal order specifies prohibitions; the national State Council order establishes martial law from 10 a.m.
Chen Xitong's June 30, 1989 government report
Contemporaneous official claim of more than 200 civilians killed, 36 of them students, and several dozen soldiers and police killed. Compare the U.S. Department of State Country Report on Human Rights Practices for 1989 with the original Chinese report. These are disputed official totals, not an independently established death count.
June 22, 1989 U.S. Embassy cable and eyewitness reports
National Security Archive, Tiananmen Square, 1989: The Declassified History, Documents 10–31, especially Document 31, What Happened on the Night of June 3/4? The archive preserves both early and subsequent accounts and their changing assessments.
June 1 cyclist collision and June 2 troop approach
U.S. Army Intelligence Center of Excellence historian, Military Attachés Witness Tiananmen Massacre (2023); distinguishes the collision of June 1 from the attempted troop movement of June 2.
