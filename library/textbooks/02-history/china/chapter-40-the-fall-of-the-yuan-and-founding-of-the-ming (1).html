Part VII — Mongol, Yuan, and Ming China

The Fall of the Yuan and Founding of the Ming

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

The Ming dynasty was not born simply because the Yuan collapsed. It emerged from a quarter-century of civil war in which flood, fiscal breakdown, religious rebellion, regional militarization, elite recruitment, and administrative reconstruction acted on one another.

In 1344, in the Huai River country of what is now Anhui province, a teenage farm boy named Zhu Chongba lost his father and brothers within days, in a season of drought, locusts, and disease. He had almost nothing to bury them with. Penniless, he entered a Buddhist monastery to be fed, and when the monastery could no longer feed him he took to the roads as a wandering mendicant. Twenty-four years later, under the name Zhu Yuanzhang, he proclaimed a new dynasty in the southern city of Nanjing and took the reign title Hongwu. His life is a thread through this chapter, but it is not the whole story. He rose within a vast field of rebels, local commanders, religious leaders, landlord militias, and regional regimes, most of which he outlasted rather than inherited.

This chapter studies the transition from the late Yuan crisis to the early Ming state. Chapter 39 explained how Yuan rule worked as both a Chinese imperial government and part of the wider Mongol Eurasian world, and why it entered crisis. Chapter 40 shows how that crisis became rebellion, how rebellion became competing regional states, and how one of those states became a dynasty. Chapter 41 then explains how the resulting Ming political and social system functioned in detail. Ming institutions therefore appear here only as responses to the experience of the transition, not as a finished system.

The period matters because it is one of the clearest examples in Chinese history of a dynastic transition that cannot be explained by a moral story about a corrupt last ruler. The Yellow River broke its dikes in the 1340s; the government tried to repair it by drafting laborers on an enormous scale while its currency lost value; sectarian networks with Buddhist, Maitreyan, and related expectations supplied organization to people in distress; and regional commanders, whether rebels or officially appointed, built private armies. Out of that environment, Zhu Yuanzhang built a more disciplined and more administratively capable regime than his rivals. Understanding why requires attention to rivers and lakes, grain and salt, ships and sieges, scribes and advisers, as well as to emperors and generals.

Much changed. A Mongol-led imperial court lost control of the Yellow and Yangtze valleys. A new dynasty arose from the south, and its rhetoric was shaped by rebellion and by the claim to expel the Mongols and restore the Chinese lands. Much continued: hereditary household categories, fiscal obligations such as labor service, the vocabulary and structure of Chinese bureaucratic government, the importance of the Grand Canal and the Yangtze, and the political weight of Mongol power on the northern frontier, where the Yuan ruling house survived as the Northern Yuan. The Ming did not simply replace the Yuan; it absorbed some of its institutions and many of its people.

The people affected were many: peasants conscripted to repair dikes and dig channels; refugees from flooded districts; salt-yard workers and boatmen; Mongol, Central Asian, and Han soldiers and officials who had to decide whom to serve; Buddhist, Daoist, and millenarian believers; scholars choosing between loyalty and opportunity; women who ran farms when men were drafted; and the households later registered, resettled, and bound to service in the new order. Their experiences differed enormously by region: the Huai valley suffered flood and famine, the lower Yangtze was wealthy and heavily taxed, Yunnan remained under Yuan loyalists into the 1380s, and the steppe remained a Mongol political world.

Evidence for the period is abundant but shaped by the winners. The Yuan shi (History of the Yuan) was compiled quickly under the early Ming, and the Ming Taizu shilu (Veritable Records of the Taizu Emperor) was revised more than once, so both tell the story in ways that served the new dynasty's claim to legitimacy. Proclamations, local gazetteers, epitaphs, literati writings, registration records, inscriptions, and archaeological work on Nanjing's walls provide checks, though each has limits. Throughout this chapter, students should ask who wrote each source, when, and why.

Finally, the chapter connects China to wider history. The collapse of Mongol rule in China was part of the breakup of a continental empire, and the Ming's relations with the Northern Yuan, Korea, Japan, and maritime Asia took shape in its aftermath. The story of 1368 is therefore at once a regional civil war, a dynastic founding, and a moment in Eurasian history.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  2. Analyze the Red Turban rebellions as a set of diverse northern and southern movements rather than a single organization.
  3. Trace Zhu Yuanzhang's progression from impoverished orphan to rebel recruit, commander, regional ruler, and emperor.
  4. Evaluate why Zhu's regime defeated rival regional powers such as those of Chen Youliang, Zhang Shicheng, and Fang Guozhen.
  5. Distinguish the loss of Yuan rule in the Central Plains from the survival of the Yuan ruling house and court.
  6. Assess the claim that the Ming was a "Han restoration" against evidence of both restorationist rhetoric and institutional continuity.
  7. Describe how reconstruction affected different households, regions, and social groups, including peasants, soldiers, artisans, women, and Jiangnan elites.

  10. Connect the Yuan-Ming transition to Mongol Eurasia, the northern frontier, and later Ming history.

Historical Context

The Yuan dynasty (1271–1368) governed an enormous space. After the Mongol conquests described in Chapter 38, the Yuan court ruled the former Jin territories of the north, the former Southern Song territories of the south, and the wider regions of Mongolia, Tibet, Yunnan, and the northeast, while acknowledging, with varying degrees of real influence, its place within the broader Mongol world. Its capital, Dadu (modern Beijing), lay in the north, while the greatest concentrations of grain, textiles, and commercial wealth lay in the lower Yangtze region. The Grand Canal and coastal shipping connected them, so that the court's food supply depended on routes vulnerable to floods and local disorder.

Yuan government combined steppe and Chinese practices. It used a hierarchy of social categories (discussed in Chapter 39) that favored Mongols and many people classified as semu, a heterogeneous administrative category that included numerous Central and West Asian groups, in appointments, while retaining Chinese-style ministries and provincial secretariats. It relied on hereditary households assigned to military, artisan, or other service, on a paper currency that began under silver convertibility but, by the fourteenth century, functioned increasingly as fiat money and was vulnerable to overissue, on monopolies such as that of salt, and on tax and labor obligations that varied by region. Examinations, restored by order in 1313 and held again from 1314–15, produced relatively few degree-holders, so many officials entered through other routes, and southern literati often felt excluded.

The geography of the transition matters. The Huai River and lower Yellow River plain, an area of flood, poverty, and frequent migration, was the cradle of the earliest Red Turban risings. The Yangtze corridor, from Sichuan through the middle Yangtze lakes to the lower Yangtze delta, was a connected zone of rivers, ports, and agricultural wealth where fleets and grain supply decided wars. The Jiangnan delta, around Suzhou and Hangzhou, was densely populated and commercial. The North China Plain, devastated by repeated floods and war, became the main target of later resettlement. Yunnan in the southwest and the Mongolian steppe in the north were distinct political worlds, linked to the Yuan court by dynastic ties and military settlement.

Students should also avoid reading the modern People's Republic back into this period. "China" in 1350 did not mean a single national state; the Yuan court presented itself as ruler of an empire that included but was not limited to the Chinese lands, and the rebels who replaced it used various languages of legitimacy, including Song restoration, millenarian renewal, and later the restoration of Zhonghua. Boundaries, such as those of Yunnan or the northern frontier, shifted during the transition and cannot be mapped onto modern provinces. The Ming that emerged was a new state with its own territorial claims, and its relationship to the Mongol world remained unresolved.

Finally, the larger background includes the weakening of other parts of the Mongol world in the same decades, the disruption of long-distance trade routes, and a period of climatic and epidemic stress in Eurasia. How closely these were linked to events in China remains debated, and this chapter treats them as context rather than as a single cause.

Section 40.1

Crisis in the Late Yuan

By the 1340s the Yuan state faced several overlapping problems. Succession disputes at court produced factional fighting and rapid turnover of ministers. The treasury relied on paper notes, salt monopoly revenue, and grain taxes, each under strain. Regional officials and military commanders gained autonomy. Hereditary military households were often impoverished. None of these problems alone ended the dynasty; their interaction left the government unable to respond to local emergencies. Historians therefore avoid explaining the fall as "decline" or moral decay and instead trace how fiscal and administrative weakness reduced the state's ability to handle shocks.

Court politics illustrate the problem. After the 1320s, struggles among imperial clans, Mongol and Central Asian ministers, and powerful chancellors such as Bayan and Toqto'a produced policy swings: purges, reversals, and short-lived reforms. Local administration depended on a thin layer of officials, many of them recruited by appointment, with relatively few examination graduates, and on hereditary households whose obligations were fixed regardless of changing circumstances. Hereditary military households were under strain in some regions, but historians now caution against assuming that the Yuan army had already collapsed: regular garrisons remained substantially functional when rebellion began. Meanwhile, the Jiangnan elite, who held much of the wealth, had mixed feelings toward a government that taxed them but offered limited career access. These conditions did not guarantee collapse, but they meant that when floods and inflation arrived, no part of the system could absorb the damage easily. Regional commanders, who had to raise their own troops and supplies, became the real beneficiaries of the breakdown.

Two ministers show how unstable central politics could be. Bayan, who dominated the court from 1335, purged rivals and imposed restrictive measures, including limits on Chinese and southern participation and a short suspension of the examinations; in 1340 his nephew Toqto'a helped remove him and reversed many of his policies, reopening the examinations and launching reforms. Policy therefore swung sharply with the fortunes of factions, and each swing created winners and losers among officials, clans, and regional interests.

Court instability mattered partly because the Yuan governed through long chains of communication. Orders and reports moved along a relay system of post stations (zhanchi) that carried officials and dispatches across the empire, and the central Secretariat governed the provinces through branch secretariats, each responsible for a large region. Alongside Chinese-style officials, many local offices also contained Mongol supervisors (darughachi) whose role was to represent the court's authority. This system could work well when the court was stable, but it depended on a clear chain of command. When ministers changed and policies reversed, officials in the provinces had to guess which instructions would last, and local commanders found it easier to act on their own.

Recruitment into government illustrates the same strain. The Yuan ordered the restoration of the civil service examinations in 1313 after decades of suspension; provincial examinations resumed in 1314, and the first restored metropolitan and palace examinations were held in 1315. The restored system reserved places by group, with quotas for Mongols, semu, northerners, and southerners, and it graduated relatively few men from the south, which had the largest and wealthiest population of educated men. Many officials entered instead as clerks or through appointments, and the examinations never became the principal route they would later be under the Ming. This helped keep many southern literati at a distance from the court. Jiangnan's elite paid heavy taxes and benefited from the commercial wealth of the region and the Grand Canal, but many of them felt excluded from the careers that carried prestige, and some would later find service with a southern regime attractive. That attitude should not be exaggerated: many southern families served the Yuan, and loyalty to the dynasty was not absent. The point is that the state's links to its richest region were less secure than its fiscal dependence on it might suggest.

The empire also rested on the hereditary household system, in which families were registered by occupation, such as military, artisan, post-station, or salt household, and owed the services attached to that status from generation to generation. The arrangement gave the state predictable labor, but it was inflexible. A household whose land was flooded or whose members had died still owed its service, and officials were tempted to demand more from the registered than from the unregistered. Military households supplied soldiers and some were under strain, but they were not collapsing across the board. As in other parts of the story, the best description is vulnerability: a system that worked acceptably in ordinary times and had few means to adapt to shock.

Yet collapse was not inevitable. Recent scholarship stresses that the regular Yuan armies were still capable in the early 1350s. In late 1354 Toqto'a led a large force against Zhang Shicheng's rebel regime at Gaoyou, and the rebels were close to defeat. Then, under pressure from court rivals, Toghon Temür dismissed him in early 1355; his army dissolved, and the rebels recovered. The episode shows how a political decision at court, not only structural weakness, shaped the outcome, and why historians avoid treating the dynasty's fall as foreordained in 1351.

The siege of Gaoyou in 1354 reveals how close the Yuan government came to regaining the initiative. Zhang Shicheng, a salt-yard figure, had risen in the area in 1353 and taken the city of Gaoyou, which sat on the Grand Canal in the Huai region and threatened the grain route to Dadu. In late 1354 Toqto'a led a large army against him, and sources describe an enormous force, though their numbers are not reliable. The Yuan army broke Zhang's outer defenses and besieged the city, and Zhang's position became desperate. Had the siege succeeded, the northern end of the rebel front would have been broken at its center and the canal secured.

Instead the court intervened. Toqto'a had made enemies, and rivals persuaded the emperor to dismiss him and strip him of his command in the middle of the campaign. His army, which depended on his authority and on the supplies he controlled, lost cohesion, and many soldiers dispersed or joined other forces. Zhang survived and later took Suzhou in 1356. Historians stress the importance of this contingency. It does not show that the Yuan would have won; rebellions elsewhere were spreading. It shows that the dynasty's final decade was shaped by political choices at court, and that after 1355 the central government could no longer direct its commanders. Regional generals such as Chaghan Temür and Köke Temür, who had risen by raising their own forces against rebels, now acted largely on their own, and from the late 1350s they fought each other as well as the rebels. The collapse of the Yuan was therefore a prolonged process in which the court gradually lost the ability to coordinate the military power it had created.

Section 40.2

Floods, Famine, and Fiscal Problems

The Yellow River's heavy silt load repeatedly raised its bed, and in the 1340s it broke its dikes and flooded large areas of the North China Plain and the Huai region, disrupting the Grand Canal and salt-producing areas. Famine followed, and in places epidemic disease, although the scale of epidemic mortality is hard to document. The government debated whether to restore the old channel or let the river take a new course. In 1351 it organized a massive repair under the minister Jia Lu, drafting labor on a very large scale from the affected provinces.

Meanwhile the government, short of silver and cash, began a monetary reform in 1350; the new Zhizheng notes themselves were issued in 1352. Inflation followed, eroding confidence in money and the value of official salaries and tax payments. Taxes, levies, and conscripted labor fell on populations already displaced. This is the key point: floods did not cause rebellion automatically, but they collided with fiscal weakness, forced labor, and a currency crisis, and religious networks offered an organized way to protest.

The river-works debate shows how politics and hydrology interacted. Officials argued over whether to force the Yellow River back to its old southern course, which would restore the canal's water supply but cost enormous labor, or to accept a northern route. Supporters of the large project saw it as the only way to protect the canal, on which Dadu's grain supply depended; critics warned of the burden on hungry populations. The works went ahead in 1351, drawing laborers and soldiers from several provinces, and later tradition connects the project to the outbreak of revolt. Historians treat this connection cautiously: rebel networks were already active, and the labor draft supplied recruits and grievances rather than a single cause.

The salt monopoly shows another pressure point. Salt revenue was a major source of income, and the producing zones along the Huai and lower Yangtze coasts were also home to poor laborers and smugglers. Zhang Shicheng, a salt-yard figure, began his revolt in this setting in the early 1350s. Inflation, floods, and monopoly enforcement therefore fed rebellion in different ways in different districts, which is why the geography of revolt followed hardship rather than a single trigger.

The Yellow River's physical behavior helps explain why a hydraulic crisis became a political crisis. The Yellow River carries a heavy load of fine loess silt eroded from the plateau upstream. As it crosses the flat North China Plain its current slows and the silt settles, raising the riverbed. People responded by building dikes higher, which raised the bed further, until the river ran above the surrounding fields and a single breach could send water across a vast area and into a new channel. In the fourteenth century the river's lower course ran south of the Shandong hills, entangled with the Huai River system and with the Grand Canal, so a breach could flood farmland, wreck the canal, and send silt into the Huai basin all at once.

The canal was not a minor matter. The Yuan capital at Dadu lay in the north, far from the great rice-producing districts of the south, and its court, garrisons, and officials depended on southern grain carried by canal and by coastal shipping. When flooding or disorder interrupted these routes, the effect reached the court as well as the villages. For the same reason the government had strong reasons to repair the river, and the project of 1351 under Jia Lu was a rational response to a real problem. Sources report that very large numbers of laborers, on the order of 150,000, and thousands of soldiers were assembled, though such figures are approximate and the burden is better understood household by household. Corvée meant that a family lost the labor of its men in the working season, had to feed itself or bear the cost of tools and food, and risked disease and abuse in crowded camps. For people already hungry, an act of public works could feel like an additional disaster.

The salt monopoly shows the same interaction of environment and finance. Salt was produced along the coast by hereditary salt households, sold under state-regulated licenses to merchants, and taxed as one of the state's largest sources of revenue. Because the price was far above the cost of production, smuggling was profitable, and the coastal and canal regions of the Huai and lower Yangtze were full of poor producers, boatmen, and private traders who lived on the edge of the law. These people knew the waterways, owned boats, and could organize themselves, so when the state weakened some of them became rebel followers or leaders. Meanwhile the currency reform of 1350 and the Zhizheng notes of 1352 reached different people differently: officials and soldiers paid in notes saw their incomes shrink, merchants preferred goods or silver, and taxpayers faced demands in a currency whose value was falling. Environmental history here should therefore not be read as determinism. The river, the canal, the salt revenue, and the notes were all parts of one system, and the crisis came from their interaction.

Cause and Effect

The Fall of the Yuan: How Pressures Interacted

Long-term conditions

Factional court politics and policy reversals, a hierarchy of recruitment that left much of Jiangnan at a distance from office, rigid hereditary household obligations, and growing regional commanders' autonomy made the system slow to adapt.

Short-term shocks

Yellow River breaches in the 1340s, the labor draft of 1351, the 1350 currency reform and the inflationary Zhizheng notes of 1352, and sectarian networks in the Huai region.

Turning points

The failure at Gaoyou and Toqto'a's dismissal in 1355 removed the Yuan's best chance of regaining the initiative and left regional commanders acting independently.

Effects

Civil war among regional regimes, a new dynasty, a surviving Great Yuan court, heavy population movement, and reconstruction combined with coercion.

Interaction: The river works were a sensible response to a real problem, but they drew labor from a hungry region while the currency lost value and sectarian networks offered organization. The same conditions did not produce the same outcome everywhere, because commanders, elites, and local resources differed.

Historical complexity: Explaining the fall as "corruption" or a dynastic cycle omits the interaction of fiscal, environmental, military, and political conditions, and it omits contingency: in 1354 the dynasty came close to a decisive success.

Section 40.3

The Red Turban Rebellions

The Red Turbans (named for their headcloths) arose in 1351 in the Huai River region. Han Shantong, associated with White Lotus and Maitreya traditions that expected a coming savior, was arrested and executed, but his followers, led by Liu Futong, rose in revolt. Believers drew on Buddhist expectations of renewal and on Manichaean-influenced imagery of light, though how much each element shaped a given group is debated. In 1355 Han Lin'er was proclaimed "Little Ming King" and a Song restoration was announced, giving the movement a political banner.

Han Shantong's movement should not stand in for every Red Turban force. Historians such as John Dardess describe independent northern and southern rebel complexes, in which Maitreyan, White Lotus, and Buddho-Manichaean elements were intertwined differently, and which changed considerably as they grew. The southern groups, for example, were organized around different leaders and regional grievances from the Huai-valley movement. Students should not imagine one army with one command. "Red Turban" covered separate groups in the Huai valley, the middle Yangtze, Sichuan, and elsewhere, led by commanders with local aims. Some fought Yuan forces, some fought each other, and landlord-organized militias sometimes fought them all. Yuan commanders such as Chaghan Temür and Köke Temür, based in the north, also pursued their own agendas, so even the loyalist side was fragmented.

Religion and organization mattered. Maitreyan expectations, which promised a coming Buddha and a renewal of the world, gave local leaders a language for mobilizing followers, and lay Buddhist societies supplied networks, ritual specialists, and cell-like structures. But political meaning varied. The 1355 Song-restoration banner tied the Huai movement to a historical memory of Chinese rule, while commanders such as Zhu increasingly distanced themselves from millenarian claims as they sought the support of landholding elites. Participants included impoverished farmers, boatmen, salt workers, deserters from Yuan forces, and local strongmen. Violence was widespread: rebel armies sacked cities, Yuan armies and militias retaliated, and civilians suffered from both. Historians therefore describe the Red Turban period as simultaneously a religious movement, a social revolt, and a militarized civil war, whose components shifted over time.

The southern complex shows how different these movements were. In 1351, in the middle Yangtze region of Hubei, Xu Shouhui led a Red Turban rising that took Qishui and then proclaimed an emperor and a dynastic state, often called Tianwan. Its commanders included Chen Youliang, a former fisherman's son and minor clerk who rose through military ability; in 1360 he killed Xu and proclaimed his own Han state. Other southern leaders, such as Ming Yuzhen in Sichuan, later founded their own regimes. These groups drew on Maitreyan and light-centered traditions, but they also built armies, fleets, and administrations, and they fought each other as well as the Yuan.

In the north, the movement around Liu Futong and Han Lin'er developed differently. Liu Futong captured Bozhou and then, in 1355, installed Han Lin'er as "Little Ming King" and announced a restored Song state; in 1358 his forces occupied Kaifeng, the old Northern Song capital, but Yuan armies led by commanders such as Chaghan Temür and his adopted son Köke Temür pushed them back. The northern complex therefore became a contest among rebel armies, Yuan loyalists, and landlord militias, with civilians suffering requisition, plunder, and massacre from every side.

How did sects become states? The answer lies in the interaction of rebels, landlords, and Yuan commanders. In the Huai and Henan regions, landowning families faced demands from rebel armies for grain and men, and many organized militias of tenants and kin for self-defense. Some of these militias remained local, while others were led by gentry who joined the Yuan cause. The most important was Chaghan Temür, a Yuan commander of Central Asian descent who built an army from Henan militias and, in 1359, recovered Kaifeng from the northern rebels. After he was assassinated in 1362 by rebels who had surrendered to him, his adopted son Köke Temür took over his forces. The Yuan court thus acquired powerful regional armies, but they were personal armies whose loyalty to the court depended on politics.

The rebels, for their part, changed as they grew. Early recruits followed leaders who promised protection and renewal, but an army that holds cities needs revenue, and rebel commanders began to tax, appoint officials, and fight over territory. In the north, the Song restoration regime under Han Lin'er was weakened by Yuan counterattacks and by rivalry among its generals; Liu Futong was killed in 1363 while Zhang Shicheng's forces attacked Anfeng, where the court was based, and Han Lin'er was moved to the territory of Zhu Yuanzhang. Zhu's regime continued to use the Song reign name until 1366. In the south, the Tianwan and Han regimes similarly shifted from religious movement to regional state, though they remained fragile because they depended on the commanders who had raised their armies.

Violence accompanied every stage. Rebel armies sacked cities and requisitioned grain and labor; Yuan armies and militias retaliated, sometimes massacring suspected sympathizers; and the contending forces burned crops, seized boats, and disrupted markets. Contemporary accounts, written mostly by officials and literati, stress the destruction, and their figures should be treated cautiously, but the pattern is clear. By the early 1360s the Red Turban movement had largely ceased to be a single religious uprising and had become a civil war among regional regimes, which is the situation in which Zhu's state-building proved decisive.

Historical Vocabulary
Red Turbans

Red Turbans (Hongjin jun, "Red Headcloth armies") is the label given to a family of rebel movements that arose in the early 1350s, named for the red cloths their followers tied around their heads. The name is found in Yuan and Ming sources and is still used in textbooks, but historians use it with care. It describes a recognizable style of rebellion, in which religious expectation, local grievance, and armed organization joined together, rather than a single army with one command or one creed.

Why the qualification? First, hostile officials wrote most of the descriptions. They tended to label any religiously flavored rebel as a "White Lotus" or "incense-burning" sectarian, whether or not the group belonged to a lay Buddhist devotional society, and the later dynasties that wrote the histories had reasons to treat all such movements as dangerous. Second, the beliefs involved overlapped. Expectations of the coming Buddha Maitreya, Pure Land devotion, and symbols of light that scholars connect, cautiously, to Manichaean traditions all appear, but the evidence does not allow us to assign one set of beliefs to one army. Third, the movements changed. Early groups were organized around ritual, vows, and the hopes of followers; later ones became armies with generals, taxes, and territory, and their leaders often moved away from sectarian language toward the language of ordinary dynastic government.

Historians therefore distinguish at least a northern complex, centered on the Huai valley and the circle of Han Shantong, Liu Futong, and Han Lin'er, from a southern complex in the middle Yangtze, associated with Xu Shouhui and the Tianwan regime. These groups developed separately and sometimes fought each other. In context: when a Yuan official wrote of "Red bandits," he was lumping together groups with different leaders, aims, and fates, and a student who repeats the label without qualification repeats his simplification.

Being aware of this does not mean abandoning the term. It means asking, whenever "Red Turban" appears: Which group is meant? Where and when? Who is describing it, and why? Is the writer speaking of belief, of organization, or of a political claim? The Ming itself emerged from this plural, contested environment, and Zhu Yuanzhang's regime later restricted the very kinds of sectarian organization from which it had partly sprung.

Section 40.4

Zhu Yuanzhang’s Rise

After his family's deaths Zhu entered the monastery in 1344, wandered as a mendicant for roughly two or three years from about 1345, and then returned to the monastery, where he remained until the rebellion reached the region in 1352. The mendicant years exposed him to the countryside's poverty and instability across a wider region than he had known as a child. In 1352 he joined Guo Zixing's band at Haozhou, where his ability brought promotion, and he married Guo's adopted daughter, later Empress Ma. After Guo's death Zhu took effective command, built a reliable core of officers, and, unlike many warlords, enforced discipline on his troops toward civilians. He began recruiting literate advisers, who urged him to seize a strong base, protect agriculture, and avoid indiscriminate plunder.

Zhu's rise was a process, not a single leap. Guo Zixing's band was one of several in the Huai region and was riven by rivalry among its leaders; Zhu gained standing by surviving those conflicts and by taking his own troops south to find new recruits and territory. His years at the monastery gave him some literacy, though the sources for this period were shaped later, and the Ming court had reasons both to dramatize his poverty and to elevate his destiny. Historians therefore read the early biography as a mixture of reliable outline and legitimating narrative. What can be shown is that he combined an unusual willingness to learn from educated advisers with a talent for command, and that he moved gradually from subordinate to independent ruler rather than declaring himself at the start.

The intermediate stages of his rise show how a subordinate became a ruler. In 1353 Zhu left Haozhou with a small force raised from his home district and took Chuzhou, where he attracted recruits, including Xu Da, and recruited local men as officers. When Guo Zixing died in 1355, Zhu became the leading commander of the force under nominal command of the Song regime of Han Lin'er, and in the same year his army crossed the Yangtze and took Taiping, which opened the lower Yangtze to him. Li Shanchang had joined him earlier as a counselor, and after 1360 Liu Ji, Song Lian, and other scholars from Zhejiang entered his service. They helped transform a rebel army into an administration that kept registers, collected grain, and justified its rule in classical terms.

These steps were not predetermined. Zhu faced rivals within Guo's faction, depended on a larger movement whose leaders could have eliminated him, and fought in a region where the Yuan, other rebels, and landlord militias all contended. His eventual success depended on contingencies as well as skill, which is why historians resist treating him as the destined founder.

Each stage changed what Zhu could do. As a recruit in Guo Zixing's band he had loyalty and a patron but no independent force, and Guo's coalition was divided by rivalry among its leaders. His marriage into Guo's household tied him to the family and made him a trusted officer, but it also placed him inside quarrels he could not control. By leaving Haozhou to raise his own troops he created a following loyal to himself, and by attracting capable officers such as Xu Da he began to build a command structure. When Guo died in 1355 Zhu became the leading commander in a force nominally under the Song regime of Han Lin'er, which gave him a title and a claim to legitimacy that cost him little, and he crossed the Yangtze to take Taiping and the districts around it. That was a decisive change: a commander on the Huai plain depended on what he could plunder or collect locally, while a commander on the lower Yangtze controlled rich land, boats, and cities.

The next change was administrative. A territory has to be governed, and that requires clerks who can keep registers, collect grain, and judge disputes. The scholars who joined Zhu, first Li Shanchang and later Liu Ji and Song Lian, brought these skills and the language of classical government, and they urged him to restrain his troops, protect the farmers, and present his regime as a restorer of order. Zhu followed much of this advice, though not always: his army still lived off the land at times, and surrendered rivals were both absorbed and watched. His treatment of defeated soldiers, who were enrolled in his own units under his own officers, increased his strength with each victory. By the mid-1350s he was no longer a rebel commander in the older sense but a ruler of a territory with a base, a revenue, and a civil staff, which is the transformation that separates him from the many rebel leaders who disappeared.

Section 40.5

Military Competition and the Capture of Nanjing

In 1356 Zhu took Nanjing, a historic southern capital on the Yangtze, naming it Yingtian. The city gave him a defensible base, rich surrounding land, and control of river traffic. From it he organized local administration, tax collection, and military farming, and acknowledged the Little Ming King's nominal authority while acting independently. He faced three principal rivals: Chen Youliang in the middle Yangtze, with a large river fleet; Zhang Shicheng, who held the rich Suzhou region; and Fang Guozhen, who controlled the Zhejiang coast.

Nanjing mattered because of what surrounded it. It stands on the south bank of the lower Yangtze, backed by hills such as the Zhong Mountains and linked by water to the grain and textile districts of the delta, and it had been a capital of southern dynasties during the Age of Division (Chapter 21). A commander holding it controlled river traffic, could draw on the surrounding farmland for grain and taxes, and could defend a long front with ships. Construction of the monumental wall associated with Ming Nanjing began in 1366 and continued for roughly two decades, and the site's advantages were already clear.

His rivals held different kinds of territory and ruled in different ways. Chen Youliang's Han state, after Chen killed Xu Shouhui in 1360, controlled the middle Yangtze with a large fleet, but it depended heavily on its military leaders, and Chen's seizure of power from Xu Shouhui left questions about its cohesion. Zhang Shicheng, a former salt-yard worker, held the rich Suzhou region, whose wealth gave him substantial resources; he too used the title of Prince of Wu in 1363, which is why the sources distinguish his "Eastern Wu" from Zhu's "Western Wu." Fang Guozhen, a Zhejiang coastal figure who had been a salt smuggler and shipper, drew on sea trade and grain shipping to the Yuan court and shifted his formal allegiances as circumstances changed. Historians such as Dardess argue that none of them built the same combination of discipline, tax collection, and administration.

At Lake Poyang in 1363 geography decided much. Chen's fleet was larger, but the lake was shallow and the large ships were awkward in confined waters; accounts describe Zhu's side using smaller, more maneuverable vessels and fire attacks against Chen's ships, though these accounts come from Ming sources and should be treated with caution. The campaign lasted weeks and depended on supplies as much as on tactics, and Chen's death, reported to have come from an arrow, ended his regime. Zhu took the title Prince of Wu in 1364.

The campaigns showed how war, logistics, and administration interlocked. The conquest of Suzhou was a longer siege (1366–67) of a rich, walled commercial city; Zhang Shicheng's regime had cultivated local elites and ruled an area with abundant revenue, but it was not able to coordinate resistance and was taken after months of blockade. Fang Guozhen's coastal state in Zhejiang, which lived on sea trade and grain shipping, surrendered late in 1367, and by then Zhu's rivals had been eliminated. Each campaign also eliminated a rival model of rule, and the cities captured supplied taxes, craftsmen, and administrators.

Zhu's advantages were geographic (a central base with a strong economy), organizational (disciplined troops, administrators), and strategic (rivals fought each other and Yuan commanders while he consolidated). Scholarship emphasizes that Zhu's success lay in building a more centralized territorial regime, not merely in winning battles. Chen Youliang, who had split from the Red Turban core and proclaimed his own Han state, built his power on fleets and the military leaders who commanded them; Zhang Shicheng drew on Suzhou's wealth; Zhu, according to John Dardess and others, built a more tightly centralized embryonic state, combining taxes, military farms, recruited literati administrators, and a command structure of loyal officers.

In practice, "centralized territorial government" meant that Zhu appointed officials who reported to his headquarters, collected taxes into central storehouses, kept registers of people and land, paid and supplied troops through the state, and kept commanders from becoming local lords. Historians argue that rivals with weaker versions of these mechanisms found their gains harder to hold. His nominal subordination to the Little Ming King let him claim legitimacy until he no longer needed it.

Recruitment and discipline mattered. Zhu absorbed defeated forces, appointed surrendered officers to posts, and issued orders against plundering civilians, though enforcement was uneven and soldiers often lived off the land. Scholars joined him because they hoped for order and because rivals offered less promising prospects. The result was a regime with a more developed civil-military structure than most of its competitors, which explains its success better than any single battle.

Section 40.6

Founding of the Ming Dynasty

Zhu proclaimed the new dynasty in Nanjing on 23 January 1368, in the first lunar month. Accounts describe sacrifices to Heaven and Earth at altars outside the city, the enshrining of ancestors, and the announcement of the Hongwu reign title, with Zhu's wife, Ma, named empress and his eldest son named heir. Nanjing, renamed Yingtian, became the capital, and the regime's early offices, including a central secretariat and military commissions, were organized around the men who had served the Wu regime. The change from Wu to Ming was therefore less a break in personnel than a change in status: a regional prince with a territorial government declared himself an emperor, and the offices he already commanded acquired a new language.

The new dynasty took the name Ming ("bright") and the reign title Hongwu ("vast martial"). An influential older interpretation connected the name with the rebels' light symbolism and alleged Manichaean antecedents, but this derivation has been challenged, and the origin of the dynastic name remains uncertain; it could also be read differently by different audiences. The regime had to settle its relationship with Han Lin'er's Song restoration as well. Han had died in 1366 when a boat sank during a crossing; sources attribute this to accident, but later writers suspected Zhu, and the sources do not permit a secure conclusion.

Legitimacy had several layers. Ritually, Zhu built altars and ancestral temples in Nanjing and proclaimed his regime heir to the Mandate of Heaven, a concept used here as a political claim rather than a mechanical explanation of success. Historically, he cast the Yuan's fall as the result of its failures of rule, a narrative that the Ming histories later developed. Personally, the court emphasized his humble origins as evidence of Heaven's choice. Militarily, the capture of Dadu offered proof of power. Different audiences found different elements persuasive: southern scholars valued the restoration of classical rites, soldiers valued pay and rank, and peasants valued security. The multiple foundations of authority explain why the regime could not rely on one slogan.

The political language of the new dynasty had in fact been articulated earlier, during preparations for the northern campaign. Zhu's 1367 proclamation called for expelling the Mongols and restoring order to the Chinese lands, language that appealed to southern and northern elites alike. Yet the Ming was not a simple "Han restoration." It inherited much Yuan machinery, including hereditary household categories and a military system partly modeled on Yuan practice. The regime also kept many Mongol and Central Asian soldiers and officers who submitted to it. The tension is the point: Zhu's proclamations used explicit Hua/barbarian language, calling for the expulsion of the Mongols and the restoration of Zhonghua, while the regime in practice incorporated Mongol and Central Asian personnel and Yuan institutions.

The founding proclamations addressed several audiences. To scholars and officials, the new dynasty promised to revive classical ritual and good government. To northerners and to people unhappy with Mongol rule, it called for the expulsion of the Mongols and the restoration of Zhonghua, with the Hua/barbarian language that the Primary Source Window examines. To soldiers and administrators, including Mongols and Central Asians who submitted, it offered rank and security. The regime continued many Yuan institutions, from hereditary households to fiscal practices, while presenting itself as a restorer. "Han restoration" therefore explains how the Ming spoke about itself and what many of its supporters wanted, but it does not describe all that the regime inherited or did.

Historical Vocabulary
Legitimacy and the Mandate of Heaven

Legitimacy is the accepted right to rule: the belief, among those who are governed and among those who serve, that a particular ruler or regime ought to be obeyed. It is not the same as power. A regime can hold a city by force and still be widely regarded as illegitimate, and a weak ruler can retain legitimacy for years. In dynastic China rulers sought legitimacy through several means at once: ritual performance, descent, claims about history, the support of educated officials, and, above all in times of change, the argument that Heaven approved.

The Mandate of Heaven (tianming) is the best known of these arguments. Originating in the Zhou period (Chapter 8), it held that Heaven grants the right to rule to those who govern well and withdraws it from those who do not. In 1368 the concept functioned as a claim made by the new regime, not as a mechanism that caused the Yuan to fall. The Ming argued that the Yuan had lost the capacity to govern and that Heaven had turned to Zhu. Historians treat that argument as evidence of how the regime wished to be seen.

Legitimacy is a historical problem because every new ruler had to persuade several audiences at once, and different arguments worked on different people. In 1368 Zhu's regime made at least four claims. It appealed to Heaven's mandate. It appealed to history, presenting itself as a restorer of Chinese rule after Mongol conquest. It appealed to ritual, building altars and ancestral temples according to classical models. And it appealed to results, offering order, protection, and tax relief.

These claims could conflict. The language of expelling "barbarians" appealed to southern scholars and some northerners, but it sat uneasily beside the regime's reliance on Mongol and Central Asian officers who had submitted. The claim to a mandate invited scrutiny of the new rulers' own conduct, so later Ming sources took care to present Zhu's humble origins and the Yuan's faults as proofs of Heaven's choice. Historians therefore read legitimacy language as evidence of what rulers thought they needed to say, not as a transparent account of what they believed or of why people obeyed. Students should ask: Who is being addressed? What does this claim ask them to believe or do? What does the regime do that fits, or fails to fit, the claim?

Historical Debate

Was the Ming a "Han Restoration"?

The question: The Ming proclaimed itself the end of Mongol rule and the restoration of Zhonghua, and later Chinese nationalist writing often treated 1368 as the return of native government. Historians ask whether this description captures what the new dynasty was, or whether it reflects what the dynasty wished to say about itself.

Interpretation A — the Ming as restoration: Supporters point to the explicit language of Zhu's proclamations, which called for expelling the Mongols and restoring Zhonghua, to the revival of classical ritual and official dress, and to the regime's reliance on southern literati. They argue that these actions and the support they won show that many contemporaries understood 1368 as a recovery of Chinese rule after a period of conquest, and that the emphasis on Hua and barbarian categories was a real political force, not a mere slogan.
Interpretation B — the Ming as inheritor and transformer: Others stress what the Ming took from its predecessor: hereditary household categories, much military organization, aspects of administration, and many Mongol and Central Asian personnel who entered Ming service. They add that the regime's autocratic style and its fiscal arrangements came from the civil war and from Zhu's own choices, not from any earlier native model. On this view, "restoration" describes the dynasty's rhetoric more than its institutions, and the Ming is better understood as a new regime built partly from Yuan materials.

Evidence: Proclamations, ritual regulations, and the writings of supporting literati support Interpretation A. Registers, military records, and personnel evidence support B. Each is partial: proclamations show intentions and audiences, while administrative records show practice but rarely explain motives.

Why it matters: The debate bears on how students understand identity, conquest, and continuity. It also shows how later national narratives can adopt a dynasty's self-description as historical fact.

Section 40.7

Expulsion of Yuan Forces From the Central Plains

Xu Da and Chang Yuchun led the northern expedition, which was launched in late 1367 and culminated in the capture of Dadu in September 1368, taking Shandong, Henan, and the Yellow River passes along the way. Emperor Toghon Temür withdrew from Dadu in the late summer, and Xu Da entered the capital with little fighting; the Ming government subsequently renamed it Beiping ("Northern Peace"). Fighting continued in Shanxi, Shaanxi, Gansu, and Sichuan and, in the southwest, Yunnan, where Yuan loyalists held out until the early 1380s.

The Yuan did not vanish. Toghon Temür retreated to the steppe, and his successors continued as the Northern Yuan, a Mongol state that remained a serious military power and a rival claimant to legitimacy. Ming armies launched campaigns into Mongolia, some successful and some disastrous. This shaped the Ming's northern defense problem, which later chapters examine.

Recent scholarship stresses that the post-1368 court, which continued to style itself the Great Yuan, remained a consequential political actor. It kept the imperial title, maintained claims over the former realm, retained commanders and allies in Shaanxi, Gansu, and Yunnan, and negotiated, fought, and sometimes defected with Ming officials. For frontier peoples, the question was less "Chinese or foreign" than which court could offer protection, trade access, and recognized rank.

The northern campaign followed a plan that reflected Zhu's strategy. He attacked the north only after securing the southeast, since an army moving north would otherwise have left rivals at its back. The scheme, adopted in 1367, was to take Shandong first, then Henan and the Tong Pass, which cut communications between the Central Plains and the northwest, and finally to approach Dadu with the Yuan court isolated. Xu Da commanded the main army, assisted by Chang Yuchun, and they moved through Shandong in the winter of 1367–68, taking the cities of the region without fighting one decisive battle. In 1368 they advanced into Henan, occupying Kaifeng, the old Northern Song capital, and Luoyang, and sent forces to seal the Tong Pass.

The advance toward Dadu followed the Grand Canal through Hebei. Yuan resistance there was weak. The court was divided, its best commanders were scattered or at odds with one another, and its supply from the south had long been disrupted. Toghon Temür, facing the arrival of the Ming armies, decided to leave the capital in the late summer of 1368 and withdrew north to Shangdu, taking the court and a number of followers. Xu Da entered Dadu in September with little fighting, and the city fell without large-scale battle destruction. The Ming government subsequently renamed it Beiping, "Northern Peace," a name that signaled the new regime's claim and its expectation of order.

The fall of Dadu did not end the war. Xu Da turned west and took Taiyuan in Shanxi, forcing Köke Temür, a major Yuan commander whose relations with the court had been strained by factional and regional rivalries, to flee to the northwest. Campaigns followed into Shaanxi and Gansu in 1369–70, and Köke Temür was defeated at Dingxi in 1370 before withdrawing toward the steppe. Sichuan, in 1371 ruled by the Great Xia regime that Ming Yuzhen, a southern Red Turban leader, had founded and that was then governed by his son Ming Sheng, fell to the Ming that year. In each region local populations, including Han, Mongol, and Muslim communities and commanders of mixed background, bargained with the advancing armies. Some surrendered and were absorbed with their ranks, others fled north or were killed, and the pattern of submission and resistance varied by region and by the stakes involved. Yunnan, in the far southwest, was held by Basalawarmi, the Mongol Prince of Liang, who ruled on behalf of the Great Yuan, and fell only in 1381–82 to a Ming army under Fu Youde, Lan Yu, and Mu Ying; the prince took his own life.

Several examples show the resilience of the Great Yuan. Ayushiridara succeeded Toghon Temür in 1370, and the court kept the imperial title, reign names, and claims over the former realm. A Ming expedition toward Karakorum in 1372 met serious defeat, and Great Yuan forces and allies continued to win local successes into the 1380s. In the northeast, the Yuan commander Naghachu held out in Liaodong until he submitted in 1387. Documents from Qara-Qoto in the northwest show Yuan-style administration continuing there after 1368. For these reasons the fall of Dadu is better understood as the loss of a capital than as the end of Yuan political power.

The political geography of the steppe explains why the Great Yuan could survive. After leaving Dadu the court withdrew first to Shangdu (1368); when Shangdu fell to the Ming in 1369 it moved onward to Yingchang, where Toghon Temür died in 1370, and his successor Ayushiridara then withdrew farther north toward Karakorum, in the old Mongol heartland. There it commanded loyalty from many Mongol princes and groups whose families had held rank under the Chinggisid line for generations. The Ming could not easily dislodge a mobile pastoral power in open country, where supply lines were long and the cavalry could withdraw. The Great Yuan kept the imperial title and the rituals of rule, received envoys, and negotiated with the Ming over rank and recognition. Hostilities alternated with truces, and trade and tribute contacts continued at the frontier alongside war.

The balance shifted gradually. In 1388 a Ming army under Lan Yu won a major victory near Lake Buir in the Mongolian steppe, capturing many members of the court and its followers, and the Great Yuan's authority fractured afterward as rival Mongol leaders competed. Even then Mongol power remained a central concern of Ming policy for the next two centuries, and later chapters return to the northern frontier. The convention that the Yuan ended in 1368 is useful because it marks the loss of the capital and the Central Plains and the proclamation of the Ming. As political history it is incomplete, because the Great Yuan court continued to claim rule, to fight, and to bargain for two decades, and because many Mongol and Central Asian families continued to serve the Ming. Students should hold both ideas together: 1368 was a turning point, and it was also not an endpoint.

Regional and Frontier Context

The Yuan-Ming transition looked different from each region. The Huai valley and North China Plain suffered flood, famine, and war first and recovered through resettlement and military agriculture; in these districts the central problem was too few people and too little capital. Jiangnan, by contrast, was densely populated, rich, and taxed heavily, and its elites saw both opportunities and dangers in the new regime. The middle Yangtze and Sichuan were governed for a time by southern rebel regimes with their own leaders and institutions, so for many people the Ming came as a conquest after years of regional government.

On the frontier, people experienced the change in terms of rank and security. In Yunnan, the Mongol Prince of Liang ruled for the Great Yuan until 1381–82, and local peoples there negotiated their positions with both courts. On the steppe, the Great Yuan court remained a political center, and Mongol groups chose among alliance, trade, and war with the Ming. Frontier communities were participants in the transition, not bystanders waiting to be absorbed.

Section 40.8

Agricultural and Social Reconstruction

War left abandoned land and fallen population in many areas, especially the North China Plain. The new government encouraged resettlement, organizing several distinct movements of households to empty lands: removals of wealthy Jiangnan families toward Nanjing and the Fengyang area began early, while large documented transfers from Shanxi into depopulated northern lands were especially important in the later decades of the reign. The state gave tax relief and tools to settlers. Water-control works were repaired. Soldiers farmed military colonies (tuntian) to feed themselves.

Reconstruction proceeded in overlapping stages across the reign. In the first years after 1368 the main problems were security and abandoned land. In 1370 the government recruited settlers to cultivate northern wasteland, allotted land, and offered tax exemptions; a broader rule of 1380 permitted people who reclaimed idle wasteland to hold it permanently, subject to later taxation. Seed, tools, and oxen were provided where they could be supplied. The Huai valley and the plains of the north were the principal areas of recovery, and the government repaired dikes and irrigation works; reports late in the reign claimed that tens of thousands of ponds, reservoirs, and embankments had been built or repaired, figures best treated as indications of effort rather than exact counts. Soldiers working military colonies supplied part of the army's grain, which reduced the pressure on taxpayers.

The state also needed to know whom it governed. The Yellow Registers, begun in 1381, recorded households by status and occupation, organized in the ideal form into units of 110 households: the ten wealthiest households supplied rotating heads (li heads) responsible for tax collection and local order, and the other 100 households formed ten groups (jia) of ten. This was the lijia system. The Fish-Scale Registers, from a land survey ordered in 1387, recorded fields with maps showing their size and ownership. Together they allowed the government to assess land tax and labor service. They were also incomplete in practice: households hid land or members, local clerks falsified entries, and registered categories often fit poorly with real family circumstances.

Regional experience diverged sharply. In the Huai valley and North China Plain, the main problem was labor and seed: recovery depended on migrants, ox-teams, and repaired dikes, and the government moved settlers in, with the largest documented transfers from Shanxi coming in the later decades of the reign. In Jiangnan, the problem was the reverse: a dense, wealthy population that the state wished to tax heavily and, in some cases, to uproot. The Suzhou region, which had backed Zhang Shicheng, carried especially high burdens, and some wealthy families were moved to Nanjing or the Fengyang area.

For ordinary families the effects varied, ranging from opportunity to coercion, as the Everyday Life and Multiple Perspectives boxes illustrate. Resettled households gained land but lost homes and kin networks. Registered artisan and military households were bound hereditarily to service wherever the state needed them: soldiers' families lived near garrisons and farmed under supervision, and artisans were registered for state workshops and owed labor service in rotation. Women worked in textile production and farm labor, and in military and resettled households their work was often what sustained the family unit. Local elites survived by cooperating with registration, serving as tax captains, or leaving the region; some accumulated land again within a generation. Evidence comes from official registers, gazetteers, and later complaints, so households that evaded registration are largely invisible.

Historical Vocabulary
Weisuo (Guards and Battalions)

The weisuo was the Ming military system of hereditary soldier households organized into guards (wei) and battalions (suo). In the standard scheme a guard was nominally about 5,600 men and was divided into battalions of about 1,100; actual strengths varied widely and declined over time. A family registered as a military household owed the state one soldier at a time, and when that soldier died, retired, or deserted, a replacement was supplied from the household. Service was therefore inherited, much as the status of artisan and other service households was.

The system drew on two sources. One was Yuan practice, which also assigned hereditary military households, so the Ming inherited a familiar category rather than inventing one. The other was the needs of Zhu's own army during the civil war, which absorbed defeated units and settled them near the places they garrisoned. To avoid burdening taxpayers with the whole cost of a standing army, the regime attached land to the garrisons: soldiers farmed military colonies (tuntian) and supplied much of their own grain, with a share of their time reserved for training and duty. In frontier areas a larger portion of the force might be assigned to defense, and in the interior more to farming.

For soldiers' families the arrangement meant security and obligation together. A household received land, a place in a unit, and a recognized status in a world of disorder, but it also owed service across generations, lived under officers who could demand labor, and could be moved to distant postings. Women and children often worked garrison land, and families paid for equipment and travel. Over time the system became difficult to sustain: officers took land and labor for themselves, soldiers deserted, registers grew inaccurate, and the force's quality fell. It was a response to wartime conditions, adapted from Yuan practice, that bound a large part of the population to hereditary service.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households in Flood, War, and Registration

Households in the Huai region experienced flood, hunger, and conscription directly, and monasteries sometimes offered refuge. During the wars villages formed militias; afterward, lijia groups tied households to tax duties. Women managed farms when men were drafted and sustained soldiers' and artisans' households. Because most sources are official, the experiences of poor families must often be inferred.

The following is a composite reconstruction, not a documented individual village or household. Imagine a village on the Huai plain in the 1360s in which many families have died, fled, or been conscripted, and fields lie in weeds. A widow with two small children has the land but no ox and no seed. When officials arrive with registers and promise tools and a few years of tax relief to settlers, some neighbors will welcome the offer while others, remembering requisitions by armies, will hide. Newcomers moved from crowded districts may be given the abandoned land, creating new relations between settlers and surviving households over boundaries, wells, and debts.

Women carried much of this reconstruction. They wove cloth that families paid as tax or traded for grain, managed households while men served in garrisons or on labor teams, and in military households supported soldiers whose pay was irregular. A soldier's wife might work land attached to the garrison; a widow of an artisan might be obliged to supply a substitute for his service. These burdens fell unevenly, and because registers recorded households under male heads, women appear in the sources mostly through the men they depended on.

Multiple Perspectives

Who Gained and Who Bore the Costs? Experiencing the Early Hongwu Reconstruction

Note: the viewpoints below are reconstructed analytical perspectives synthesizing patterns historians have identified across many sources. They are not quotations from any single named historical actor.

The recovery of the 1360s–1380s was a single policy field, yet it looked very different from different positions in society. Land, labor, tax, and registration were the same for everyone in principle, but people differed in what they could bargain over, what they could hide, and what they could lose. The cards below are not equally powerful voices: some groups shaped policy, others only absorbed it.

From the perspective of a resettled peasant household: Moving from a crowded district to depopulated land meant leaving kin, graves, and local networks, which mattered deeply in a society organized around family and ancestral obligation. In return came land, tools, seed, and several years of tax relief, a real opportunity for people who had owned little. Whether the move felt like a chance or a punishment depended on how voluntary it was, on the quality of the land assigned, and on whether neighbors accepted the newcomers. Registration was the price: a settled household was a countable household, and the state could now find it for tax and labor.
From the perspective of a wealthy Jiangnan landowner, especially around Suzhou: These families held the richest land in the empire, and many had lived under rival regimes such as Zhang Shicheng's before the Ming conquest. The new government regarded the region as a source of revenue and, in some cases, as a population of doubtful loyalty, so the tax burden was heavy and some wealthy households were relocated to Nanjing or the Fengyang area. For them reconstruction could mean confiscation, forced removal, and the loss of local standing. Some adapted by serving as tax captains or cooperating with registration; others left or concealed land, and within a generation some rebuilt their holdings.
From the perspective of a military household: A hereditary soldier family received a place in a guard or battalion, land to farm, and some security in a world of disorder. The same arrangement bound sons to service in perpetuity and placed the family under officers who could demand labor and supplies. Wives and children often worked the garrison land, and an unlucky family might see its men sent to distant frontiers. Security and obligation were two sides of one status, and the balance shifted as the system aged and pay and land became harder to sustain.
From the perspective of a Mongol or Central Asian soldier or official who submitted: After 1368 individuals in this position faced a choice shaped by rank, family, safety, and the geography of the war. Some entered Ming service and received posts and hereditary rank, because the new regime needed experienced officers and had adopted much Yuan military practice. Others withdrew north to the Great Yuan court or fought on, notably in Shanxi, the northwest, and Yunnan. Even those who served lived within a regime whose public rhetoric called for expelling Mongol rule, so their position could be both valued and precarious.
From the perspective of a scholar or literatus: Educated men weighed opportunity against danger. The new regime needed clerks, advisers, and officials, and men such as Liu Ji and Song Lian gained influence by joining Zhu. Yet the same emperor distrusted officials, imposed severe penalties, and demanded personal obedience, so service could end in disgrace or execution. Some scholars refused to serve at all, out of loyalty to the Yuan or fear of the regime. Their writings preserve much of what we know about the period, which means the educated viewpoint is heard more loudly than its numbers deserve.
From the perspective of women in farm, artisan, and soldier households: Women's labor in farming, weaving, and household management made reconstruction possible, and in households whose men were away on garrison or labor service they often headed day-to-day production. Widows and soldiers' wives might hold land without legal authority to match, and tax and service obligations were assessed through male household heads. They appear in the sources mainly as dependents, even when they were essential workers, so their perspective must be inferred from what the household had to accomplish.

Historical complexity: These perspectives are not equal in power or in how voluntary their choices were. The state set the framework and enforced it; households responded by cooperating, adapting, evading, or fleeing. Historians reconstruct the less powerful perspectives largely through official records that were not written to record them, so what is known about peasants and women comes with a built-in distortion.

Section 40.9

The Hongwu Political Order

Hongwu built a strongly personal monarchy shaped by the experience of civil war. He distrusted regional military power, powerful ministers, and scholars who might shelter factions.

The habits of the Hongwu regime were formed in the 1350s and 1360s, when Zhu ruled as a commander among rivals, appointed officers personally, and had good reason to fear defection. Defeated generals, absorbed troops, and educated advisers all had to be supervised, and these habits did not disappear when the army became a government. Some foundations of the Hongwu order, especially hereditary military organization, household registration, and administrative practices developed during Zhu's territorial rule, grew from the Wu regime or had Yuan precedents. Other measures, above all the abolition of the Secretariat in 1380 and direct imperial supervision of the Six Ministries, represented later and more radical concentrations of authority.

Memorials, the written reports and proposals sent to the throne, flowed to the emperor through the central administration, whose chief body was the Secretariat, headed by chancellors who could coordinate the six ministries of Personnel, Revenue, Rites, War, Punishments, and Works. Zhu came to see powerful ministers as a danger, because they could build networks and speak with authority between the emperor and the officials.

In 1380 he accused the chancellor Hu Weiyong of conspiracy, executed him and many associates, and abolished the Secretariat and the post of chancellor, so that the six ministries reported to him directly. The extent and nature of any conspiracy cannot be securely reconstructed, and many historians regard at least some of the later charges as politically constructed; the sources are hostile to Hu, and the question remains debated.

The same suspicion shaped the military. Zhu's generals had made him emperor, and he feared they might act independently. He rewarded them with titles and hereditary ranks but kept them from building independent regional bases, and he later purged several, including the general Lan Yu in 1393, while enfeoffing his sons as princes with frontier commands as a family-based alternative to powerful commanders. Casualty estimates for the purges, including the Hu Weiyong and Lan Yu cases, run into the tens of thousands, but the numbers are uncertain and come from sources shaped by later politics. This arrangement had consequences for the next reigns.

The regime also built a legal and ideological order. The government compiled laws, including a code revised in the 1370s and 1390s, and issued proclamations such as the Grand Proclamations (1385–88), which described cases of official abuse and punishments in plain language, and the Six Maxims of 1398, which urged filial conduct, respect for elders, and neighborly harmony. These village instructions accompanied a state-sponsored Confucian curriculum, while independent religious groups such as the White Lotus and other unauthorized religious organizations were restricted. Households were grouped into units with shared responsibility for taxes and order.

The experience of the order depended on position. Officials worked under an emperor who read memorials personally, punished perceived disloyalty severely, and demanded detailed reports; many served with anxiety. For ordinary villagers the new state appeared as registers, tax collectors, and instructions: it registered them, drafted their labor, and sought to supervise moral conduct through instructions read aloud at community gatherings. Military households were bound to hereditary service, and merchants and wealthy landowners faced heavy fiscal pressure. At the same time, many peasants valued the end of warfare and the return of land. The early regime thus combined reconstruction with coercion: it rebuilt farms and villages while demanding obedience and reporting.

Historians explain Hongwu's severity in several ways: as the product of his personality and suspicion, as a legacy of civil war when commanders were rivals, as a reaction to the corruption he saw in the Yuan, and as part of a wider effort to build a state from a devastated society. None of these explanations excludes the others, and for this reason historians resist explanations of the Hongwu order based on Zhu's personality alone, and they also resist treating it as simply inherited. Chapter 41 explains how the full institutional framework worked, including the examination system.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Yuan to Early Ming

What Changed

  • Political authority: a Mongol-led imperial court gave way to a dynasty founded by a former rebel, whose rule became more personal after 1380.
  • Bureaucracy and elite recruitment: the Ming relied more heavily on southern literati, and the Secretariat was abolished.
  • Ideological rhetoric: expulsion of the Mongols and restoration of Zhonghua replaced the language of Mongol imperial rule.
  • Regional effects: population moved to the depopulated north, and Jiangnan faced heavy taxes and relocations.

What Stayed the Same

  • Household categories: hereditary military and artisan households continued, with Yuan precedents.
  • Taxation: grain tax and labor service remained central to state revenue.
  • Military organization: hereditary soldiers and military farming had Yuan antecedents.
  • Frontier relations: the northern steppe remained a major political and military problem.

Who experienced change most directly: northern peasants, Jiangnan landowners, and Mongol and Central Asian elites. Who experienced continuity: many hereditary service households, who owed obligations to new masters. Why the pattern matters: The transition was both a rupture and a re-formation, which is why neither "restoration" nor "conquest" alone describes it.

China and the Wider World

The Yuan collapse was part of a wider breakup of the Mongol political world. In the fourteenth century the other Mongol khanates were also fragmenting, and the ties that had linked Eurasian courts and merchants weakened. This did not end long-distance exchange, but it changed its political framework. The Great Yuan court that withdrew to the steppe remained one of several Mongol powers, and its relations with Ming China became a lasting concern.

Neighboring states responded to the new situation. Korea, under the Goryeo court, had been tied to the Yuan by marriage and service and now had to decide whom to recognize; Japan, whose coasts and merchants were already active in maritime trade, became a concern of the early Ming. Ming foreign relations therefore grew out of the Yuan transition rather than beginning from zero, and Chinese rulers, generals, and envoys were active participants in shaping them. Chapter 42 follows the maritime dimension.

Section 40.10

Chapter Review and Yuan-Ming Transition Checkpoint

This chapter has traced a chain of transformation. Systemic crisis in the late Yuan, made acute by floods, forced labor, and inflation, became rebellion; rebellion fragmented into competing regional states; and one of those states, under Zhu Yuanzhang, became a dynasty by developing the capacity to win wars, collect revenue, recruit administrators, and rebuild. Students should now be able to explain each link, and to explain why the process cannot be reduced to a dynastic cycle or to the character of a single ruler.

Before moving to Chapter 41, check that you can state what 1368 did and did not settle: the capital of the Yuan fell and a new dynasty was proclaimed, but the Great Yuan court survived in the north, loyalists held out for years, and the new regime's institutions were partly inherited. The Ming's mature government is the subject of the next chapter.

Historical Evidence for the Yuan-Ming Transition

Few transitions in Chinese history are as thickly documented, or as thoroughly shaped by the victors, as the passage from Yuan to Ming. Students should treat each category of source below as a window with a particular angle, not as a neutral record.

Official Histories: the Yuan shi and the Ming Taizu shilu

The Yuan shi was compiled in 1369–70 under the first Ming emperor, in a very short time, from Yuan court records and veritable records. It preserves enormous information about Yuan government, but its haste, its uneven editing, and its purpose of closing the books on a defeated dynasty mean that it must be used with care, especially for the last decades. The Ming Taizu shilu records the Hongwu reign and was revised more than once under later emperors, so its account of Zhu's early life, his rivals, and the circumstances of his rise reflects later political needs. It can establish the claims the court wished to make and the administrative facts it recorded; it cannot by itself establish motives, the private views of Zhu, or the experience of ordinary people.

Proclamations, Edicts, and Inscriptions

Founding proclamations, including those addressed to the north in 1367, show how the regime presented itself to different audiences. Stone inscriptions, temple records, and epitaphs written for officials and local figures supply independent evidence for local events, for the moral language elites used, and for the networks that joined the new regime. Their weakness is that they are commissioned texts, often flattering, and that they survive unevenly.

Local Gazetteers, Literati Writings, and Registers

Gazetteers, compiled later in the Ming, preserve local memories of flood, war, resettlement, and taxation, though they are also elite productions that sometimes smooth over conflict. Literati writings, letters, and poems provide eyewitness impressions, but few come from peasants, soldiers, or women. Household and land registers, once compiled, allow historians to see the state's categories and to estimate population, but registration undercounts evasion and varies by region.

Archaeology and Material Evidence

Excavation and survey at Nanjing's city walls, at tombs of Zhu's officers, and at military and kiln sites document the scale of construction and the organization of labor and materials. They can show size, technique, and logistics, but not intention. Evidence for popular religion, such as sectarian texts and temple practice, is especially hard to reconstruct, since hostile officials wrote most descriptions and surviving sectarian scriptures often date later.

Reading Conflicting Sources

Historians compare sources by asking who wrote them, when, for whom, and what they omit. Casualty and population numbers, in particular, are uncertain estimates, not measurements. Where accounts conflict, as in the death of Han Lin'er, the responsible approach is to present the competing readings and say what evidence would settle the question.

Chapter Summary

The Yuan fell through the interaction of fiscal breakdown, flooding, forced labor, currency failure, and political fragmentation. Red Turban rebellions gave organized form to popular distress but did not form a single movement. From among many regional regimes, Zhu Yuanzhang's succeeded by securing Nanjing, disciplining troops, recruiting administrators, and defeating rivals at Poyang and Suzhou. The 1368 founding of the Ming and the fall of Dadu were major turning points, but Yuan power survived in the north. The Hongwu regime rebuilt agriculture and registration through resettlement and registers, while creating an autocratic political order shaped by wartime suspicion.

The chapter's central claim is that the Ming was not the automatic result of Yuan failure. The Yuan's weaknesses, including factional court politics, a fragile fiscal system, and a paper currency that lost credibility, made it vulnerable, but they did not determine who would replace it. Environmental disaster on the Yellow River and Huai plains supplied the shock, while forced labor and inflation turned the shock into grievance.

Rebellion took many forms. Red Turban movements drew on Maitreyan and related traditions, and northern and southern complexes developed separately. Landowner militias, Yuan commanders, and independent warlords added further layers, so the period is best described as a civil war among several regional regimes rather than a single uprising against the Yuan.

Regional differences shaped outcomes. Nanjing and the lower Yangtze gave Zhu a productive, defensible base. His rivals in the middle Yangtze, Suzhou, and Zhejiang had their own resources but weaker administrative cohesion, and the Poyang campaign and the sieges of 1366–67 showed how logistics, river power, and discipline decided the contest.

The founding of 1368 was a turning point, but the Yuan court survived in the north, and campaigns against loyalists continued into the 1380s. Ming rhetoric of expelling the Mongols coexisted with the incorporation of non-Han personnel and the inheritance of Yuan household and military categories.

Reconstruction affected groups unevenly. Settlers, military households, artisans, women, and Jiangnan elites each bore different burdens and opportunities, and the state's registers and relocations show both its capacity and its coercion. The Hongwu order grew from wartime suspicion and produced a strongly personal monarchy.

The evidence for this period requires care. Ming-compiled histories shaped the story of both the Yuan's end and Zhu's rise, and the sources for popular religion and everyday suffering are fragmentary. Historians therefore combine official records, gazetteers, epitaphs, and archaeology, and treat triumphant narratives as arguments.

Finally, the chapter's three-part logic can be stated simply: systemic crisis became rebellion, rebellion became competing regional states, and one of those states became a dynasty by building a territorial government. That sequence shows that dynastic change was a process with many actors, regional variation, and uncertain outcomes.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter shows that dynastic change was a process of civil war and reconstruction, not a clean switch. It connects the Mongol Eurasian world of Chapter 39 to the Ming institutions of Chapter 41, and reveals how environment, finance, religion, and local militarization together shaped political outcomes.

It also invites students to read triumphant narratives critically, to recognize the survival of the Northern Yuan, and to see Chinese history as regionally varied, contested, and unfinished.

Later Chinese history inherited much from this transition: a capital moved between Nanjing and Beijing, a registration-based tax system, a military household model, a persistent northern frontier problem, and a suspicion of sectarian movements. These legacies were not inevitable, and later chapters show how they were adapted.

The chapter also connects China to wider Eurasian history. The end of Mongol rule in China was part of the unraveling of a continental empire, and the Ming's relations with the Northern Yuan, Korea, and maritime Asia took shape in that aftermath. Understanding the Yuan-Ming transition thus teaches students to see state formation as regional, contingent, and shaped by environment, finance, belief, and war.
