Part IV — Division, Migration, and Religious Transformation
23

Buddhism, Migration, and Cultural Transformation in the Age of Division

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

In the 540s, an official named Yang Xuanzhi walked through the ruins of Luoyang. A little more than a decade earlier, the Northern Wei capital had held well over a thousand Buddhist temples, a nine-story wooden pagoda visible from far across the plain, and a population that included northern aristocrats, Central Asian merchants, Indian and Sogdian monks, and refugees from the south. Then civil war split the Wei state, the court moved to Ye, and the city was stripped of much of its population. Yang set out to write down what he remembered, temple by temple, before it vanished from memory. His book is a work of mourning, but it is also a census of a transformed world: a capital whose most conspicuous buildings were neither imperial halls nor Confucian academies, but monasteries.

This chapter asks what the Age of Division actually did to Chinese society. Chapters 19 through 22 followed the political sequence: the collapse of Han authority, the Three Kingdoms, the brief Western Jin reunification and its breakdown, the Sixteen Kingdoms of the north, the Eastern Jin and Southern Dynasties, and the Northern Wei and its successor states. Those chapters explained who ruled where, and why regimes rose and fell. They could not fully explain what several centuries of political fragmentation meant for the millions of people who lived through it. This chapter steps back from the succession of courts to examine the deeper currents that ran beneath them: migration, religious change, regional development, ethnic interaction, family life, and artistic creativity.

The central argument is that the period from the end of the Han in 220 CE to the Sui reunification in 589 was one of the most transformative in Chinese history, and that this transformation is easy to miss if the period is read only as a gap between two great empires. Migration redistributed population and elite culture across the map, moving northern families into the Yangtze region and moving pastoral and frontier peoples deep into the northern plains. Buddhism, which had been a marginal foreign presence in Han times, became a religion embedded in households, courts, landscapes, and economies. Daoism developed from popular healing and millenarian movements into organized traditions with scriptures, ordination, and liturgy. The south grew in population and productivity. The north became a society of mixed origins in which the distinction between "Han" and "non-Han" was repeatedly negotiated and often blurred.

Students should be careful about two opposite errors. The first is to treat the period as a catastrophe, an age of chaos from which Chinese civilization was rescued by reunification. The evidence does not support that view. Warfare, displacement, and the collapse of regimes caused enormous suffering, and this chapter does not minimize it, but the same centuries also produced new religious institutions, new literary and artistic forms, new agricultural regions, and new mixtures of peoples. The second error is to treat all of this as leading inevitably to the Sui and Tang. The societies that the Sui eventually brought together in 589 were the products of contingency, conflict, and choice, and they were not preparing themselves for reunification. Historians can identify legacies without assuming purpose.

The chapter also asks students to think carefully about scale and evidence. Some of the best sources for this period concern elites: family instructions, biographies in the standard histories, poetry, and monastic biographies. Others, such as cave temples, donor inscriptions, tomb furnishings, and household registers from the northwest, allow glimpses of nuns, artisans, soldiers, lay devotees, and farmers. The gap between these two kinds of evidence is a recurring theme. A great deal of what can be said with confidence concerns the powerful; historians work to bring other people into view without inventing what cannot be known.

Geography organizes the chapter. The north, with its dry-land agriculture, garrison frontiers, and connection to the Eurasian steppe and Central Asian oases, developed differently from the south, with its wet-rice fields, river networks, and maritime connections. But north and south were never sealed off. Monks traveled between Jiankang and Chang’an, texts and craftsmen crossed the frontier, and ideas moved along the same roads as armies and merchants. Comparing the two regions, without treating either as normal, is one of the chapter’s main tasks.

By its end, students should be able to explain how migration, religion, ethnic interaction, family organization, and economic change reshaped Chinese society between the Han and the Sui; to compare the northern and southern experience; to evaluate the evidence historians use for religion, identity, and everyday life in a period with limited administrative records; and to explain why the label "Age of Division" describes a political condition but not a cultural one. The chapter draws on the political narratives of Chapters 19 through 22 without retelling them, and it stops short of the Sui reunification, which is the subject of Chapter 24.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Trace the major migrations of the third through sixth centuries, including northern refugees in the south and forced and voluntary relocations within northern states.
  2. Explain how migration changed both migrants and receiving societies in language, landholding, administration, and regional identity.
  3. Analyze why Buddhism expanded so widely between the Han and the Sui, giving attention to monks, nuns, lay devotees, rulers, and ordinary households.
  4. Describe the work of translation teams, the institutions of monastic learning, and the pilgrimage and trade networks linking China to Central Asia and India.
  5. Interpret cave temples and sacred landscapes as evidence of patronage, pilgrimage, regional style, and social organization.
  6. Compare the development of Celestial Masters, Shangqing, and Lingbao traditions and assess the interaction and competition between Daoism and Buddhism.
  7. Evaluate evidence for cultural exchange and changing identities among Han and non-Han communities without treating either category as fixed.
  8. Compare aristocratic society, family organization, and women’s roles in northern and southern regimes, while distinguishing elite norms from ordinary life.
  9. Assess the relationship between earlier southern development and northern migration in shaping the economy of the Yangtze region.
  10. Distinguish the legacies of the Age of Division from any claim that the period inevitably led to Sui and Tang reunification.

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  2. How did migration change the societies that sent migrants, the societies that received them, and the migrants themselves?
  3. Why did Buddhism attract followers in courts, monasteries, and villages, and did these groups seek the same things from the religion?
  4. In what sense was translation an act of interpretation, and how did it shape Chinese Buddhist thought?

Historical Context

The Han Empire, examined in Chapters 14 through 18, had governed a population that its census of 2 CE recorded at roughly fifty-eight million people, spread across the Yellow River plain, the Yangtze basin, Sichuan, and the frontier commanderies of the northwest and south. Its institutions included a bureaucracy recruited through recommendation, a tax system based on household registration, and a state-sponsored Confucian learning that gave officials a shared vocabulary. By the second century CE, the empire was weakened by court factionalism, land concentration, and epidemic and rebellion. The Yellow Turban uprising of 184 and the warlord wars that followed, discussed in Chapter 18, broke the central administration into competing regional armies. When Cao Pi accepted the abdication of the last Han emperor in 220, the empire did not end in a single moment so much as it ceased to have a plausible unitary successor.

The centuries that followed are conventionally known as the Age of Division, and the sequence is examined in detail in the preceding chapters. Three states, Cao Wei, Shu Han, and Eastern Wu, competed from 220 to 280 (Chapter 19). The Western Jin then briefly reunited them, but by 311 the collapse known as the Yongjia Crisis had ended its power in the north (Chapter 20). For roughly a century and a quarter, from 304 to 439, a shifting group of regimes, later classified as the Sixteen Kingdoms, ruled parts of the north, many of them founded by leaders from Xiongnu, Jie, Di, Qiang, and Xianbei backgrounds. In the south, the Eastern Jin and then the Liu Song, Southern Qi, Liang, and Chen dynasties governed from Jiankang, near modern Nanjing (Chapter 21). In the north, the Tuoba Xianbei founded the Northern Wei, unified the north in 439, and later divided into Eastern and Western Wei and then Northern Qi and Northern Zhou (Chapter 22). This chapter presupposes that narrative and asks a different question of it.

That question needs a demographic and social frame. The registered population fell sharply in the third century. Western Jin recorded about 2.5 million households and roughly sixteen million people in 280 CE. Registered numbers, however, measure the reach of the state as much as the number of living persons: households sheltered by great families, families fleeing conflict, and communities outside administrative control disappear from the counts. Historians therefore treat the drop as evidence of both real mortality and displacement and of a decline in the state’s capacity to count. The registers of the period are informative less as a census than as a record of how governments tried, and often failed, to keep people fixed on the land.

The religious background was equally unsettled. Confucian learning and the ritual system of the Han court had lost their institutional anchor. Buddhism had arrived by the first or second century CE along the oasis routes and the southern coasts, and some translation work had begun in Luoyang under figures such as the Parthian An Shigao and the Kushan-connected Lokaksema in the second century. But the religion remained small, urban, and often associated with foreign communities. Alongside it, popular healing cults, millenarian movements such as the Way of Great Peace and the Way of the Celestial Masters, and long-standing practices of ancestor worship and local deities shaped religious life without any single institution governing them. The question of what would replace the Han synthesis was, in this sense, open.

The environmental and economic background also matters. The north depended on dry-land agriculture in millet, wheat, and beans, with pastoral and mixed economies on its frontier; the south depended increasingly on irrigated rice and the water routes of the Yangtze, Huai, and Zhejiang rivers. The boundary between them was not a single line but a shifting zone across the Huai River valley, the Han River, and the Sichuan basin, where control passed repeatedly among northern and southern states. Chapter 2 introduced these regions, and Chapter 21 discussed the beginnings of southern development. Here the focus is on cumulative effects over three centuries.

Finally, students should recall that "China" in this period was not a single state, and that no ruler in the north or south could claim to represent the whole civilization without contest. Southern dynasties regarded themselves as heirs of the Han and Jin and treated the north as occupied territory. Northern regimes, whether founded by Xianbei or other leaders, claimed legitimacy through their own combinations of steppe traditions, Chinese ritual and administration, and Buddhist patronage. Chapters 20 and 22 discussed these claims; this chapter shows how they interacted with everyday religion, family, and economy.

Historical Evidence

Historians of the Age of Division have unusually varied evidence, but its variety produces distortions. The standard histories of the period, including the Records of the Three Kingdoms, the Book of Jin, the Book of Wei, and the histories of the Southern and Northern Dynasties, were compiled largely by scholar-officials at courts, and several were written after the regimes they describe had ended. They are strongest on politics and on elite biography and weakest on non-elite communities. The Book of Wei includes a treatise on Buddhism and Daoism, a rare official account of religious institutions, but its author had his own loyalties and reflects a court perspective.

Religious texts are another major source. Buddhist biographies such as the Lives of Eminent Monks and the Lives of Nuns, translation prefaces, monastic catalogs, and pilgrimage accounts record ideals, institutional history, and the conflicts of the clergy. They were written by believers, often with hagiographic purposes, and they sometimes reproduce miracle stories. Daoist texts, including revelations, ritual manuals, and catalogs, similarly come from communities that claimed divine authority for their scriptures. Historians read both types for what the authors wanted their audiences to believe, and also for what these claims reveal about religious practice.

Material evidence complements the texts. Cave temples such as Yungang, Longmen, and Mogao preserve sculpture, painting, and donor inscriptions. Stone steles record the names of lay societies and donors, including women and men of modest means. Tombs, with their wall paintings, pottery figurines, and epitaphs, illustrate burial customs, dress, and connections to Central Asia, as in the tomb furnishings of Sogdian officials in the sixth century. Manuscripts from Dunhuang and Turfan, though many are later than this chapter’s period, include household registers and Buddhist texts that show how documents circulated in oasis communities.

A few specialized texts deserve particular mention. The Essential Techniques for the Common People (Qimin yaoshu), a sixth-century agricultural manual associated with Jia Sixie, describes farming methods in the northern plain. Li Daoyuan’s Commentary on the Waterways Classic describes rivers and landscapes across the north and beyond. Liu Yiqing’s New Account of Tales of the World collects anecdotes about southern elite conversation and conduct. Each reflects the interests of a literate minority. Where an administrative record or an inscription survives, it can correct or complicate such accounts, but such records are scarce.

This chapter therefore reads three kinds of sources together: narrative texts that show official and elite frames, religious and family writings that show ideals and practices, and material remains that show donors, artisans, and pilgrims. When students meet a claim about "the north" or "the south" in the sections below, they should ask whose evidence supports it and which groups are missing from it.

Section 23.1

Migration and the Remaking of Chinese Society

Migration was the largest social process of the Age of Division, and it did not happen once. Earlier chapters described particular episodes: the flight of northern families and the Jin court after the fall of Luoyang in 311, the deportations and resettlements carried out by the Sixteen Kingdoms, and the Northern Wei’s relocation of people to Pingcheng and later to Luoyang. Taken together, these episodes redistributed people across the map for more than two centuries. They moved farmers, artisans, soldiers, clergy, scholars, and aristocrats, sometimes voluntarily, often under compulsion, and they moved them in more than one direction. This section considers what such movements meant for the societies that sent people away and for those that received them.

Movement to the South

Northern families moved south in waves, with the largest movements coming in the first half of the fourth century as warfare, famine, and the collapse of Western Jin authority pushed people toward the Huai and Yangtze regions. They continued in smaller flows as northern regimes fell or as campaigns disrupted the frontier. Estimates of the total are uncertain because they depend on registration figures. Using a government record from 464 CE, the modern historian Tan Qixiang estimated that successive waves of southward migration since the Yongjia crisis had added roughly one million northerners to the southern population, about one-sixth of the registered Liu Song population. This is a reconstruction from later registration evidence rather than a direct count and should be treated as approximate. Some migrants were aristocrats with retinues, some were peasant households moving in groups under a local leader, and some were military units that stayed together as armed communities.

The southern governments faced an administrative problem: how to register and tax a large population that regarded itself as temporary. The solution was the creation of "sojourner commanderies" and counties, which carried the names of northern places and initially registered migrants separately from local households. The migrants were listed in what sources call "white registers," while established southern households appeared in "yellow registers." The arrangement often exempted migrants from some taxes and labor, which pleased them and irritated local households, and it also reduced the revenue of the state. Governments periodically carried out "land-division" reforms (tuduan), most notably in 364 and in 413, that reassigned migrants to ordinary local registration. Progress was uneven, because powerful families could protect their dependents, and the process illustrates the recurring tension between the state’s desire to count people and the interests of those who benefited from not being counted.

Relations between northern migrants and local southern families were shaped by rank and by politics. Southern families of Wu and Yue origin, such as the Gu, Lu, Zhu, and Zhang lineages of the lower Yangtze, had held office under Eastern Wu and possessed land, retainers, and local prestige. The northern aristocratic families that arrived with the Jin court, such as the Wang of Langya and the Xie of Chenjun, controlled the highest offices in the new regime. The relationship combined cooperation and competition. Marriages between the groups occurred, southern elites received offices, and northern families acquired estates in the Kuaiji region and elsewhere, often in less densely settled areas where land could be developed. The court’s dependence on the military, especially on the "Beifu" troops raised from northern refugees in the Jingkou area under Xie Xuan in the late fourth century, gave migrants an additional political role that became important during the wars of the late fourth and early fifth centuries.

Language and speech marked identity. Sixth-century observers such as Yan Zhitui commented that the elite speech of the southern capital derived from the northern koine of the Luoyang region, while the speech of the lower Yangtze region had distinct features, and that both differed from what could be heard in the northern regimes of his own time. Historians infer that the dialects of the Yangtze delta, and later the Wu-speaking region, formed through contact between older local varieties and the speech of northern arrivals, but the details are hard to reconstruct. A rhyme dictionary prepared shortly after this period, the Qieyun of 601, was compiled in part to reconcile variant northern and southern pronunciations, showing that contemporaries were aware that the language they read aloud had regional forms.

Movement Within and Into the North

In the north, migration meant something different. Rulers of successive states used deportation as a state-building tool, moving populations to capitals, agricultural districts, and garrison zones. According to the Book of Wei, the Northern Wei ruler moved hundreds of thousands of people, including many artisans, into the Pingcheng region in the late fourth century. The figures are reported by an official history and should be treated as approximate, but the practice itself is well documented. These forced movements concentrated skills, labor, and sometimes elite families around the court, and they mixed populations from different regions. The later movement of the Wei court to Luoyang in the 490s repeated the pattern on a large scale, bringing the northern military aristocracy and many others to the Central Plains.

Not all northern migration was compelled. Some families relocated to the northwest, where the Former Liang and later regimes in the Gansu corridor preserved scholarly and administrative traditions from the Han period and hosted Buddhist and other communities. Others moved to the northeast, into the Liaodong and Liaoxi regions under the Murong Xianbei, whose states drew in refugees and offered them positions. In Sichuan, the Cheng-Han state established by Di leaders from the Hanzhong region in 304 depended on the movement of large populations into the basin, including groups known in the sources as Liao, from upland areas. When the Eastern Jin conquered Sichuan in 347, the region was reintegrated into a southern regime with a population that had been reshaped by these movements.

Many northerners who stayed behind did not simply submit to changing regimes; they organized their own defense. Fortified villages and strongholds, sometimes called wu or bi, sheltered lineages and dependents under local leaders, particularly in the chaos of the early fourth century. These communities collected supplies, trained militias, and negotiated with successive powers. They illustrate an important point about the north: local society continued to operate through kinship and community organization even when central authority changed frequently. When the Northern Wei introduced its own systems of household registration and land allocation, discussed in Chapter 22, it had to work with, and sometimes against, these local structures.

Section 23.2

Buddhism’s Expansion Across China

In 220 CE, Buddhism in China was small. It had a handful of translators, a few communities in Luoyang and the Yangtze region, and a limited audience among elites. By the sixth century, it was a mass religious institution with thousands of monasteries, a large clergy, a lay following that included people at every level of society, and a physical presence in cities, mountains, and desert oases. Some northern source traditions report tens of thousands of monasteries at particular peaks, but such figures varied greatly by regime and date, were affected by persecution, and cannot be treated as precise censuses. The rate and extent of this change is one of the most striking features of the period, and explaining it requires more than pointing to the patronage of rulers.

Why Buddhism Spread

Historians propose several complementary explanations. One is political uncertainty. After the Han collapse, the ritual and philosophical order that had legitimized the empire lost its institutional anchor, and many people, from aristocrats to farmers, looked for explanations of suffering and for practices that promised protection. Buddhism offered teachings about karma and rebirth, a moral code, and rituals of merit, and it offered a monastic community that could operate within any regime. A second explanation concerns rulers. For leaders of states in the north who came from frontier or steppe backgrounds, Buddhism was a religion that was not identified with the Han imperial tradition, had connections across Central Asia, and provided monks who could serve as advisers, ritual specialists, and sometimes diplomats. The monk Fotudeng, for example, served the Later Zhao rulers in the early fourth century and gained a reputation for divination and healing.

A third explanation is economic and social. Monasteries received land, labor, and donations, and they could provide services: lodging for travelers, lending, medical care, education, and storage. Ordinary people could contribute small amounts to lay associations that sponsored images and inscriptions, and could gain a share of merit that they might transfer to deceased parents or to the emperor. Merit was an especially adaptable idea in a society that already placed great weight on obligations to ancestors. The religion could therefore be presented as a form of filial piety, an argument that Chinese Buddhists made repeatedly against critics who accused monks of abandoning their families.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Geyi (格義), traditionally “matching concepts”

Geyi is a term used in early Chinese Buddhist sources for a method of exposition practiced by some fourth-century teachers. The traditional account, long repeated in histories of Chinese Buddhism, holds that geyi meant explaining Buddhist ideas by matching them to familiar Chinese, especially Daoist and Confucian, concepts, for example by relating emptiness to non-being (wu). On that view, Dao’an and later the translators around Kumārajīva marked a turn away from such matching toward more faithful renderings.

That account is now seriously disputed. Victor Mair has argued that geyi was a short-lived exegetical technique built on numbered doctrinal categories, not a general strategy of translating Buddhism through Daoist vocabulary, and other scholars question the older picture of a sharp paradigm shift under Dao’an. Historians agree on a broader point: Buddhist ideas entered Chinese through choices of terminology and explanation, and the Chinese vocabulary that resulted was shaped by the philosophical language available to translators and teachers, whatever the label given to one particular method. Students should treat “matching concepts” as one historiographical position, not as settled fact.

Monks, Nuns, and Lay Devotees

The Buddhist community was not a single group. The clergy, or sangha, included monks and nuns who took vows, lived in monasteries, and followed monastic rules. Knowledge of those rules grew as translations of the disciplinary texts (vinaya) appeared in the early fifth century. Chinese monks adopted the surname Shi, from the Buddha’s clan name Shijia (Sakya), a practice associated with the monk Dao’an in the fourth century, and the adoption signaled membership in a new kind of family. Women entered the religion as well. The Lives of Nuns, compiled around 516 by the monk Baochang, records more than sixty women of the fourth through early sixth centuries, from noble households and from more modest backgrounds, and it presents Jingjian as an early Chinese nun. The book is an idealized collection, but it establishes that female monastic communities existed in both regions and that some nuns became teachers, founders, and confidantes of the court.

Lay devotees far outnumbered the ordained. Some were aristocrats who studied doctrine and wrote on the subject, some were officials who supported temples, and many were households that made offerings and commissioned images. Lay associations, known in inscriptions as yi (or, in later usage, she), pooled resources to sponsor statues, sutra copying, and vegetarian feasts. Steles that record these associations list members by name and, in many cases, by social role, including monks and nuns, local officials, village heads, and women, and they show that Buddhist devotion could be organized at the level of a village or lineage. The dedication commonly asks that merit go to the donors’ parents, ancestors, fellow villagers, and the ruler, a pattern that links the new religion to older ideas of family obligation and collective welfare.

North and South

Northern and southern Buddhism were connected but distinct. In the north, the religion was closely tied to states. The Northern Wei created an official office to supervise the clergy, and early leaders of that office, such as the monk Faguo, reportedly presented the ruler as an embodiment of the Buddha, which made obeisance to the emperor an act of worship. Northern Buddhism gave prominent attention to meditation, to the making of images, and to communal merit, and its most visible monuments are cave temples and large urban monasteries. It also depended on the state for land and supervision, which made it vulnerable to political shifts, as the persecutions of 446 under the Northern Wei and of 574 to 577 under the Northern Zhou show.

In the south, Buddhism developed in the milieu of aristocratic conversation and doctrinal debate. Monks such as Zhi Dun in the fourth century interacted with the literati who practiced "pure conversation" and studied the Dark Learning (xuanxue), and the shared vocabulary of emptiness and non-being made the exchange possible. Huiyuan, who led a community at Mount Lu, argued that monks should not be required to bow to secular rulers, a claim that reflected the southern clergy’s attempt to maintain a space independent of the court. Southern rulers were also important patrons. Emperor Wu of Liang, who reigned from 502 to 549, supported monasteries, lectured on scripture, promoted vegetarian rules for clergy, and made highly publicized gifts of himself to a temple, from which ministers redeemed him with large payments. These acts expressed personal piety and served, in effect, as a way of transferring wealth to the monastic community and asserting the ruler’s stature as a bodhisattva-like figure.

Buddhism did not provoke only admiration. Critics questioned the doctrine of rebirth, the economic burden of monasteries, and the moral status of monks. The southern thinker Fan Zhen wrote a treatise arguing that the spirit did not survive the body and provoked a court-sponsored refutation. Others objected that the clergy avoided taxes and labor obligations. Buddhism also became a vehicle for popular movements. In the early sixth century, uprisings in the north drew on Buddhist messianic language, notably the revolt led by the monk Faqing in 515, which claimed that a new Buddha had arrived. Religion could therefore support the state, provide autonomy from it, or challenge it. Any account of Buddhism’s rise must include all three roles.

Cause and Effect

The Expansion of Buddhism, 220–589

Long-term causes

Trade and diplomatic networks linking China to Central Asia and India; the weakening of the Han imperial ritual and philosophical order; existing practices of ancestor veneration and merit that could accommodate new ideas; and the availability of texts and teachers along the oasis and maritime routes.

Short-term causes

War, displacement, famine, and epidemics that increased demand for consolation and protection; rulers in both regions who wanted legitimacy and advisers with links to wider networks; and the initiative of translators, monks, and lay associations who created institutions.

Event or transformation

Between the third and sixth centuries, Buddhist communities expanded from relatively limited networks of translators and devotees into widespread monastic and lay institutions across northern and southern societies, supported by translation, ordination, patronage, pilgrimage, image-making, and local devotional associations.

Immediate effects

Construction of large numbers of temples and cave sanctuaries; growth of the clergy; new landholding and labor arrangements around monasteries; and debates over taxation, loyalty, and filial obligation.

Long-term effects

A religion deeply rooted in household, village, and court life; a large body of Chinese-language scripture and commentary; new artistic traditions; and continuing competition and interaction with Daoism and Confucian learning.

Historical complexity: No single factor explains the expansion. Rulers mattered, but state patronage also came after, and in response to, changes among clergy and lay communities. Explanations that emphasize only political chaos overlook the active work of translators and monastic institutions; explanations that emphasize only doctrine overlook the material and social reasons people supported temples.

Section 23.3

Translation, Monasteries, and Buddhist Learning

A religion that arrives in another language must be translated, and the translators of the Age of Division did more than convert words. Their work created a Chinese vocabulary for ideas that had no obvious equivalents, produced scriptures that could be read, recited, and copied, and established institutions to preserve them. This section examines the translation projects, the monasteries that hosted them, and the networks that carried people and texts across long distances.

Translators and Translation Teams

Early translation in the second and third centuries was often carried out by foreign monks, working with Chinese assistants, in small groups. An Shigao, of Parthian origin, translated meditation manuals and doctrinal texts in Luoyang, and Zhi Qian and Zhu Fahu, both of Central Asian descent, produced translations in the third century, including an early version of the Lotus Sutra by the latter. As Buddhism grew, translation became an organized enterprise. Bilingual monks recited the text, others checked meaning, Chinese scholars refined the prose, and scribes copied the result. Such teams, common by the fourth century, allowed several people to review a work, though they also meant that the final text represented a negotiated result rather than the voice of a single translator.

The best-known project was directed by Kumārajīva. Born in the oasis kingdom of Kucha, he was captured in a northern campaign and held for many years in the northwest before the Later Qin ruler Yao Xing brought him to Chang’an in 401. There, with the support of the court and the assistance of a large community of monks, he translated a substantial body of Mahayana literature, including the Lotus Sutra, the Vimalakirti Sutra, and texts of the Madhyamaka school. Sources report that many hundreds of monks took part, although the numbers in Buddhist biographies are probably enlarged. His translations are noted for readable style and for an effort to convey meaning rather than word for word, and his disciples, including Sengzhao, produced original Chinese treatises on emptiness that show the maturing of Chinese Buddhist thought. Other centers were also productive: Dharmaksema translated at the Northern Liang court in the northwest, Buddhabhadra worked at Mount Lu and at Jiankang, and Paramartha, who arrived in the south in the 540s, produced translations of Yogacara texts under uncertain conditions during the wars of the period. In the north, Bodhiruci and Ratnamati, working at Luoyang in the early sixth century, translated and commented on texts that influenced later doctrinal schools.

Translation shaped ideas. Terms such as dao, xin (mind or heart), and wu (non-being), which were already part of Chinese philosophy, acquired additional meanings in Buddhist contexts. New terms and transliterations entered the language. Debates over the Buddha-nature, sudden versus gradual enlightenment, and the fate of the wicked were carried on in commentaries in both regions. Chinese Buddhists also composed scriptures presented as translations from Indian originals but written in China, often called apocryphal scriptures. Some, such as texts stressing the debt owed to parents, addressed distinctively Chinese concerns about filial piety, and they show that Buddhism was not only imported but actively adapted.

Monasteries as Institutions

Monasteries were centers of prayer and study, but they were also economic and social institutions. Large urban temples, such as those in Luoyang recorded by Yang Xuanzhi, owned land, hosted festivals, and displayed images and pagodas that shaped the city’s skyline. Rural monasteries controlled estates worked by tenants and dependents. In the Northern Wei, a policy adopted in the later fifth century allowed for "sangha households" that delivered grain to the clergy, and "Buddha households" that provided labor, both of which linked lay obligations to monastic support. In the south, monasteries lent money and goods through institutions sometimes called "inexhaustible treasuries," a practice that made them financial intermediaries as well as religious centers. These roles brought benefits to people who used the services, but also raised concerns about tax evasion and the concentration of resources, which is why rulers periodically tried to regulate ordination and monastic wealth.

Monastic learning developed in stages. Vinaya texts were translated in the early fifth century, giving communities clearer models of discipline, although different versions circulated and monasteries often mixed rules. Catalogs of scriptures, beginning with the fourth-century work of Dao’an and continuing with the early sixth-century catalog of Sengyou, sorted translations by date and authenticity and thereby shaped a canon. Monasteries also trained scholars in lecturing and debate, and they housed libraries. Students of the period should note that only a small number of clergy were engaged in doctrinal study; many were ritual specialists, artisans, and administrators, and some were ordained mainly to avoid taxation.

Pilgrimage and Networks

The movement of texts depended on the movement of people. Faxian set out from Chang’an around 399 with companions, crossed the northwestern deserts, traveled through Khotan and Gandhara into the Ganges valley, and later reached Sri Lanka before returning by sea to the Shandong coast around 412. His record describes monasteries, ritual practices, and political conditions in the places he visited. In 518 the Northern Wei court sent a mission under the lay official Song Yun and the monk Huisheng to seek scriptures in the northwest of the Indian subcontinent; its account survives through Yang Xuanzhi’s work. These journeys were rare, but they were part of a larger pattern of monks, merchants, and diplomats traveling among the oasis states of the Tarim basin, the cities of Central Asia, and the kingdoms of India.

Sea routes also mattered. Monks and merchants traveled by ship between Southeast Asia, South Asia, and the ports of Guangzhou and the Yangtze delta, and Paramartha reached China this way. The connections were not limited to Buddhism. Along with monks came traders, craftsmen, and envoys, and along with scriptures came images, medicines, music, and new artistic forms. The next section examines one of the most visible results: the transformation of landscapes through cave temples and sacred sites.

Section 23.4

Cave Temples, Sculpture, and Sacred Landscapes

Chapter 22 introduced the Northern Wei cave temples at Yungang and Longmen as expressions of imperial patronage and as part of the story of the Wei state. This section takes a wider view. Cave temples and other sacred sites were places where communities acted out their religion, and they were also products of networks of exchange, donors, craftsmen, and pilgrims. Comparing them shows that Buddhist art in the Age of Division was not a single style but a set of regional and social responses to a religion with many centers.

What a Cave Temple Was

Cave temples were sanctuaries cut into cliffs or mountain slopes, an architectural form that came to China from India and Central Asia by way of oasis sites such as Kucha and, further west, Bamiyan. Their functions varied. Some contained meditation cells for monks, some housed colossal images for communal worship, and some served as places for circumambulation, a ritual of walking around a sacred image or pillar. Cutting a cave required a large investment of labor and money, and the sculptures, paintings, and inscriptions that filled them were made by artisans working within traditions of workshop training. Because the carvings often include names of donors and dedications, they are among the few sources in which people outside the elite speak, though always through the formulas of the inscription.

Yungang, Longmen, and the Court

At Yungang, near the Northern Wei capital of Pingcheng, work began in the 460s under the supervision of the monk Tanyao, and it produced large caves with colossal seated Buddhas. The early caves show influences from Gandhara and Central Asia in their figure styles and drapery, which reflects the movement of craftsmen and images into the Wei territory as captured populations and trade brought in artisans. At Longmen, near the new capital of Luoyang, the work that began soon after the move of the court in the 490s shows a shift in style. Buddhas and attendants appear in robes resembling the court dress of the period, with elongated proportions and flowing lines, which many art historians link to the cultural policies of the Wei rulers and to influences from southern artistic traditions. The change illustrates how sculpture recorded shifting ideas about how a Buddha should look in a Chinese setting.

Court projects were only part of the story. Longmen’s walls are filled with hundreds of smaller niches and inscriptions donated by individuals and groups: clergy, officials, military men, lay societies, and families. Many dedications name the donors’ deceased parents, wish for rebirth in a better realm, or ask for protection for the ruler and the realm. The patterns of donations show that the site was a shared space in which people of varied status could participate, each according to means. At the same time, the prime locations and largest caves were commissioned by the court and aristocracy, and the site’s hierarchy of scale reflects the hierarchy of society.

Dunhuang and the Oasis Network

The Mogao caves near Dunhuang, at the western end of the Gansu corridor, stand at a different kind of place: a garrison and trade center connecting the Chinese heartland to the Tarim basin. Tradition dates the beginning of the caves to 366, when a monk is said to have had a vision there, and archaeological evidence confirms that caves were being cut and painted during the fifth century under the rule of regional states such as the Northern Liang and later the Northern Wei. Cave 285, decorated in the sixth century under the Western Wei and containing dated inscriptions from 538 and 539, is especially informative. Its paintings combine Indian and Central Asian Buddhist themes with figures from Chinese mythology, such as Fuxi and Nüwa and celestial beings associated with immortality beliefs, and its donors’ inscriptions record the involvement of local elites. Dunhuang therefore shows how a frontier community drew on sources from several directions and blended them.

The sites in the northwest, such as Maijishan in Gansu and the Bingling Temple, extend this picture. Their clay sculptures and paintings show connections to both the Central Asian oases and the Wei court, and they suggest that monasteries in mountains and along river valleys served travelers and local communities alike. Similar networks operated in the northeast, where the Northern Qi dynasty sponsored cave temples at Xiangtangshan near its capital Ye, and around Taiyuan.

The South and Sacred Mountains

The south had fewer large cave complexes but no lack of Buddhist art. Sculptors in the Yangtze region created stone and bronze images, carved steles, and shrines, and there are important Southern Dynasties remains near Jiankang and in Sichuan. Excavated sculptures from Chengdu, dating to the Liang period, show links across the Yangtze to Central Asian and maritime influences. Historians have also found that sculptures from workshops in Shandong, such as those recovered from a temple site at Qingzhou in a deposit discovered in the 1990s, combined northern and southern features and thereby show interaction across the political frontier. Southern painting, known mainly through later copies and textual descriptions, was equally significant, with painters such as Gu Kaizhi developing narrative and portrait techniques and the critic Xie He formulating principles of painting in the early sixth century.

Mountains were sacred landscapes. Mount Lu in the south, Mount Song and Mount Wutai in the north, and Mount Mao near Jiankang attracted monasteries, hermitages, and Daoist retreats. Tradition holds that the Shaolin monastery on Mount Song was founded in the 490s for an Indian monk, although the details of its early history are hard to recover. The idea that a mountain could be a residence of holy beings or a place for spiritual practice drew on earlier Chinese beliefs and was reinforced by Buddhist and Daoist practice. Pilgrimage to such places was not only religious but also social, bringing together clergy, aristocrats, and local people.

Taken together, cave temples and sacred mountains made religion visible. They turned cliffs and valleys into shared spaces of ritual, and they gave patrons ways to display piety and status. They also register the interconnections of the period. The sculptors and painters who worked at these sites drew on Gandharan, Central Asian, Indian, northern Chinese, and southern Chinese traditions, and they adapted them to the tastes of patrons. Understanding sacred landscapes as products of interaction, rather than as isolated regional monuments, is one of the main lessons of the Age of Division.

Section 23.5

Daoist Religious Institutions

Buddhism’s growth invites the misleading conclusion that it was the only religion in motion. During these same centuries, organized Daoist communities and new scriptural traditions, with ordination, priests, and ritual calendars, expanded and diversified alongside older texts (discussed in Chapter 12), self-cultivation practices, healing traditions, and local religious movements. Modern scholars caution against describing this as a simple transition from “philosophical Daoism” to “religious Daoism.” The word "Daoism" covers many things in this period, and historians distinguish among lineages that competed and interacted. This section considers the major ones and their relationship to states, elites, and Buddhism.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Celestial Masters (天師道 Tianshi dao)

The Celestial Masters, also called the Way of the Celestial Masters or Way of Orthodox Unity in later usage, were a religious community that traditionally traces its origin to Zhang Daoling in Sichuan in 142 CE and to his successors. Followers held that the god Laozi had granted a covenant, organized themselves in local administrative units under priests known as libationers, kept household registers, made written confession of illness and misdeeds, and contributed grain to the community.

When the leader Zhang Lu submitted to Cao Cao in 215, many followers dispersed to other regions. From then on, "Celestial Masters" refers to a dispersed set of communities and family lineages rather than a single institution. It is best understood as a religious tradition that changed by region and by period: a household-based community in early Sichuan and Hanzhong, a gentry-supported religion among southern families, and a reformed court-associated order in the northern regime of Kou Qianzhi.

From Movement to Institution

The Celestial Masters community of the Han period built a system of local religious administration. After its dispersal in 215, the tradition spread among households in the north and south. Southern gentry families, including some of the great Langya Wang lineage, are recorded as adherents. Not all authorities welcomed the tradition. The movement’s emphasis on collective organization made it sometimes attractive as a base for popular action, and in the late fourth century a rebellion led by Sun En arose along the southeastern coast. Sun En and many of his followers were associated with Five Pecks of Rice/Celestial Masters traditions, although historians debate how directly the rebellion should be characterized as a Celestial Masters movement; political, fiscal, and regional grievances were also central. Daoist writers and priests, in turn, criticized practices they regarded as corrupt, including some rituals and the collection of fees, and worked to reform the tradition.

Southern Traditions: Ge Hong, Shangqing, and Lingbao

In the south, Daoist religion developed in the milieu of aristocratic families and their estates. Ge Hong, who lived in the early fourth century and belonged to a gentry family of the lower Yangtze region, wrote the Baopuzi, a work that discusses alchemy, dietary and breath practices, and the possibility of becoming an immortal (xian). He collected recipes and methods, defended the pursuit of immortality against skeptics, and combined his interest in religious cultivation with a commitment to Confucian ethics. His work shows that Daoist practice was not necessarily separated from officeholding or family duties.

The Shangqing ("Highest Clarity") revelations began in the 360s, when a medium named Yang Xi is said to have received messages from perfected beings, including a female figure identified as Lady Wei Huacun, at the request of the Xu family who were patrons in the Jiankang area. The texts describe meditation, visualization of inner deities, and ascent to heavens, and they present a path of individual cultivation open to those who could read and understand elaborate poetic scriptures. They were written in an elegant calligraphy and copied within elite circles, which made them an aristocratic religious literature. Later, Tao Hongjing (456 to 536), who withdrew to Mount Mao, gathered and edited these manuscripts and wrote commentaries. He also compiled works on medicine and pharmacology, corresponded with the court of Emperor Wu of Liang, and combined interests in Buddhism, Daoism, and Confucian learning. His life shows the fluidity of religious identity among educated men.

The Lingbao ("Numinous Treasure") scriptures, composed around the beginning of the fifth century, emphasized cosmic order, communal ritual, and the salvation of both the living and the dead. They drew on Buddhist ideas such as universal rescue, cycles of rebirth, and elaborated hell-like realms, and they presented liturgical retreats as ways to gain merit and avert misfortune. Lu Xiujing (406 to 477), a Daoist scholar from the lower Yangtze region, played an important role in organizing this material. In 471 he presented a catalog of scriptures to the Liu Song court, and he arranged texts under the categories later known as the Three Caverns, an early step toward a systematic canon. He also revised ritual procedures, laying the ground for the liturgical structure of later Daoist practice.

The North: Kou Qianzhi and State Daoism

In the north, the reform of Celestial Masters Daoism took a different form. Kou Qianzhi claimed a revelation from Laozi in 415, and another in 423, that gave him authority to reform the tradition. His program removed the payment of rice as a fee, prohibited certain sexual rites, and emphasized moral discipline, ritual, and obedience to the state. He came to the attention of the Northern Wei court, where the emperor Taiwu and his minister Cui Hao supported Daoist ritual. In 440 the ruler adopted a reign title with a Daoist meaning, and in 442 he received Daoist talismans at a ceremony, actions that placed the Wei ruler within a Daoist cosmic order. The alliance between Daoism and the Wei court was politically significant, and it coincided with the suppression of Buddhism in 446, discussed in Chapter 22, though historians debate how much this measure reflected Daoist influence as opposed to political and military concerns about monasteries.

After Taiwu’s death, the Wei court restored Buddhism and Daoism continued in a subordinate role, with an official altar and priests. In the sixth century, Daoism remained active in the north through communities, temples, and ritual specialists. The Northern Zhou ruler Wu, who suppressed the organized Buddhist and Daoist institutions in the 570s, also established an academy devoted to research on the "Way" (Tongdao guan) with about one hundred twenty scholars, an effort to have a state-supervised learning that would draw on Confucian, Buddhist, and Daoist sources.

Daoism and Buddhism

The relationship between Daoism and Buddhism was mixed. Both religions competed for patronage, and polemics flourished. One theme, expressed in a text known as the Scripture on the Conversion of the Barbarians, held that Laozi had gone west and taught the Buddha, a claim that placed Buddhism in a subordinate position. Buddhist writers responded with their own arguments, and courts in the sixth century convened debates. At the same time, the two traditions borrowed from each other. Daoists developed monastic communities, ritual retreats, and canons partly on Buddhist models, while Buddhists adopted terminology and framed practices in ways that made them intelligible to Chinese audiences. Ordinary people probably did not regard the traditions as exclusive alternatives, and household practice might involve monks, Daoist priests, mediums, and offerings to ancestors in the same year. Treating Buddhism as an intruder and Daoism as a native tradition oversimplifies a relationship in which both changed.

Section 23.6

Ethnic Interaction and Cultural Exchange

Any discussion of ethnic groups in this period should begin with a caution about words. Chinese historians used terms such as Hu for peoples from the north and west and Han for the people of the former empire’s core, and later historians grouped several peoples as the "Five Hu." These categories were meaningful to contemporaries, but they were not fixed. People classified as Xiongnu, Jie, Di, Qiang, and Xianbei spoke different languages, followed different economies, and had different histories, and each category included diversity and changed over time. Meanwhile, "Han" people were not uniform, and included communities from different regions with different dialects and customs. Modern categories of nationality should not be read back onto these groups.

States and Institutions

Rulers of northern states came from varied backgrounds and adopted varied strategies. The Later Zhao ruler Shi Le, of Jie background, governed with a division between administration of Hu communities and of Chinese households, while also supporting a Confucian school and consulting Han advisers. The Former Qin ruler Fu Jian, of Di origin, attempted to build a multiethnic state and resettled populations, but his defeat at the Fei River in 383 was followed by the fragmentation of his realm. The Northern Wei, founded by the Tuoba Xianbei, combined steppe military organization with Chinese-style bureaucracy. These states show that the transformations of the period were not simply Chinese absorption of outsiders. They were negotiations in which rulers, aristocrats, and local communities drew on multiple traditions.

Violence was also part of the story. In 350 the Han general Ran Min, who had seized power in the collapsing Later Zhao state, ordered the killing of people identified as Hu, and sources report very large numbers of victims. The episode reflects the ethnic tensions that developed amid warfare, and it also shows how identity could become a political weapon. Elsewhere, the relationship between newcomers and settled populations was managed through tribute, marriage, military service, and land grants. The result was a shifting pattern of cooperation and conflict.

Names, Dress, Language, and Food

The daily life of the north shows continuing exchange. Clothing of steppe origin, including trousers, boots, and belted tunics, spread among soldiers and eventually more widely, because it was suited to riding. Foods, furniture, and music entered Chinese life from the west and north, including foreign music associated with Kucha and Sogdiana, the lute called the pipa, and the folding stool, which had a long career in later Chinese furniture. Military technology also circulated. The stirrup appears in a Jin-period tomb in the south in the early fourth century and was widespread in the following centuries, alongside armored cavalry. Techniques for horse breeding and pastoral products became more visible in the north’s economy.

Names and languages changed. In the 490s the Northern Wei court at Luoyang promoted the use of Chinese at court, encouraged Xianbei elites to adopt Chinese-style surnames, and forbade court dress of the older style. The ruling Tuoba lineage took the surname Yuan. These policies were debated in their own time and are still discussed by historians under the label of "sinicization." That term should be used with caution. It suggests a one-way process in which non-Chinese people become Chinese, whereas the sources show an interaction. Many Xianbei families maintained their languages and customs, marriages linked them with Han aristocrats, and after the fall of the Wei some regimes reversed the policies. In the 540s and 550s, leaders of the Western Wei state restored Xianbei surnames, and the ruling elite of the Northern Zhou was a mixed group that later included the families of the Sui and Tang founders. A sixth-century writer, Yan Zhitui, reported that some northern fathers taught their sons the Xianbei language and skill with the lute to serve powerful officials, a remark that shows both the prestige of Xianbei culture at the Northern Qi court and the writer’s disapproval.

Sogdians, Southerners, and Other Communities

Not all communities in the north were of steppe or Han origin. Sogdian merchants from Central Asia, with communities along the trade routes to the northwest and in northern cities, served as traders, caravan leaders, and sometimes officials. The Northern Zhou tombs of Shi Jun and An Jia, discovered in Xi’an, contain stone funerary couches with reliefs showing Sogdian dress, banquets, and religious imagery, and they illustrate how a Central Asian family could hold office in a northern state while maintaining religious and artistic traditions from home. In the south, the historical record includes upland and river-valley peoples known in the sources as Man, Yue, and Liao, whom the state gradually registered, taxed, and sometimes fought. Their incorporation was slow and often contested, and it created new communities in the hills of the middle Yangtze and the southeast.

Northerners and southerners also constructed images of each other. Southern writers referred to northern regimes with contempt, with terms such as "braided captives" that referred to steppe hairstyles, and northerners used equally disparaging terms for the south. Yet at the Northern Wei court in Luoyang, southern refugees and emissaries held positions, and sources record both prejudice and admiration. This mutual awareness formed the basis for a later tradition of regional stereotypes and also for contact that shaped literature, ritual, and fashion.

What Interaction Produced

What emerged was not a single blended culture but a range of mixtures. The elites of the Northern Zhou and Sui, drawn from garrison families of the northwest, combined Xianbei and Han ancestry and traditions. Buddhist temples and images visible across the north incorporated influences from Central Asia. Northern Wei state-building combined Tuoba political and military traditions with agrarian administrative practices rooted in earlier Chinese states; its land-allocation and registration reforms developed within that mixed political environment. The identity called "Han" became clearer partly because it was contrasted with others, and partly because rulers and writers had reason to define it. Understanding the period requires holding together two points: that categories of identity were real to people, and that they changed as people lived across them.

Section 23.7

Family, Gender, and Aristocratic Society

Families were the basic unit of society throughout the Age of Division, and in an era of weak central authority, they took on additional importance. Households organized labor and inheritance, lineages provided protection and social standing, and family instructions taught how to behave. This section asks how families and gender relations were arranged in northern and southern societies, and how aristocratic status worked. It relies heavily on elite sources, and each claim should be read with the question of how far it applied to ordinary households.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Shizu (士族) and Menfa (門閥), “gentry lineages” and “gate-and-clan”

Shizu refers to lineages of the educated and officeholding class, and menfa to the prestige attached to a family’s "gate," meaning its standing over generations. In the Age of Division, such families dominated high office, married among themselves, kept genealogies, and cultivated distinctive manners and learning. The term "aristocracy" is a convenient translation, but the status of a shizu or great family was not itself a hereditary peerage. Courts of the period did confer hereditary noble titles and formal ranks, but the standing of great lineages rested more broadly on officeholding, pedigree, marriage alliances, education, landholding, dependents, and social recognition that had to be renewed.

The system was linked to the nine-rank procedure for evaluating candidates for office, introduced under Cao Wei, in which local evaluators rated people by family background and ability. Over time, family origin counted for more, and the influence of the great families grew in both north and south. Understanding shizu is important because the aristocratic order shaped politics, marriage, education, and culture during the period, and because the Sui and Tang later tried to limit its role.

Aristocratic Status in South and North

In the south, families such as the Wang of Langya and the Xie of Chenjun held the highest positions at the Eastern Jin court and continued to enjoy prestige under the Southern Dynasties. Status was supported by genealogical registers and by rules of marriage that discouraged unions with families of lower rank. A memorial from the late fifth or early sixth century by the official Shen Yue criticized a member of the Wang family for marrying his daughter into a family of low standing, which shows how closely such alliances were monitored. Yet the political power of southern aristocrats had limits. Many dynasties were founded by generals of modest background, such as Liu Yu of the Liu Song, and the practical work of government, including drafting and military command, increasingly fell to men from lower-ranking families sometimes called "cold gates." By the sixth century, some aristocrats were noted for refinement rather than administrative skill, and the rebellion of Hou Jing in 548, discussed in Chapter 21, struck their households severely.

In the north, aristocratic status developed in a different environment. The great Han families of the Hebei and Shandong regions, including the Cui of Qinghe, the Lu of Fanyang, the Li of Zhaojun, and the Zheng of Xingyang, retained local prestige and landholdings, and the Northern Wei court sought their cooperation while ranking them within its own system. Around 495 the Wei court classified families in an official scheme that included Xianbei lineages and Han lineages, a move that tied status to court recognition. Xianbei military aristocrats had their own hierarchies, and the mixed northwestern elites of the Western Wei and Northern Zhou joined the two. Northern great families continued to be significant into the Sui and Tang periods, and the Tang court later sponsored a national genealogy of clans to reflect its own preferences rather than the reputations of the old lineages.

Household, Marriage, and Inheritance

Family organization in the Age of Division rested on assumptions shared with the Han: descent through the male line, patriarchal authority, and obligations toward ancestors. Property was divided among sons, while daughters received dowries, and elite families preferred marriages that matched rank. In practice, arrangements varied. Some great families in the north maintained large extended households that lived together for generations and were praised as models. Ordinary rural households tended to be smaller. The Northern Wei’s land allocation system, discussed in Chapter 22, counted households in ways that encouraged registration of married couples and their dependents, and it allotted land to women in smaller amounts than to men, a recognition of women’s role in agricultural and textile work.

Textile production was essential to household economies and to the state. In the north, taxes were often collected in silk, hemp cloth, and grain, and women’s labor produced the cloth. This point is easy to miss when reading sources concerned with courts and aristocrats. The economic contribution of women, in weaving, farming, and household management, was central to the fiscal system, even though they rarely appear as named individuals in the written record.

Women’s Roles in North and South

Sources suggest that gender norms differed between northern and southern elite society, although they must be treated cautiously. Yan Zhitui, who had lived in both regions, wrote that in the northern capital of Ye, women managed family business, took part in lawsuits, and visited officials on their families’ behalf, activities that southerners considered unusual. This contrast fits other evidence of the influence of steppe traditions, in which women could hold significant authority within the household and clan. Empress dowagers in the Northern Wei, such as Feng, who acted as regent in the fifth century, and Hu, who dominated the court in the early sixth century, held real power, and Hu was a major patron of Buddhist construction in Luoyang. Her fate, deposed and killed in 528 during the disorder that accompanied the military takeover of the court, illustrates the risks of political power. Sources also report a Tuoba custom of executing the mother of a designated heir, which was later abandoned. Historians debate how such practices should be interpreted, but they show that succession politics in the Northern Wei differed from Han practice.

Women in southern aristocratic society appeared as poets, letter writers, and members of literary families. Xie Daoyun of the Xie lineage was remembered for her wit and learning in the anecdotes preserved in A New Account of Tales of the World. At the same time, ideals of chastity, filial devotion, and household management were emphasized in texts of instruction. Widows, in particular, faced pressures. Ritual texts praised chaste widows, but remarriage occurred in practice, and the sources do not allow a precise account of how common it was among ordinary families.

Religion opened new roles. Women became nuns, founders of temples, donors, and patrons, and a number of early Chinese women were known as Buddhist teachers. In Celestial Masters Daoism, women could serve as libationers and priestesses, and revelations of the Shangqing tradition were attributed to a female perfected being. The existence of these roles did not remove restrictions, and monastic rules also regulated nuns more strictly than monks, yet they gave some women an alternative to marriage and household life. A woman’s decision to become a nun might reflect piety, widowhood, family strategy, or an attempt to escape a marriage.

Instruction, Education, and Family Life

The best-known work on family life from the period is Yan Zhitui’s Family Instructions for the Yan Clan, written in the late sixth century. The author had experienced the fall of the Liang dynasty, served under Northern Qi and Northern Zhou, and lived into the Sui, and he wrote to instruct his descendants on how to survive in an unstable world. He advocated a strong education for children, criticized aristocrats who neglected practical learning, warned against extremes of religious devotion, though he himself supported Buddhism, and offered opinions on family harmony, marriage, funerals, and language. The work should be read as the perspective of a particular scholar-official, but it offers rare detail about the assumptions of elite households, and it appears in the Primary Source Window in Section 23.10.

The overall picture is of aristocratic societies that differed in their emphases but shared some features: high value on lineage and genealogy, patrilineal inheritance, and a fusion of religious and family obligations. They were also more vulnerable than they seemed. In the south, the rise of officials from other backgrounds and the destructive war of the mid-sixth century reduced the importance of the old families. In the north, the mixing of steppe and Han elites and the shifts of court politics reshaped the aristocracy again and again. Families adapted, and lineage status remained a resource, but its meaning changed.

Section 23.8

Economic Development in Southern China

Chapter 21 introduced the economic development of the south under the Eastern Jin and Southern Dynasties. This section places that development in a longer perspective and asks how earlier growth and northern migration interacted. It does not describe the fuller economic transformation of the Tang and Song periods, discussed in Chapters 26 and 32, and it cautions against treating the south of the sixth century as already the economic center of the Chinese world.

Foundations Before the Migrations

The south had been developing before the northern refugees arrived. The state of Eastern Wu, founded in 222, promoted agricultural colonies, boat building, and reclamation in the lower Yangtze and along the Zhejiang coast, and it maintained maritime contacts with Southeast Asia. Its registered population at its fall in 280 was small, about two and a third million, but the low figure reflects limits on registration, since large parts of the hills and river valleys were beyond the reach of officials. Embankment and water-control projects, such as the dam at Mirror Lake near Kuaiji that dates to the Han, provided a base for expanding rice cultivation in the Zhejiang region. Southern agriculture combined rice with other crops, fishing, and gathering, and its methods differed from the dry-land farming of the north.

The Interaction of Migration and Development

Migration added people, skills, capital, and demand. Northern families brought knowledge of wheat cultivation and other crops, which in some areas of the south became a complement to rice, and refugee soldiers and farmers cleared land. Aristocratic estates (zhuang) developed on hills and marshes, with fields, orchards, fisheries, workshops, and dependent labor. The poet Xie Lingyun, in a fifth-century description of his family’s holdings near Kuaiji, listed the crops, forests, streams, and buildings of an estate, giving a picture of a self-sufficient landscape. The Liu Song government tried to limit private claims to mountains and lakes by setting quotas according to rank, a sign that elites were extending their holdings and that the state noticed. Landless and dependent households also worked for these estates, and the state’s attempts to register migrants and control labor formed part of the same process, as Section 23.1 discussed.

The relative weight of migration and preexisting development is a historians’ question, not a settled matter. Some scholars emphasize the contribution of the northern refugees, noting the founding of estates and the growth of registered population in the fifth century. Others emphasize the strength of local development, including the work of southern families and the incorporation of upland groups into the agricultural system. The evidence supports interaction rather than a single cause. Northern migration increased labor and demand and brought elites who shaped the region’s social order, while southern landscapes, communities, and earlier projects created the conditions in which growth could take place.

Markets, Transport, and Cities

The Yangtze and its tributaries served as highways for grain, salt, cloth, and other goods, and southern governments collected tolls and taxes at ports and markets. Jiankang was the largest city of the south, with markets along the Qinhuai River, workshops, monasteries, and neighborhoods for officials and soldiers. Some sources suggest that its population approached a million at the peak of the Liang dynasty, but such figures are uncertain, and historians treat them as indications of a very large city rather than exact counts. Other centers included Jingkou at the river’s mouth, Guangling, Jiangling in the middle Yangtze, Kuaiji in the southeast, and Chengdu in the west. Handicrafts flourished. Kilns in the Zhejiang region produced green-glazed stoneware, known as Yue ware, that became a durable tradition, and textile production expanded in the Yangtze region and in Sichuan, which was famous for its brocades. Money circulated unevenly. Coins were used in the Yangtze region, but in many areas of the far south, cloth, grain, and salt served as means of payment.

The Coast and the South Seas

Connections to the sea grew. Guangzhou, the main port of the south, received ships carrying goods from Southeast Asia and India, including incense, spices, and pearls, and it served as an entry point for monks and merchants such as Paramartha. Southern courts exchanged embassies with Southeast Asian states such as Funan and Linyi, and the volume of maritime trade, while smaller than in later periods, was significant. The southeast coast, including modern Fujian, remained more sparsely settled, though settlement and administration expanded in the sixth century. These maritime links prepared no direct path to the Song maritime economy of Chapter 37, but they show that the south was open to the ocean.

Limits of the Southern Story

It is important not to exaggerate. The north retained large populations and productive agriculture. The sixth-century agricultural manual Qimin yaoshu describes intensive dry-land techniques, including crop rotation, use of manure, and management of orchards, animals, and fermented products, and it demonstrates that northern farmers were sophisticated. Political and economic power in 589 remained heavily weighted toward the north, and the Sui and Tang capitals lay in Guanzhong. The south’s gain was relative and cumulative: a higher proportion of the population, more land under cultivation, more cities and ports, and a more developed system of regional trade. The full shift of economic weight toward the Yangtze region is a later story.

Section 23.9

Cultural Legacies of the Age of Division

What did the centuries between the Han and the Sui leave behind? This section identifies several legacies, and it does so with a caution stated at the outset: to say that developments persisted is not to say that they were preparing for reunification. Many of the things listed here would have existed without the Sui, and some did not survive. The claim is only that when the Sui state emerged in 581 and conquered the south in 589, it governed societies that had been transformed.

A Different Demography and Geography

The regional balance had changed. The south held more people, more cities, and more cultivated land than in Han times, and it had developed local elites, dialect regions, and economic networks. The north had become a society of mixed origins, with Xianbei and Central Asian communities as part of the elite and population. The two regions had developed different institutions and habits, and the awareness of difference between "north" and "south" was a lasting feature of Chinese cultural and political life.

Religious Institutions

Buddhism had become a permanent part of Chinese society, with a canon of translated and composed texts, monastic institutions, lay associations, and a visible presence in art and landscape. Daoism had acquired scriptural traditions, ordination systems, and ritual forms. Both had learned to coexist with the state through negotiation and, at times, suppression. The relationship of religious institutions to imperial power, whether as supporters, rivals, or dependents, was a question later dynasties would confront repeatedly, as later chapters will show.

Literature, Art, and Thought

Elite culture flourished, particularly in the south. The poets Tao Yuanming and Xie Lingyun wrote about rural retreat and mountain landscapes, and landscape became a major theme of Chinese poetry. Critical writing developed, including Liu Xie’s Literary Mind and the Carving of Dragons and Zhong Rong’s Gradings of Poets, and the prince Xiao Tong of Liang supervised the anthology Selections of Refined Literature. Calligraphy was reshaped by Wang Xizhi and his circle, whose gathering at the Orchid Pavilion in 353 later became a model of elite refinement, and the regular script that became standard was developing in this period, with distinctive stone-inscription styles in the north. Philosophical currents such as the Dark Learning, associated with Wang Bi and Guo Xiang, brought a new reading of the Daoist and Changes traditions. In the north, the scholarship of the classics continued, and writers such as Li Daoyuan, in the Commentary on the Waterways Classic, combined geography and history in a work of great learning.

Science and Technology

Knowledge advanced in several fields. The mathematician and astronomer Zu Chongzhi, in the fifth century, calculated an approximation of pi that was more precise than earlier Chinese values and proposed a new calendar. Medical writers such as Ge Hong, Huangfu Mi, who compiled an early text on acupuncture and moxibustion, and Tao Hongjing, who edited a pharmacological work, systematized knowledge. Paper became the main medium of writing, which supported both the copying of Buddhist scriptures and the circulation of secular texts. The stirrup and armored cavalry changed warfare, and agricultural techniques advanced in both regions. These developments involved scholars, physicians, artisans, and farmers, and they came from many settings, including monasteries, courts, and families.

Institutions and Practices

Some institutions and practices that the Sui and Tang later used had been developed or tested in the divided centuries. Land allocation and household registration systems of the Northern Wei and its successors, the militia arrangements of the Western Wei and Northern Zhou, and the administrative experience of both northern and southern courts provided models and personnel. The aristocratic families that staffed courts brought traditions of learning and ritual. At the same time, later governments modified or abandoned other features, including the nine-rank system and the privileged position of some clans. Continuity was selective.

What Was Lost, and What Was Not Inevitable

Not everything endured. War, famine, and disease killed very large numbers of people. The destruction of Luoyang in the 530s and the devastation of the Yangtze region during Hou Jing’s rebellion in the late 540s were among many disasters. Temples, libraries, and communities were destroyed. Regimes fell, and regional traditions vanished. Nor was reunification in 589 the necessary outcome of these processes. It resulted from particular military and political choices, in the wake of contested successions and campaigns, examined in Chapter 24. The Sui inherited a diverse and changed society, not a restored Han. Recognizing this makes it possible to see the Age of Division as a period with its own historical significance, in which political fragmentation coexisted with major social, religious, and cultural change.

Section 23.10

Chapter Review and Transformation Checkpoint

This chapter has argued that the Age of Division transformed Chinese society more deeply than its political fragmentation suggests. Its argument has five parts. First, migration in several directions redistributed population, moved elites and artisans, and created new regional communities, and it changed both the migrants and the societies that received them. Second, Buddhism grew from a small foreign presence into a religion embedded in courts, villages, landscapes, and economies, and this growth depended on translators, monasteries, lay associations, and the choices of rulers. Third, Daoism developed organized traditions with revealed scriptures, ordination, and liturgy, and it interacted and competed with Buddhism. Fourth, interaction among Han and non-Han communities produced new mixtures of language, dress, institutions, and elite identity in the north, and slower incorporation of upland peoples in the south, while categories of identity themselves shifted. Fifth, family life, aristocratic status, women’s roles, art, and regional economies changed in ways that made the society of 589 substantially different from the society of the Han.

The features below use these themes to develop the skills of historical analysis: reading a chronology for social change rather than dynastic turnover, interpreting maps of movement and religious centers, working with sources from a family instruction and a temple record, weighing competing interpretations, and comparing how different groups experienced the same developments. Students should use them to test the argument above and, where the evidence warrants, to qualify it.

Primary Source Window

Primary Source Window: Yan Zhitui’s Family Instructions for the Yan Clan

Creator: Yan Zhitui (born 531, active into the Sui period) · Date: Late sixth century · Source type: Family instruction, a genre of advice for descendants

Context: Yan Zhitui was born in the south, served the Liang dynasty, was taken north after the fall of Jiangling in 554, and later served the Northern Qi, the Northern Zhou, and the Sui. He wrote the work to guide his sons and descendants and drew on his experience of both regions and of several regimes. The book covers education, family order, marriage, language, religion, and practical advice for survival.

Paraphrase of representative themes (not a translated quotation):

Yan observes that in the northern capital women handle household business, attend to lawsuits, and call on officials, while in the south women seldom leave the house and relatives may communicate mainly by messenger. He warns his descendants to value education and practical learning, criticizes the idleness of some aristocrats, and remarks that a father who has his son learn the Xianbei language and play the lute in order to serve powerful men may gain favor, but he does not approve. He also defends Buddhist belief in rebirth and karma while cautioning against extremes and against those who use religion to avoid obligations.

Analysis: The text shows a reflective observer comparing northern and southern customs and reveals concerns of elite fathers in a time of insecurity, including the need to retain status when regimes fall. It illustrates the mixing of steppe and Han elite culture and the place of Buddhism in an educated family. Its limits are considerable. It reflects one man’s prejudices, describes mostly elite households in cities, and offers little on peasants, servants, or the women whose behavior he describes. His comparison of northern and southern women is an observation rather than a measurement, and it may exaggerate contrasts. The source also gives a male author’s view of female activity, without direct testimony from women.

Questions for students:

  1. What does this source say or show about differences between northern and southern society?
  2. Who created it, and what might his purpose have been in writing for his descendants?
  3. What can historians learn from it about family life, education, religion, and identity?
  4. What can historians not learn from it alone, and what other sources would help fill the gaps?

Creator: Yang Xuanzhi, a Northern Wei official · Date: Approximately 547 · Source type: Descriptive record of a city and its temples, with historical anecdotes

Context: After the Northern Wei split and its capital moved from Luoyang to Ye in the 530s, the old capital was largely abandoned. Yang wrote a work that reconstructs the city’s temples and neighborhoods from memory and from visits to the ruins. He describes the founding of temples by empresses, princes, and officials, gives details of buildings and images, and includes stories about people connected to them, including foreign monks and missions.

Paraphrase of representative themes (not a translated quotation):

The record reports that Luoyang held over a thousand temples at the height of the Wei capital and describes some in detail, including the Yongning Temple sponsored by Empress Dowager Hu, whose towering wooden pagoda could be seen from a great distance. It also notes foreign monks who admired the city’s Buddhist buildings and records the mission to the northwest of the Indian subcontinent that went out under the Wei court. The author writes with nostalgia, and links the city’s decline to political misrule.

Analysis: This is an important source for urban Buddhism, elite patronage, and the connections between Luoyang and the wider Buddhist world. It shows that in the early sixth century religious buildings dominated the city, and that empresses and aristocrats competed to sponsor them. However, Yang was writing in retrospect, with a moral message about the causes of collapse, and his numbers and descriptions may be exaggerated. He focuses on grand temples and gives less attention to small shrines, nuns of modest background, or the poor. The record shows the world of patrons, not necessarily of ordinary worshippers.

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Fragmentation or Transformation?

The question: Should the centuries between the Han and the Sui be understood primarily as a period of political and social breakdown, or as a period of creative transformation? The question affects how historians organize the history of China, and how much they attribute to reunification.

Interpretation A — Breakdown and loss: Scholars who emphasize breakdown point to the collapse of centralized administration, the shrinking of registered population, repeated warfare, forced relocations, and the destruction of cities and libraries. They stress the suffering of ordinary people and the disruption of institutions, and they suggest that many later achievements depended on the restoration of unity under the Sui and Tang, which provided security and resources.
Interpretation B — Creative transformation: Other scholars stress the productivity of the period: the spread of Buddhism and organized Daoism, the development of the south, the mixing of peoples and cultures in the north, and innovations in literature, art, and technology. They suggest that division allowed regional and religious experiments that a single empire might have restricted, and that reunification built on these developments and did not simply restore an earlier order.

Evidence: Both interpretations draw on the same body of evidence. Demographic records, accounts of warfare, and narratives of collapse support the first view; cave temples, translations, literary works, and agricultural and technological texts support the second. The two are not mutually exclusive. Many historians argue that catastrophe and creativity coexisted, and that the same forces, such as migration and the weakness of central authority, caused both suffering and innovation. Some also warn that both interpretations can be teleological, either treating unification as the natural goal or treating division as a preparation for it.

Why it matters: How the period is interpreted shapes the place of non-Han rulers, religion, and regional societies in Chinese history, and whether political unity is treated as the standard against which other periods are measured.

Student task: Using evidence from this chapter, decide which interpretation is better supported for a particular region (for example, the Yangtze region or the Gansu corridor) and explain what additional evidence would help. Alternatively, explain how the two interpretations could be combined without ignoring either suffering or creativity.

Multiple Perspectives

Multiple Perspectives: Five Communities in the Sixth Century

Note: the perspectives below are analytical reconstructions based on patterns in the sources, not quotations from named individuals. Power and security were unevenly distributed, and the perspectives are not equally documented.

A northern migrant family in the south: Families that had crossed the Yangtze generations earlier might still hold onto northern identity through genealogies, place-names, and marriages with other migrants, while adjusting to local conditions. Elite households sought office and estates; poorer migrant households worked marginal land and faced pressure from tax officials when the state ended special registration.
A southern landholding family: Long-established families of the lower Yangtze had local prestige and dependents, and often welcomed marriages and alliances with newcomers while resenting northern dominance at court. They also stood to gain from new land clearance and from labor that migrants provided, and they defended their holdings against state limits.
A Buddhist monastic community: Monks and nuns lived under rules and depended on donors, land, and the protection of rulers. They valued the merit and learning of their institution, and they were also vulnerable to changes in state policy, as the Northern Zhou measures show. Their concerns included ordination, property, and the relationship between religious vows and family obligations.
A frontier military household in the north: Garrison families in the north served in the army in return for land and status, and often had steppe or mixed backgrounds. Their situation depended on the court’s treatment of military service, and their relative standing shifted as the court promoted Chinese-style aristocracy, a source of tension examined in Chapter 22.
A farming household in the northern plain: Peasant families worked dry-land fields, paid taxes in grain and cloth, and provided labor and military service. Successive regimes and forced relocations made their lives unpredictable, while local strongholds, kin groups, and temples offered some protection. They rarely appear in the sources except as taxpayers or as anonymous victims of war.
Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change Over Time: From Han Society to the Eve of Reunification

What Changed

  • Buddhism and organized Daoism became major institutions, with scriptures, clergy, and lay networks
  • The south’s population, cities, and agricultural development expanded, and a strong regional identity formed
  • Northern elites became ethnically and culturally mixed, and steppe and Central Asian elements entered dress, food, music, and military practice
  • Great-family status became increasingly formalized and influential in recruitment and high office compared with the Han, before those aristocratic structures themselves faced new challenges
  • Household registration, land allocation, and militia service were reorganized in the north

What Stayed the Same

  • Agriculture and household labor remained the basis of the economy, and women’s textile work stayed fiscally important
  • Patrilineal family structure and ancestor veneration continued, even as Buddhist practice was added
  • Classical learning, the written language, and the idea of a legitimate imperial order remained shared reference points across regimes
  • The state continued to struggle to register and tax populations that local elites protected

Who experienced change most directly: Migrants, monastics, and frontier households experienced change most directly. Farming families experienced many changes as new burdens and dislocations, while the routines of planting and harvest continued.

Why the pattern matters: The mix of change and continuity shows that unity and division are not the only useful categories for describing this era. Much of what students recognize as characteristic of medieval China was shaped in these centuries.

Everyday Life and Social History

Households in a Divided World

Everyday life varied greatly by region and status. In the north, a farming household might live in a compound of earthen buildings, cultivate millet, wheat, and beans, keep animals, and grow hemp and mulberry, following techniques of the kind described in the Essential Techniques for the Common People. Women spun and wove cloth that formed part of the household’s tax, while men owed grain, labor, and military service. Diet included porridges and, increasingly, wheat products, with foods from steppe and Central Asian traditions gradually entering common use. Nearby, a fortified village or a temple might provide security and a setting for festivals.

In the south, households in the Yangtze and Zhejiang plains worked irrigated rice fields, fished, and gathered products from marshes and hills, and lived in wooden or bamboo houses suited to a wet climate. Rivers linked them to markets, and boats were a major form of transport. On estates, tenant and dependent families worked under landowners, while in towns artisans, boatmen, and small traders lived in crowded neighborhoods.

Religion was part of daily life. Families might invite monks to perform funerary rites, sponsor a statue or the copying of a scripture, join a lay association, or consult a Daoist priest for healing or protection. Buddhist festivals, such as celebrations of the Buddha’s birthday with processions of images in cities like Luoyang, brought crowds together. People also continued to make offerings to ancestors and local gods. These practices show that religious life was not divided neatly into exclusive traditions. Hardship was common. Epidemics, floods, warfare, and forced relocation could destroy a household’s security, and refugees and captives formed a large group whose experiences appear rarely in the sources.

Regional and Frontier Context

The transformations described in this chapter played out differently across regions. The North China Plain and the Wei and Yellow River valleys were the sites of the heaviest warfare, forced relocations, and administrative experimentation, and they also contained the great cities of Luoyang, Ye, and Chang’an. The northwest, especially the Gansu corridor and its oases, functioned as a corridor of trade and religious transmission where regional states preserved Han-period learning and hosted Buddhist communities, as the Mogao caves show. Sichuan, connected to the Yangtze by river and to the northwest by mountain roads, passed between Cheng-Han, Eastern Jin, and northern control and developed its own Buddhist and Daoist traditions.

The lower and middle Yangtze regions, where southern states were centered, absorbed migrants and developed estates, cities, and monasteries. Farther south, Lingnan and the southeast coast were more thinly settled, with mixed populations, ports, and frontier administration. In the southwest and the middle Yangtze hills, upland peoples were slowly drawn into registration and taxation, sometimes through conflict. In the northeast, the Murong Xianbei and later states, and the peoples of Liaodong and the Korean peninsula, formed a zone of interaction. On the Inner Asian frontier, Tuoba and other groups were both threat and source of political innovation, and the frontier was a place where identities were negotiated, not simply a line between China and outsiders. Peoples in these regions experienced the states of the period from many angles, as allies, subjects, captives, and rivals, and their perspectives are mostly known through Chinese sources and material remains.

China and the Wider World

The Age of Division was also a period of intense connection with the wider world, and Chinese actors were active in this process. Buddhist monks traveled to India and Central Asia and brought back texts, while Indian and Central Asian monks came to China. Rulers in the north negotiated with oasis kingdoms and steppe powers, and northern courts received embassies from Central Asia and beyond. Sogdian merchants operated along overland routes, and Chinese officials tried to regulate and tax them. Maritime networks connected the south to Southeast Asia and India, and southern courts sent and received embassies from states such as Funan.

These connections extended eastward as well. Buddhism reached the Korean peninsula in the later fourth century through contacts with northern Chinese states, and it spread to Japan by way of Korean kingdoms in the sixth century. Chinese writing, legal ideas, and artistic styles entered these societies, where they were adapted to local conditions. In the west, the ideas and objects that came through the oases linked China with Iran and the Mediterranean world. Chinese silk continued to move westward, and Central Asian goods, images, and religious ideas moved east. Understanding these exchanges as two-way processes involving Chinese states, merchants, monks, and artisans helps avoid the view that China was either isolated or simply the recipient of foreign influence.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined the Age of Division from the standpoint of society, religion, and culture, and it has argued that the centuries between the fall of the Han in 220 and the Sui conquest of the south in 589 were a formative period in Chinese history. Political fragmentation was real, and it brought war, displacement, and the loss of life and institutions. But beneath the sequence of regimes, described in Chapters 19 through 22, lay longer processes that reshaped population, belief, family, economy, and identity.

Migration was the first of these processes. Northern families moved south in successive waves after 311, and southern governments responded with separate registration of migrants and repeated attempts to bring them under ordinary taxation. In the north, states deported and resettled people to build capitals, farms, and garrisons, while others fled to the northwest and northeast or defended themselves in fortified communities. These movements changed the migrants, who adapted to new places, and the receiving societies, which absorbed new elites, workers, and customs. They also produced a lasting sense of difference between northerners and southerners.

Buddhism was the second. It expanded from a small foreign presence to a mass religious institution with thousands of monasteries (some northern sources report tens of thousands at particular peaks, though such figures are not precise censuses), a large clergy, and a lay following that reached into villages. Its growth reflected the uncertainties of the age, the initiative of translators and monks, the appeal of merit and rebirth, the interests of rulers, and the economic and social functions of monasteries. The work of translation teams, notably that of Kumārajīva, and the networks of pilgrims and merchants that carried texts and people, gave Chinese Buddhism a body of scripture and doctrine. Cave temples and sacred mountains, from Yungang and Longmen to Mogao, made religion visible and recorded the involvement of rulers, donors, and artisans.

Daoism was transformed at the same time. The Celestial Masters, dispersed after 215, developed into regional traditions, including the reformed northern order of Kou Qianzhi, while in the south the Shangqing and Lingbao revelations and the work of Lu Xiujing and Tao Hongjing produced new scriptures, rituals, and canons. Daoism and Buddhism competed, argued, and borrowed from one another, and households did not necessarily treat them as exclusive choices. The relationship of religious institutions to state power varied from patronage to suppression.

Interaction among Han and non-Han communities was a fourth process. Rulers of Xiongnu, Jie, Di, Qiang, and Xianbei backgrounds built states that combined steppe and Chinese institutions. Clothing, food, music, language, surnames, and military technology moved in several directions, and the Northern Wei, Western Wei, and Northern Zhou elites became mixed. Violence and prejudice accompanied these exchanges. Historians therefore use terms such as "sinicization" with caution, since identities shifted through service, marriage, and political choice, and neither "Han" nor "non-Han" was a fixed bloc.

Family and aristocratic society formed a fifth area of change. Great families, with their genealogies, marriage networks, and claims to office, dominated politics in both regions but faced challenges from men of lower rank, from military leaders, and from the destruction of war. Sources suggest differences between northern and southern women’s public roles, and religion opened new roles for nuns, donors, and Daoist priestesses, although most of the evidence concerns elite households and women’s labor in textile production and agriculture remained essential and poorly documented.

The southern economy grew through the interaction of earlier development and northern migration. Wu-period reclamation, local families, and upland peoples formed a base, while migrants added labor, capital, and demand, contributing to the growth of estates, markets, cities like Jiankang, and maritime contacts. The north, however, remained powerful and productive, and the full shift toward the Yangtze region belongs to later chapters.

The legacies of the period included a more developed south, deeply rooted religious institutions, transformed elite cultures in literature, calligraphy, and scholarship, advances in mathematics, medicine, and agriculture, a mixed northern aristocracy, and institutional experiments in land and military organization that the Sui and Tang later used. These legacies did not make reunification inevitable. It came through contingent political and military events discussed in Chapter 24. The society the Sui governed was the product of three centuries of change, and understanding those centuries on their own terms is necessary for understanding the medieval Chinese civilization that followed.

Review Materials

Key Terms

Age of Division
The period from 220 to 589 during which no state ruled the whole of the former Han territory for long; a political label, not a description of cultural stagnation.
Qiaoren (僑人)
“Sojourners”; northern migrants in the south who were registered separately for a time.
White register and yellow register
Southern lists distinguishing migrant households from established local households.
Tuduan (土斷)
“Land division”; reforms that reassigned migrants to ordinary local registration.
Household registration
The state’s listing of households for taxation, labor, and military service.
Shizu (士族)
Lineages of the educated and officeholding class that dominated the period’s politics.
Nine-rank system
A method introduced under Cao Wei by which local evaluators ranked candidates for office, increasingly by family background.
Cold gate (寒門)
Term for families of lower status, from which many practical officials and soldiers came.
Sangha
The Buddhist monastic community of monks and nuns.
Vinaya
The body of Buddhist monastic disciplinary rules.
Merit
Religious credit gained through donations and good actions, which could be dedicated to ancestors or rulers.
Lay association (yi 邑)
A group of lay devotees who pooled resources to sponsor images, feasts, and inscriptions.
Geyi (格義)
An early method of Buddhist exposition, traditionally understood as “matching concepts” with Chinese philosophical terms; scholars now debate its nature and significance.
Translation team
A group of translators, scholars, and scribes who produced Chinese versions of scriptures collectively.
Apocryphal scripture
A text composed in China but presented as a translation from an Indian original.
Cave temple
A Buddhist sanctuary cut into a cliff, often containing sculpture, painting, and donor inscriptions.
Celestial Masters (天師道)
A Daoist tradition tracing itself to Zhang Daoling, organized around priests, registers, and covenant.
Shangqing (上清)
“Highest Clarity”; a Daoist tradition based on revelations of the 360s and focused on meditation and ascent.
Lingbao (靈寶)
“Numinous Treasure”; a Daoist tradition emphasizing communal ritual and universal salvation.
Revelation
A scripture or message said to be received from a divine being.
Xuanxue (玄學)
“Dark Learning”; a philosophical movement drawing on the Changes, Laozi, and Zhuangzi.
Hu (胡)
Chinese term for peoples of the north and west; its meaning varied and was often imprecise.
Sinicization
A debated term for the adoption of Chinese practices by non-Chinese groups; often criticized as one-directional.
Sogdians
Central Asian merchants and settlers active in trade and administration in northern China.
Equal-field system
A land allocation system of the Northern Wei and its successors, discussed in Chapter 22.
Zhuang (莊)
An estate with fields, workshops, and dependent labor.
Yue ware
Green-glazed stoneware produced in the Zhejiang region.
Family instructions
A genre of writing in which an author advises descendants on conduct and survival.
Genealogy (譜)
Registers of descent used to certify status and regulate marriage.
Libationer (祭酒)
A priest of the Celestial Masters community.
Three Caverns
The threefold arrangement of Daoist scriptures associated with Lu Xiujing.
Pure conversation (清談)
Elite philosophical discussion in the Wei-Jin period.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it changes the question that students ask of the centuries between the Han and the Sui. Instead of asking why unity failed, it asks what people made in the absence of unity. The answer includes new religious institutions, new regional societies, new mixtures of peoples, and new forms of art, scholarship, and technology, alongside suffering that should not be minimized.

These developments shaped later Chinese history in ways that Chapters 24 through 30 will explore. The Sui and Tang inherited a Buddhism embedded in society, a south with growing capacity, a northern elite of mixed origins, and institutions for land and military organization tested in the north. They also inherited tensions: between regions, between religious institutions and the state, and between older aristocratic status and new forms of recruitment. None of these outcomes was fixed in advance.

The chapter also connects China to a wider Asia. Monks, merchants, and craftsmen moved among Chinese states, Central Asia, India, Southeast Asia, and Korea, and the religion that took root in China was also spreading across East Asia. Chinese actors were participants in these exchanges as translators, pilgrims, patrons, and artisans.

Finally, the chapter models a way of seeing Chinese history as diverse, interconnected, and unfinished. Identity, religion, family, and region were negotiated over time, and the period shows how a civilization can be transformed while its political form is in flux. Students carrying this perspective into Chapter 24 should be prepared to ask of the Sui reunification not only how it happened, but what kind of society it brought together.
