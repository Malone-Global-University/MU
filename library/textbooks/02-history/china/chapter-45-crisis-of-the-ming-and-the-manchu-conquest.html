Part VII — Mongol, Yuan, and Ming China

Crisis of the Ming and the Manchu Conquest

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

On the morning of the nineteenth day of the third lunar month of 1644, the capital of the Ming empire fell to a rebel army. It did not follow that the rebels would rule, nor that the Manchus, already waiting beyond the Great Wall, would be the ones to succeed the Ming. The year 1644 was a convergence of several forces, not a single event.

Students often meet this period as a sequence: the Ming declined, rebels rose, the Manchus invaded, and the Qing began. The sequence is too tidy. It treats five related but distinct developments as one: the weakening of the Ming state, the fall of Beijing, the rise of the Manchu state, the Qing occupation of Beijing, and the completion of Qing conquest. They did not occur simultaneously, they had different causes, and none of them was inevitable. This chapter asks two questions that are easy to confuse. First, why did the Ming state collapse in 1644? Second, why did a Manchu-led Qing, and not Li Zicheng’s regime or a restored Ming court, emerge from the crisis?

Chapter 44 showed a late Ming society that was commercially sophisticated and culturally creative. This chapter shows that cultural vitality and state crisis can coexist. The distinction at the center of Sections 45.1 to 45.3 is between a wealthy society and a fiscally capable state. The Ming economy was large, monetized, and connected to world markets, yet the government’s revenue system, built on quotas set long before, could not easily capture or adapt that wealth when pressures mounted. When the pressures arrived together, in the form of expensive frontier war, harvest failure, disrupted silver flows, and rural distress, the system lacked the flexibility to absorb them.

What changed? In the span of roughly a generation, from the 1610s to the 1640s, the Ming lost control of Liaodong to a new northeastern state, saw armies go unpaid, watched rebel bands grow from local uprisings into regional regimes, and finally lost its capital. What continued? Local society did not simply vanish. Lineages, gentry households, villages, merchants, religious communities, and officials kept making decisions: some defended their communities, some fled, some joined rebels, some negotiated with whoever held power. Chinese administrative practice, the Confucian vocabulary of loyalty, and much of the bureaucracy outlasted the dynasty and were taken over by the new rulers.

Who was affected? Almost everyone, but unevenly. Farmers in the northwest faced drought and tax demand together; soldiers on the frontier served without pay; officials were forced to choose between loyalty to a failing court and survival; residents of Beijing experienced a rebel occupation and then a Qing one within weeks; Chinese families in Jiangnan faced armies and, after 1645, orders about hairstyle and dress that many read as attacks on identity. Manchu, Mongol, and Han people served in the same Qing armies, and Han Chinese generals and officials were essential to Qing success.

The evidence is rich and difficult. Official Ming records, memorials, gazetteers, diaries, eyewitness accounts of sieges, rebel proclamations, Qing court records in Manchu and Chinese, Korean and Jesuit observations, and later histories compiled under the Qing all survive. Many were written by participants with strong reasons to explain failure, justify survival, or establish legitimacy. Ming sources often labeled rebels “bandits,” and Qing histories had reasons to emphasize Ming misrule and their own role as restorers of order. Reading these sources critically is essential.

Finally, the chapter connects to wider histories. The Ming’s silver economy depended on flows linked to Japan, the Philippines, and the Americas. The Little Ice Age affected Eurasia widely. Korea, Mongolia, and the Jesuit-linked cannon-founding of the 1630s were all part of the story. And the Qing conquest began a new phase in the history of Inner Asia and East Asia, to be taken up in Part VIII. This chapter ends not with a clean victory but with a transition still under way, a boundary it shares with Chapter 46, which asks how the conquerors turned military victory into durable government.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the difference between a wealthy society and a fiscally capable state, using the Ming fiscal system as an example.
  2. Analyze how environmental stress became political crisis through institutions, markets, and state capacity, without climate determinism.
  3. Evaluate the role of silver disruption as an amplifier within a larger fiscal, environmental, military, and political crisis.
  4. Trace the connected frontier wars in Korea, Liaodong, and the north, and explain how war, taxation, arrears, and unrest reinforced each other.
  5. Describe the origins and character of the rebellions of the 1620s to 1640s, and explain the choices available to ordinary people.
  6. Interpret Li Zicheng’s movement, including its attempts at legitimacy, and the fall of Beijing in 1644.
  7. Explain the development of the Jurchen/Manchu state under Nurhaci and Hong Taiji, including its multiethnic composition.
  8. Reconstruct the sequence of 1644 and evaluate Wu Sangui’s choices in context.
  9. Distinguish the fall of the Ming, the fall of Beijing, the Qing entry into Beijing, and the completion of conquest.
  10. Assess Southern Ming regimes, loyalism, collaboration, and violence during the conquest.
  11. Compare explanations for the causes of Ming collapse with explanations for Qing victory.

Historical Context

The Ming dynasty (1368–1644) was founded by Zhu Yuanzhang after the collapse of Yuan rule (Chapter 40). Its early fiscal design reflected his vision of a rural, grain-based order: land taxes assessed in fixed quotas, labor service obligations, and hereditary military households that were supposed to feed themselves through garrison farming. By the sixteenth century, as Chapters 41 and 43 showed, the reality was very different. Population had grown, the economy had become monetized with silver, and the Single Whip reforms, spreading from the late sixteenth century, consolidated tax and labor obligations into silver payments. Those reforms made administration more rational and gave the government a more predictable revenue under stable conditions. They also tied the state’s revenue to silver and to the ability of rural households to obtain it.

Politically, the empire was governed by an emperor, a bureaucracy recruited largely through examinations, and an inner court of eunuchs and palace officials. The Hongwu emperor had abolished the chief ministership; later emperors relied on grand secretaries, whose power depended on the emperor’s confidence. The sixteenth and seventeenth centuries saw the long reign of the Wanli emperor (1572–1620), whose later decades were marked by disputes over succession, vacant posts left unfilled, and strained relations with senior officials. Under the Tianqi emperor (1620–27) the eunuch Wei Zhongxian dominated the court and persecuted critics associated with the Donglin movement discussed in Chapter 44. The Chongzhen emperor (1627–44) removed Wei and tried to restore discipline, but governed in an atmosphere of suspicion and rapid turnover of senior officials.

Geographically, the empire’s core was the agrarian lands of the Yellow and Yangtze river basins, with Jiangnan as its commercial heart. The northwest, including Shaanxi, and the northern provinces of Shanxi and Henan were poorer, drier, and heavily involved in supplying the northern military frontier. The northeast, Liaodong, was a frontier of garrisons and mixed Han, Jurchen, Mongol, and Korean contacts. Beyond it lay Jurchen homelands in the valleys of what is now Manchuria. To the north lay Mongol groups with whom the Ming alternated between trade, diplomacy, and war. The southwest included regions governed through local hereditary chieftains (tusi), with large non-Han populations. To avoid anachronism, students should recall that “Manchuria” and “Manchu” are later terms; “Manchu” as a name for a people was adopted in the 1630s, and these regions were not governed as parts of a modern Chinese nation-state.

The chapter’s boundaries matter. Chapter 43 explained silver, taxation, markets, and the Single Whip; Chapter 44 explained culture and ideas. Chapter 46 will explain how the Qing consolidated rule: Banner institutions, governing a conquered empire, the Three Feudatories, Taiwan, and the Kangxi emperor. This chapter explains how the Ming crisis became a Qing conquest.

Historical Evidence

The sources for the Ming collapse and Qing conquest are abundant but partisan. Students should ask of each: who wrote it, for whom, and after which events?

Ming government records and memorials. Court documents, grand secretariat papers, and officials’ memorials describe fiscal deficits, troop shortages, and rebel movements. They reveal what officials believed and wished the throne to hear, and often emphasize the writer’s own diligence. Local gazetteers and diaries record weather, harvest failures, epidemics, prices, floods, and local resistance, but vary in quality, and compilation dates may be later than the events. Eyewitness accounts of sieges and massacres are vivid and emotionally powerful, but written by survivors with their own perspectives, and numbers are often uncertain. Rebel sources are scarce; much of what is known about Li Zicheng and Zhang Xianzhong comes from hostile Ming and Qing writers, who often called them “bandits.” Qing sources in Manchu and Chinese, including the early reign records, show how the new rulers presented themselves, and many were compiled later to legitimize the dynasty. Korean, Jesuit, and other foreign accounts offer outside observation, but depend on limited access. Loyalist writings and later histories shaped memory; Ming loyalist writers, and later Qing and modern historians, have each framed the transition differently. Material and environmental evidence, including tree rings, ice-core and historical climate reconstructions, and archaeological evidence of fortifications and cannon, supplements texts, though regional resolution is limited.

Limits. Population and casualty figures are rarely reliable. The experiences of illiterate farmers, soldiers, and women are recorded mostly through the eyes of literate observers. Historians combine sources, test claims against one another, and stay explicit about uncertainty.

Section 45.1

Fiscal Problems and State Weakness

The first problem was not poverty but the gap between a wealthy society and a fiscally capable state. By the late sixteenth century the Ming economy was among the largest and most commercialized in the world (Chapter 43). Markets, silver, regional specialization, and merchant networks generated enormous wealth. Yet the central government drew most of its revenue from land taxes and related levies whose basic quotas had been set in the early Ming and were difficult to raise. Commercial wealth was held by merchants, landowning lineages, and officials’ families, many of whom enjoyed tax privileges or the means to evade assessments. The state had no effective way, such as a broad commercial or income tax, to tap this wealth quickly when emergencies demanded it.

Historical Vocabulary
Fiscal quota and arrears

A quota (e) was the fixed amount of tax a locality was expected to deliver, based on registers compiled long before. It gave the state predictability, but quotas could not easily be adjusted when population, land use, and wealth changed. Arrears were unpaid obligations: taxes uncollected in a locality, or salaries and rations owed to soldiers. Chronic arrears were a sign that nominal revenue and real revenue had diverged. Both terms matter because many late Ming crises, from mutinies to rebellions, grew out of unpaid obligations.

The reforms of the sixteenth century improved matters for a time. Zhang Juzheng, who dominated government as grand secretary from 1572 to 1582, tightened tax collection, conducted a land survey, and restored a surplus in the treasury. The Single Whip reforms, discussed in Chapter 43, combined many levies into a single silver payment. These reforms made the system more transparent. But they did not alter the underlying problem of fixed quotas and privileged exemptions, and after Zhang’s death his critics undid parts of his work.

Then came the wars. The Wanli emperor’s reign saw what are sometimes called the Three Great Campaigns of the 1590s: the suppression of a revolt in Ningxia, the defense of Korea against Japanese invasion, and the suppression of an uprising in the southwest among the chieftaincy of Bozhou. These campaigns drained the treasury, which was drawn on again to pay for later frontier wars. The Wanli court also sought new revenue by sending eunuch commissioners to collect mining and commercial taxes from the 1590s onward. These agents often acted heavy-handedly and provoked urban protests, and many officials saw the practice as predatory. It reveals the structural problem: the state needed money, but the regular tax channels could not supply it.

From 1618, war with the Jurchen state in Liaodong (45.4) generated new emergency levies. A “Liaodong surcharge” was added to land taxes in 1618 and increased in following years; later surcharges were added for suppressing rebellion and for training troops. These were small in percentage terms for wealthy households, but burdened poorer farmers who already struggled to find silver (45.3). At the same time, tax collection was difficult. Local clerks and gentry intermediaries held considerable power, and magistrates relied on them. Households could evade tax by sheltering land under privileged families. Arrears accumulated.

Military expenditure was the largest drain. The Ming army was dispersed among the old hereditary garrison households, whose farm-based system had decayed, and units paid in silver. Frontier soldiers, in particular, depended on regular silver and grain deliveries over long supply lines. When payments were late, soldiers suffered, deserted, or mutinied. Transport costs, notably moving grain to the northeast, were substantial. In 1629, as an economy measure, the Chongzhen court reduced the postal-station system, laying off many workers, including soldiers and couriers. Historians debate how significant that single policy was, but it illustrates how efforts to save money could create unemployed men with weapons and grievances.

Politics affected capacity. Court politics could paralyze decision-making. The Wanli emperor, from the 1580s, withdrew from regular audiences and left many posts vacant, partly in a protracted dispute over the succession; Ray Huang’s influential study of the year 1587 stresses institutional paralysis in the face of a ruler who could neither be replaced nor easily guided. Under Tianqi, Wei Zhongxian’s persecution of Donglin-associated officials, several of whom died in prison, deepened factional hostility. The Chongzhen emperor, who was conscientious, dismissed or executed many senior officials, including the general Yuan Chonghuan in 1630, and changed his grand secretaries often, which discouraged long-term policy. Corruption and diversion of funds certainly existed, but it should not be treated as a universal explanation. It operated within a system of fixed quotas, difficult supervision, and competing demands.

The result by the 1620s and 1630s was a state that could not borrow easily, could not raise tax rates quickly, and was dependent on a silver-based system exposed to external shocks (45.3). Society was rich; the state was in difficulty. That difference lies at the heart of the Ming crisis.

Section 45.2

Climate and Environmental Stress

Climate reconstructions from tree rings, historical records, and other evidence indicate that much of the Northern Hemisphere experienced cooler conditions during parts of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, a period often associated with the Little Ice Age. In China, gazetteers and diaries record severe winters, floods, droughts, locust plagues, and harvest failures, with notable clusters in the 1580s and, especially, from the 1620s to the early 1640s. The northwest and north, including Shaanxi, Shanxi, and Henan, suffered repeated drought, while the Yangtze region experienced flood and cold in some years. Conditions varied greatly from region to region and year to year.

It is crucial to avoid climate determinism. A drought does not automatically overthrow a dynasty. The question is how environmental shocks interacted with institutions and economies. The early 1640s were extreme. Drought, locusts, and famine struck the north in 1640 and 1641, and reports describe food shortages, soaring grain prices, people eating bark or grass, abandoned villages, and sales of children. Epidemic disease also spread in the north between about 1640 and 1644, recorded in local sources, including in the Beijing region. Some scholars suspect plague; others caution that the diagnosis of particular outbreaks from short historical descriptions is uncertain. The physician Wu Youxing wrote a treatise on epidemic disease in 1642 based on observation of outbreaks, evidence that contemporaries took epidemic disease seriously as a new problem.

Cause and Effect

Why Drought Becomes Political Crisis

Causes (environmental)

Colder, drier conditions in northern regions; repeated crop failures; locusts; epidemic disease.

Transmission

Harvest failure → food scarcity → price increases → households cannot pay taxes in silver → debt, land sale, and migration → greater demand for relief.

Institutional conditions

Fixed quotas and arrears; limited and unevenly distributed granary stocks; strained treasury; unpaid troops; local officials with little ability to forgive taxes without central approval.

Effects

Large numbers of displaced people and idle men; banditry and rebellion; state demands that grew while capacity shrank; social instability.

Historical complexity: The same weather did not produce the same results everywhere. Where granaries, local elites, and officials provided relief, communities coped better. The shock became a crisis because of the state’s weakened capacity and the pressure of taxes and war. Environmental shock is not the same as political collapse.

Relief capacity mattered as much as the weather. The Ming ideal was a network of state and community granaries that could be opened in famine, supplemented by tax remissions and charitable relief organized by gentry and lineages. By the seventeenth century, many state granaries were depleted or poorly managed, and remitting taxes cost revenue the treasury could not spare. Where local elites and magistrates mobilized grain and managed distribution, communities fared better; where they did not, flight and revolt were more likely. Evidence about epidemic disease is similarly uncertain. Gazetteers record sudden mass deaths and the emptying of villages in the north between about 1640 and 1644, but descriptions are brief, and historians continue to debate how much was plague, how much other disease, and how much was the effect of hunger, cold, and movement of armies and refugees. What is clear is that famine, epidemic, and war overlapped in the same places, and that each worsened the effects of the others.

Regional variation matters. Shaanxi in the northwest, an arid region long dependent on military supply and subject to frequent drought, was especially vulnerable. Henan, hit by drought, locusts, and later by floods from the Yellow River, suffered heavily. The Jiangnan core, with denser markets and more resources, was affected too, with harvest failures in 1640 and rising prices, but it had more capacity for relief and grain imports. In the south and southwest, other dynamics prevailed. Understanding the crisis means understanding these differences.

Environmental stress also affected the people’s responses. Some migrated to cities, to regions with better conditions, or to mountains and frontier areas; some turned to robbery; some joined armies; some sold land and became tenants or laborers. Local religious communities, charitable lineages, and granary managers organized relief where they could. Environmental history helps students see that the crisis was experienced as a series of hard decisions made by families and communities, not just an abstract force.

Section 45.3

Silver Shortages and Economic Crisis

Chapter 43 explained why silver made the Ming economy more monetized and more connected. This section asks what happens when a fiscal system dependent on silver experiences monetary disruption. The answer is not simple, and the most important warning is not to say that silver shortage caused the Ming collapse. Silver was an amplifier inside a larger fiscal, environmental, military, and political crisis.

By the late Ming, taxes were largely payable in silver, as a result of the Single Whip and related reforms. But most farmers earned their living in grain, cloth, or copper coins, not silver. To pay taxes, they had to sell produce for silver or exchange copper cash for silver. The terms of those exchanges depended on the supply of silver and the relative value of copper coins. In normal times, the system worked: commercial networks moved silver into rural markets, and households sold goods to obtain it. In stressed times, it could become a burden.

Silver arrived from Japan and, through Manila, from the Americas, in exchange for Chinese goods such as silk and porcelain. In the 1630s and 1640s, those flows appear to have been disrupted. The Tokugawa government of Japan restricted foreign trade in the 1630s and expelled the Portuguese in 1639, and conditions in the Spanish Philippines and the Atlantic and Pacific trades changed. Historians debate the extent and timing of the decline, and the data are imperfect; some scholars, including William Atwell, have argued that a contraction in silver inflow contributed to economic difficulty in the 1630s and 1640s, while others emphasize that estimates of flows are uncertain and that the effects were uneven.

In some regions, the effect appears to have been that silver became harder to obtain relative to copper and to grain. In the same period, copper coinage was affected by debasement and private minting, so the relationship between silver and copper fluctuated. Households earning copper or grain thus faced a higher real burden when taxes were fixed in silver. Merchants and landowners with silver reserves might do better; indebted farmers did worse. Market towns dependent on trade could see activity slow. Deflationary pressure on prices of some goods, together with local harvest failures that raised food prices, produced mixed and regionally varied effects. The key point is not a single price trend but that a silver-denominated tax system was exposed to disruptions in silver supply and in the silver–copper relationship.

This connects directly to the Single Whip. In Chapter 43, the reform was presented as a rationalization that made tax collection more predictable and reduced some forms of abuse. It also deepened the dependence of rural households on the market and on silver. Under one set of conditions, a reform that makes administration more efficient strengthens the state; under another, it can increase vulnerability. This is an example of how institutions’ strengths and weaknesses depend on context.

Silver stress also interacted with famine and taxation. Farmers hit by crop failure faced the same silver tax demands, plus emergency surcharges (45.1), and had less grain to sell. The state’s own receipts fell short, while military costs rose. Soldiers’ pay, also in silver, was delayed. Silver disruption therefore did not act alone; it helps explain how harvest failure, war finance, and rural debt reinforced one another.

Section 45.4

Frontier Warfare

Frontier war turned fiscal weakness into a military crisis. The key mechanism was a loop: war required taxation, which led to arrears and burdens on the population; arrears produced troop discontent and desertion; unrest weakened domestic control; and weaker control led to still more demands for military spending. This loop developed across several theaters, not only in the northeast.

Korea. In 1592, Japanese forces under Toyotomi Hideyoshi invaded Korea, aiming ultimately at China. The Ming sent armies to support the Korean kingdom of Joseon, and the war, with a pause and renewed fighting, lasted until 1598. The campaigns were costly, and Korean sources record both gratitude for the aid and hardship from provisioning Ming armies. For the Ming, the war exhausted resources just as another challenge was growing in the northeast.

China and the Wider World

Korea, Japan, Mongolia, and Europe in the Ming Crisis

The Ming crisis cannot be understood inside China alone. The Imjin War in Korea linked Ming finances to Japanese ambitions. Mongol groups on the northern frontier, with whom the Ming had traded since the 1571 settlement with Altan Khan, were courted and contested by both Ming and Jurchen leaders. Japanese and Spanish colonial policies shaped silver supply. Portuguese expertise mattered too: in the 1620s and 1630s, Ming officials, including Christian converts such as Xu Guangqi, sought European cannon and gunnery experts from Macao, and the Jesuit Johann Adam Schall von Bell was asked to supervise cannon-casting in 1636. Such technology helped in specific engagements but could not solve the larger fiscal and organizational problems. Later, Korea itself would be drawn into the Qing orbit after Hong Taiji’s invasions of 1627 and 1636.

The point is that Chinese diplomacy, trade, and military activity were part of active regional systems, not passive reactions to outside events.

The northern frontier and Liaodong. The Ming maintained fortifications and garrisons along its northern borders, often called the Great Wall defenses in later centuries, and supplied them with grain and silver. The northeast, Liaodong, was a zone of mixed settlement and military posts. By the late sixteenth century, Jurchen groups there had grown stronger through trade in furs, ginseng, and horses and through contact with Ming and Korean markets. Nurhaci’s consolidation of the Jurchens (45.7) brought a new, organized adversary. After he proclaimed the Later Jin in 1616 and attacked Ming positions in 1618, including the capture of Fushun, the Ming mounted a large offensive in 1619 that was defeated at Sarhu. Further losses, including the fall of Shenyang and Liaoyang in 1621, gave the Jurchens control of much of Liaodong. The cost of responding led to the Liaodong surcharge and to garrisons along defensive lines such as the Shanhai Pass–Ningyuan corridor.

Frontier forces were expensive and sometimes unreliable. Commanders often had private retinues, as with the Ningyuan garrison’s generals, and relied on supply of silver and grain. At Ningyuan in 1626, forces under Yuan Chonghuan, using cannon, inflicted a setback on Nurhaci’s army, and Nurhaci died later that year. Yet the court’s distrust of generals, leading to Yuan’s execution in 1630 after a Qing-side raid had reached the Beijing area in 1629 by a route through Mongol territory, showed how politics undermined military continuity. Hong Taiji’s repeated raids into the North China Plain in the 1630s demonstrated that the Ming could not secure all approaches to the capital.

Military reliability also depended on pay. Unpaid troops mutinied, deserted, or joined rebel bands. In the 1630s and early 1640s, the Ming had to fight on two fronts: against the Manchus in the northeast and against rebels in the interior. Troops were moved between them, and commanders such as Hong Chengchou and Sun Chuanting faced competing demands. In 1641–42 a major campaign in the Songshan–Jinzhou region, intended to relieve a besieged Ming garrison, ended in disaster; Hong Chengchou, who commanded, and the general Zu Dashou later surrendered to the Qing. These defeats drained remaining reserves and left the northeast defenses dependent on the Ningyuan line commanded by Wu Sangui.

Two further problems compounded the shortage of money: logistics and command. Supplying the northeast required moving grain and silver over long distances, by canal, road, and sea, to garrisons whose own farms no longer fed them, and every delay produced shortages at the front. Command was divided. Civilian governors-general and grand coordinators supervised generals, censors reviewed their conduct, and in the 1630s the court again used eunuch supervisors to monitor military affairs, a practice that many officials resented. Generals were dismissed, imprisoned, or executed after defeats, which gave commanders reasons to avoid risk or to keep personal retinues loyal to themselves. Troops differed in quality: frontier veterans and well-paid household soldiers were more effective than conscripts and unpaid garrison units. The result was an army that could win local engagements, as at Ningyuan, but struggled to sustain campaigns or to concentrate force where it was needed, especially when the court had to divide resources between the northeast and the interior.

This section leads in two directions. Fiscal and military strain created conditions for domestic rebellion (45.5). And the same strain, together with the growth of the Manchu state, opened an opportunity for the Qing (45.7).

Section 45.5

Rural Rebellion and Social Breakdown

Rebellion in the late Ming did not begin with a single leader or a single movement. It emerged from many local uprisings in the late 1620s and 1630s, especially in the northwest. Shaanxi had suffered drought and famine, tax and supply demands for the northern army, and an influx of unemployed soldiers and post-station workers. In 1627, local risings began in places such as Chengcheng; in the following years, bands formed around leaders such as Wang Jiayin and Gao Yingxiang. By 1630, several groups were active, including one led by Zhang Xianzhong, and Li Zicheng joined the rebel forces as a lieutenant, later becoming a leader himself.

Historical Vocabulary
“Bandits” (zei, kou) and the problem of sources

Ming and Qing officials usually called rebels zei or kou, terms translated as “bandits” or “brigands.” The words implied illegitimacy. Historians use them cautiously, because the writers who used them were hostile, and because the movements included many different people and aims: desperate farmers, deserters, local strongmen, and, later, leaders who sought to found dynasties. “Rebel” is more neutral but also imperfect. The terminology is itself evidence of how the state interpreted dissent.

Why did rebellion become possible and sustainable? Several conditions combined. First, famine and tax pressure left many without livelihoods. Second, disbanded or unpaid soldiers brought military skill and weapons; many early rebel units included former soldiers. Third, state coercion was weakened: units were stretched between fronts, and local officials often lacked force to pursue mobile bands. Fourth, rebel groups could move across jurisdictional boundaries, exploiting divisions between provinces. Fifth, the Ming response was inconsistent, alternating between campaigns and offers of amnesty. Rebel leaders sometimes accepted surrender and then rebelled again when promises of relief or pay were not kept. Zhang Xianzhong, for example, accepted pardon in 1639 and resumed revolt in 1640.

The movement was fragmented. Later accounts describe a major rebel council at Xingyang in Henan in 1635, though historians dispute whether the famous conference occurred exactly as those accounts portray it. The Ming achieved successes: Sun Chuanting and Hong Chengchou defeated Li Zicheng in Shaanxi in 1638, and Li withdrew with a small band into the mountains. But the Ming’s diversion of resources to the northeast, the famine of 1640–41, and further unpaid troops allowed the rebels to recover. Li returned to Henan in 1640 and gained followers among famine-stricken populations. In 1641 his forces took Luoyang and killed the Prince of Fu, a member of the imperial family; they then besieged the provincial capital Kaifeng several times. During the siege of 1642, the Yellow River’s dikes were breached, whether by rebels or by Ming officials attempting to repel them, accounts differ; the resulting flood devastated Kaifeng and killed very large numbers, and historians’ estimates vary widely.

Rebellion was not a faceless mass movement. People chose among options. Some stayed and endured; some fled to cities or hills; some joined rebels, willingly or under pressure; some joined government forces or local militias; some organized community defense with walls and watchtowers; some negotiated protection with armed groups; and some sought to protect family and property by paying tribute. Women, children, and older people were affected by raids and siege, and some women took active roles; in the southwest, for example, Qin Liangyu, a woman commander associated with the hereditary Shizhu tusi establishment in eastern Sichuan, commanded troops for the Ming against rebel forces for years. Non-Han groups in the southwest and elsewhere were also drawn into conflict.

Rebel organization changed over time. The early bands of the late 1620s and 1630s were mobile, loosely coordinated groups led by men with nicknames and followings built on personal loyalty and kinship. Their members included farmers driven off their land, former soldiers and post-station workers, miners, salt smugglers, and local bandits; some educated men and minor gentry also joined. Many bands lived by raiding, moving between provinces to avoid pursuit, and recruited by persuasion, promise of food, or pressure on villagers. As leaders such as Li Zicheng gained territory in the early 1640s, the movement changed: it began to hold cities, appoint officials, issue proclamations, and recruit administrators, and its social composition broadened. These changes created tensions between the habits of a raiding army and the needs of a government. At the same time, many communities and gentry households organized militias, repaired walls, and resisted rebels, so rebellion also divided local society.

Rebel leaders also developed political programs. Li Zicheng’s movement, joined by educated men such as Li Yan, circulated slogans promising to ease the tax burden and treat the poor better, and by the early 1640s Li styled himself as a ruler rather than a bandit chief. Section 45.6

Li Zicheng and the Fall of Beijing

Li Zicheng (c. 1606–45) came from northern Shaanxi. Later accounts describe him as a post-station worker or soldier who lost his livelihood, though the details of his early life are uncertain and have been embroidered by legend. He joined rebel forces around 1630, and after the death or capture of senior leaders became a leading commander, known by the title “Dashing Prince” (Chuangwang). After the setback of 1638, he rebuilt his following in Henan in 1640–41, recruiting among the famine-stricken. Educated men, such as Li Yan, joined him and helped turn a military movement into a political one. Slogans attributed to his movement promised relief from taxes and a fairer order; the extent to which these were implemented is debated, but they show that Li sought support beyond plunder.

By 1643 Li’s forces had taken much of Henan and Hubei, and in late 1643 captured Xi’an, the old northwestern center. Early in 1644 he proclaimed a new dynasty, the Shun, in Xi’an, with an administrative structure modeled on Chinese tradition: ministries, appointed officials, and a reign title. This was an attempt to construct legitimacy, not simply to loot. He then advanced into Shanxi and toward Beijing. Along the way, many Ming garrisons surrendered or offered little resistance. The Ming court, with its armies divided and its treasury depleted, could not mount an effective defense. The Chongzhen emperor, who had often distrusted his advisers, called on generals including Wu Sangui in the northeast to bring troops to defend the capital; Wu began withdrawing from the Ningyuan line, a move that abandoned positions beyond the Shanhai Pass.

On the nineteenth day of the third lunar month, corresponding to April 25, 1644, rebel forces entered Beijing, reportedly with the aid of defectors and little fighting. The Chongzhen emperor, finding his situation hopeless, took his own life on a hill behind the palace. Some officials and members of the court died with him or later; others surrendered to Li. Li’s administration then set about collecting money from officials and wealthy residents, using coercion and sometimes torture. This policy, intended to fill a depleted treasury and punish Ming elites, alienated many in the capital whose support Li needed. Reports also describe disciplined troops in the early days and growing disorder later; the evidence comes largely from hostile sources and must be weighed carefully.

Li’s Shun regime faced the problems of any new government. It borrowed Ming forms, with ministries, appointed prefects and magistrates, and reign titles, but it had few trained administrators of its own and relied on Ming officials who defected, many of whom were of doubtful loyalty. Its army remained a mobile force accustomed to living off plunder and tribute, while its program of tax relief was hard to reconcile with the need to feed a large army. Control of the countryside was thin: Li held cities and routes, but local gentry and garrisons in many areas remained outside his authority. In Beijing, the policy of extracting money from officials and wealthy families, enforced through coercion by his commanders, produced funds but also fear, and it undercut hopes that the new regime would restore order. When the news of Wu Sangui’s opposition arrived, Li left the capital to meet him with most of his main army, leaving a thin garrison, which helps explain how quickly the capital was abandoned after his defeat.

The essential point is that the fall of Beijing ended the Ming court’s control of the capital. It did not by itself determine who would rule China. Ming officials and princes survived in the south; Ming commanders held armies; Li’s new regime had control of Beijing but little of the empire’s military or fiscal infrastructure; and a rival power, the Qing, stood on the northeastern frontier. Li also faced the question of Wu Sangui, whose army remained the largest force near the capital. According to accounts, Li’s representatives sought Wu’s submission, holding Wu’s family in Beijing; Wu at first appeared willing to negotiate but then, for reasons discussed in 45.8, turned against Li.

1644 should therefore be seen as the convergence of competing forces: a collapsing Ming state, an ambitious rebel regime, a powerful frontier army under Wu, and a Qing state eager to exploit the opening.

Section 45.7

The Rise of the Manchus

The Manchus should not appear as an external horde waiting beyond the Great Wall. They built a sophisticated, expanding political and military state involving Manchu, Mongol, and Han constituencies, and they did so partly through contact with the Ming itself. The people who would be called Manchus descended largely from Jurchen groups, who had earlier founded the Jin dynasty (Chapter 36) and who lived in the forests and river valleys of the northeast, practicing farming, hunting, fishing, and trade. “Manchu” as a name for a people was adopted only in the 1630s, and identity within the group was a developing political creation, not a timeless ethnic fact.

Nurhaci (1559–1626), a leader of the Jianzhou Jurchens, began uniting rival groups in the 1580s. He gained wealth from trade in ginseng, furs, and horses, and from agriculture and tribute exchanges with the Ming, which for a time recognized him as a frontier commander. He promoted a written Manchu script, adapted from Mongolian script, in 1599. His most important institutional innovation was the Eight Banners, organized from the early 1600s and fully established by 1615. The banners grouped households into military and administrative units that provided soldiers, labor, and a basis for distribution of land and spoils, and tied them to Nurhaci’s leadership. In 1616 he proclaimed the Later Jin (also called the Latter Jin) and in 1618, in a document called the Seven Grievances, he explained his hostility to the Ming. His victory at Sarhu in 1619 and the capture of Shenyang and Liaoyang in 1621 gave him Liaodong; in 1625 he moved the capital to Shenyang (later called Mukden).

Historical Vocabulary
Banner (qi; Manchu gūsa)

A banner was a military and administrative unit identified by a colored flag. Originally four, later eight, banners grouped households under commanders who supplied troops and organized settlement and production. Under Hong Taiji the system expanded to include separate Mongol and Han banners. This chapter introduces the Banners to explain conquest. How the Banners structured Qing rule after 1644 is the subject of Chapter 46.

Nurhaci’s state was not simply a tribal confederacy. It relied on conquered populations, including Han farmers in Liaodong, many of whom were incorporated, sometimes as bondservants or laborers, and on skilled Han advisers. Its economy combined agriculture, trade, raiding, and the redistribution of captured resources. Nurhaci died in 1626 after the failed attack on Ningyuan, and the succession was resolved in favor of Hong Taiji (r. 1626–43).

Hong Taiji turned the Later Jin into a more centralized state. He recruited Han advisers and officials, such as Fan Wencheng, and adopted institutions modeled on the Ming, including a system of boards or ministries in 1631. He expanded the use of artillery, capturing Ming cannon and Han artisans and gunners, and in 1631 formed a Han artillery unit; Han military men organized into separate Han banners by 1642. Chinese defectors, among them Kong Youde, Geng Zhongming, and Shang Kexi, brought troops and expertise, and several would play prominent roles in the conquest. He also incorporated Mongols: after defeating the Chahar Mongols in 1635, he gained their leader’s submission and what was regarded as the seal of the Yuan emperors, a symbol with legitimacy value, and created Mongol banners. In 1635 he adopted the name Manchu for his people, and in 1636 he proclaimed a new dynasty, the Great Qing (Da Qing), at Mukden. He invaded Korea in 1627 and 1636, forcing the Joseon court into submission in 1637, and raided Ming territory repeatedly, probing defenses and capturing resources. By the early 1640s, with the surrender of Ming commanders after the Songshan–Jinzhou campaign (1641–42), the Qing had broken much of the Ming defensive structure in the northeast.

When Hong Taiji died in 1643, succession was settled by compromise: his young son, who would reign as the Shunzhi emperor, was installed, with the princes Dorgon and Jirgalang as regents. Dorgon quickly became the dominant figure. The Qing was thus a state with a multiethnic army, Ming-derived institutions, claims to dynastic legitimacy, and experience of siege warfare and cannon. It did not conquer China because the Ming was weak alone; it had also built substantial capacity of its own. Whether it could have conquered China without the domestic collapse of the Ming is a counterfactual that historians debate, but few would argue that the Qing was merely lucky.

Regional and Frontier Context

Four Regions, Four Different Crises

The northwest and north (Shaanxi, Shanxi, Henan): arid, dependent on the military supply system, hit by drought, famine, disease, and flooding; the cradle of the major rebellions. Liaodong and the northeast: a frontier of Ming garrisons, Han settlements, and Jurchen communities, where the Later Jin/Qing state formed; its people experienced conquest, incorporation, flight, and forced labor. Jiangnan: wealthier and better supplied, less directly affected by the early rebellions, but burdened by surcharges and then the scene of the fiercest resistance to Qing rule in 1645. Sichuan and the southwest: a region of mountains and non-Han chieftaincies, devastated by the movements of Zhang Xianzhong and later wars, with severe population loss; estimates vary widely. Frontier regions were places of negotiation, migration, and shifting allegiance, not simply the periphery of a Chinese center.

Section 45.8

Qing Entry Into China Proper

The events of spring 1644 hinge on Wu Sangui (1612–78), commander of the Ming forces at Ningyuan, the strongest Ming army in the northeast. He came from a military family in Liaodong, and his troops were experienced and well supplied relative to other Ming units. In early 1644, as Li’s forces advanced, the Chongzhen emperor ordered Wu to come to the defense of Beijing. Wu began withdrawing, evacuating Ningyuan and moving toward the Shanhai Pass. When Beijing fell on April 25, he was still some distance away.

Wu faced a stark choice. He could submit to Li, who held the capital and, reportedly, his father and family; he could resist Li on his own; or he could ally with the Qing, whose leaders under Dorgon were weighing intervention in China proper in response to the Ming collapse. Wu appears at first to have considered accepting Li’s offer. Then, according to accounts, he learned of ill treatment of his family and of the extortion of Beijing’s elites, and he decided to oppose Li. Wu wrote to Dorgon seeking help against the rebels. The later story that Wu acted out of rage over the seizure of a favorite concubine, Chen Yuanyuan, comes from romanticized literary accounts and should not be treated as established fact.

Dorgon agreed, with conditions. Wu’s forces, supported by the Qing, met Li’s army near Shanhai Pass on May 27, 1644. Wu’s troops bore much of the fighting, and the decisive moment came when Qing cavalry attacked Li’s flank. Li’s forces were defeated and retreated toward Beijing. Li formally took the imperial title in Beijing on June 3 and abandoned the city the following day, taking treasure and burning parts of the palaces. Dorgon entered Beijing on June 5–6 (sources and modern chronologies differ by a day) at the head of Qing and allied troops, and was received by officials who, in many accounts, hoped for restored order. The Qing presented itself as having come to avenge the Chongzhen emperor and suppress rebels, ordered mourning for the dead emperor, and retained many Ming officials. In autumn, the child emperor Shunzhi was brought to Beijing and enthroned, formally proclaiming the Qing capital at Beijing. Wu received a princely title.

The negotiations between Wu and Dorgon were shaped by calculation on both sides. After Fan Wencheng urged the Qing to exploit the crisis in China proper, Dorgon led the Qing Grand Army out of Shenyang (Mukden) on May 14, 1644. As news of Chongzhen’s death and Wu Sangui’s appeal clarified the situation, Fan Wencheng and the former Ming general Hong Chengchou advised presenting the Qing as avengers of the fallen Ming emperor and suppressors of the rebels, while restraining Qing troops from looting. Wu’s appeal gave Dorgon a pretext and a partner, and Dorgon in turn offered Wu rank and the chance to defeat Li. After the victory, Wu was rewarded with a princely title and sent in pursuit of Li’s forces, which also helped keep him occupied and under Qing supervision. Northern officials and gentry also made choices. Many Ming officials who had stayed in or near Beijing, and many local elites, had been alienated by Li’s demands and feared social disorder; they were prepared to accept the Qing as the force that restored order, avenged the emperor, and promised to retain Ming offices and institutions. Others, as the next section shows, refused. Acceptance was therefore conditional, shaped by fear of rebels, hope for stability, and the absence of any alternative authority in the north, not a general belief that the Qing was the rightful successor.

Students should avoid the formulation that Wu “opened the gates and handed China to the Manchus.” His decision occurred amid collapsing authority, competing armies, family and political interests, and rapidly changing information. He did not intend, as far as the evidence shows, to give the Qing the empire, and he likely expected Dorgon’s forces to help him defeat Li and perhaps restore a Ming prince. Equally, Qing entry should not be depicted as the inevitable consequence of Ming weakness. It resulted from Dorgon’s decision to seize an opportunity, from Wu’s choice, from Li’s mistakes, and from the political calculations of Ming officials and Beijing residents.

Section 45.9

Conquest, Resistance, and Dynastic Transition

It would be a serious error to imagine that China became Qing the day Beijing fell. The year 1644 was a turning point, not the completion of conquest. The Qing held Beijing and parts of the north; Li Zicheng’s forces were in retreat but not destroyed; Zhang Xianzhong was building a regime in Sichuan; and Ming princes and officials in the south were organizing new governments. The conquest of the rest of the Ming empire would take nearly two decades, and the Qing’s success depended heavily on Han Chinese generals and soldiers, including many who had served the Ming.

The Southern Ming. After Beijing’s fall, Ming officials in Nanjing selected Zhu Yousong, Prince of Fu, who became regent on June 7 and was proclaimed the Hongguang emperor on June 19, 1644. The regime was weakened by factional quarrels and by the independent power of generals, and its leaders disagreed about whether to prioritize resisting the Qing or defeating rebel forces. In 1645 Qing armies under Prince Dodo advanced south. The city of Yangzhou, defended by the official Shi Kefa, fell in May after a siege, followed by a massacre lasting several days; Nanjing then surrendered in June. Further Ming courts were set up: the Longwu emperor in Fuzhou (1645–46), supported by the powerful maritime figure Zheng Zhilong, who later surrendered to the Qing; the Shaowu emperor in Guangzhou; and the Yongli emperor, whose court at Zhaoqing and later in the southwest lasted until 1662, when he was captured in Burma and executed by forces under Wu Sangui. Zheng Zhilong’s son Zheng Chenggong (Koxinga) refused to surrender and continued resistance from the sea, a story that leads to Taiwan and Chapter 46.

Violence, collaboration, and the queue order. In Jiangnan, resistance was fierce. In mid-1645 the Qing regent ordered Han men to adopt the Manchu hairstyle, a shaved forehead with a braided queue, and Manchu-style dress, a policy that had been issued and withdrawn briefly in 1644 under pressure. Many Chinese regarded this as an assault on bodily integrity and cultural identity, associated with filial duty to preserve the body given by parents. Resistance flared in towns such as Jiangyin, which held out for weeks, and Jiading, where Qing troops carried out massacres. Violence by Qing forces was real and extensive, and its human cost is well documented in surviving local accounts, but numbers are uncertain and often contested. The Qing was not a monolithic army of Manchus: Han generals and troops, many of them former Ming soldiers, did much of the fighting.

Responses varied. Some Ming officials and scholars died in defense of the dynasty and were later honored as martyrs; some withdrew from public life and became loyalists who wrote in seclusion, as did later-famous thinkers such as Gu Yanwu, Huang Zongxi, and Wang Fuzhi; some, such as the poet and official Qian Qianyi, surrendered and served the new regime, often drawing the contempt of loyalists; some negotiated local arrangements. Tradition says that Qian’s companion, the courtesan-poet Liu Rushi, whom students met in Chapter 44, urged him to die rather than submit, and that he declined, an anecdote that shows how moral judgments about the transition were bound up with gender, loyalty, and memory. Women were also victims and actors: some died in sieges or by suicide to avoid capture, an act later commemorated, while others protected families or ran households in the aftermath.

Rebel regimes were also defeated. Li Zicheng, driven from Beijing, was pursued south and died in 1645, though the circumstances are uncertain. Zhang Xianzhong, who had proclaimed a Great Western kingdom in Sichuan in 1644, was killed in battle in 1647. His rule in Sichuan, and the wars that followed, caused severe population loss and disruption; historians debate the scale of his own responsibility compared with other armies and with famine and disease. Local people, including leaders such as Qin Liangyu, resisted him. Many of his followers joined Ming loyalist forces in the southwest.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From Ming to Qing, 1644–1650s

What changed

  • The ruling house and its core military elite: a Manchu-led regime with Banner forces, though dependent on Han and Mongol allies.
  • The northern capital passed through rebel and then Qing hands.
  • Hairstyle and dress policies challenged cultural norms for Han men.
  • Many communities, especially in Jiangnan, Sichuan, and the north, suffered violence, displacement, and population loss.

What stayed the same

  • Much of the bureaucratic apparatus, official titles, and administrative practice, with many Ming officials retained.
  • Local society: lineages, village life, gentry households, markets, and religion continued.
  • The Confucian vocabulary of loyalty and legitimacy, used by both Qing and loyalists.

Who experienced change most directly? Residents of besieged cities, men subject to the hairstyle order, and officials forced to choose. Who experienced continuity? Many villagers and clerks, for whom the day-to-day tasks of farming and tax payment continued. Boundary: How the Qing consolidated these gains into a durable system is the topic of Chapter 46.

The central question of this section is how the Ming collapse became a Qing conquest. The answer lies in the interaction of Ming fragmentation, Qing military capacity, the defection and cooperation of Han Chinese commanders, local divisions, and the weakness of rival regimes. It was not a single decision but a series of campaigns, negotiations, and betrayals.

Section 45.10

Chapter Review and Ming–Qing Transition Checkpoint

This chapter has traced several interacting causal chains. The first concerns the Ming’s internal crisis: fiscal weakness, environmental stress, monetary disruption, and military expenditure intensified rural hardship and state weakness, which led to rebellion and political fragmentation. The second concerns the Manchu state: Jurchen consolidation produced Manchu state formation, frontier victories, and stronger military and political capacity. The third is the convergence of 1644: Li Zicheng captured Beijing, Ming authority collapsed, and Wu Sangui’s decision and Qing intervention led to the Qing entry into Beijing and to a prolonged conquest and resistance.

Historical Debate

Why Did the Ming Fall?

The question: Historians have long debated how much weight to give long-term structural conditions versus political choices and contingent events.

Interpretation A — Structural and environmental pressures: Geoffrey Parker’s work on the seventeenth-century “global crisis” stresses climate stress and its effects across Eurasia, and William Atwell’s studies argue that falling silver imports were one factor, though not the only one, in the Ming’s economic difficulty in the 1630s and 1640s. On this line of argument the empire faced pressures that would have strained almost any government.
Interpretation B — Political, institutional, and contingent factors: Ray Huang’s work emphasizes institutional limits and administrative paralysis, while Frederic Wakeman’s study of the conquest gives far more weight to political and military processes and to the contingent events of 1644. On this line of argument, decisions by rulers, officials, and commanders, and the capabilities of the Qing, shaped outcomes that climate or silver alone did not determine.

Evidence: Supporters of A cite climate reconstructions, price and silver data, and records of famine and epidemic. Supporters of B point to variation among regions, the role of the Manchu state, and the actions of particular leaders. Many historians combine both: structural strain made the crisis likely, while political choices and contingencies shaped its course. A and B are analytical simplifications; the historians named do not form two organized schools, and each has a more specific argument than the box can show.

Why it matters: The debate bears on how historians treat environmental history, state capacity, and the role of contingency, and on whether the Ming’s fall is explained as inevitable or as the outcome of choices made under pressure.

The Fall of the Ming and the Rise of the Qing

Long-term causes of Ming collapse

Fixed quotas and limited revenue; privileged exemptions; decayed garrison system; dependence on silver; court factionalism.

Short-term and contributing causes

Frontier war and surcharges; famine, epidemic, and flooding of the 1630s–40s; unpaid troops; the rebellions; the loss of Ming northeast defenses.

Causes of Qing victory

Banner organization; incorporation of Mongols and Han; artillery; Han generals and officials; Wu Sangui’s decision; Li Zicheng’s defeat; divisions among Southern Ming regimes.

Effects

A prolonged conquest; severe regional destruction; loyalist and collaborationist legacies; a new ruling house in Beijing; the beginning of the Qing order (Chapter 46).

Historical complexity: Calling a dynasty “corrupt” does not explain why it collapsed when it did, or why this successor prevailed. The causes of the Ming’s fall and of the Qing’s victory are different questions.

The concluding question is: why did the Ming fall, and why did the Qing, not Li Zicheng’s regime or a restored Ming court, eventually emerge from the crisis? The Ming fell because a fiscally constrained state faced simultaneous pressures it could not absorb. The Qing prevailed because it had built a capable multiethnic state, because Han commanders and officials chose to serve it, and because rival regimes were divided and short of resources. Students must keep these two questions separate. They must also keep separate the fall of the Ming, the fall of Beijing, the rise of the Manchus, the Qing occupation of Beijing, and the completion of Qing conquest.

Chapter Summary

This chapter explained the collapse of the Ming state in 1644 and the beginning of the Qing conquest as a convergence of interacting forces, not as a morality tale of dynastic decline. Its central argument is that long-term fiscal and institutional weaknesses combined with environmental shocks, silver disruption, expensive frontier war, rural hardship, rebellion, and political fragmentation to bring down the Ming; that the fall of Beijing ended Ming rule at the capital without determining who would rule; and that Manchu military organization, Han and Mongol alliances, the defeat of Li Zicheng, and nearly two decades of conquest and resistance turned a Ming crisis into a Ming–Qing transition.

The first theme was the gap between a wealthy society and a fiscally capable state. The Ming economy was large and commercial, but government revenue depended on quotas that were hard to adjust, privileged exemptions, and a collection system reliant on local intermediaries. Wars of the 1590s and the northeastern wars after 1618 required surcharges that burdened poorer households, and arrears left troops unpaid. Court politics, from Wanli’s withdrawal to Wei Zhongxian’s persecutions and Chongzhen’s distrust of generals, further impaired decision-making.

The second theme was environmental stress. Cooler and drier conditions, drought, locusts, floods, famine, and epidemic disease struck the north especially hard in the 1620s to 1640s. Environmental shock became political crisis only through institutions: fixed taxes, limited relief, and a weakened army. Regional variation mattered, and so did the choices of households and communities.

The third theme was silver. A tax system denominated in silver exposed rural households to disruptions in the silver supply and the silver–copper relationship, and the Single Whip’s rationalization increased this exposure. Silver stress was an amplifier within a larger crisis, not a sole cause.

Fourth, frontier warfare tied the loops together. War in Korea, in Liaodong, and on the northern frontier raised taxes, produced arrears and desertion, and weakened domestic control. The Ming lost much of Liaodong, suffered raids on the capital region, and in 1641–42 lost the northeastern campaign that broke its defenses.

Fifth, rebellion emerged from famine, taxation, disbanded soldiers, and weak coercion, especially in Shaanxi, and spread across the north. Ordinary people chose among many options, and rebel leaders such as Li Zicheng and Zhang Xianzhong built regimes with political aims. Li’s capture of Beijing in April 1644 and the death of the Chongzhen emperor ended Ming control of the capital.

Sixth, the Manchu state had been developing in parallel. Nurhaci and Hong Taiji unified Jurchen groups, built the Eight Banners, incorporated Mongols and Han, adopted Ming-style institutions and artillery, and proclaimed the Qing in 1636. The Manchus were a developing political community with their own capacity, not an external horde.

Seventh, the decisions of 1644 shaped the outcome. Wu Sangui, facing competing options, joined with Dorgon, defeated Li at Shanhai Pass, and the Qing entered Beijing. Wu’s choice, Li’s mistakes, and Dorgon’s opportunism, rather than any single cause, produced the result.

Eighth, conquest was prolonged. Southern Ming courts, the sack of Yangzhou, the queue order, resistance in Jiangnan, the end of Li and Zhang, and the final defeat of the Yongli court in 1662 show that 1644 was a turning point, not the end. Han generals and officials were essential to Qing success, and communities and individuals responded with resistance, collaboration, flight, and mourning.

The chapter contributes to the larger history of Chinese civilization by showing that cultural vitality (Chapter 44) and state crisis can coexist, that dynasties fall and rise through concrete mechanisms, and that the Qing’s multiethnic origins shaped the empire to come. It leaves open the question that Chapter 46 will take up: how did the conquerors turn military victory into durable government?

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a dynasty ends, and how another begins, through concrete mechanisms rather than moral formulas. A rich society with a limited state, a series of environmental and financial shocks, costly wars, unpaid soldiers, and rebellion together brought down the Ming; but they did not guarantee a Qing victory. That required a capable frontier state, defecting commanders, and a decade of campaigns.

The transition shaped later Chinese history in lasting ways. The Qing inherited much of the Ming bureaucratic apparatus while preserving Manchu institutions such as the Banners, and its rulers faced the challenge of governing a population many of whom had resisted them. Loyalist memory, debates about collaboration, and the legacy of violence in Jiangnan and Sichuan would inform political and intellectual life for generations, and again in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries when writers revisited the conquest.

The chapter also connects China to wider Asian and global history: the Little Ice Age, the silver trades, the Japanese invasion of Korea, Korea’s subordination to the Qing, Mongol alliances, and European gunnery all appear in the story. Chinese rulers, commanders, merchants, and communities acted within, and helped shape, these connections.

Seen this way, Chinese history appears diverse, interconnected, contested, adaptive, regionally varied, politically changing, and unfinished. Part VIII takes up the next question: how the Qing turned conquest into a durable imperial order, beginning with Chapter 46.
