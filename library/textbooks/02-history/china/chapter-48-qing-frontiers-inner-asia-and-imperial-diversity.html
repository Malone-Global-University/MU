Part VIII — The Qing Imperial Order
48

Qing Frontiers, Inner Asia, and Imperial Diversity

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

In the summer, the Qing emperor left Beijing. At Chengde, north of the Great Wall, he received Mongol princes, hosted Tibetan lamas, hunted in imperial parkland, and presided over temples whose inscriptions were carved in Manchu, Chinese, Mongolian, and Tibetan. The Qing empire was governed from more than one place, in more than one language, and in more than one political idiom.

Visitors to Chengde (then often called Rehe, or Jehol) in the later eighteenth century saw a landscape that had been designed to make an argument. The Qing court built a summer residence of lakes, pavilions, and meadows, and ringed it with temples. One of them, completed in the late 1760s, was modeled on the Potala Palace in Lhasa. Another honored the Panchen Lama. In these settings the Qianlong emperor could appear to a Chinese official as a Confucian sovereign, to a Mongol noble as a lord of the steppe and a patron of Buddhism, to a Tibetan cleric as a protector of the faith, and to his own Manchu bannermen as the head of the house that had conquered a continent. None of these roles was a disguise. Each was a real dimension of how Qing rule operated.

This chapter studies the Qing as what it actually was: a Manchu-ruled, multiethnic, Eurasian empire. Chapter 47 examined the High Qing state at its most prosperous and administratively sophisticated, concentrating on the court, the bureaucracy, the economy of the eighteen provinces, and the ideology of imperial rule. This chapter turns outward and asks what kind of empire that state governed. It examines Mongolia, the Zunghar Khanate, Xinjiang, Tibet, and Manchuria, and the institutions the Qing developed to rule them: banners, leagues, garrisons, military colonies, resident officials, and religious patronage.

The central argument is that Qing expansion cannot be described accurately either as the natural "unification of China" or as the arrival of an outside conqueror in territories that had nothing to do with Chinese history. Both formulas read the present backward into the past. Qing rulers did not possess a modern national map to fill in. They built political space through marriage alliances, military campaigns, negotiated submissions, garrisons, religious patronage, trade regulation, and administrative improvisation. Incorporation did not erase local institutions, religious authority, or distinct identities. It often depended on preserving them.

The chapter also takes the human costs seriously. The destruction of the Zunghar state in the 1750s was one of the most devastating episodes of eighteenth-century Eurasian history. Warfare, epidemic disease, flight, enslavement, and deliberate killing together removed most of a population from the historical record. Historians disagree about how to characterize it, and Section 48.3 presents that debate. Other developments, such as the cooperation of many Mongol nobles with the Qing, the Tibetan government's use of Qing power for its own ends, and the migration of farmers and soldiers into new regions, were more complicated and do not fit a simple story of conqueror and conquered.

What changed during this period was the scale and shape of the empire. By the 1760s the Qing governed or supervised a political space far larger than any earlier dynasty ruling from Beijing had held, and the Qing court had defeated the last major nomadic power on the steppe. What continued was the multiplicity of the arrangements: no single "frontier policy" ever replaced the many local ones. The people affected included Mongol princes and herders, Zunghar warriors and families, oasis farmers and Muslim religious leaders in the Tarim Basin, Tibetan monks and aristocrats, Manchu bannermen and their households, Han soldiers, convicts, and settlers, and the hunters and fishers of the northeast.

The evidence is as multilingual as the empire. Historians use Chinese-language documents, but also the large Manchu-language archives of the Qing court, Mongolian legal and administrative texts, Tibetan government and monastic records, Persian and Turkic sources from the Tarim oases, and Russian diplomatic correspondence. Imperial maps, military dispatches, stone inscriptions, and the architecture of the temples at Chengde supplement the written record. A history that relied only on the central government's Chinese-language records would reproduce the view from Beijing that this chapter aims to complicate.

The chapter connects Chinese history to the wider history of Eurasia. The Qing campaigns against the Zunghars occurred as the Russian empire pushed eastward across Siberia, as the Tibetan Buddhist world competed for political patronage, and as the great nomadic steppe empires gave way to expanding agrarian-gunpowder states. Qing frontier history belongs equally to the history of China, Inner Asia, Russia, and the early modern world.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain why the Qing is best described as a layered, multiethnic Inner Asian empire rather than a uniform Chinese state.
  2. Distinguish the different political arrangements the Qing used in Mongolia, Xinjiang, Tibet, Manchuria, and the eighteen provinces.
  3. Analyze why many Mongol nobles cooperated with Qing rule and what privileges and autonomy they retained.
  4. Trace the rise and destruction of the Zunghar Khanate and evaluate competing historical interpretations of its end.
  5. Describe how Qing rule in the Tarim Basin and northern Xinjiang was established and negotiated, and explain the historical terminology for its peoples.
  6. Interpret how Qing authority in Tibet changed between 1720 and 1793 without projecting later sovereignty claims backward.
  7. Explain Manchuria as a continuing homeland and frontier region, including the Willow Palisade and Banner society.
  8. Evaluate the roles of the Lifan Yuan, garrisons, ambans, military colonies, and logistics in frontier administration.
  9. Assess religion as an instrument of imperial authority, especially Tibetan Buddhism and Islam.
  10. Analyze how migration and settlement changed identities and political space, and why Qing categories differ from modern ethnic categories.
  11. Interpret a Qing frontier map and explain why it differs from a map of a modern nation-state.

Historical Context

The Qing empire grew out of the northeastern borderlands of the Ming. In the early seventeenth century the Jurchen leader Nurhaci united many Jurchen groups under a new military and social organization, the Banners, and founded a state later called the Later Jin (1616). His son Hong Taiji expanded the state, incorporated Mongol and Han Chinese households into the Banner system, and in 1636 adopted the dynastic name Qing. The ethnonym "Manchu" was formalized in these same years. From the beginning, therefore, the Qing was not a purely Jurchen or Manchu state. It was a coalition in which Mongol allies and Han military and administrative personnel held significant positions. Chapter 46 followed the conquest of Beijing in 1644, the consolidation of rule in the Ming provinces, and the long reign of the Kangxi emperor.

Geographically, the empire ultimately connected several very different environments. The eighteen provinces of the Ming heartland, which the Qing inherited, contained the densest agricultural populations. To the north lay the Mongolian steppe, divided into the "Inner" Mongols closer to Beijing and the Khalkha Mongols farther north in what is now the modern state of Mongolia. To the northwest lay the Zunghar basin, the Tianshan mountains, and the oasis cities of the Tarim Basin. To the southwest lay the Tibetan plateau, whose governance involved the Dalai Lama and other Buddhist hierarchs. To the northeast lay the forested river valleys of Manchuria, the Amur region, and the Liaodong peninsula, the homeland of the dynasty.

Political thought in these regions differed. In the Chinese tradition the emperor's authority drew on Confucian ideas of virtuous rulership, ritual, and the Mandate of Heaven (Chapter 8). In the Mongol and Inner Asian tradition legitimacy was connected to descent from Chinggis Khan, military leadership, and the ability to distribute rewards and protection. In the Tibetan Buddhist world authority was often articulated through the relation of a lama and a lay patron, in which a religious teacher and a powerful protector sustained each other. Manchu rulers participated in all of these idioms, and they skillfully presented themselves in different ways to different audiences.

The wider international setting mattered. The steppe had for two thousand years produced powerful nomadic confederations (Chapter 16 discussed the Xiongnu), but by the seventeenth century the balance was shifting. Russia was advancing across Siberia to the Pacific, and the Zunghars, western Mongols of the Oirat confederation, were building an expansive state that combined pastoral warfare, Tibetan Buddhist patronage, oasis taxation, and firearms. Both the Qing and the Zunghars competed for the loyalty of the Mongols and the favor of the Tibetan Buddhist establishment. Concepts of "China" shifted accordingly (see Chapter 1). Within Qing documents the word Zhongguo could denote the Qing realm as a whole or the inner provinces, depending on context, and Manchu-language sources used the term Dulimbai gurun (the "central state") in diplomatic texts. These shifts show that the question of what "China" meant was itself part of imperial politics.

Historical Evidence

How Historians Know: The Multilingual Archive of the Qing Frontiers

The evidence for this chapter is deliberately plural. Manchu-language archives in Beijing, Taipei, and elsewhere preserve palace memorials, Grand Council records, and Banner documents, many with no complete Chinese counterpart; the Veritable Records (Shilu) and campaign histories known as fanglue record court-approved accounts of wars. Mongolian-language legal texts and banner records show how Mongol nobles administered their lands. Tibetan-language government documents, biographies of lamas, and monastic records offer the Tibetan view of relations with Beijing. Persian and Turkic chronicles and local documents from the Tarim oases preserve Muslim and Khoja perspectives. Russian archives record negotiations at Nerchinsk and Kiakhta and observations of Zunghar and Qing affairs. Maps, such as the Jesuit-assisted surveys produced for the Kangxi and Qianlong courts, show how the court saw its territory. Stone inscriptions, paintings, and architecture (the temples at Chengde, the Yonghe Temple in Beijing) reveal imperial self-presentation.

Limits: Court-sponsored campaign histories describe victory and loyalty and say little about those who died or fled; Qing officials wrote about frontier peoples in categories that suited administration; and the surviving Zunghar voice is largely absent because the state itself was destroyed. Historians must combine sources and read each against its purpose.

Section 48.1

The Qing as an Inner Asian Empire

The opening question for this chapter is how one dynasty could rule peoples who did not necessarily understand political authority, religion, territory, or identity in the same way. The Qing answer was not a single doctrine. It was a set of overlapping relationships, each adapted to a region and its history.

One Ruler, Many Roles

Within the eighteen provinces, the emperor governed through the civil bureaucracy inherited from the Ming, staffed by examination graduates and supported by the Grand Secretariat, the Six Boards, provincial governors, and county magistrates (Chapters 41 and 47). Among Mongol constituencies he could appear in a khan-like role as a military leader and distributor of honors. Tibetan Buddhist institutions understood him through patron-priest relationships. In Manchuria he was the head of the house whose Banner institutions and ancestral sites defined the dynasty. In Xinjiang he became, eventually, the overseer of garrisons that stood over local Muslim communities governed through their own leaders. The Qing was therefore layered rather than uniform.

Historical Vocabulary
Layered Empire

A layered empire is a state in which different regions and peoples are governed through different institutions, legal norms, and ideological appeals, with the ruler occupying a distinct role in each layer. The term is used by historians to contrast the Qing with a model in which a single administrative system spreads outward from a capital. Historians sometimes also speak of "Inner Asian" and "Chinese" modes of rule within the Qing.

The term should not suggest rigid separation. The layers interacted: Han Chinese officials served in Banner garrisons, Mongol nobles studied at the Beijing court, Tibetan lamas lived in Beijing temples, and silver collected as taxes in provinces such as Jiangnan supported frontier armies in Xinjiang.

Not a Modern Map

It is tempting to read the eighteenth-century Qing realm through the borders of the modern People's Republic of China, or to treat the history of Mongolia, Tibet, and Xinjiang only in terms of how they relate to those borders. Neither approach captures how contemporaries understood political space. Qing officials distinguished the inner provinces (neidi), the Banner homeland, Mongol banners, Tibetan polities, Muslim oases, and "outer" or "vassal" realms such as Korea, each according to a different relationship to the throne. A modern boundary line was not the organizing principle. Authority was exercised through personal allegiance, garrisons, tax and tribute obligations, religious ties, and the court's ability to project force along specific routes. Boundaries were often zones rather than lines, and were demarcated in some places (for instance, with Russia in 1689 and 1727) and left undemarcated in others.

Why Expand?

Historians offer several explanations for Qing expansion into Inner Asia, and none excludes the others. Security was one: the steppe had repeatedly produced threats to dynasties based in the agricultural heartland, and the Qing, as a dynasty with steppe connections, understood this danger intimately. Competition for the allegiance of Mongols and Tibetan Buddhists was another, since the Zunghars could mobilize the same religious and genealogical authority. Imperial ideology also mattered: Qing rulers wished to be seen as universal rulers, and military victories and the incorporation of diverse peoples reinforced that claim. Fiscal and commercial interests, trade in horses, tea, and silk, and the opportunity to relieve population pressure by settlement played smaller but growing roles. Finally, momentum counted; each campaign created new commitments, garrisons, and enemies.

Section 48.2

Mongolia and Qing Rule

"The Mongols" were not a single political community. By the seventeenth century, Mongol-speaking peoples were divided among the Inner Mongol groups nearer the Ming frontier, the Khalkha Mongols in the north, the Oirat or western Mongols including the Zunghars, and smaller groups elsewhere. They were linked by language, descent claims, and, increasingly, Tibetan Buddhism, but were politically fragmented. The Qing negotiated separately with each.

Alliance with the Inner Mongols

The Manchus and Inner Mongols had interacted for generations. Nurhaci and Hong Taiji arranged marriages with Mongol noble families, a practice that continued for generations and tied Mongol aristocrats to the imperial house. Hong Taiji's campaigns against the Chahar Mongol ruler Ligdan Khan, the last Mongol leader with a strong claim to Chinggisid supremacy, ended with Ligdan's death in 1634. His son Ejei surrendered to Hong Taiji in 1635 and was said to have transferred the Yuan imperial seal, and in 1636 Mongol princes formally acknowledged Hong Taiji's new imperial position. The Manchu ruler could thus claim a role connected with Mongol imperial legitimacy. Mongol allies took part in the conquest of the Ming, and Mongol Banners were created within the Eight Banners.

Banners, Leagues, and Privileges

Qing rule over Mongols was organized through the banner (qi), a territorial and military unit under a hereditary prince known as a jasak, and through leagues (meng), assemblies of banners that met periodically under Qing supervision. Banners restricted movement: herders were generally expected to remain within their banner's pasture, which limited the formation of large, mobile military forces and reduced the possibility of the kind of confederation that had threatened earlier dynasties. Nobles received titles, stipends, intermarriage with the imperial family, and recognition of their rights over herders. They also took part in the court's ceremonial life, attending audiences and hunts at Chengde and its surrounding hunting ground, Mulan.

Historical Vocabulary
Jasak, Banner, and League

A jasak (Mongolian zasag, Chinese zhasake) was the ruling prince of a Mongol banner, with hereditary authority subject to confirmation by the Qing court. A banner (qi) was both an administrative territory and a military unit. A league (meng) grouped several banners for periodic meetings, conducted under a Qing-appointed league chief. These terms should not be confused with the Eight Banners (baqi) of the Manchu dynastic military order, although the two systems shared a name and some features; the Mongol banner was a territorial structure of Mongol society, while the Eight Banners were an organization of households, many living in garrisons.

The Khalkha and the Zunghar Threat

The Khalkha Mongols had long been outside Qing control. They were divided among several noble houses, and their religious leader, the Jebtsundamba Khutughtu, was a major figure in Mongol Buddhism. Conflicts among them, and the expansion of the Zunghar ruler Galdan, brought crisis. In 1688 Galdan invaded Khalkha territory; thousands of Khalkha fled south. In 1691, at a great assembly at Dolonnor, Khalkha leaders formally accepted Qing overlordship, and Kangxi confirmed their rank and organized them into banners. The submission was not simply a defeat. Khalkha nobles recognized that the Qing could provide protection against the Zunghars, which neither a divided Khalkha nor Russia could. Many Khalkha families retained high rank and their religious leader remained influential.

Why Cooperate?

Historians stress that Mongol cooperation was neither passive nor uniform. Nobles gained security, legitimacy, wealth through stipends and trade, marital ties to the throne, and recognition of their local authority. The Qing, in turn, gained military manpower, buffers on the steppe, and a ruling coalition that could claim Mongol support. The costs, however, were real: loss of independent war-making, restrictions on movement, the shift of Qing policy toward Mongol herders in times of famine, taxes and corvée obligations, and, over time, the pressure of Han Chinese farming settlement and merchants' debts on pastureland. Mongol commoners were bound to the land in a hereditary hierarchy and experienced Qing rule through their own nobles first. Those dynamics help explain the continuing combination of loyalty and grievance in Qing Mongolia.

Qing administration in Outer Mongolia was supervised from garrison centers such as Uliastai and Khobdo, with resident officials at Urga (now Ulaanbaatar), the seat of the Jebtsundamba Khutughtu. Qing law placed Mongols under special legal codes administered through the Lifan Yuan, rather than under the general Chinese code, a separation that reinforced differences between Mongol and Han populations.

Section 48.3

The Zunghars and Inner Asian Competition

A Serious Inner Asian State

The Zunghars (also Dzungars) were Oirat Mongols whose power centered on the Ili valley and the Zungharia basin north of the Tianshan. Under Galdan and his successors they built a state that combined pastoral military power with oasis taxation in the Tarim Basin, artisans and farmers, trade with Russia, and cannon and firearms production. Tibetan Buddhism supplied a religious framework: Galdan had studied in Tibet and received recognition from Tibetan authorities. The Zunghar state was not a rebellion against the Qing. It was a rival empire, with its own political tradition, its own diplomacy with Russia and Central Asian states, and its own claim to lead the Mongols and protect Tibetan Buddhism.

Galdan and Kangxi

Galdan's invasion of Khalkha in 1688 led to a series of Qing campaigns. In 1696 Kangxi personally led an army across the Gobi; at the Battle of Jao Modo, Qing forces with artillery defeated Galdan's army. Galdan died in 1697, but the Zunghar state survived under his nephew Tsewang Rabtan, who expanded westward and eastward, took Hami and the Tarim oases under pressure, and in 1717 sent an army to Tibet that seized Lhasa and killed the Khoshud ruler Lhazang Khan. Qing forces entered Lhasa in 1720 (Section 48.5). Conflicts continued in the 1720s and 1730s; negotiations in 1734 failed to produce a treaty because the two sides differed over the Khanggai and Altai line, a truce was maintained, and truce and boundary negotiations were completed by 1739.

Crisis and Qing Intervention

After the death of the Zunghar ruler Galdan Tseren in 1745, succession disputes divided the Zunghar nobility. In the early 1750s the contender Dawachi took power, and the noble Amursana, defeated by Dawachi, sought Qing support. In 1755 Qianlong sent armies into the Ili valley with Amursana's help. They captured Dawachi and the Zunghar state collapsed. When Qing plans left Amursana without the leadership he expected, he rebelled; the Qing campaigns of 1756–1757 pursued him and other Zunghar leaders. Amursana fled to Russian territory, where he died of smallpox in 1757.

Catastrophe

The campaigns coincided with smallpox epidemics, famine, flight to Kazakh and Russian lands, and killing by Qing and allied forces. In 1757 the Qianlong emperor issued orders to exterminate Zunghars who continued to resist, a policy of mass killing documented in Qing records. Survivors were enslaved, distributed as servants to Banner households, or resettled; some Zunghar groups were integrated into Qing Banner structures. The region was largely depopulated. Later Chinese writers, notably Wei Yuan in the nineteenth century, offered figures of several hundred thousand dead. Modern historians treat such numbers with caution, since they are retrospective estimates, but there is wide agreement that a large majority of the Zunghar population died, fled, or was displaced.

The Qing court commemorated victory with inscriptions in four languages, paintings by court artists, and a campaign history. Its language of "pacification" (pingding) conceals the scale of destruction. Students should read such terms as the official vocabulary of the victors rather than as neutral description.

Historical Debate

How Should Historians Characterize the Qing Destruction of the Zunghars?

The question: Historians broadly agree on the core facts: Qing campaigns between 1755 and 1759 ended the Zunghar state, Qianlong ordered the extermination of Zunghars who continued to resist, and the population was devastated by killing, smallpox, famine, flight, enslavement, and displacement. The scholarly question is how to characterize this combination of an exterminatory policy and a demographic catastrophe, and how much of the total loss resulted from Qing killing as opposed to disease, flight, and famine.

Interpretation A — The policy and intent justify the term genocide: Some scholars argue that Qing edicts ordering the extermination of resisting Zunghars, the scale of killing, the enslavement of survivors, and the subsequent resettlement of the land show an intent to destroy the Zunghars as a people, and that the modern term genocide is therefore appropriate. They stress that epidemic disease, though responsible for a very large share of deaths, operated alongside and was compounded by deliberate violence and deprivation.
Interpretation B — The exterminatory policy is acknowledged, but the modern legal category is questioned: Other scholars, while accepting the documentary evidence of deliberate exterminatory orders, caution against applying a twentieth-century legal concept to an eighteenth-century imperial war. They point to the aim of destroying Zunghar political power, to the incorporation of some Zunghar nobles and soldiers into the Banners, and to the very large role of smallpox, flight, and famine in total mortality. Some prefer terms such as mass killing or exterminatory warfare.

Evidence: Qianlong's edicts and Qing campaign records, Manchu and Chinese court documents, Russian and Kazakh reports, and later reconstructions. The best-known estimate, by the nineteenth-century writer Wei Yuan, attributes roughly 40 percent of the loss to smallpox, 20 percent to flight, and 30 percent to Qing killing, but these are retrospective estimates and not census-quality data. The Zunghar voice is largely absent from the surviving record.

Why it matters: The debate concerns how historians apply modern legal and moral categories across time, how they weigh imperial rhetoric against practice, and how they separate deliberate killing from epidemic and displacement without minimizing either.

Student task: What kinds of evidence would help estimate how much of the population loss resulted from Qing killing versus disease, flight, and famine? Why does the absence of Zunghar-authored sources limit both interpretations?

Section 48.4

Xinjiang and Qing Expansion

A "New Frontier"

The name Xinjiang (新疆), "new frontier" or "new dominion," was applied to the lands conquered in the 1750s, and it was not used for a single province until 1884. In the eighteenth century the region comprised several parts that the Qing governed separately: the northern former Zunghar lands (Zungharia, the Ili valley, and Tarbagatai), the Tarim Basin oases to the south (Kashgar, Yarkand, Khotan, Aksu, and others), and the eastern oases of Hami and Turfan, which had been connected to the Qing earlier.

Conquest of the Tarim Oases

The Tarim oases had been under Zunghar dominance, and the Zunghars held prominent Khoja leaders as hostages. The Khojas, descendants of respected Muslim holy lineages, had political influence. After the Zunghar collapse, the brothers Burhan al-Din and Khoja-i Jahan, whom the Qing had released, refused to accept Qing supervision. A Qing army besieged Kucha and then pursued them to Kashgar and Yarkand in 1758–1759. The brothers fled and were killed in Badakhshan. Qing control was established, but resistance and local rivalries continued.

How Qing Rule Was Negotiated

The Qing did not attempt to remake the oases in the image of Chinese counties. In the Tarim oases, local Muslim officials known as begs, headed in each major oasis by a hakim beg, handled daily administration, tax collection, and local justice, while Qing resident officials and garrisons provided oversight. Hereditary privileges were reduced, and begs were ranked and salaried within a Qing system, which tied them to the court. Taxes on oasis agriculture were relatively light compared with the provinces and were often collected in kind or in local currency, and the court sought to avoid provoking religious opposition. The Qing military governor, the Ili General (office created 1762), supervised the whole region from the new garrison town of Huiyuan in the Ili valley. Qing officials in the oases often disliked the begs' reliance on customary exactions, while many begs benefited from the new order; abuses by both Qing officials and begs were a source of later grievance.

Trade was important. The Qing regulated horse and textile trade and the movement of merchants from Central Asian states such as Kokand, a polity that gained commercial privileges and later became a rival for influence in Kashgaria. Islamic law and institutions such as mosques, shrines, and schools continued, and Qing officials supervised but did not abolish them.

Peoples and Terminology

Care is needed with names. In the eighteenth century Qing texts referred to the Tarim Basin Muslims in several ways, including "Hui" (a term whose meaning varied), "Chantou" (turbaned) in some texts, and regionally as "the Muslim frontier" (Huijiang). Local people more commonly identified by oasis (people of Kashgar, Khotan), by religion, by language (Turki), and by lineage. The term Uyghur as a modern ethnic label for Turkic-speaking Muslims of the oases was adopted in the early twentieth century, building on the name of a medieval Turkic people (Chapter 27). Applying it automatically to eighteenth-century Tarim residents imposes a later classification, though historians recognize continuities of language, religion, and region. Historical writing therefore often says "Turkic-speaking Muslims of the Tarim Basin" or uses local terms.

The north was repopulated in new ways: Manchu and Sibe bannermen and Solon families were moved from the northeast as garrison communities; Tarim farmers (Taranchi) were resettled to farm in the Ili valley; Han and Hui merchants and settlers entered; Kazakh and Mongol pastoralists used the grasslands; and in 1771 the Torghut Mongols, who had migrated to the Volga in the seventeenth century, returned eastward in a dramatic mass movement and were settled in parts of the region. The northern region thus became a patchwork of newly assigned communities.

Section 48.5

Tibet and Imperial Relations

Qing relations with Tibet are among the most politically charged subjects in modern historical writing, because they are cited in contemporary disputes about sovereignty. A historian's task is to establish what Qing authority actually meant in the eighteenth century, in institutions and practice, without imposing either later claims of continuous, uniform rule or later claims that Qing involvement was only ceremonial.

Before Direct Qing Intervention

By the seventeenth century the Gelug school of Tibetan Buddhism, led by the Dalai Lamas, dominated Tibetan religious and political life, with the backing of Mongol patrons. The Fifth Dalai Lama (1617–1682) established the Ganden Phodrang government with the military support of the Khoshud Mongol leader Gushri Khan. The Manchu court sought relations with the Dalai Lama from the 1640s; the Fifth Dalai Lama visited Beijing in 1652–1653 and was received with great honor. The relationship was framed through Buddhist patronage and Mongol politics rather than administrative subordination, and was not governed by a single shared understanding of what it implied.

Zunghars, Lhasa, and the 1720 Expedition

Tibetan politics became entangled in succession disputes after the death of the Fifth Dalai Lama and in the rivalry between Khoshud Mongol leaders and the Zunghars. In 1717 Zunghar troops took Lhasa and killed Lhazang Khan. Many Tibetans, who had questioned Lhazang's choice of Dalai Lama, initially tolerated the Zunghars, but the occupation became oppressive and damaged monasteries. In 1720 Qing armies, accompanying a young Kelzang Gyatso whom Tibetans and Mongols recognized as the Seventh Dalai Lama, expelled the Zunghars from Lhasa. Qing forces then withdrew most of their garrison, and the Qing court appointed Tibetan nobles to administer.

Ambans and Tibetan Government

After a civil conflict in 1727–1728, in which a leading minister was assassinated and the Qing sent troops, the court established resident officials, the ambans, in Lhasa. The Tibetan noble Polhané Sonam Topgyé governed Tibet with Qing backing until 1747. A period of tension followed; after his successor was killed by the ambans in 1750 and the ambans in turn were attacked, the Qing reorganized Tibetan government to place more authority with the Dalai Lama and a council (kashag) of lay and monastic ministers, supervised by the ambans.

Historical Vocabulary
Amban

The word amban is Manchu for "high official" and was used for Qing resident officials in Lhasa (Chinese zhuzang dachen, 驻藏大臣) and elsewhere. Their authority changed over time. Early ambans were largely observers and liaisons with a small escort; later, especially after 1793, they were formally entitled to take part in major decisions about Tibetan foreign relations, military affairs, and important appointments. Historians debate how effective ambans were in practice, since their power depended on the quality of the individual official, the strength of Qing garrisons, and the attitude of Tibetan leaders.

The Gurkha Wars and the 1793 Settlement

The Gurkha rulers of Nepal clashed with Tibet over trade and coinage in the late 1780s and invaded in 1788; after a first Tibetan–Nepalese settlement was broken, they invaded again in 1791, plundering Tashilhunpo. In 1792 a large Qing army marched from Sichuan and Qinghai, drove the Gurkhas out, and forced a treaty. The Qing victory was among the "Ten Great Campaigns" that Qianlong commemorated. Following it, the court approved a set of reforms. The Golden Urn initiative was already under way in 1792, when an urn was sent to Lhasa, and was incorporated in 1793 into the Twenty-Nine Article regulations, which strengthened the ambans' role, introduced the Golden Urn for identifying the reincarnations of leading lamas, set limits on foreign contact, regulated finance and the Tibetan army, and established garrisons.

What Did Qing Authority Mean?

The eighteenth-century evidence points to a changing relationship. Qing authority in Tibet included military protection and intervention (1720, 1728, 1750, 1792), a resident supervisory presence, influence over appointments, a role in religious succession, and expectations of loyalty and gifts. It did not include direct taxation by Qing officials, replacement of Tibetan law and administration with Chinese counties, or settlement by Han farmers on any significant scale. The Dalai Lama and the Tibetan government, in turn, drew on Qing power to settle disputes and fend off external threats, while preserving broad control of internal affairs. Both sides described the relationship in different vocabularies: the Qing used terms of imperial patronage and incorporation, and Tibetans stressed religious relationship and the patron-priest bond. Historians disagree about how to label this relationship in modern terms such as sovereignty, suzerainty, or protectorate, and these modern terms were not categories used in the eighteenth century. The prudent approach is to describe the institutions and let readers compare them to later claims.

Section 48.6

Manchuria and the Northeast

Textbooks often treat Manchuria only as the place the Qing came from. For the Qing it was a continuing imperial homeland, a military reservoir, a source of tribute goods, and a frontier with Russia. Its history under the Qing was marked by an effort to preserve a distinct society and the gradual failure of that effort.

Homeland and Capital

Shengjing (Mukden, modern Shenyang) remained a secondary capital with its own boards, a palace, and the tombs of early rulers. Other ancestral sites, including the tombs near Xingjing, were maintained, and emperors, including Kangxi and Qianlong, traveled to the northeast to perform rituals. Qianlong's compilations on Manchu origins, ritual, and genealogy reflect the court's concern to define Manchu identity. Hunting, riding, and archery were treated as defining Manchu skills, and the Mulan hunt near Chengde served to maintain them.

Banner Society and Military Governors

The northeast was governed through military governors (jiangjun) at Shengjing, Jilin, and Heilongjiang, with Banner garrisons and Banner households holding land and rights. Many Banner families were granted land and provisions and had a legal status separate from commoners. Banner society, however, changed: Manchu language skills declined even among bannermen, some Banner households struggled financially, and, in some areas, Han bannermen and Han tenants and laborers worked the land. Within Banner communities, Manchu, Mongol, and Han bannermen overlapped in complex ways.

Peoples of the Northeast

The northeast was home to many Tungusic and Mongolic peoples, including the Daur, Evenki, Solon, Orochen, Nanai (Hezhe), and others, who fished, hunted, herded, and farmed. The Qing incorporated many into Banner-like units (the "New Manchu" groups) and demanded tribute in sable and other furs, which fed court consumption and diplomacy. Ginseng, pearls, and timber were valuable resources subject to imperial monopoly and regulation. Relationships between these peoples and the Qing were shaped by exchange, obligation, and coercion, and the peoples themselves were not simply Manchu.

The Willow Palisade and Settlement Controls

The Qing sought to control movement into the northeast with the Willow Palisade (Liutiaobian), a system of ditches, earth embankments, and willow plantings with gates, built mostly in the seventeenth century around Liaodong and extended into Jilin. It was not a fortress wall but a boundary and customs line regulating entry and protecting reserves of ginseng, hunting grounds, and Banner lands. Han settlement was officially restricted, although the early Qing also encouraged some settlement in Liaodong to restore agriculture. Over the eighteenth century, however, famine and land pressure in Shandong and Zhili drove migrants northward. Officials turned a blind eye, enforced rules sporadically, or granted exemptions, and large numbers of Han farmers settled illegally. By the nineteenth century formal restrictions were repeatedly relaxed and then abandoned, as the court acted to settle and defend the region against Russian pressure.

Russia and the Amur

Russian expeditions entered the Amur basin in the mid-seventeenth century. The Qing responded with campaigns and negotiations, culminating in the Treaty of Nerchinsk (1689), negotiated with the help of Jesuit interpreters, which set a boundary along rivers and mountains and recognized Qing control of the Amur region, and the Treaty of Kiakhta (1727), which regulated commerce and the boundary of Mongolia. The treaties show the Qing in diplomatic engagement with a European power on terms of negotiated equality, a topic the textbook takes up in Chapter 51 and later. The nineteenth century would see these arrangements overturned (Chapter 55).

The northeast thus shows the Qing's attempt to hold a homeland apart and its inability to prevent change: its society was transformed by in-migration, commercialization, and Russian pressure.

Section 48.7

Frontier Administration and Military Institutions

The main lesson of the regional sections is that there was no single Qing frontier policy. The court developed different arrangements according to local conditions, existing institutions, and the strength of the Qing position.

The Lifan Yuan

The Lifan Yuan (理藩院), usually translated as the Court of Colonial Affairs or Office for Managing the Outer Regions, originated in the 1630s as a body for Mongol affairs. It later supervised Mongol banners and leagues, Tibetan relations, aspects of Muslim affairs in Xinjiang, and relations with Russia. It drafted law codes for Mongols and managed ranks, stipends, and audiences. It was staffed largely by Manchus and Mongols rather than by Chinese examination graduates, which reflected the court's wish to administer the Inner Asian layers separately from the civil bureaucracy of the inner provinces.

Garrisons, Generals, and Residents

Military power rested on Banner garrisons and, in many areas, on Green Standard troops drawn from Han soldiers. Military governors or generals (jiangjun) in Shengjing, Jilin, Heilongjiang, Ili, Uliastai, and elsewhere combined military command and civil oversight. Resident officials or ambans held posts in Lhasa, Urga, Kashgar, and other places. Local hereditary or appointed intermediaries, such as Mongol jasaks, Tibetan nobles, and Muslim begs, handled daily administration. This combination allowed the Qing to rule at modest cost by relying on locals, and to keep potential rivals divided, but it also left the court dependent on the loyalty and competence of intermediaries.

Military Colonies

The tuntian (屯田), military-agricultural colony, had a long history (see Chapter 16). In Xinjiang the Qing established several kinds: soldiers' colonies (bingtun), where Banner and Green Standard troops farmed; "Muslim colonies" (huitun), where Tarim farmers were resettled to produce grain for the garrison; "household colonies" (hutun) of Han civilian settlers; and "convict colonies" (qiantun) of exiled criminals. These produced grain for armies, reduced transport costs, and brought settlers to areas that had been depopulated by war. They also created tension with local pastoralists and with older settlers over land and water.

Logistics

Conquering and holding Inner Asia was a logistical task of extraordinary scale. Armies needed grain, fodder, horses and camels, weapons, silver, and information. The court built roads, relay stations (yi, tai) and courier systems to speed memorials, established granaries, and bought horses and camels from Mongols and Kazakhs. Xinjiang long relied on silver transfers (xiexiang) from the inner provinces to cover the gap between local revenue and military expenses. The cost was substantial; the frontier armies absorbed a large share of the revenue that supported the High Qing state (Chapter 47), and the reliance on inland subsidies would become a liability when finances tightened in the nineteenth century.

Cause and Effect

How Military Conquest Became Imperial Administration

Causes

Long-term: Steppe security concerns; competition with the Zunghars for Mongol and Tibetan allegiance; Banner military organization; fiscal capacity of the High Qing state. Short-term: Galdan's invasion of Khalkha (1688); the Zunghar occupation of Lhasa (1717); the Zunghar succession crisis (1750s); Khoja resistance (1757–1759).

Event or Transformation

Campaigns defeated rivals and left Qing forces in new regions. To hold them, the court created garrisons, appointed generals and residents, relied on local intermediaries, organized colonies, and funded the system with provincial silver.

Effects

Immediate: Qing control of Mongolia, Zungharia, Tarim, and an enlarged role in Tibet; depopulation and resettlement. Long-term: a large, costly frontier system; ethnic and religious complexity in the empire; precedents for later claims and for later settlement and provincial reorganization.

Historical complexity

Conquest did not automatically become administration; it required improvisation and negotiation, and the result differed region by region. Costs, local resistance, and the quality of intermediaries all affected outcomes. The same institutions that secured control also created dependency on subsidies and local cooperation.
Section 48.8

Religion and Imperial Authority

This section concerns religion as imperial politics on the frontiers. Chapter 50 treats religion more broadly as part of Qing cultural and everyday life. In Inner Asia, religion was not merely private belief; it organized legitimacy, diplomacy, community leadership, territorial networks, and economic life.

Tibetan Buddhism and the Court

Qing rulers cultivated ties with the Gelug hierarchy from the 1640s. They invited lamas to court, built and funded monasteries, and sponsored the translation and printing of Buddhist texts in Manchu, Mongolian, Tibetan, and Chinese. The Yonghe Temple in Beijing, formerly a princely residence, was converted into a Tibetan Buddhist monastery in 1744. At Chengde the "Outer Temples" combined Tibetan architectural forms with Chinese and Manchu elements. The Putuo Zongcheng Temple, built in 1767–1771 to celebrate Qianlong's sixtieth birthday and the Zunghar victories, was modeled on the Potala. The Panchen Lama (the sixth) came to Chengde in 1780 for Qianlong's birthday and was received with great ceremony; he died of smallpox in Beijing the same year. Qianlong was depicted in some Tibetan-style paintings as an incarnation of Manjushri, the bodhisattva of wisdom, an image that appealed to Tibetans and Mongols.

Historians read these actions on several levels. They show genuine imperial interest in Buddhism and reflect sincere patronage, and also served political purposes: they tied Mongol and Tibetan elites to the throne, provided a counterweight to Zunghar claims to be defenders of the faith, and gave Qing rule a place in the sacred geography of Inner Asia. The relationship carried risks for the court, since powerful lamas could attract Mongol loyalty and exert political influence, which is one reason the Golden Urn reforms were adopted.

Mongol Buddhist Communities

For Mongols, Buddhism had become a defining institution since the sixteenth century. Monasteries were major landholders and centers of education; the Jebtsundamba Khutughtu in Urga held spiritual leadership. The Qing sought to monitor the selection of high reincarnations and to restrict the number of Mongol religious figures, and it also provided monastery subsidies. Large numbers of Mongol men entered monasteries, which affected demography and the labor base of Mongol society.

Islam in Xinjiang

In the Tarim Basin, Islam was the religion of the majority population and the framework of law, education, and community leadership through mosques, shrines, and Sufi networks. Qing officials generally supervised but did not suppress it, because they feared revolt; in practice, begs and religious leaders mediated. Policy was not uniform: attitudes toward Khoja lineages were hostile after 1759, while local religious judges continued to apply Islamic law in family and community matters. Qing officials also regulated pilgrimages and contacts with Central Asian rulers, and Khoja descendants in exile in Kokand later led incursions into Kashgaria in the nineteenth century (Chapter 53). Muslim communities elsewhere in the empire, such as the Hui of the northwest, had different histories (Chapter 53).

Religion and Imperial Self-Presentation

The court's multilingual inscriptions, ritual feasts, and temple building communicated an image of a ruler who protected diverse faiths. This was not mere tolerance. The Qing also punished religious movements it regarded as seditious, as in the White Lotus uprisings (Chapter 47), and attempted to control religious leadership where it saw political risk. In the frontier, religion was thus both a resource and a potential source of challenge.

Section 48.9

Migration, Identity, and Political Space

Expansion changed who lived where. Migration here is treated insofar as it changed frontier rule, settlement, identity, and political space; Chapter 49 will examine population movement as part of Qing demography and society as a whole.

Who Moved

Soldiers and Banner households were assigned to garrisons from Manchuria to Ili, sometimes with their families. Han settlers moved to Manchuria despite prohibitions and to Xinjiang under state-sponsored colony schemes and as merchants and laborers. Convicts were exiled to Xinjiang and the northeast, and some later became settlers. Displaced and resettled peoples included surviving Zunghars, Tarim farmers moved to Ili, Sibe and Solon bannermen sent west, and the Torghuts who returned from the Volga in 1771 after a perilous journey with great losses. Mongols faced restrictions on movement but also experienced migration due to famine and to the encroachment of Han farmers on pasture, especially in Inner Mongolia. Merchants from Shanxi and elsewhere followed the armies and became central to frontier trade, often extending credit to Mongol nobles and herders, a practice that created lasting indebtedness.

What Qing Categories Meant

Qing administration sorted the population into categories that were legal and administrative, not racial or national in the modern sense. Bannermen (Manchu, Mongol, Han Martial) had status, stipends, and obligations. Commoners in the provinces were registered in households. Mongols were registered in banners under jasaks. Tibetans were governed through their own institutions. Muslims in the oases were governed through begs. These categories could overlap or change: a Han Martial bannerman might be culturally Manchu by the nineteenth century, while a Mongol banner member could be a monk, herder, or farmer; Hui communities of the northwest were distinguished by religion more than by language; and a person's identity could be local (oasis, valley, monastery, clan) before it was imperial. The Qing itself promoted "Manchu" identity through genealogies and language policy even as the lived reality was more mixed.

Producing Political Space

The key conceptual point is that Qing political space was produced historically. It was assembled in specific moments, through campaigns, alliances, treaties, and administrative acts, and it contained zones of different intensity of control. A map drawn in 1760 would show the court's claims, but those claims included direct provincial administration, banner territory, garrison-supervised oases, and a Tibetan polity under supervision. A modern national map imposes a single color; the Qing's own understanding recognized gradations. Recognizing this does not decide modern disputes. It allows readers to ask what the eighteenth-century institutions actually were before considering later arguments.

Continuity and Change Over Time

From the Ming Frontier to the Mature Qing Imperial Geography

What Changed

  • The Ming frontier, anchored on the Great Wall and the northern defense lines, gave way to a political space extending far beyond the wall, with the wall losing much of its strategic role for the Qing
  • The steppe was no longer an independent source of major rival states after the Zunghar destruction
  • Xinjiang, much of Mongolia, and a Qing role in Tibet were added to the empire's orbit
  • Banner garrisons, colonies, and new administrative offices (Ili General, ambans) were created

What Stayed the Same

  • Uneven control and dependence on local intermediaries, as in earlier dynasties' frontier policies
  • The importance of trade, horses, and diplomacy, and the Silk Roads' connection to Central Asia (Chapters 16 and 27)
  • The Mongol and Tibetan religious and political institutions, which continued under Qing supervision
  • Chinese bureaucracy within the provinces, with the examination system and county government

Who experienced change most directly? The Zunghars, Tarim oasis communities, Mongol herders affected by banner rules, Tibetan elites, and northeastern peoples. Who experienced continuity? Provincial farmers and county officials, whose daily lives were affected indirectly through taxes funding frontier armies. Why does it matter? It shows that the Qing was both heir to earlier Chinese dynastic institutions and an innovator in Inner Asian rule.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living on the Qing Frontiers

Pastoral Mongolia. A Mongol herding family lived in felt tents, moved seasonally within banner boundaries, raised sheep, horses, camels, and cattle, and paid obligations to its noble and the Qing. Monasteries offered education and medicine; many families sent a son to the monastery. Trade with Han merchants brought tea, cloth, and metal goods, while debts could burden households. Women managed household production, milk processing, and felt-making and in some situations managed herds in the absence of men on military service.

Oasis farming. In the Tarim oases, irrigated agriculture produced wheat, millet, cotton, fruit, and silk. Villages organized water by canals and irrigation customs, bazaars linked caravans from India, Central Asia, and the inner provinces, and mosques and Sufi shrines structured community life. Women worked in textile production and households; religious norms shaped dress and seclusion in different ways in different communities.

Banner garrisons. In garrison towns, bannermen drew stipends and rations, practiced archery and riding, and lived in separate quarters from the local population. Their households included women and children who also relied on the Banner system. Prestige and obligations did not always match income, and many Banner households fell into debt.

The northeast. Hunters and fishers of the Amur region paid tribute in furs, while Han settlers cleared land for millet, soybeans, and sorghum. Winters were severe; the soybean trade later transformed the economy. Evidence for ordinary lives comes from gazetteers, travelers' accounts, tribute registers, and archaeology; elite and official sources dominate and the voices of non-literate peoples are mediated by outsiders.

Section 48.10

Chapter Review and Qing Frontier Map Study

This chapter has argued that the Qing empire is best understood as a layered, multiethnic Eurasian empire whose rulers governed different peoples through different institutions. Before the review materials, three further perspectives, regional, global, and chronological, consolidate the chapter's themes.

Regional and Frontier Context

Comparing the Qing Frontiers

  • Inner Mongolia: Early allies; banners and leagues; marriage ties; increasing Han farming settlement on pastureland.
  • Outer Mongolia (Khalkha): Submitted 1691 under Zunghar pressure; Qing garrisons and residents at Uliastai, Khobdo, Urga; Buddhist hierarchy retained influence.
  • Zungharia and Ili: Former Zunghar heartland; devastated and repopulated with garrisons, colonists, and resettled groups under the Ili General.
  • Tarim Basin: Oasis communities under Qing supervision with Muslim begs, Islamic institutions, and trade links to Central Asia.
  • Tibet: Tibetan government under Dalai Lama with Qing ambans, periodic military intervention, and religious patronage.
  • Manchuria: Homeland with Banner governors, Willow Palisade, tribute peoples, increasing Han settlement, and Russian frontier.
  • Southwest and Taiwan: Treated in other chapters; they illustrate that frontier governance also involved non-Han peoples in the south (Chapters 46 and 49).

Frontier regions are best understood as zones of interaction, trade, conflict, migration, and negotiation. Frontier peoples had their own political traditions, and their relations with the court were not only passive; they negotiated, resisted, used Qing power for their own ends, and sometimes shaped Qing policy.

China and the Wider World

The Qing frontier story was part of larger Eurasian processes. Russia and the Qing both expanded into Siberia and Inner Asia, and their treaties at Nerchinsk and Kiakhta were among the earliest agreements between the Qing and a European state. Kazakh groups, Kokand, and Qing and Russian officials competed and traded on the Central Asian steppe. Tibetan Buddhism connected Tibet, Mongolia, the Zunghar lands, and the Qing court, while Islamic networks connected the Tarim oases to Central Asia and India. Qing campaigns used firearms and artillery, and Jesuit missionaries assisted in mapping, interpretation, and cannon-casting. The Qing was an active participant in these networks, as a diplomat, a trade regulator, a military power, a patron of religion, and a cartographer. It should not be seen as passive or isolated, nor as simply expanding "China" in the modern sense. The nineteenth-century Russian advance and British and Central Asian competition (Chapters 53 and 55) would test the system the eighteenth century had built.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Qing was a Manchu-ruled, multiethnic, Eurasian empire and not simply a Chinese dynasty that extended its borders. Its rulers governed different peoples and regions through different institutions, and the court presented itself in different idioms to different audiences. Incorporation created a much larger imperial political space but did not erase local identities, religious authority, or the legacies of earlier states.

The Manchu state began as a coalition of Manchu, Mongol, and Han households organized in Banners. Alliance with the Inner Mongols, formalized through marriage, banners, and leagues, gave the Qing a Mongol dimension from the start. The Khalkha submission of 1691 extended it north, in response to Galdan's invasion, and showed that Mongol nobles often cooperated for reasons of protection, status, and advantage, while accepting limits on mobility and independence.

The Zunghar Khanate was a major Inner Asian state that competed with the Qing for the allegiance of the Mongols and Tibetan Buddhists. After the wars of Kangxi against Galdan and the later rivalry in Tibet, the Qianlong campaigns of the 1750s destroyed it. Warfare, epidemic disease, flight, enslavement, and killing removed most of its population. Historians disagree on how to characterize this, and the absence of Zunghar-authored sources limits the evidence.

After the Zunghar collapse the Qing took the Tarim oases and created Xinjiang, administered through a military governor at Ili, garrisons, colonies, and Muslim begs. Resettled Banner families, Tarim farmers, Han merchants and convicts, and returning Torghuts reshaped the population. Historians avoid assuming that eighteenth-century oasis Muslims were identified as "Uyghurs" in the modern sense, while recognizing continuities.

In Tibet, Qing authority developed from patronage and military assistance (1720) to a resident presence (ambans from 1727–1728) and formal supervisory regulations (1793). It never involved the kind of direct administration used in the provinces, and its meaning changed over time. Modern sovereignty arguments should not be projected backward without examining those institutions.

Manchuria was a continuing homeland and frontier. The Qing preserved Banner society, ancestral sites, and tribute relations, and tried to restrict Han settlement through the Willow Palisade, but migration eroded the restrictions. The treaties of Nerchinsk and Kiakhta show diplomacy with Russia.

There was no single frontier policy. The Lifan Yuan, garrisons and generals, ambans, jasaks, begs, military colonies, and a vast logistics system made expansion possible, and were funded by provincial silver. The system was effective but costly and dependent on local intermediaries.

Religion was central to frontier governance. Patronage of Tibetan Buddhism connected the court to Mongol and Tibetan elites, while the court supervised Islamic communities in Xinjiang with pragmatic caution. Migration and administrative classification changed identities, and Qing categories were legal and administrative rather than modern ethnic ones.

The chapter's contribution to the larger history of China is to show that the modern territorial and demographic shape of the region owes much to the Qing's Inner Asian empire, and that its history cannot be reduced either to unification or to foreign conquest. The institutions and relationships created in the eighteenth century would be tested in the nineteenth century by Russian expansion, internal rebellion, and imperialism, which Chapters 51 through 57 examine.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it changes the question students ask about the Qing. Instead of asking how "China" expanded, it asks how a Manchu-led dynasty assembled and governed a political space in which Mongols, Tibetans, Muslims, Han, Manchus, and the peoples of the northeast stood in different relationships to the throne. That question reveals the creativity and the costs of the Qing order: new institutions, religious diplomacy, and administrative improvisation on one hand, and mass death, displacement, and coercion on the other.

The settlements of the eighteenth century shaped later history. Garrisons, colonies, settlement patterns, and treaties laid foundations that nineteenth- and twentieth-century governments would inherit, reinterpret, and contest. This does not mean the later history was inevitable. Chapters 51 through 57 will show how Russian expansion, rebellion, imperialism, and revolution transformed or broke the system described here.

The chapter also connects Chinese history to Eurasia. The Qing competed and cooperated with Russia, Kokand, Tibetan Buddhist institutions, and steppe societies, and it was shaped by those relations as well as shaping them. Students who understand the Qing frontier as a space of interaction, negotiation, and unequal power are better prepared to read Chinese history as diverse, interconnected, contested, adaptive, regionally varied, politically changing, and unfinished.
