Part IX — Nineteenth-Century Crisis and the End of the Qing
54

Self-Strengthening and the Transformation of Qing Power

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

In 1861 the Qing dynasty was fighting for its life against the Taiping, the Nian, and a foreign army that had just burned the Summer Palace. Within a generation its officials were casting guns, launching steamships, laying telegraph wires, translating chemistry textbooks, and posting diplomats to London and Washington. Whether this amounted to transformation or merely adaptation is the question of this chapter.

In the early 1860s, at Anqing on the Yangtze, a small group of Chinese craftsmen and scholars set out to build a steam engine. Hua Hengfang and Xu Shou, mathematically trained men with no formal position in the examination hierarchy, worked under the patronage of Zeng Guofan, the Hunan scholar-general whose army had just taken the city from the Taiping. They had no foreign instructors. They had a few translated books, some imported machine parts to examine, and the skills of local metalworkers. Accounts based on Zeng's diary and on later biographies of the craftsmen suggest that Zeng watched the early trials with great interest, understanding that a state that could reproduce such machines would no longer need to buy its military technology from the very powers that had defeated it. A few years later the same circle produced a small Chinese-built steamboat. The scene is modest, but it contains the main elements of the chapter: a regional official acting on his own authority, Chinese artisans and translators doing the technical work, a tight link between military need and technical experiment, and a political setting in which the central court was watching but not directing.

This chapter studies what historians call the Self-Strengthening Movement (ziqiang yundong), also known in Chinese-language scholarship as the Foreign Affairs Movement (yangwu yundong). It was not a single program or an organization with members and a charter. It was a cluster of projects, running from roughly the 1860s to the mid-1890s, through which Qing officials tried to rebuild state power after the Opium Wars, the Taiping Rebellion, and the other mid-century upheavals (Chapters 52 and 53). The projects included arsenals, shipyards, a foreign-affairs office, language schools, translation bureaus, a student mission to the United States, a steamship company, coal mines, telegraph lines, and the first railways. Most were launched by provincial officials rather than by the Beijing court, and each had its own financing, management, and fate.

The subject matters for several reasons. It shows how the Qing state responded to defeat, not only by yielding, but by experimenting. It explains how regional power, already expanded by the wartime armies described in Chapter 53, acquired institutional form. It marks the first sustained encounter between Chinese statecraft and industrial technology, and it created a generation of Chinese engineers, interpreters, diplomats, and entrepreneurs who would shape later reform and revolution (Chapters 56 and 57). It also supplies the background for the war with Japan in 1894–1895 (Chapter 55), which is often invoked to prove that the whole effort was futile. This chapter argues that such a judgment, made from the vantage point of a single defeat, hides more than it reveals.

Much changed in this period. The Qing created a permanent foreign-affairs institution, accepted resident foreign envoys in Beijing, and sent its own ministers abroad. Revenue increasingly came from sources outside the old land tax, including customs duties and the internal transit tax called lijin. Provincial governors-general commanded armies, factories, and schools. Technical knowledge from Europe and America, selected and translated by Chinese and foreign experts, entered the empire's intellectual life. Much also continued. The examination system remained the main path to office; Confucian moral language framed almost every argument; the court retained ultimate authority; and most Qing subjects, who were farmers, were affected only indirectly. Reform was layered upon existing institutions rather than replacing them.

Many kinds of people were affected. Regional officials and their private staffs gained influence. Artisans and laborers at arsenals and shipyards learned new routines and new skills. Merchants and compradors invested in steam shipping and mining. A few hundred students entered new schools, and about a hundred boys went to Connecticut. Examination scholars were divided: some resisted, some adapted, and some quietly read translated works. Foreign advisers, from Robert Hart of the Customs to engineers and missionaries, held positions that gave them real influence but also made them dependent on Qing officials who employed them. Farmers in the provinces, meanwhile, paid taxes that helped fund the new enterprises and watched the telegraph poles and railway tracks arrive, sometimes with suspicion about their effect on local spirits, graves, and livelihoods.

The evidence is rich but must be read carefully. Memorials by Zeng Guofan, Li Hongzhang, Zuo Zongtang, and others survive in large collections. The records of the Zongli Yamen, the Imperial Maritime Customs' trade reports, translated treatises, school registers, diaries of Chinese diplomats, accounts by foreign advisers, and archaeological and surviving physical remains of factories and ships all help. Each has built-in biases: memorials were written to persuade the court and justify spending, while foreign accounts often exaggerated the role of foreign technicians. The Historical Evidence section that follows the vocabulary preview discusses these sources in more detail.

Finally, this chapter places Self-Strengthening in wider Asian and global history. The Qing were not alone in trying to acquire industrial and military technology while preserving a political order: the Ottoman Empire, Egypt, Russia, and Japan pursued their own versions at the same time, with varied results. Comparing them does not mean assuming that any outcome was inevitable. The chapter's nineteen sections follow the movement from postwar reconstruction through institutions, enterprises, and diplomatic tests to a final assessment, and Chapter 55 will show what happened when the resulting system faced its first great trial against Japan.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Describe the conditions of postwar reconstruction in the provinces most damaged by the mid-century rebellions.
  2. Explain how wartime commanders such as Zeng Guofan, Li Hongzhang, and Zuo Zongtang became regional administrators with control of armies, revenue, and industrial projects.
  3. Define ziqiang and distinguish it from later slogans such as zhongti xiyong, avoiding anachronistic use of either.
  4. Analyze the establishment and functions of the Zongli Yamen and the place of the Imperial Maritime Customs in Qing finance and diplomacy.
  5. Compare the structure, purposes, and results of the Anqing and Jiangnan arsenals, the Fuzhou Navy Yard, and merchant-managed enterprises.
  6. Evaluate the roles of Chinese artisans, translators, students, merchants, and officials, alongside foreign advisers, in technology transfer.
  7. Assess the aims, achievements, and recall of the Chinese Educational Mission and the controversy surrounding the Tongwen Guan.
  8. Interpret the Taiwan expedition of 1874, the Ili crisis, and the Sino-French War as tests of Qing diplomatic and military capacity.
  9. Distinguish technological, institutional, fiscal, military, and political measures of success when evaluating Self-Strengthening.
  10. Contextualize Qing reform alongside parallel efforts in Japan, the Ottoman Empire, and elsewhere, avoiding teleological explanations.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. How did the Qing recover from the mid-century rebellions, and who bore the costs of recovery?
  2. Why did the main initiatives of Self-Strengthening come from provincial officials rather than from the central government?
  3. What did Qing officials believe foreign technology could and could not accomplish?
  4. How did new revenue sources, such as customs duties and lijin, make reform possible and also limit it?
  5. Who actually did the technical work in arsenals, shipyards, schools, and enterprises, and how did they learn it?
  6. Why did the Qing create a separate institution for foreign affairs instead of folding it into existing ministries?
  7. How should historians judge Self-Strengthening: by the standards of its own goals, by the standards of Japan's later success, or by some other measure?
  8. What did the Sino-French War reveal about the strengths and weaknesses of regionally organized modernization?

Historical Context

By 1864, when Qing and provincial forces took Nanjing and ended the Taiping capital, the empire had endured a decade and a half of overlapping crises. The First Opium War (1839–1842) and the Second (1856–1860) had produced the treaty-port system and, in 1860, the Convention of Beijing and the destruction of the Summer Palace. The Taiping Heavenly Kingdom had devastated the Lower Yangtze, the Nian had raided across the North China Plain until 1868, and Muslim-led revolts in Yunnan and the northwest, together with unrest among the Miao in Guizhou, continued into the early 1870s. The Yellow River had also shifted its course in 1855, breaching its northern dike and flowing to the sea through Shandong, damaging the Grand Canal and disrupting the system that carried tribute grain to the capital. Chapters 52 and 53 described these events; this chapter begins in their aftermath.

The Qing empire of this period was far larger than the agrarian core usually called "China proper." It included Manchuria, Mongolia, Tibet, and the vast territories of Xinjiang, as well as Taiwan, which until 1885 was administratively part of Fujian Province. Its tributary or protective relations extended to Korea, the Ryukyu Islands, Vietnam, and others, and the nature of these relationships was becoming contested as Japan, France, and Britain defined their own claims. Historians must therefore resist equating the Qing state with modern China. Many decisions discussed here, such as the campaigns in Xinjiang and the negotiations over Ili, concerned territories whose relationship to the Beijing court had changed over the preceding century.

Qing government rested on a small central bureaucracy, a provincial structure of governors and governors-general, and a vast reliance on local gentry, lineages, and village institutions. Formal revenue came mainly from the land tax, whose quotas had been largely fixed since the eighteenth century, supplemented by grain tribute, salt revenue, and customs. The system worked reasonably well in peace but lacked flexibility. The rebellions forced emergency measures: provincial officials raised lijin, solicited gentry contributions, sold ranks and titles, and borrowed. Armies recruited through personal ties, like Zeng's Xiang Army from Hunan and Li's Huai Army from Anhui, became the dependable instruments of the court because the hereditary Banner forces and the Green Standard Army had proved inadequate. The court, in turn, depended on those same commanders, creating a delicate balance that Chapter 53 examined and that shapes this chapter.

Intellectually, the 1860s were a moment of controlled opening. The statecraft (jingshi) tradition in Confucian thought, which emphasized practical administration, had already produced scholars such as Wei Yuan, whose Illustrated Treatise on the Maritime Nations (1840s) urged learning the foreigners' strong techniques in order to restrain them. This tradition, not a sudden conversion to Western ideas, supplied the vocabulary of Self-Strengthening. At the same time, a powerful conservative current opposed anything that seemed to compromise Confucian principles. The tension between these currents appears in nearly every section that follows.

Finally, the global setting mattered. The 1860s and 1870s were years of rapid industrial and imperial expansion: steamships, telegraphs, breech-loading rifles, and ironclad warships were transforming warfare and commerce worldwide, and the opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 shortened the voyage between Europe and East Asia. Other states, among them Japan after 1868, the Ottoman Empire, and Egypt, were experimenting with similar institutional responses. The Qing were thus not isolated, but they faced distinctive challenges: an enormous territory, a decentralized administration, a fiscal system built for a slower age, and a treaty regime that limited their control over tariffs and trade.

Historical Evidence

Historical Evidence

Sources for Studying Self-Strengthening

Memorials and official correspondence from Zeng, Li, Zuo, Shen, and others survive in collected writings and archives, and reveal justification, cost, and policy debate, though they were written to persuade. Zongli Yamen archives document diplomacy and the management of treaty affairs. Customs reports and statistics offer quantitative evidence on trade and revenue, produced by an agency with its own interests. Translated treatises, textbooks, and periodicals show what knowledge was selected and how it was rendered. School records and student letters illuminate the Tongwen Guan and the Educational Mission. Diaries and travel accounts by Chinese diplomats such as Guo Songtao and Zhang Deyi provide direct impressions of foreign societies. Foreign accounts by advisers, missionaries, and journalists are rich in detail but often biased toward foreign roles. Physical remains, including surviving arsenal buildings, machinery, and ships' records, supplement documents.

All of these sources come mostly from officials, merchants, and foreigners. Workers, women, indigenous communities, and farmers appear mainly through indirect evidence, and historians must read against the grain to recover their experiences.

Section 54.1

Reconstruction After the Mid-Century Rebellions

Reconstruction began before the fighting ended and was uneven across regions. In the Lower Yangtze provinces of Jiangsu, Zhejiang, and Anhui, where the Taiping and the campaigns against them had been most destructive, cities such as Nanjing, Suzhou, and Hangzhou lost large parts of their populations and buildings. Estimates of mortality vary widely, and demographic historians caution that the figures are uncertain; in the most devastated prefectures the losses may have exceeded half of the population, through killing, famine, epidemic disease, and flight. Meanwhile, Shanghai, protected by the treaty-port settlements, swelled with wealthy refugees and capital, which helps explain its rise as the commercial and industrial center of the next decades. The northwest and the southwest, scarred by the Muslim-led revolts, had their own long and violent recoveries, as Section 54.16 notes.

Provincial officials relied on a mix of administrative tools. They cut the heavy land-tax quotas of ravaged areas, most famously reducing the burdensome assessments in the Suzhou–Songjiang region in the 1860s, which relieved cultivators while also recognizing the reality that the old rates could not be collected. They recruited settlers from other provinces, especially Hunan, Hubei, and Henan, to farm abandoned lands, and they supported reclamation of fields. Reconstruction bureaus (shanhou ju), generally led by officials but staffed by local gentry, organized burial of the dead, relief for widows and orphans, restoration of dikes and irrigation, and rebuilding of granaries. Charitable halls and orphanages, often run by gentry, expanded. Women's experience of the aftermath was distinctive: widowhood was widespread, abduction and sale during the wars left many families fractured, and women's household textile and farm work was often the first resource families rebuilt.

Cultural restoration was part of the program. Officials rebuilt Confucian temples and academies, reopened schools, and sponsored provincial book bureaus, such as the Jinling Book Bureau founded in Nanjing in 1864, to reprint the classics and histories destroyed by the war. Examination quotas were adjusted, and in several provinces extra places were granted as rewards for gentry who had financed or led militia. Lineages reconstructed ancestral halls and compiled new genealogies, a process that also allowed families to assert claims to land and status. These measures reveal that Qing leaders saw reconstruction as moral and cultural as well as material: the restoration of order meant the restoration of the Confucian social order they believed the rebellions had challenged.

Reconstruction was never complete. The land tax base in some areas remained below its pre-war level, local power shifted toward gentry who had organized militias, and the very instruments used to win the war, such as lijin and regional armies, became permanent. Natural disasters compounded the damage. The Yellow River's northward shift after 1855 disrupted the Shandong and northern Jiangsu countryside, and the "Incredible Famine" of 1876–1879 in the north (Shanxi, Henan, Zhili, Shandong, and Shaanxi) killed millions. Historians use gazetteers, reconstruction memorials, genealogies, and relief records to study these recoveries, but all are skewed toward elite concerns. The ordinary cultivator appears mostly as a taxpayer, a refugee, or a recipient of charity.

Section 54.2

The Tongzhi Restoration and the Postwar Court

The Xianfeng Emperor died at Rehe in August 1861, having fled Beijing before the Anglo-French advance. A power struggle followed. Prince Gong, who had negotiated with the foreigners, joined the emperor's widows, Empress Dowager Ci'an and Empress Dowager Cixi (the mother of the child heir), against the regents named in Xianfeng's will. The coup of late 1861 removed the regents, and the two empress dowagers, assisted by Prince Gong, began a joint regency for the young Tongzhi Emperor. The reign title, often understood as "joint rule" or "common order," reflected this arrangement. The Tongzhi reign (1861–1875) is sometimes called the "Tongzhi Restoration," a phrase popularized by the historian Mary Wright, who stressed the revival of Confucian administration, the recruitment of able Han officials, and the reassertion of dynastic authority.

Restoration meant first of all placing trust in men who had proved themselves in the civil wars. Zeng Guofan, Hu Linyi, Zuo Zongtang, and Li Hongzhang held high provincial posts. Within Beijing, Prince Gong, the Manchu Grand Councilor Wenxiang, and others supported practical policies, from tax relief to arms manufacture, and sought to demonstrate to the foreign powers that the dynasty could keep its treaty obligations. They also supervised the revival of the examinations and schools, hoping to rebuild the loyalty of the literati. The dynasty's strategy was to strengthen rather than replace the existing system, and to present innovation as the means of preserving it.

The court was never unified. Conservative officials such as Grand Secretary Woren objected to the study of foreign subjects at the Tongwen Guan (Section 54.12). In the 1870s and 1880s, a group of vocal censors and scholars called the qingliu ("pure stream") demanded forceful resistance to foreign encroachment and criticized officials accused of compromise or waste; the group included figures such as Zhang Peilun and, at an early stage of his career, Zhang Zhidong. Foreign relations also generated friction over protocol: the first formal audience for foreign ministers with the Tongzhi Emperor did not occur until 1873, after protracted disputes over whether envoys would kowtow. The Tianjin Massacre of 1870, in which a mob killed French and other foreign residents, including missionaries and nuns, and the resulting indemnity and apology mission to France, displayed the pressures of an uneasy diplomatic settlement.

The Tongzhi Emperor died in January 1875 without an heir, and Cixi arranged for her nephew, the young Guangxu, to succeed. Ci'an died in 1881. Prince Gong, who had led the Zongli Yamen for over two decades, was dismissed from the Grand Council in 1884 amid criticism of the French crisis, and his place went to rivals, among them Prince Chun, the Guangxu Emperor's father, who later headed the Navy Yamen. Historians continue to debate how much the "Restoration" actually restored. It stabilized the dynasty and encouraged practical reform, but critics note that it also left the dynasty dependent on regionally based commanders whose power the court could not easily reduce.

Section 54.3

Zeng Guofan, Li Hongzhang, Zuo Zongtang, and Regional Power

The three most influential figures of the era came from different backgrounds. Zeng Guofan, a Hunan jinshi and Hanlin scholar, organized the Xiang Army in the 1850s as a Confucian moral and military enterprise, selecting commanders through local ties and teacher-student relationships. Li Hongzhang, a jinshi of 1847 and Zeng's protégé, raised the Huai Army from Anhui militia in 1862 and, aided by British transport, brought it to Shanghai, where it learned from foreign weapons and drill. Zuo Zongtang, a Hunan juren who never earned the highest degree, was a self-taught student of geography and statecraft who campaigned in Zhejiang before taking command in the northwest. Each combined scholarly credentials with military experience, and each drew on a private staff, the mufu, of secretaries, advisers, and technical experts whose salaries and careers depended on the patron.

After the war, these men held posts as governors and governors-general, offices that had previously been constrained by the court's rules of avoidance and by rotation. Now, because the armies they had raised remained loyal primarily to them, and because they controlled lijin and a share of customs and provincial revenue, they could undertake projects without waiting for Beijing. Zeng served as Governor-General of Liangjiang (Jiangsu, Anhui, and Jiangxi) and died in 1872. Li became Governor-General of Zhili in 1870 and, concurrently as Superintendent of Trade for the Northern Ports, remained at that post until 1895, giving him unmatched influence over foreign affairs, military supply, and industrial projects in North China. Zuo served in Fujian and Zhejiang, then in Shaanxi and Gansu, led the reconquest of Xinjiang, and later held posts in Beijing, Nanjing, and Fujian.

It would be misleading to say that the court lost control. Cixi and Prince Gong consciously balanced one official against another, appointed rivals to adjacent posts, accepted Zeng's disbanding of much of the Xiang Army in 1864, and kept a close watch on the loyalty of regional leaders. The court appointed the governors and could dismiss them, and the officials themselves rarely, if ever, sought to break away. Their power was institutional and personal, but it operated within the dynasty's legitimacy. The relationship was symbiotic: the court needed their competence and revenue, and they needed the court's authority to justify projects and to protect them from critics. This nuanced picture, already introduced in Chapter 53, should be kept in mind, as it prevents us from reading later republican warlordism back into the 1860s.

Regionalization shaped Self-Strengthening in practical ways. Because each patron's projects reflected local priorities, a Hunan-based network around Zeng and Zuo emphasized shipyards and the northwest, while Li's Anhui network emphasized the north's defense, steam navigation, and communications. Projects competed for funds and talent, duplicated one another, and sometimes failed to share lessons. Yet regional initiative allowed experimentation that a cautious court might not have approved, and it distributed knowledge across several centers. Whether this was more a strength or a weakness remains central to the debate in Section 54.18.

Section 54.4

What "Self-Strengthening" Meant

The term ziqiang, "self-strengthening," comes from the Book of Changes (Yijing), where the image of Heaven's unceasing movement is paired with the idea that the exemplary person strengthens himself without rest. Officials and scholars in the 1860s used the word to claim that acquiring Western weapons and techniques was an act of Confucian moral and political self-cultivation, not capitulation. Later, as the focus of projects expanded to include mining, shipping, and textiles, officials also spoke of seeking wealth (qiufu), the idea that a strong state needs a productive economy. Many modern Chinese historians prefer the term yangwu, "foreign affairs" or "foreign matters," which the officials themselves used to describe all business involving foreigners and their techniques.

One of the most influential statements came from Feng Guifen, a Jiangsu scholar who had taken refuge in Shanghai during the Taiping war and written essays in 1861 collected as Protests from the Jiaobin Studio. Feng argued that China could learn from foreign states in shipbuilding, weapons, and mathematics, and that Chinese ethical principles and institutions should remain the foundation. He drew on Wei Yuan's earlier call to learn the foreigners' superior techniques in order to control them. Feng's essays circulated among officials and gained renewed attention in later decades, but they did not constitute a plan. They reflect a widening belief that the problem was one of technique and organization, not necessarily of values.

Historical Vocabulary
Ziqiang (自强) and Zhongti xiyong (中体西用)

Ziqiang, "self-strengthening," was the 1860s slogan for adopting foreign military technology to restore Qing power. It carried no commitment to wholesale reform. Zhongti xiyong, often translated "Chinese learning as the essential structure, Western learning for practical use," is a later formulation, associated especially with Zhang Zhidong's Exhortation to Learning (1898). The underlying distinction between a moral core and practical techniques appears in the writings of Feng Guifen and others before that, but historians should not describe zhongti xiyong as the founding slogan of the 1860s. Using it that way flattens a diverse set of positions into a single doctrine.

Zeng, Li, and Zuo did not seek wholesale Westernization. They wished to acquire techniques while defending the political order and Confucian ethics, but their definitions of "technique" kept widening: from guns to ships to mines to telegraphs to translation to diplomacy.

What counted as "technique" was contested. Zeng Guofan emphasized that machinery could be learned and that Chinese craftsmen were capable of making it. Li Hongzhang, more cosmopolitan in outlook, quickly concluded that weapons depended on supporting industries and revenue, and that the "unprecedented changes" of the age required broader institutional adaptation (see the Primary Source Window in Section 54.10). Zuo Zongtang, though a nationalist critic of foreign influence in some respects, built a modern shipyard and used foreign loans and weapons in the northwest. Conservatives such as Woren argued that moral character, not technique, was the foundation of strength, and that employing foreign teachers would corrupt the literati. Between them lay a range of positions, and the same official could shift as events changed.

Section 54.5

The Zongli Yamen and a New Diplomatic Order

The Treaty of Tianjin (1858) and the Convention of Beijing (1860) provided for resident foreign ministers in Beijing, and the Qing needed a body to deal with them. In January 1861, on Prince Gong's proposal, the court created the Zongli Geguo Shiwu Yamen, the Office for the General Management of Affairs Concerning the Various Countries, usually abbreviated Zongli Yamen. It was staffed by senior figures, including grand councilors and grand secretaries, rather than by a separate ministry. It was intended as a temporary expedient, but it lasted until 1901, when the Boxer Protocol required its replacement by a Ministry of Foreign Affairs (Chapter 56).

The Yamen handled treaty negotiations, foreign legation relations, customs matters, translation, and the supervision of the Tongwen Guan. Its officials gradually developed expertise in treaty law. In 1864 the American missionary W.A.P. Martin, working with Chinese assistants, translated Henry Wheaton's Elements of International Law, and the Yamen supported its publication, hoping to use the foreigners' legal rules to defend Qing interests. The Qing also used foreign advisers and envoys: the American diplomat Anson Burlingame, who had been the United States minister in Beijing, led a Qing mission to the United States and Europe in 1868–1870, and in 1877 Guo Songtao became the first Chinese minister to Britain, followed by envoys to other capitals. Their reports and diaries, such as those of Guo Songtao and Zhang Deyi, gave officials new information, though they sometimes provoked controversy at home.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Qing Foreign-Affairs Institutions, 1840–1885

What Changed

  • A permanent office, the Zongli Yamen, managed relations with treaty powers; foreign ministers resided in Beijing.
  • Qing envoys were posted abroad beginning in the late 1870s.
  • Officials studied international law and foreign languages, and relied on translated treaties and legal texts.

What Stayed the Same

  • The Lifan Yuan continued to manage Mongolia, Tibet, and other Inner Asian relations, and the Board of Rites handled Korea and other tributary matters, so the Qing operated parallel systems.
  • Court ritual and hierarchy shaped how envoys were received, though increasingly with compromise.
  • The Yamen remained staffed by officials who held other posts, rather than by a specialized career service.

Who experienced change most directly: treaty-port officials, translators, and the first diplomats. Who experienced continuity: most provincial gentry and subjects, for whom foreign affairs remained distant. Why it matters: the Qing did not abruptly replace a "tribute system" with a Western diplomatic system; it layered new institutions on top of old ones, and that layering shaped its responses to crises over Korea, Vietnam, and the Ryukyus.

Section 54.6

Customs, Revenue, and the Financial Foundations of Reform

The Imperial Maritime Customs Service originated in Shanghai in 1854, when the local authorities lost control of the port during the Small Sword uprising and agreed to a system of foreign inspectors collecting duties on foreign trade. After the Treaty of Tianjin, the arrangement was extended to all treaty ports. Horatio Nelson Lay served as the first Inspector-General, but a dispute over a flotilla he had purchased in Britain for the Qing (the Lay-Osborn Flotilla, 1863) led to his dismissal. Robert Hart took over in 1863 and held the position until the early twentieth century. The Customs was a Qing institution, staffed at its higher levels by British, French, German, American, and other foreign employees and at lower levels by many Chinese, and it operated under treaty constraints on tariff rates and Qing sovereignty. Calling it a "foreign" agency oversimplifies; calling it simply Qing ignores that its leadership and its protection by the treaty regime made it unusual.

Customs revenue grew with trade, and by the late nineteenth century it was among the central government's most reliable sources of revenue. The service also provided services beyond collection: lighthouses, harbor management, trade statistics, and, from the late 1870s, the beginnings of a modern postal service, which became the Imperial Post in 1896. Hart's friendship with Prince Gong and the Zongli Yamen made him an informal adviser on diplomacy and technical affairs. Customs funds also paid for the early Tongwen Guan and contributed to naval and other projects. At the same time, revenue from the Customs was increasingly pledged as security for foreign loans, giving foreign creditors a stake in its operation.

Historical Vocabulary
Lijin (厘金)

Lijin was a transit and commercial tax, first levied in the 1850s as an emergency means of paying for the war against the Taiping. Collected at barriers on trade routes and at points of sale, it was administered by provincial authorities with little central supervision, and it became a permanent source of provincial revenue. Merchants complained of its multiple collection points, and its administration was open to abuse, but it funded armies and many Self-Strengthening projects. Historians see lijin as an example of how wartime necessity turned fiscal power toward the provinces.

Self-Strengthening depended on finance, and the fiscal structure shaped its limits. The land tax, the old backbone, remained essentially fixed and could not be raised without risk of unrest. Customs and lijin provided new money, but provincial authorities controlled lijin, and the Customs revenue was committed to indemnities and loans. Officials supplemented these with the sale of official titles, gentry contributions, salt revenues, and "contributions" from merchants. Foreign loans, first arranged by regional officials in the 1860s and in larger amounts from the mid-1870s, financed Zuo Zongtang's northwest campaigns and later the defense of coastal areas. Modernization therefore meant creating new streams of income outside the traditional structure, and this fragmentation, with each project dependent on its patron's revenue, helps explain the uneven results discussed later.

Section 54.7

Rebuilding the Military

Qing military power in the early 1860s rested on three layers: the hereditary Manchu, Mongol, and Chinese Banners, the Green Standard garrison army, and the newer "braves" (yongying) recruited by regional commanders. The first two had declined in training and equipment. After Nanjing fell, Zeng Guofan, anxious about both the costs and the political implications of a large personal army, disbanded much of the Xiang Army, though some units remained in service. Li Hongzhang's Huai Army, strengthened by the experience of fighting at Shanghai alongside the foreign-led "Ever Victorious Army" and by purchases of Western rifles and artillery, was retained and used to defeat the Nian in 1868. When Li became Governor-General of Zhili in 1870, the Huai Army became the primary force defending the capital region.

Reorganization combined foreign weapons, foreign instructors, and domestic institutions. Li and others purchased breech-loading rifles, Armstrong and Krupp artillery, and ammunition from European firms, and some units adopted European-style drill under British and, later, German instructors. In the 1880s Li founded military and naval academies at Tianjin to train officers. But there was no national army with a uniform command. Each regional commander maintained forces under his own control and financed through provincial revenue, and differences in weapons and training made coordination difficult. The Banner and Green Standard forces continued to exist, often as expensive anachronisms. The central government attempted some standardization, but the rivalry among regional leaders and the court's concern about concentrating too much power in any one hands limited it.

The result was a more capable military than the one that had faced the Taiping in the 1850s, but one organized around regional networks. It could suppress internal rebellion and, in the northwest, reconquer a vast frontier, but its naval and coastal forces were divided among several commands, as the Sino-French War would show (Section 54.17). The armies' loyalties were also personal, tied to commanders whose careers and patronage networks they sustained, a pattern with long-term consequences for twentieth-century politics (Chapters 58 and 59).

Section 54.8

Anqing and the First Modern Arsenals

After the capture of Anqing in September 1861, Zeng Guofan established a military equipment institute there, staffed by craftsmen and mathematically minded scholars including Hua Hengfang, Xu Shou, and Xu Shou's son Xu Jianyin. They studied imported machines and models, experimented with casting and boring, and built a small steam engine in 1862. Their work culminated in the Chinese-built steamboat Huanghu (黄鹄), completed and successfully tested at Nanjing in 1865–1866 (sources differ on the exact year). The efforts were small in scale, but they proved that Chinese technicians could reproduce steam technology with limited foreign help, and they created a nucleus of expertise for later institutions.

In 1863 Zeng sent Yung Wing, the first Chinese graduate of a US university, to the United States to buy machine tools, and this machinery later equipped the Jiangnan Arsenal. Li Hongzhang started his own Jinling Machinery Bureau at Nanjing in 1865, after his Huai Army's experience of artillery and munitions at Suzhou. In North China, Chonghou, the Superintendent of Trade for the Three Northern Ports, established the Tianjin Machine Bureau in 1867, which Li expanded after taking over the Zhili governor-generalship. These early establishments mixed old and new: they used traditional casting and forging alongside imported lathes and steam power, and they relied on Chinese artisans, many with experience in handicraft metalworking and the mint, and foreign engineers hired as supervisors and trainers.

Cause and Effect

Why Officials Moved From Buying Weapons to Making Them

Causes

Short-term: battlefield experience against the Taiping, where foreign-led and foreign-armed units and artillery proved effective; the 1860 defeat; the Lay-Osborn Flotilla fiasco. Long-term: the statecraft tradition's emphasis on practical solutions; the cost and uncertainty of imports; recognition that dependence on foreign suppliers was a strategic weakness; a pool of skilled Chinese artisans.

The Transformation

Regional officials established machine bureaus and arsenals at Anqing, Nanjing, Shanghai, Tianjin, and Fuzhou, financed by lijin and customs revenue, staffed by Chinese craftsmen, scholars, and some foreign experts.

Effects

Immediate: domestic production of rifles, cannon, ammunition, and small steamships; training of technicians. Long-term: the first industrial workforce, translation programs, a model for later enterprises, and a pattern of regionally managed industrial projects.

Historical Complexity

Arsenals did not solve the problem of supply: they depended on imported machinery, raw materials, and components, and they competed for funds with other priorities. They also reflect that the motive was not simply "modernization" but the defense of a dynasty and its officials.
Section 54.9

The Jiangnan Arsenal and the Expansion of Military Industry

The Jiangnan Arsenal (Jiangnan Zhizaoju) was founded in Shanghai in 1865, when Li Hongzhang, with Zeng Guofan's support, purchased an American-owned machine shop in Hongkou and combined it with machinery from earlier foreign-gun workshops. The arsenal was moved in 1867 to a larger site south of the city, where it eventually became the largest industrial establishment in the empire. It produced rifles, cannon, ammunition, and later gunboats and other steam vessels. Its first steamship, launched in 1868, was an early milestone. The arsenal employed a growing workforce, from roughly 1,300 Chinese workers (and a handful of foreigners) by 1869 to about 2,000 by the mid-1870s, and to roughly 3,000 only after 1890 (figures from scholarly estimates, which vary), with Chinese engineers, foremen, and workers working alongside a smaller number of foreign technical specialists.

A translation department, opened in 1868, became one of its most important contributions. John Fryer, a British missionary-educator, together with Chinese collaborators such as Xu Shou, Xu Jianyin, and Hua Hengfang, produced well over a hundred translated works (the exact count depends on what is counted as a separate work or volume) on mathematics, chemistry, physics, metallurgy, mining, navigation, and military science. Xu Shou helped devise Chinese terms for many chemical elements, some still in use. The arsenal also supported technical training and, in 1874, Fryer helped found the Shanghai Polytechnic Institution and, in 1876, the Chinese Scientific Magazine (Gezhi huibian). Section 54.11 returns to translation. The Jiangnan site thus combined manufacturing, instruction, and publication, though always under the arsenal's priority of military supply.

Everyday Life and Social History

Working in an Arsenal

For the first time in the empire, large numbers of men worked in an enterprise organized around steam power, fixed hours, and divisions of labor. Many early workers were artisans from the Lower Yangtze, Ningbo, or Guangdong, recruited for skills in metalwork, carpentry, or casting, and they worked alongside apprentices who learned by watching and imitating. Hierarchies mixed Chinese and foreign supervisors, with a layer of Chinese contractors and foremen mediating between management and laborers. Wages were paid in cash, and the work brought risks such as explosions and machine injuries. Workers might join the same native-place associations and guilds that organized other trades, and these ties influenced hiring and mutual aid.

Evidence for these experiences is thin. Arsenal records emphasize costs, output, and technical matters rather than workers' voices, and the surviving accounts are mostly by officials and foreign advisers. Historians must infer from budgets, staffing lists, and later recollections. What is clear is that the arsenal created a new kind of urban workforce in Shanghai, a precursor to the industrial laborers who would become politically important in the twentieth century (Chapters 60 and 61).

The expansion of military industry also exposed constraints. The Jiangnan Arsenal remained dependent on imported machinery, steel, and some components, and its production often cost more than imported equivalents. Different provinces had different types of rifles and calibers, complicating supply. Officials such as Li defended the investment, arguing that domestic production was a necessary step toward self-reliance, while critics at court questioned the expense. Historians have studied the arsenal's accounts to measure its efficiency, and the verdicts vary: some emphasize cost overruns and dependency, others the long-term value of technical training and institutional experience.

Section 54.10

The Fuzhou Navy Yard and Naval Modernization

In 1866, as Governor-General of Fujian and Zhejiang, Zuo Zongtang proposed a shipyard at Mawei, on the Min River below Fuzhou, to build steam warships and train Chinese in their construction and operation. The court approved, and before Zuo was transferred to the northwest later that year, he arranged for Shen Baozhen, a respected Fujian official, to take charge. The French naval officers Prosper Giquel and Paul d'Aiguebelle were hired under contract to organize the yard and its schools. The Fuzhou Navy Yard (Fuzhou Chuanzheng Ju), which included a naval school with a French-language section for shipbuilding and an English-language section for navigation, launched its first steamship in 1869 and went on to build dozens of vessels over the next fifteen years.

The yard's naval school trained a notable generation. Graduates included Yan Fu, later a famous translator of Western social thought, and officers who later served in the Beiyang and Fujian fleets. In 1877, Giquel and the Chinese officials Li Hongzhang and Shen Baozhen arranged to send a first group of Fuzhou graduates to Britain and France for advanced naval study. Fujian's maritime setting, with experienced sailors, fishermen, and a coastal commercial tradition, helped provide labor and expertise. Yet the yard struggled with funding, as it required steady revenue from Fujian's customs and provincial accounts, and with the cost of imported materials and foreign advisers. Maintaining a technical staff, replacing contracts, and keeping ships in repair proved harder than launching them.

Primary Source Window

Li Hongzhang's Memorials on Shipbuilding and Foreign Affairs, early 1870s

Li replied with memorials to the throne defending continued investment. Source type: memorial (zouzhe), an official communication from a high official to the emperor. Translations vary, and historians quote it from collected editions of Li's writings.

Brief excerpt (a common English rendering of a famous phrase): China faces "great changes unseen in three thousand years." In paraphrase, Li argued that the foreign powers' steam power, steel, and machines had altered the conditions of warfare and trade, and that China must learn to produce its own ships and weapons rather than remain dependent on others.

Analysis: The memorial reveals how reformers framed their case to a skeptical audience: not as imitation of foreign ways, but as a response to an unprecedented historical situation. It also shows Li's awareness of costs and his need to justify them. The memorial represents the perspective of a regional official with a stake in the projects he defended. It tells us less about what workers, merchants, or local gentry thought, and it should not be treated as a neutral assessment of results. The critics' arguments survive in other memorials and should be read alongside it.

Section 54.11

Translation, Science, and Technical Knowledge

Knowledge moved into the Qing empire through translation, and the translators were a mixed group. The Jiangnan Arsenal's translation department, the Tongwen Guan, and Protestant missionary presses produced translated texts. Chinese scholars such as Xu Shou, Xu Jianyin, Hua Hengfang, and the mathematician Li Shanlan worked with foreign collaborators including John Fryer, Alexander Wylie, and W.A.P. Martin. The usual method was oral: the foreigner read and explained the text, and the Chinese partner wrote it in literary Chinese. This process shaped what was translated and how, as the Chinese scholars often adapted concepts to existing categories, supplied terminology, and corrected mistakes.

Selection reflected priorities. Translators concentrated on mathematics, physics, chemistry, geology, mining, navigation, shipbuilding, military science, and international law. Fewer works dealt with political theory, history, or social science, although these became more important in the 1890s. This selectivity matters: it shows that officials treated Western knowledge as practical expertise, but it also means that readers of the earlier translations encountered an incomplete picture of the societies that produced it. The works circulated to arsenals, schools, officials, and a limited reading public. Some, such as the Chinese Scientific Magazine, found readers beyond official circles, while others stayed on library shelves.

Imported knowledge was adapted, not simply copied. Chinese technical traditions in mathematics, astronomy, medicine, and metallurgy remained, and scholars sometimes sought to reconcile new and old. The invention of new terms, such as for chemical elements and mechanical parts, created a technical vocabulary that later generations inherited and, in many cases, replaced with terms borrowed from Japanese in the 1890s and after. Translation also brought changes to status: men like Xu Shou held no high examination degrees but became influential, suggesting that technical competence could yield recognition outside the traditional ladder.

Section 54.12

The Tongwen Guan and New Forms of Education

The Tongwen Guan, the School of Combined Learning, opened in Beijing in 1862 under the Zongli Yamen, with Robert Hart's practical support, to train interpreters. It began with English, adding French and Russian in 1863 and German later, and recruited young Banner students. In 1866 Prince Gong proposed adding astronomy and mathematics and recruiting older students, including holders of examination degrees. This provoked sharp opposition. Woren, a Mongol Grand Secretary and a leading defender of Neo-Confucian orthodoxy, argued that it was the cultivation of moral character, not technical knowledge, that would strengthen the state, and that scholars should not study under foreigners. Prince Gong replied that the dynasty's survival required practical skills. The court upheld the school but did not compel degree-holders to enroll, and recruitment of established scholars was disappointing.

Despite the controversy, the school survived, with W.A.P. Martin as its head from 1869 and Chinese teachers such as Li Shanlan in mathematics. It produced a modest number of interpreters, translators, and diplomats. Zhang Deyi, one of its early students, accompanied the 1866 mission to Europe and later wrote about his travels and served as a diplomat. Similar schools were founded in Shanghai (the Guang Fangyan Guan, 1863) and Guangzhou (1864), and the Tongwen Guan was eventually absorbed into the Imperial University of Peking in 1902. Its direct output was small, but it established the principle that the state could sponsor instruction in foreign languages and sciences.

Section 54.13

Overseas Students and the Chinese Educational Mission

Yung Wing, born near Macao, educated at a missionary school and Yale (class of 1854), proposed that China send boys to study in the United States. With support from Zeng Guofan and Li Hongzhang, who recognized that technical education abroad could produce engineers and officers, the Qing approved the plan in 1871. The first group of thirty boys, aged roughly ten to sixteen, left for Connecticut in 1872, and four contingents eventually brought about 120 students. They lived with host families in New England, attended local schools, and were supervised by Chinese commissioners, with Yung Wing as one of the directors and Chen Lanbin as senior commissioner. The program was intended to run fifteen years, after which the students would return to serve the government.

Problems developed. Some Chinese supervisors, especially the conservative Wu Zideng, complained that students were adopting American manners, abandoning Confucian ritual, cutting their queues, converting to Christianity, or playing sports instead of studying. In 1881 the Qing recalled the mission. Of the 120 students sent over the years, 94 who were still in the program were recalled and returned that year; the others had already left the program, returned earlier, or died. Yung Wing later claimed that U.S. refusal to admit Chinese students to West Point and Annapolis helped undermine the mission. Archival research has questioned whether a formal application was ever made, although Li Hongzhang had previously been told that such admission was unlikely. The students' later careers nonetheless show the value of the experiment: Zhan Tianyou became a leading railway engineer, and Tang Shaoyi became a diplomat and, after the 1911 revolution, a prime minister of the Republic. The episode reveals both the ambitions of Self-Strengthening and the political and cultural resistance to long-term education abroad, and it also shows how Chinese officials and students themselves, not only foreigners, drove the exchange.

Section 54.14

Steamship Companies, Mining, and State-Sponsored Enterprise

Military industry depended on transport, coal, and iron, and officials turned to commercial ventures that could generate revenue. The China Merchants Steam Navigation Company (Lunchuan Zhaoshang Ju) was founded in Shanghai in 1872 under Li Hongzhang's sponsorship. Its initial purpose was to carry tribute grain by sea to the north, since the Grand Canal had been disrupted by the Yellow River's shift and the wars, and to compete with foreign shipping firms that dominated coastal and Yangtze steam navigation. Merchants with experience in foreign firms, including Tang Tingshu, Xu Run, and Zhu Qizhao, provided capital and expertise. In 1877 the company bought the fleet of the American firm Russell and Company, and it competed with British firms such as Butterfield and Swire and Jardine Matheson, sometimes through rate wars and agreements.

Historical Vocabulary
Guandu shangban (官督商办)

Guandu shangban means "government-supervised, merchant-managed." Under this arrangement, an official sponsor, often a governor-general like Li Hongzhang, provided protection, privileges, or initial loans, while merchants raised capital and ran daily operations. Officials appointed or approved managers and often took a share of profits or required contributions. The model is not the same as a modern state-owned enterprise, nor a private company in a modern sense: it combined elements of both and depended on personal ties. Its strengths included access to capital and official protection; its weaknesses included uncertain property rights, patronage, and interference.

The Kaiping Mining Bureau, authorized in the late 1870s with Tang Tingshu as manager, developed coal mines near Tangshan in Zhili, using Western equipment and engineers, and began production in 1881. Coal fueled steamships, arsenals, and railways. To carry coal, the company built a short railway from Tangshan, which extended toward Tianjin in the later 1880s. Other ventures included textile mills, notably the Shanghai Machine Weaving Bureau, and in the northwest, Zuo Zongtang's Lanzhou woolen mill. Iron and steel appeared later, with Zhang Zhidong's Hanyang Ironworks in 1890. Historians have emphasized how varied these ventures were: arsenals and naval yards were government-owned and financed from public revenue, while steamships and mines were merchant-managed with official supervision, and telegraphs followed a third pattern. Their results differ accordingly, and treating them as one category misleads.

These enterprises had real accomplishments. The steamship company won a substantial share of Chinese coastal trade and created a Chinese-managed fleet; the Kaiping mines were among the most productive in China. They also had weaknesses. Official supervisors sometimes imposed political appointees, demanded funds, or diverted resources; merchants worried about losing control; and competition with foreign firms, which had capital and treaty privileges, was intense. Managers like Sheng Xuanhuai, who later headed the telegraph and steamship companies, accumulated both wealth and influence, raising persistent questions about whether such enterprises served the state, the investors, or the managers.

Section 54.15

Telegraphs, Railways, and Communications

Telegraph technology entered China through foreign companies, including the Danish Great Northern Telegraph Company, which laid submarine cables to Shanghai in 1871. The Qing initially refused to allow foreign-built overland lines, but military and commercial needs changed attitudes. A short line was built in Taiwan in the 1870s, and in 1880 Li Hongzhang established the Tianjin Telegraph Administration, building a line between Tianjin and Shanghai completed in 1881. Sheng Xuanhuai directed the project, and Chinese operators were trained at a telegraph school. By the 1880s, lines linked major cities and ports. The telegraph let officials communicate within hours rather than days or weeks, benefiting military coordination, trade, and the press, though the capital's court remained slower to adopt it than provincial leaders.

Railways proved more controversial. In 1876 British merchants built the Woosung Railway, a short line between Shanghai and Wusong, without full official approval. After a fatality and local protests, and amid arguments over fengshui, costs, and sovereignty, the Qing authorities bought the line in 1877 and dismantled it, shipping the materials to Taiwan. Some saw this as proof of reaction, but the episode was more complicated: officials objected to foreign merchants acting without permission, not necessarily to railways as such. Li Hongzhang and others continued to argue for railways, and in 1881 a short Tangshan–Xugezhuang line built for the Kaiping mines began operation, using a locomotive built by the company's engineer, Claude Kinder, and Chinese workers. In 1887–1891 Liu Mingchuan built the first railway in Taiwan. Opposition came from officials concerned about expense, peasants worried about graves and fields, boatmen and cartmen fearing lost livelihoods, and strategic thinkers fearing that railways would help invaders. Support rested on the military and commercial advantages. Large-scale railway building would not come until after 1895 (Chapter 56).

Section 54.16

Diplomatic and Strategic Tests in the 1870s and Early 1880s

In late 1871, shipwrecked sailors from the Ryukyu Islands were killed on southern Taiwan by members of a Paiwan community. Japan, which was extending its claim over the Ryukyus, used the incident to send a military expedition to Taiwan in 1874. Qing officials, caught between asserting sovereignty over Taiwan's indigenous areas, which were not fully administered, and avoiding war, negotiated a settlement in Beijing in October 1874, with British mediation, under which the Qing paid compensation and Japan withdrew. The episode revealed coastal vulnerability and led Shen Baozhen to push for stronger administration in Taiwan, including the opening of mountain areas and the strengthening of defenses. It also initiated a debate: should the empire put limited resources into maritime defense (haifang) or frontier defense (saifang)? Li Hongzhang, concerned with Japan and the coast, favored the first; Zuo Zongtang, who was campaigning in the northwest, argued that losing Xinjiang would endanger Mongolia and the capital. The court supported both, though with limited funds.

Zuo's campaign from 1876 to 1878 destroyed the state of Yakub Beg, a Khoqand-born commander who had established control over the Tarim Basin oases amid the Muslim uprisings, and restored Qing authority over most of Xinjiang. Foreign loans and a long logistics effort supported it. Xinjiang became a province in 1884. In the Ili valley, Russia had occupied the territory in 1871 during the unrest. Negotiations led by the Manchu official Chonghou produced the Treaty of Livadia in 1879, which the court denounced as too concessive, and Chonghou was condemned. Zeng Guofan's son Zeng Jize, then the Qing minister in Britain and France, negotiated the Treaty of St. Petersburg in 1881, in which Russia returned most of Ili in exchange for an indemnity and some territorial and commercial concessions. Qing officials regarded it as a diplomatic success compared with the earlier treaty, though Russia retained gains.

Regional and Frontier Context

Taiwan and Xinjiang as Frontiers of Self-Strengthening

Taiwan. Western Taiwan was settled by Han migrants from Fujian and Guangdong, while Austronesian-speaking indigenous peoples held much of the mountains and east, and the Qing used legal categories that distinguished communities by their degree of incorporation. After 1874 Shen Baozhen promoted a policy of opening the mountains and settling the interior, and Liu Mingchuan, the first governor after Taiwan became a province in 1885, built telegraph lines, a railway, and defenses. Indigenous communities experienced these measures as pressure on land and autonomy, a perspective largely missing from official records.

Xinjiang. The Tarim oases, Dzungaria, and Ili were home to diverse communities, including Turki-speaking oasis Muslims (many later classified as Uyghurs), Taranchis in Ili, Kazakhs, Mongols, Hui, Han, and others, whose relations with the Qing had varied since the eighteenth-century conquest (Chapter 48). Zuo's campaign reasserted Qing control after years of local regimes, and the province that followed brought Han officials and settlers and a new civil administration. The campaign cannot be described simply as "unification" or "foreign conquest": it was a Qing reassertion of an earlier imperial claim in the face of Russian and British interest, and the people who lived there experienced it in diverse ways, including war, displacement, and new taxes.

Section 54.17

The Sino-French War and the Limits of Regional Modernization, 1884–1885

France had been expanding in Vietnam since the 1860s, taking Cochinchina in 1862 and, after the 1870s, pressing northward into Tonkin. Vietnam's Nguyen court had long maintained ritual relations with the Qing, and Chinese forces, including irregulars of the Black Flag Army led by Liu Yongfu, resisted the French. In 1883, French forces fought in Tonkin, and after a brief agreement with Li Hongzhang in May 1884, an incident at Bac Le in June, in which Chinese troops clashed with a French column, led to escalation. Qing leadership was divided between those, like Li, who wanted a negotiated settlement and the hawkish qingliu critics, who urged resistance.

On 23 August 1884, a French squadron under Admiral Courbet, which had been anchored in the Min River near Fuzhou during negotiations, attacked the Fujian fleet and the Mawei dockyard, destroying most of the Chinese ships in less than an hour and damaging the yard. The Qing court ordered active resistance, and hostilities widened into an undeclared war. The blow was severe, in part because the Chinese fleet had been ordered not to fire first and was poorly positioned, and Zhang Peilun, the official responsible for coastal defense, was held accountable. The French also bombarded Keelung, occupied it in October 1884, imposed a blockade of Taiwan, and later seized the Pescadores. Yet the French were repulsed at Tamsui in 1884, and Liu Mingchuan's forces held northern Taiwan. In Tonkin, Chinese armies fought hard, and on 23–24 March 1885 Chinese forces under Feng Zicai defeated the French at Bang Bo (Zhennan Pass). The subsequent French retreat from Lang Son, beginning on 28 March, triggered a political crisis in Paris and helped bring down the government of Jules Ferry.

The Treaty of Tianjin, concluded in June 1885, recognized the French protectorate over Annam and Tonkin, opened the border to trade, and included no indemnity. The Qing thus lost its traditional role in Vietnam, but it avoided a larger concession, and its armies had shown competence on land. The war pushed the court toward centralizing naval policy: the Navy Yamen (Haijun Yamen) was established in 1885 under Prince Chun, with Li Hongzhang as an assistant, and Taiwan became a separate province. It also exposed the regional structure's weakness, since the Fuzhou fleet was a southern force with limited support from northern squadrons. The war was no simple proof that Self-Strengthening had accomplished nothing: its battlefield results were mixed. Diplomatically, however, France achieved its central objective by ending Qing claims in Vietnam and securing recognition of its protectorate, although China paid no indemnity and France evacuated Taiwan and the Pescadores. The war also demonstrated that decentralization could both mobilize local resources and impede coordination.

China and the Wider World

Self-Strengthening in a Global Setting

Qing reform belonged to a wider age of defensive modernization. The Ottoman Tanzimat, Egypt under Muhammad Ali and his successors, Russia after the Crimean War, and Japan after the Meiji Restoration of 1868 each combined military reform, technical education, and institutional change. Japan's early efforts also relied on foreign experts, translations, and overseas students, but its government was more centralized after the 1870s, and its leaders could direct policy through a unified state after abolishing domains in 1871. Comparisons can illuminate differences in political structure, finance, and external pressure, but they should not imply that Japan's later success was predetermined or that Qing leaders chose "wrongly."

The Qing were also active participants in global networks. Chinese merchants, students, and diplomats traveled abroad; Chinese officials used international law and negotiated through treaties; Chinese labor and capital moved through Southeast Asia and the Americas; and Chinese markets and Customs revenue interacted with global trade. Foreign powers, for their part, competed and cooperated, with British, French, German, American, and Russian interests shaping what technology was available and on what terms.

Section 54.18

Achievements, Contradictions, and Limits of Self-Strengthening

The achievements were tangible. The Qing created arsenals that produced modern weapons, shipyards that built steam warships, a merchant fleet, mines that supplied coal, a telegraph network, and the first railways. They trained interpreters, diplomats, engineers, and naval officers; translated well over a hundred scientific and technical works at the Jiangnan Arsenal alone; created permanent diplomatic institutions; and, through the Customs, secured a growing revenue stream. They suppressed internal rebellions and reasserted control over Xinjiang. The changes also created a group of Chinese technical and managerial experts, some of whom, like Zhan Tianyou, Yan Fu, and Tang Shaoyi, played important roles later.

The limits were equally clear. Command and finance remained fragmented, with regional officials funding and directing projects that were not integrated into a national plan. Funds were inadequate and insecure, with the old land tax unable to support industrial investment. Dependence on imported machinery, materials, and expertise persisted. Official supervision of enterprises brought patronage, interference, and, where documented, corruption and waste, though historians caution that accusations of corruption were also political weapons. The examination system continued to privilege classical learning over technical training, so talented students often avoided technical careers. Conservative resistance, though often exaggerated in older accounts, did slow projects like railways and the Tongwen Guan's expansion. And naval and military administration was uncoordinated, as the Sino-French War illustrated.

Historical Debate

Did Self-Strengthening Fail?

The question: Many accounts treat Self-Strengthening as a failure because the Qing lost to Japan in 1895 and the dynasty fell in 1912. Historians disagree about whether that judgment is fair, which measures of success should be used, and how much the limits were structural, cultural, or contingent.

Interpretation A — The movement was limited by structural and cultural constraints: Scholars in this tradition stress the limits on what the reforms could achieve. Mary Wright's influential account of the Tongzhi Restoration argued that the effort to revive Confucian government, however vigorous, was constrained by the very conservatism it relied upon. Albert Feuerwerker, in his study of Sheng Xuanhuai, analyzed how official supervision, patronage, and uncertain control hampered guandu shangban enterprise. Together with arguments about fiscal fragmentation and the persistence of the examination order, such work has often been used to contrast the Qing with Japan, which restructured its government and education more thoroughly (a comparison that later scholars have qualified).
Interpretation B — The movement achieved significant results and its failure is overstated: Other historians, including those who study the movement on its own terms, stress that the Qing built institutions, trained personnel, and strengthened capacities in ways that persisted, and that judgments based on 1895 ignore earlier achievements. Stephen Halsey argues that later military defeat should not be used to infer uniformly weak Qing state capacity, and emphasizes the dynasty's adaptive state-building. Benjamin Elman challenges simplistic readings of naval defeat as proof of wholesale scientific or technological failure, analyzing instead how naval and technical reforms were shaped by institutional and political conditions. Some scholars add that external pressures, including imperial rivalries and treaty limits, constrained Qing options.

Evidence: Both sides use arsenal and shipyard accounts, memorials, Customs statistics, enterprise records, school registers, and comparative data on Japan and other states. They differ in what they treat as the right comparison and in how much weight they give to fiscal structure, ideology, and external constraints.

Why it matters: The debate shapes how historians explain the end of the Qing and how they understand the relationship between reform and revolution. It also warns against reading outcomes backward into causes.

Student task: Choose two measures of success (for example, military capacity and institutional integration). How would the movement look on each? What evidence would you need to compare it fairly with Japan's early Meiji reforms?

Section 54.19

Chapter Review and Self-Strengthening Checkpoint

This chapter has argued that Self-Strengthening was a cluster of regionally driven projects through which Qing officials, aided by Chinese technicians, merchants, and foreign advisers, tried to rebuild state power after the mid-century crises. Before moving to Chapter 55, which examines Japan's challenge and the war of 1894–1895, students should be able to answer the synthesis question: How far did Self-Strengthening transform Qing state capacity, and why did significant modernization coexist with continuing institutional weakness? A strong answer will distinguish technological, military, fiscal, educational, and political measures; will use specific examples such as the Jiangnan Arsenal, the Fuzhou Navy Yard, the steamship company, and the Educational Mission; and will avoid both celebratory and dismissive generalizations.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined how the Qing state tried to rebuild its power after the Taiping, Nian, and Muslim rebellions and the Second Opium War. The central argument is that Self-Strengthening was a decentralized program of institutional adaptation in which regional officials combined Confucian statecraft with imported technology, new diplomatic institutions, technical education, and communications. It substantially expanded Qing capabilities but remained uneven, fiscally constrained, and only partially integrated into the central state.

Reconstruction came first. The devastated provinces of the Lower Yangtze recovered through tax relief, resettlement, and gentry-led relief and cultural restoration, though demographic and fiscal damage lingered, and famine and floods in the north added new burdens. The Tongzhi Restoration stabilized the court under the joint regency of Empress Dowagers Ci'an and Cixi, with Prince Gong as a leading court adviser and sponsor of reform, and placed trust in Han officials who had proved themselves in war. Zeng Guofan, Li Hongzhang, and Zuo Zongtang became regional leaders whose armies, staffs, and revenue gave them unusual initiative while remaining within the dynastic framework.

The term ziqiang expressed the belief that foreign techniques could strengthen the dynasty without abandoning its political and moral foundations. Its meaning widened from weapons to wealth, and conservative critics contested each step. The famous formula zhongti xiyong belongs to a later period and should not be projected back onto the 1860s.

New institutions reshaped Qing government. The Zongli Yamen created a permanent channel for foreign affairs, while older bodies continued to handle Inner Asian and tributary relations. The Maritime Customs, led by Robert Hart, supplied revenue and services under treaty constraints. Alongside the old land tax, lijin and customs funded projects but also fragmented fiscal control.

Military and industrial projects followed. The Xiang and Huai armies adopted foreign weapons and drill, while arsenals at Anqing, Nanjing, Shanghai, and Tianjin and the Fuzhou Navy Yard moved the Qing from buying to making arms and ships. Chinese artisans, scholars, and managers did most of the technical work, with foreign advisers in supporting roles. Translation bureaus and schools, including the Jiangnan translation department and the Tongwen Guan, brought selected scientific and technical knowledge into Chinese, creating new vocabularies and a small cadre of experts.

Overseas study and commercial enterprise broadened the effort. The Chinese Educational Mission sent boys to Connecticut, but political and cultural suspicion led to its recall in 1881. The China Merchants Steam Navigation Company and the Kaiping mines used the guandu shangban model, combining official sponsorship with merchant management, and telegraphs and early railways began to change communication and transport despite resistance.

The 1870s and 1880s tested the new capacities. Japan's expedition to Taiwan in 1874 raised the problem of coastal defense and prompted the debate between maritime and frontier priorities. Zuo Zongtang's reconquest of Xinjiang and the Treaty of St. Petersburg showed that the Qing could mobilize armies and diplomats. The Sino-French War produced a catastrophic naval defeat at Fuzhou but also successful resistance in Taiwan and in Tonkin, and it led to a French protectorate over Vietnam, the creation of the Navy Yamen, and the establishment of Taiwan as a province.

Regional variation and everyday experience complicate the picture. Arsenal workers, indigenous Taiwanese communities, Xinjiang's diverse populations, merchants, students, and farmers experienced these changes in very different ways, and the surviving sources often overlook their voices. Taxes, new technologies, and administrative expansion were felt unevenly across regions and classes.

The assessment of Self-Strengthening remains debated. Its achievements were real, but its limits, including fragmented command, uncertain finance, patronage, a persistent examination order, and dependence on imports, were equally real. Historians should avoid reading 1895 backward and should instead evaluate the movement against its own goals and against comparable efforts elsewhere. It contributed to the formation of regional power, a technical and diplomatic elite, and industrial and communication foundations that would shape late Qing reform and republican politics.

Chapter 55 now turns to the Sino-Japanese War and intensifying imperialism, which tested the system built in these decades. Understanding what had actually been built, and how, is essential to understanding what that war revealed and what it did not.

Maritime defense and frontier defense; the competing strategic priorities debated in 1874–1875.

A report or proposal from an official to the emperor, a principal source for this period.

The naval administration created in 1885 under Prince Chun, with Li Hongzhang as assistant.

The movement and adaptation of technical knowledge between societies, involving selection and local innovation, not mere copying.
Extraterritoriality
Treaty provision exempting foreign nationals from Qing law; part of the treaty regime that constrained Qing policy.

A scholarly term for efforts by states under external pressure to adopt new technologies and institutions while preserving existing political orders.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows how a large agrarian empire responded to defeat not by passively yielding or simply resisting, but by improvising institutions, borrowing techniques, and reworking old structures. The officials, craftsmen, translators, merchants, students, and diplomats of Self-Strengthening were not imitators of an outside model; they were Qing actors making choices under fiscal, political, and international constraints.

The movement shaped later Chinese history in lasting ways. It strengthened regional power and the personal networks that would matter in the late Qing and the Republic. It trained engineers, diplomats, and entrepreneurs who appear in later chapters. It established arsenals, shipyards, mines, telegraphs, and translation traditions that later reformers built on or criticized. And it left questions, about the relationship between technology and institutions, about state and enterprise, and about how much change was needed, that Chapters 55 through 57 take up.

The chapter also connects China to wider Asian and global history. Qing reform paralleled efforts in Japan, the Ottoman Empire, Egypt, and Russia to meet industrial and imperial pressures, and it was shaped by treaty regimes, global trade, and the movement of people and ideas. Chinese agency was central in each of these processes, from the memorials of provincial governors to the work of arsenal laborers and translators.

Above all, Self-Strengthening helps students see Chinese history as diverse, interconnected, adaptive, contested, regionally varied, politically changing, and unfinished. Its outcomes were neither inevitable nor simply failures; they were the results of choices, constraints, and contingencies. Reading it that way prepares students to approach the war of 1894–1895 and the upheavals that followed with a clearer sense of what the Qing state had, and had not, become.
