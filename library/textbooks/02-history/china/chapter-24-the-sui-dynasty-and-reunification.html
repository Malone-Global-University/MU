Part V — Sui, Tang, and the Post-Tang Transition

The Sui Dynasty and Reunification

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

In 605, according to the dynastic history compiled later under the Tang, the newly enthroned Emperor Yang set out from the eastern capital of Luoyang toward the lower Yangtze on a flotilla of towering, multi-deck vessels, drawn along a freshly dug canal by hauliers numbering in the tens of thousands. On the banks stood the people who had made the journey possible: villagers of Henan and the Huai plain who had been summoned, in numbers the sources put above a million, to dig the channel, and who, once it was finished, were expected to supply the food, boats, and labor for the imperial procession that used it. The canal was a technical achievement of the first order. It was also a measure of what the Sui state now claimed from its people.

This chapter studies the Sui dynasty (581–618), the regime that reunified northern and southern China in 589 after nearly three centuries of sustained north–south political division following the collapse of the Western Jin, which had itself briefly reunited the country between 280 and 316. Chapters 19 through 23 traced how the collapse of the Han empire gave way to the Three Kingdoms, the Western Jin, the Sixteen Kingdoms, the Southern Dynasties, and the Northern Dynasties, and how those centuries of division were also centuries of migration, religious transformation, agricultural expansion in the south, and institutional experiment in the north. Chapter 24 asks how one northern regime converted those inheritances into a reunified imperial state, and why that state, so successful at gathering resources, collapsed within a single generation.

The Sui matters because it was a hinge. Its rulers did not restore the Han empire, and they did not simply invent the system that the Tang would later administer. They drew on institutions developed under the Northern Wei, Western Wei, and Northern Zhou, on the fiscal and legal experiments of the Northern Qi, on the cultural and commercial resources of the Yangtze region, and on Buddhist, Confucian, and other traditions of imperial legitimacy. The result was a state that registered households, allocated land, standardized law and coinage, rebuilt provincial administration, and linked the Yellow River, Huai, and Yangtze systems by canal. Many of these measures endured, in modified form, into the Tang and beyond.

What changed during the Sui was the scale of what one government could organize. Central authorities counted and taxed households across a territory that included the Wei River valley, the North China Plain, the Yangtze delta, and parts of the far south. They mobilized labor for capital cities, granaries, walls, roads, canals, and campaigns. What continued was much of the social world described in Chapter 23: great aristocratic families, Buddhist monasteries, regional economies with their own rhythms, and differences of dialect, custom, and political memory between north and south. Reunification was a political achievement, not an erasure of these differences.

The people affected included court officials from the northwestern military aristocracy who staffed the new regime, southern landowners who had served the Chen dynasty, peasant households who owed grain, cloth, and labor, soldiers drawn from garrison and militia households, boatmen and merchants who used the new waterways, and the conscripted laborers, many of them women and boys in the most stressed years, on whom construction and campaigns depended. They also included the Goguryeo kingdom of the Korean peninsula and Manchuria, the Turkic khaganates of the steppe, the Tuyuhun of the northwest, and states of Southeast Asia, whose relations with the Sui were as much about diplomacy and trade as war.

Historians reconstruct the Sui largely from sources that were written after it ended. The main narrative, the Sui Shu (Book of Sui), was compiled in the early Tang under imperial commission, by officials with an interest in explaining why the previous dynasty fell and the new one deserved to rule. This chapter therefore pairs textual evidence with archaeology, including the remains of the Sui capital Daxingcheng, the excavated granaries near Luoyang, and surviving sections of the canal network, and treats the dynastic history's moral judgments as arguments to be weighed rather than conclusions to be accepted.

The chapter connects to larger histories in several directions. It links the Age of Division in Chapters 19–23 to the Tang chapters that follow; it shows the long-distance importance of the Yangtze region that Chapters 21 and 23 described; it places China in a wider Northeast Asian world of Korean kingdoms, steppe powers, and Japanese embassies; and it offers an early case study, developed later in the Qin, Yuan, and Ming chapters, of how ambitious infrastructure and military programs can strengthen an empire and, when demands outrun capacity, destabilize it.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the political conditions in the late sixth century that made reunification possible without treating it as inevitable.
  2. Trace Yang Jian's path from Northern Zhou regent to Emperor Wen of Sui and identify the regional and aristocratic base of his regime.
  3. Analyze the conquest of the Chen dynasty in 588–589 and the political problems of governing the conquered south.
  4. Distinguish Sui institutional continuities from Northern Zhou and Northern Wei practice from genuine Sui innovations.
  5. Describe how registration, the equal-field system, and taxation in grain, cloth, and labor supported the Sui state.
  6. Evaluate the common claim that the Sui "built the Grand Canal," explaining what the Sui actually constructed, linked, and enlarged.
  7. Assess how the canal system and granaries integrated regional economies, and whose labor and resources made that integration possible.
  8. Interpret the Goguryeo campaigns as a case of strategic ambition, logistical strain, and cumulative burden rather than the product of one ruler's vanity alone.
  9. Analyze the interacting causes of rebellion and the collapse of Sui authority, including labor, taxation, conscription, elite defection, and regional grievance.
  10. Compare competing interpretations of the Sui collapse and evaluate the reliability of the Tang-compiled sources.
  11. Connect the Sui's short duration to its long institutional legacy, without collapsing the Sui into a mere prelude to the Tang.

Historical Context

By the mid-sixth century, the lands once ruled by the Han empire were divided among rival states with very different social foundations. In the north, the successors of the Northern Wei, the Eastern Wei and its successor Northern Qi in the Yellow River plain and the Western Wei and its successor Northern Zhou in the Wei River valley, drew on Xianbei military traditions, Han Chinese landed families, garrison communities, and Buddhist institutions (Chapter 22). In the south, the Liang and then the Chen ruled from Jiankang over a society shaped by migrant aristocracies, expanding rice agriculture, commercial towns, and a literary culture prized by its elites (Chapter 21). These were not merely halves of one country pulled apart. They were distinct political systems with distinct administrative habits, tax bases, legal traditions, and understandings of legitimacy.

Geography shaped the possibilities. The Wei River valley, known as Guanzhong, and the adjoining northwest were defensible, agriculturally productive, and close to the steppe, giving their rulers strong cavalry and a militarized aristocracy. The North China Plain was larger, more populous, and more exposed. The Yangtze basin and the lower Yangtze delta were protected by wide rivers and increasingly wealthy through rice cultivation, but were vulnerable to any northern power that could organize river warfare. Between the Yellow River and the Yangtze lay the Huai region, a low-lying zone of lakes and rivers that had long been both a barrier and a corridor. Chapter 2 introduced these regions; this chapter shows how a state learned to connect them.

The Age of Division had also changed the social order. Migration had moved northern families and northern customs into the south, and peoples of Inner Asian origin into the north (Chapters 20 and 23). Buddhism had become a major landholder, patron of art, and source of imperial legitimacy. Great aristocratic clans dominated appointments in both halves of the country, though the northwestern military elite that supported Western Wei and Northern Zhou was less oriented toward genealogical prestige than the southern aristocracy and more toward military service. Households were increasingly recorded by the state in the north, less consistently in the south, where large estates and dependent households sheltered many people from registration.

The geographic meaning of "China" in this period was therefore unsettled. The Sui claimed a universal imperial authority and adopted the political language of earlier dynasties, but its actual territory, its populations, and its ruling circle were products of the sixth century, not the Han. Its rulers came from a family that claimed Han Chinese descent while serving in a Xianbei-led military regime; the founding emperor's wife, Empress Dugu, came from a Xianbei family; its capital stood in the Wei valley; and its subjects included southern literati, Turkic-speaking groups on the frontier, and the peoples of Lingnan and the southeast whose relationship to Han-style administration was still developing. Students should keep these distinctions in mind and avoid reading later national categories back into this world.

The stakes of the period were high. If the Sui succeeded, a single government could again draw on the grain of the south and the horses and soldiers of the north, control the frontiers, and reorganize the tax and labor systems that sustained large armies. If it failed, the region would return to competing regional states. The Sui succeeded quickly and then failed quickly, and the Tang that followed inherited both its institutional achievements and a cautionary lesson about the cost of ambition, a lesson that early Tang rulers and historians made explicit. Chapter 25 takes up the Tang; this chapter ends where the Sui collapses and the conditions for the Tang emerge.

Historical Evidence: Reading the Sui Record

Historical Evidence

The Sui lasted only thirty-seven years, and most of the surviving written record was produced afterward. The Sui Shu was compiled under Tang auspices in two stages: the annals and biographies (55 chapters) were completed in 636 by a commission of Tang officials that included the statesman Wei Zheng, and the treatises (30 chapters) were added in 656. Its compilers wrote in a political setting where the Tang court wanted lessons from the previous dynasty's fall. Its annals, treatises on law, finance, and administration, and biographies are indispensable, but its portraits of Emperor Yang and of the last Chen ruler follow moral templates in which a ruler's personal vices explain a state's failure. Historians treat such judgments as claims to be tested against other evidence.

The Sui Shu treatises on legal, fiscal, and administrative matters are especially valuable because they preserve figures for households and population, summaries of the tax system, and descriptions of institutions. These numbers reflect what the state's registers recorded, not necessarily the actual population, and they can vary with the effectiveness of registration. Other evidence includes stone inscriptions and tomb epitaphs, which record careers and family relationships from the point of view of the deceased's family; Buddhist sources that describe Emperor Wen's patronage of monasteries and relics; the later compilation Zizhi Tongjian (Comprehensive Mirror for Aid in Government, eleventh century), which reorganizes earlier material; and sources from Korea and Japan, including later Korean accounts of the campaigns against Goguryeo and Japanese records of embassies to the Sui court.

Archaeology supplements and sometimes corrects the texts. Excavations at the Sui capital Daxingcheng, at the Hanjia and Huiluo granary sites near Luoyang, and along surviving canal channels document urban planning, storage capacity, and engineering. Numismatic evidence shows the state's efforts to standardize coinage. Because tomb finds and stone epitaphs tend to represent the elite, they say less about the peasants and laborers on whom Sui projects depended, whose experiences must be inferred from official descriptions of levies, from laws, and from the patterns of rebellion.

Section 24.1

The Road to Reunification

Reunification in 589 can look, in hindsight, like the natural conclusion of a long process. It was not. For nearly three centuries after the collapse of the Western Jin, which had itself reunited the country from 280 to 316, several ambitious northern and southern regimes had tried and failed to conquer their rivals, including the Former Qin's disastrous southern campaign of 383 (Chapter 20) and the Liu Song's northern expeditions (Chapter 21). The sixth century produced a new set of conditions. In the north, the Eastern Wei and Northern Qi on the plain and the Western Wei and Northern Zhou in Guanzhong had settled into a long rivalry, and in 577 the Northern Zhou, under Emperor Wu (Yuwen Yong), conquered the Northern Qi. For the first time in generations, a single northern state controlled both the Wei valley and the North China Plain.

That conquest rested on institutional preparation. Zhou rulers had reorganized their military into a system of militia units that combined soldiers with land and household status, had drawn Han Chinese local elites into military service, and had, in the 570s, confiscated Buddhist and Daoist property and returned many monks and nuns to lay status, a policy that expanded the tax base and manpower available to the state. They inherited a Xianbei-led military ethos, but also relied on officials educated in the Zhou-influenced texts that Western Wei and Northern Zhou rulers had promoted. The result was a state better able to raise and supply armies than its Qi rival, which suffered from factional politics and court instability.

Emperor Wu of Zhou died in 578, apparently while preparing further campaigns. He was succeeded by an erratic son, Emperor Xuan (Yuwen Yun), who abdicated in 579 in favor of his child, nominally Emperor Jing, while retaining real influence until his death in 580. Yang Jian, whose daughter was Emperor Xuan's empress, was a senior official of the northwestern aristocracy with a strong record of military and administrative service. When the emperor died in 580, Yang Jian moved quickly to take control of the court, positioning himself as regent for the child emperor. This was a decisive moment: the Yuwen clan's authority had been undermined by their own succession crises, and a leader from within the northwestern elite was well placed to replace it.

Not everyone accepted this. Several regional officials resisted, most notably Yuchi Jiong, the governor at Xiangzhou in the plain, whose rebellion in 580 rallied opposition to Yang Jian's seizure of power. It was defeated within a few months. The suppression of that revolt is important: it showed that the Yang faction commanded the loyalty of the core military forces of Guanzhong, it eliminated leading potential rivals, and it demonstrated that regional resistance in the newly conquered eastern plain was not strong enough to reverse the shift of power. It also foreshadowed a recurring pattern in Sui history, in which the center in Guanzhong was formidable and the eastern plains were politically and socially more difficult to manage.

The southern situation also mattered. The Chen dynasty, founded in 557 after the fall of the Liang, controlled the Yangtze basin and points south but was smaller and weaker than its predecessors, having lost the Huai region to northern armies in the 570s. It faced a court dominated by aristocratic factions and regional military leaders and relied on a shrinking fiscal base. The Chen was not defenseless, and it possessed real advantages in river warfare, but by the 580s its northern neighbor was far larger. Once the north was unified and its military reformed, the strategic balance had shifted decisively, though it still required years of preparation.

Students should draw two lessons from this section. First, reunification was the product of institution-building, military organization, and contingent political events, not the inevitable resolution of a long division. If Emperor Wu had lived longer, or if Yang Jian had failed to win the succession struggle, the political map might have developed differently. Second, the transformations of Chapter 23, including migration, Buddhism, and the growth of the south, formed the background but not the direct explanation of Sui success. The Sui state was built by a small circle of men within a specific northwestern political world, using the practical tools available in the 580s.

Section 24.2

Emperor Wen and the Founding of Sui

In 581, after months as regent, Yang Jian accepted the abdication of the child Emperor Jing and took the throne, establishing the Sui dynasty and reigning as Emperor Wen (r. 581–604). The transfer followed the well-established pattern of abdication in which a ruler ceded the throne to a successor in a ritualized act of legitimate transfer, a form that presented a coup as a peaceful handover. The new dynasty took its name from the title Duke (later Prince) of Sui that Yang Jian had held; later tradition claimed that Yang Jian changed 隨 to 隋 because the walking radical seemed inauspicious. Epigraphic and textual research has challenged this story and suggests that his pre-imperial title may already have been written 隋, so there may have been no character-changing event. The Zhou imperial family was then largely eliminated within a few years, a step that eased the Sui's security but shows how thoroughly the regime removed potential rivals.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Guanlong Aristocracy (關隴集團)

The term refers to the intermarried military and civil elite of Guanzhong (the Wei River valley) and Longyou (the northwest) that rose to power in the Western Wei and Northern Zhou and staffed the Sui and early Tang governments. Its members included families of Xianbei origin, such as the Yuwen, Dugu, and Zhangsun, and families claiming Han descent, such as the Yang and the Li, who shared military service, marriage ties, and often Xianbei-derived names or habits. The historian Chen Yinke (1890–1969) argued influentially that this group was the key to political continuity from the Northern Zhou through the early Tang. Later scholars have qualified this view, noting that the group was not entirely closed, that its members sometimes competed with each other, and that other elites, including eastern plain families and southern literati, also mattered. The term is useful as a description of a dominant political network, not as a fixed ethnic or class category.

Emperor Wen's political base was this northwestern elite. Yang Jian himself was a Zhou officer whose family was of the Hongnong Yang lineage, which claimed Han Chinese descent but had served in Xianbei-led regimes for generations. His wife, Dugu Qieluo, came from a Xianbei family whose daughters had married into other Zhou families. Their sons and daughters in turn married into elite families. Thus the court's central circle was tied together by kinship, and Emperor Wen's early reliance on senior ministers such as Gao Jiong, Yang Su, Su Wei, and Niu Hong meant that he governed with men whose careers had been made in the same military and administrative environment. Empress Dugu was a conspicuous participant in politics, and the sources record that she attended court discussions and influenced appointments, earning the reputation of a co-sovereign, though the extent and precise nature of her influence is hard to reconstruct from sources written by men after her death.

Legitimacy required more than kinship. Emperor Wen presented the Sui as the rightful heir to the Zhou's claim to authority while also drawing on older imperial models, including the Han, through rituals, official titles, and the rebuilding of a capital in the traditional region of Guanzhong. He commissioned a new capital, Daxingcheng, in 582, laid out on a grid by the architect Yuwen Kai to the southeast of the ruined Han city of Chang'an, and moved the court there in 583. The new city, with an enclosed imperial precinct, an administrative zone, and residential wards, became the physical template for the Tang Chang'an described in Chapters 26 and 27. Its scale reflected an intention to rule a large and diverse empire from the northwest.

Buddhism was central to Sui legitimacy. After the Northern Zhou's suppression of Buddhism in the 570s, Emperor Wen and Empress Dugu restored and patronized monasteries, and Emperor Wen came to be presented as a righteous, Buddha-honoring ruler in the manner of the Indian king Ashoka. Between 601 and 604 the court distributed relics to dozens of prefectures and sponsored the construction of stupas to house them, a highly visible program that tied local communities to the throne. Confucian ritual and learning also received attention, with Niu Hong leading efforts to reorganize court ritual and music, and Daoist figures also received patronage. Emperor Wen's approach was less an exclusive endorsement of one tradition than a use of multiple traditions to reinforce a single throne, and the Buddhist emphasis was especially important in the diverse, formerly divided empire (see Chapter 23).

Frontier and diplomatic policy in this early period was shaped by the presence of the Turkic khaganate on the northern steppe, which by the 580s had split into eastern and western branches, in part because of internal rivalries that Sui diplomacy encouraged. Emperor Wen used a combination of military deterrence, gifts, marriage alliances (most famously the marriages of Princess Anyi to the khagan Qimin in 597 and, after her death, of Princess Yicheng to him in 599), and support for rival claimants to keep the Turks from uniting against him. This was a pragmatic policy of managing a powerful neighbor rather than treating it as a subordinate, and it foreshadowed the Tang's own complex dealings with the steppe (Chapters 25 and 27).

Emperor Wen's reign also had dark sides. He was personally frugal and suspicious, and his court could be harsh; officials were punished by beating in some cases, and he demonstrated little tolerance for criticism late in his reign. Family politics turned violent. In 600, the crown prince Yang Yong was deposed, in part because of the enmity of Empress Dugu and the ambition of his brother Yang Guang, who became the new heir. The empress died in 602; Gao Jiong had been dismissed in 599, and Emperor Wen himself died in 604, with Yang Guang succeeding him. The dynastic history hints that Yang Guang may have had a hand in his father's death, but this remains uncertain, and the account is shaped by the Tang historians' wish to depict Emperor Yang unfavorably. What is clear is that the transition brought a very different style of rule.

Section 24.3

Conquest of the South

Preparation for the southern campaign began early in the reign. In 587 the Sui absorbed the small state of Later Liang, a Xiao-family regime based at Jiangling on the middle Yangtze that had acted as a northern client, placing a Sui presence on the river. The Sui also built the Shanyang Canal (587), connecting the Huai to the Yangtze near Yangzhou, to support the movement of troops and supplies (Section 24.6). Naval construction and training took place on the upper and middle Yangtze, and Sui diplomacy and propaganda emphasized the failings of the Chen court. A Sui proclamation reported in later sources denounced the last Chen ruler, Chen Shubao, and was reportedly circulated widely in the south, an early example of the use of persuasion alongside force.

The campaign of 588–589 was a coordinated assault by multiple armies along the Yangtze line, from the upper river to the coast. Emperor Wen's second son, Yang Guang, held nominal supreme command, while experienced generals including Yang Su, He Ruobi, and Han Qinhu directed the operations. Sui forces crossed the Yangtze at several points, and the Chen defenders, hampered by internal divisions and a lack of coordinated response, were unable to stop them. Jiankang fell in early 589. Later tradition, drawn from Tang-era accounts, describes Chen Shubao being found hiding in a well with his consorts. This vivid anecdote fits the Tang historians' portrait of a decadent ruler, and students should treat it as a literary trope as much as a record of fact. The Sui took the Chen ruler and much of the court to the north, where Chen Shubao lived comfortably until his death in 604.

The conquest was quick but the settlement was harder. The Sui destroyed the palaces of Jiankang and downgraded the city to a prefectural seat, symbolically and practically ending its role as a political capital. The region's elite, including aristocratic families that had governed the Southern Dynasties, were expected to accept Sui rule, and many did, but the Sui attempted at first to bring southern society into the northern administrative framework quickly, imposing northern-style officials, registration requirements, and a legal code. In 590 and 591 revolts broke out across the former Chen territory, from the lower Yangtze to the southeast coast, driven by resentment at the new officials, attempts to register households that had previously escaped notice, and suspicions among former officeholders. Sui armies under Yang Su and others suppressed the rebellions, and Yang Guang was sent to govern the south from Yangzhou, where he spent a decade developing relationships with southern literati and Buddhist leaders.

The southern settlement shows that reunification depended on local negotiation as well as force. In Lingnan, Lady Xian, the leader of a powerful lineage of the Gaozhou region, led local forces that recognized Sui authority and helped suppress a local rebellion; the court awarded her titles and confirmed her lineage's local standing. Her case, recorded in the dynastic history, illustrates how the Sui relied on intermediaries who commanded local loyalty, including women and leaders of non-Han or partly assimilated communities, to extend authority into regions where Han-style bureaucracy had shallow roots. It also shows the terms of incorporation: local leaders received recognition and rank in return for acceptance of imperial authority.

Emperor Wen also treated southern Buddhism and culture with respect. Southern monks such as Zhiyi, associated with the Tiantai tradition, received patronage from Yang Guang, and southern literary culture influenced court life. Yet the Sui court remained dominated by northwesterners, and in the following years southern elites found their opportunities limited compared to their predecessors' under the Southern Dynasties. The persistence of regional differences in dialect, custom, economic organization, and political memory meant that "reunification" was a process rather than a moment. As Section 24.4 shows, the Sui's institutions were designed in part to break down the intermediate structures, such as regional aristocratic control over local appointments, that had supported division.

Section 24.4

Administrative Reconstruction

Sui government was built on inherited foundations. From the Western Wei and Northern Zhou came a central administration modeled on textual descriptions of early Zhou offices, which Emperor Wen soon replaced with a simpler structure closer to the Northern Wei, Northern Qi, and Southern Dynasties. In its mature form, the Sui central government was organized around the Three Departments and Six Ministries. The Secretariat (Neishi Sheng) drafted imperial edicts, the Chancellery (Menxia Sheng) reviewed them, and the Department of State Affairs (Shangshu Sheng) carried out policy through six ministries handling personnel, revenue, rites, military affairs, justice, and public works. Some details of naming and function were fixed only later, in the Tang, and the fully developed system should not be read back into the 580s, but the basic division of drafting, review, and execution was established under the Sui and provided the skeleton of later government (Chapters 25 and 26).

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Prefecture (zhou 州) and Commandery (jun 郡)

These were the two main units above the county (xian) in local government. During the Age of Division, rulers had multiplied prefectures and commanderies, so that many small units overlapped and created a large number of offices to fill. In 583 the Sui abolished the commandery tier, leaving a two-level system of prefectures and counties, and cut the number of local officials. In 607, under Emperor Yang, the naming was reversed: prefectures were renamed commanderies. The changes were more than labels. They reflected repeated attempts to make the number of jurisdictions smaller, easier for the center to supervise, and less dependent on local aristocratic appointments.

The most important local reform was the reduction of layers. In 583 the Sui abolished the intermediate commandery level, leaving prefectures and counties. The number of local offices, which had grown enormously under the divided states, each of which created posts to reward supporters, was cut sharply, saving money and reducing the bureaucratic overlap that made central supervision difficult. A second reform concerned appointments. Under the Northern and Southern Dynasties, provincial governors had often appointed their own subordinates, who came from local families. The Sui shifted the appointment of most local officials to the center, controlled through the Ministry of Personnel, and imposed rules of avoidance under which officials generally could not serve in their home prefectures. These measures aimed to weaken local aristocratic networks and increase loyalty to the throne, though in practice local clerks and elites remained essential to any actual administration.

The Sui also changed the mechanisms of recruitment. The old Nine-Rank system, under which local evaluators graded candidates by family background and reputation and thereby helped preserve aristocratic dominance, was set aside, and in 587 and later the court ordered prefectures to send qualified men to the capital, using recommendation and, in some cases, testing. Emperor Yang expanded this approach, and the tradition later credits him with establishing the jinshi examination in 605, but historians debate the date and the nature of the early examinations. It is safer to say that the Sui moved toward more central selection and toward literary and classical criteria, laying the ground for the examination system fully developed under the Tang and Song (Chapters 26 and 31). Aristocratic families continued to dominate high office throughout the Sui, and examination candidates remained a small minority.

Law was another area of continuity and change. The Kaihuang Code (Kaihuang lü), issued in 581 and revised in 583, drew on the Northern Qi and Northern Zhou codes and on earlier southern legal traditions. It organized punishments into five categories, light and heavy beating, penal servitude, exile, and death, and reduced the number of capital offenses and the severity of some penalties compared to the Zhou code. It also formalized the "Ten Abominations," serious offenses such as rebellion, treason, and offenses against parents, that would remain central to Chinese legal codes for centuries. Emperor Yang issued a revised Daye Code in 607. These laws were not the same as the Tang code of 653, which Chapter 25 will examine, but they were essential precursors, and their existence shows the importance the Sui attached to written law as an instrument of standardization.

Sui government also relied on measures of standardization and infrastructure. The court reissued a standard five-zhu coin in 581 and later ordered older coins removed from circulation, an attempt to unify a currency that had varied between north and south. It promoted standard weights and measures and built granaries in the capital and along transport routes. These measures had a fiscal logic, connecting administrative reform to the tax system described in Section 24.5. And yet the reach of the state should not be overstated. Provincial officials were few relative to the population, they depended on local clerks, elites, and community leaders, and the Sui lacked the tools to closely monitor distant officials. The state's capacity, impressive as it was, depended on cooperation with local society, and that cooperation eroded under pressure in the years after 610.

Section 24.5

Taxation and Land Policies

The Sui state's power rested on its ability to know who lived where and to claim a share of what they produced. Chapter 22 explained the origins of the equal-field system under the Northern Wei, in which the state allocated land to households in return for taxes and service. The Sui inherited that framework from the northern regimes, especially the Northern Qi and Northern Zhou, and adapted it to a much larger territory. In principle, adult men received grants of land, part of it returnable to the state at death and part of it heritable, in amounts that varied by status and region, and were liable for a tax in grain (zu), a tax in cloth or silk (diao), and labor service (yong). In practice, the amount of land available varied enormously, and the system worked best in regions with abundant unclaimed land and less well in densely settled areas, including parts of the eastern plain and much of the south, where land shortage and private estates limited the state's ability to reallocate.

Registration was the first problem. Aristocrats, monasteries, and local strongmen sheltered many households from the tax rolls, and the state's knowledge of the population was limited. The minister Gao Jiong led two important initiatives. One was the practice known as da suo mao yue (大索貌閱), a large-scale inspection in which officials checked the appearance and age of individuals against registers to expose concealed adults and false claims of age or disability. The other was a system of setting standard household categories and tax "patterns" by locality, so that tax burdens were assessed according to household resources and less open to local manipulation. These measures added large numbers of households to the registers. The dynastic history reports several million households at the beginning of the dynasty and roughly nine million, with a population of over forty-five million, by 609, but these figures reflect a combination of true growth, recovery from disruption, and the addition of previously unregistered people, and should not be read as precise demographic counts.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Yong (庸) and Labor Service

Yong refers to labor service owed by registered adults to the state, a form of taxation in labor rather than goods. Governments used it to build walls, roads, canals, palaces, and to transport supplies. Sui law set a regular annual period of service, which Emperor Wen reduced (from thirty to twenty days in 583) and allowed households to commute in some cases by paying cloth. Conscripted laborers, distinct from soldiers, worked under officials and were often required to travel and supply their own food. Because the obligation was tied to household registers, the same institution that made state projects possible also created the risk of overuse, since the state could raise demands simply by issuing orders. Understanding yong is essential for understanding both Sui achievements and Sui collapse.

Emperor Wen also reformed the relationship between the army and the tax system. Under the Western Wei and Northern Zhou, soldiers of the militia (fubing) had been registered separately as military households, exempt from ordinary taxes but obliged to serve. In 590 Emperor Wen ordered military men to come under prefectural and county administration, with land allotments and household registration like ordinary subjects. The reform weakened the separate hereditary status of military households and helped demilitarize much of the North China Plain, but it did not abolish the military command structure, which remained especially important in Guanzhong and Hedong. The resulting arrangements helped prefigure the locally based militia system later developed under the Tang (Chapter 25).

The state stored what it collected. Emperor Wen's government invested heavily in granaries, including the capital-region granaries and large storage facilities at Luokou, Huiluo, and Hanjia near Luoyang, as well as local "charitable granaries" (yicang and renshou type storage) intended to buffer famine. Archaeological work at the Hanjia and Huiluo sites has uncovered rows of underground storage pits, some capable of holding large quantities of grain, that provide material confirmation of the dynastic history's descriptions. The court's stored grain allowed it to respond to famine, such as the drought that struck Guanzhong in the early 580s and again in later years, when the emperor allowed the population to move east in search of food. This capacity was a real achievement, and the Sui was regarded by the Tang as a state that had accumulated remarkable reserves.

The critical point is that these fiscal successes produced the temptation, and the ability, to expand demands. A state that has enumerated its households, established granaries, and built a reliable system of levies can raise armies and build canals on a scale earlier governments could not. The system also had structural weaknesses. Land allocations were often theoretical; aristocratic and monastic estates remained; taxes were fixed by household rather than by ability to pay at every moment; and the state depended on annual registers that could be manipulated by local officials. When labor demands escalated after 605, many households could meet them only by neglecting their farms, and the harvests, on which the entire fiscal system rested, suffered. The relationship between land, registration, and labor thus links this section directly to the collapse analyzed in Section 24.9.

Section 24.6

The Grand Canal

The Grand Canal is often described as a single project built by Emperor Yang. The history is more complicated and more interesting. The Sui did not create the idea of a north–south waterway, and they did not dig a single new channel from Hangzhou to Beijing. Chinese states had built canals since the Warring States period, including the Hangou channel linking the Yangtze and Huai near Yangzhou, constructed by the state of Wu in the fifth century BCE, and other regional waterways of the Han and Three Kingdoms period. What the Sui did was to enlarge, repair, and connect existing rivers and canals with new stretches into a much more integrated system, one that made it possible to carry bulk goods between the Yangtze, Huai, Yellow, and Hai river basins and the northern capitals.

The sequence of construction can be summarized. In 584 the Guangtong Canal, engineered by Yuwen Kai, connected the capital Daxingcheng to the Yellow River near Tong Pass along the Wei River, improving the supply of grain to the capital. In 587 the Shanyang Canal reopened and widened the older Hangou route between the Huai and the Yangtze. In 605, after Emperor Yang's accession, the Tongji Canal was constructed to link the eastern capital Luoyang with the Huai region, using the Luo and Yellow rivers and channels southeast toward the Huai, while the Shanyang route was expanded. In 608 the Yongji Canal extended the system northward from the Yellow River toward the region of modern Beijing, then called Zhuo Commandery, to support campaigns in the northeast. In 610 the Jiangnan Canal linked Jingkou on the Yangtze with Yuhang (modern Hangzhou) in the lower Yangtze delta. The Sui-era network thus centered on Luoyang and extended in several directions, and the familiar north–south line from Beijing to Hangzhou took its later form under the Yuan and Ming dynasties (Chapters 39 and 43).

Labor mobilization was central. The Sui Shu reports that in 605 more than a million men and women were drafted from Henan and the Huai region to dig the Tongji Canal, and similarly enormous numbers for the Yongji Canal in 608, when, according to the account, women were called up because too few men were available. These numbers are large and should be read cautiously, since dynastic historians could exaggerate to make a point about the emperor's excess, and many of those counted may have served only briefly or been assigned lighter tasks. But the general picture is credible: the state used its household registers to summon vast numbers of people, and the burden fell heavily on the plains of Henan, Hebei, and the Huai. Overseers and local officials organized work gangs by county, and the death rate from exhaustion, exposure, and disease is reported, again probably with exaggeration, to have been severe.

Construction also required engineering knowledge. Canal builders needed to manage differences in elevation, water supply, silting, and the seasonal swings of the rivers. In the flat eastern plains the challenge was to maintain channel depth and flow; in other regions, locks, embankments, and sluices were required. The Sui inherited this knowledge from earlier hydraulic work and combined it with the administrative ability to organize it at scale. The width of the main channels reported in the sources, with roads and trees along the banks, and relay stations and imperial lodges along the route, indicate that the canal was conceived not only as a freight route but as an imperial corridor, used for Emperor Yang's ceremonial journeys to Jiangdu in 605, 610, and 616.

Historical Vocabulary
Historical Vocabulary: Grand Canal (大運河)

"Grand Canal" is a modern English label for a system of linked waterways that developed over many centuries. It does not refer to a single project or a single alignment. The Sui-era network was oriented around Luoyang and the Huai region; the Yuan and Ming dynasties later rebuilt the system to run more directly between the lower Yangtze and the capital at Beijing. In 2014, sections of the "Sui-Tang Grand Canal" and later channels were inscribed together as a UNESCO World Heritage property. When reading historical accounts, students should ask which section, and which dynasty's system, is being described.

Section 24.7

Transport and Economic Integration

Why did the canal system matter? The answer lies in the geography of the reunified empire. The political and military heart of the Sui, Guanzhong, was a productive but limited region, unable to feed a large capital, court, and army without imports. The North China Plain, larger and more populous, had been devastated repeatedly by war and flooding. The Yangtze basin, especially the lower Yangtze, had been developing since the fourth century into the empire's most productive rice-growing region, aided by the migration of northern farmers and the expansion of irrigation (Chapters 21 and 23). The Sui did not create southern productivity. It built the systems by which the imperial state could draw on that productivity, transferring southern grain and goods to northern political and military centers.

Water transport was far cheaper than moving goods overland by cart or pack animal. A single boat could carry the loads of many carts, and canals permitted movement of bulk commodities including grain, salt, and timber, as well as cloth, tea, and other goods, over long distances. In the Sui period the great majority of the state's tax grain was still collected and consumed within regions, but the canal system gave the court an option it had not had for centuries: to move large quantities of grain to Luoyang and to the northeast, and to stock granaries such as Luokou and Huiluo, sustaining both a metropolis and military campaigns. The eastern capital at Luoyang, built in 605–606 at the center of the waterway system, was in part a logistical decision to shorten supply lines, and Emperor Yang spent much of his reign there and in the southeast rather than at Daxingcheng.

Integration also had a communications dimension. Along the canals ran roads and postal stations, and the court used the waterways to send officials, troops, and messages. The presence of a reliable transport corridor made it easier to monitor distant areas and to respond to local unrest, at least until unrest became too widespread. It also encouraged commerce. Market towns and ports along the canal, including Yangzhou, Bianzhou (near modern Kaifeng), and Luoyang, gained in importance, and merchants, boatmen, and artisans found opportunities in the traffic. The later flourishing of Kaifeng and Yangzhou (Chapters 30 and 32) built on this Sui infrastructure. It also helped extend a common currency and tax system, since goods and officials could move more easily across old political divides.

The benefits were unevenly distributed. The court, the capitals, and the military gained most directly. Southern landowners and merchants could profit from increased trade, but they also faced heavier taxation and the loss of the southern capital's political and commercial role, since Jiankang had been reduced to a provincial city. Communities near the canal bore the costs of labor drafts, corvée transport duties, and supplies for imperial travel, and areas far from the waterways benefited little. A modern historian must therefore distinguish between the long-term significance of the canal, which lasted for centuries and shaped Chinese economic geography, and its immediate effects, which included heavy strain on the households who built and maintained it. Both facts belong to the same historical episode.

Finally, the canal must be seen as part of a broader Sui effort to link regions. The court sponsored roads, including a major northern highway, promoted travel on the routes of the northwest, and expanded contacts with Central Asia. In 609 Emperor Yang traveled to Zhangye in the Gansu corridor, where, following a campaign against the Tuyuhun, he received rulers and envoys from Central Asia in a large ceremonial and commercial gathering organized by the official Pei Ju, who compiled a survey of the western regions (the Xiyu Tuji). These actions show a state trying to integrate its own regions and connect them to wider Eurasian networks, a theme developed under the Tang (Chapter 27). They also increased the state's expenses.

Section 24.8

Military Campaigns Against Goguryeo

The Sui campaigns against Goguryeo (Koguryŏ) were among the largest military operations in East Asian history to that date, and they were the decisive strain on the dynasty. To understand them, students must look at Goguryeo itself. It was a powerful kingdom centered in the Yalu and Liao river region and northern Korea, with fortified mountain cities, a large army, and a sophisticated diplomatic strategy that sought to balance the Sui against the Turks and against Korean neighbors Baekje and Silla. It had long been a dominant power in Manchuria and had a complicated relationship with Chinese states, at times sending envoys and accepting titles while resisting subordination. Sui rulers regarded Goguryeo as a threat because of its potential alliance with the Turks and its control of the Liaodong region, historically claimed by Chinese states.

The first Sui attempt, in 598 under Emperor Wen, ended in failure. A large army under the emperor's son Yang Liang was hampered by heavy rains, disease, supply difficulties, and, at sea, storms; it withdrew with heavy losses, and Goguryeo's king sent an apologetic letter. Emperor Yang was determined to do better. He had seen a Goguryeo envoy at the tent of a Turkic khagan in 607, which persuaded him that Goguryeo was part of a hostile network, and in 611 he ordered mobilization. Grain was shipped to the northeast by the newly built canal to the Zhuo Commandery region; ships were built; soldiers and laborers were summoned from across the empire.

The campaign of 612 is reported to have involved more than a million combat troops with a far larger number of support personnel, though these figures, from the Sui Shu, should be treated as indicators of scale rather than precise counts. The army crossed the Liao River and besieged the fortress of Liaodong, but the Goguryeo defenders held out. A separate force of about 300,000 was sent overland toward the Goguryeo capital at Pyongyang, and was defeated at the Salsu River (modern Chongchon) by the Goguryeo commander Eulji Mundeok, who is said to have drawn the Sui army into a deep advance, exhausted its supplies, and then attacked as it retreated. The sources report that only a few thousand of the original force returned. The account emphasizes Goguryeo's defensive ingenuity, and Korean sources treat the victory as a national triumph, though the details come largely from later records.

Emperor Yang did not abandon the effort. A second campaign in 613 was cut short when Yang Xuangan, son of the powerful minister Yang Su, who was in charge of grain supplies, rebelled at Liyang, forcing the emperor to withdraw and the army to break up. A third campaign in 614 achieved a partial result: Goguryeo, its army under pressure and its people suffering, sent an envoy offering submission, and Emperor Yang, whose forces were weary, accepted. When the Goguryeo king refused to come to court in person, Emperor Yang began preparing another campaign, but by then rebellion had spread across his own territory and the plan collapsed. The Sui had spent enormous resources for a formal submission that was never fulfilled.

Why did the campaigns fail, and why were they so damaging? The logistical challenge was immense: supplying armies of this size across hundreds of kilometers of northeastern terrain required moving grain by canal, cart, and porters through regions with limited infrastructure, and the growing season and river conditions constrained movement. Goguryeo's fortified cities, many on high ground, resisted siege, while its defenders could retreat and wait for supply problems to weaken the invaders. The Sui command structure relied on court-appointed commanders who were sometimes required to follow detailed instructions from the emperor, limiting flexibility. Most importantly, the campaigns were repeated in rapid succession. Each mobilization removed farmers from their fields and imposed heavy demands on households in Henan, Hebei, and Shandong, which were the regions nearest the front. The strain was cumulative. A single failed campaign would have been costly, but repeated campaigns, combined with the canal projects, imposed sustained pressure that the fiscal and administrative system could not absorb.

Emperor Yang's motives deserve careful treatment. The Tang tradition attributes the campaigns to vanity and a desire for glory, and personal ambition surely played a role. But there were strategic reasons as well: concern about a Goguryeo-Turk alignment, the historical claim to Liaodong, the desire to demonstrate Sui superiority after successes in the west and south, and the ordinary logic of an empire that had won recent wars and expected to win more. Historians therefore evaluate the campaigns as a case of imperial overextension, in which a capable state pursued objectives at a scale beyond its administrative and social tolerance, and where failure was intensified by the concentration of decision-making in a single ruler. This analysis leads directly into the causes of rebellion.

Section 24.9

Rebellion and the Fall of Sui

The Tang tradition explained the fall of the Sui largely through the character of Emperor Yang, whose posthumous name, "Yang" (煬), carries the sense of a ruler who overturned proper order. Modern historians take seriously the emperor's role, especially his concentration of decision-making, his frequent absences from the capital, and his impatience with criticism, but they place his choices in a broader structure of pressures. The collapse was not a single event but a sequence in which several kinds of strain reinforced each other, and in which regional differences were important.

The first pressure was labor. Between 605 and 611 the court launched construction of the Eastern Capital at Luoyang, the Tongji, Yongji, and Jiangnan canals, palaces, roads, and the reconstruction of the Great Wall in the north, each requiring hundreds of thousands of workers and repeated summons in the same regions. The second was military. The Goguryeo campaigns of 612–614 drew soldiers, laborers, boatmen, and carters from the plains of Hebei, Shandong, and Henan, often the same households that had built the canals. The third was fiscal and environmental. Taxes and requisitions continued to rise, while natural disasters, including floods in Shandong and Hebei around 611 as reported in the dynastic history, damaged harvests and reduced the ability of households to meet demands. When farmers left fields or fled to avoid conscription, the tax base that supported the state shrank.

Rebellion began in the most burdened regions. In 611, Wang Bo, a local leader in the Shandong region, gathered followers in hills and marshes near Changbai Mountain, and similar groups formed across the eastern plain. Many rebel bands originated as groups of fugitives, unpaid soldiers, and desperate villagers, some led by local strongmen with military experience, and they fed on discontent with the levies. Larger leaders emerged: Zhai Rang and Li Mi at Wagang in Henan, who built a major force that threatened Luoyang and its granaries; Dou Jiande in Hebei; and others across the Yangtze. Some rebel leaders proclaimed kingdoms of their own. In 613 the crisis moved into the elite when Yang Xuangan, a member of the Guanlong aristocracy and the son of one of the regime's founders, rebelled while managing the army's grain supply. Although his revolt failed, it showed that the ruling class itself was fracturing.

Frontier pressure compounded the crisis. The Eastern Turks, whose khagan Shibi had prospered under Sui policies of dividing and managing them, grew more assertive as Sui power weakened. In 615, on a tour of the northern frontier, Emperor Yang was trapped by Turkic forces at Yanmen (in modern Shanxi) and relieved only after reinforcements arrived and, according to the traditional account, Princess Yicheng sent Shibi Khagan a false report of an emergency to the north, helping prompt him to lift the siege. The episode damaged imperial prestige and drove home the vulnerability of the northern defenses. As Sui garrisons were pulled to the northeast for Goguryeo and inland to fight rebels, Turkic influence expanded in the north, and some rebel leaders and Sui commanders sought Turkic support.

Court politics deteriorated. Trusted ministers such as Gao Jiong had been removed or executed, Yang Su had died in 606, and Emperor Yang increasingly relied on a smaller circle, some of whom, according to the dynastic history, concealed the extent of rebellion from him. In 616 he left the north and went to Jiangdu in the south, where he remained, apparently hoping to hold the Yangtze region and perhaps to relocate the capital, and never returned. His absence left the north to commanders who increasingly acted on their own. Sui officials and generals across the empire faced a choice between loyalty and survival, and many defected to the rebels or established their own bases.

Among these commanders was Li Yuan, the Duke of Tang, a member of the Guanlong aristocracy, a relative of the imperial family through marriage, and the Sui official in charge of Taiyuan in Shanxi. In 617 he rose in revolt, encouraged by his ambitious son Li Shimin and supported by contacts with Turkic leaders, marched on the capital, and took Daxingcheng in November, installing a child member of the Sui house as nominal emperor. In the spring of 618, at Jiangdu, Yuwen Huaji, a Sui officer whose troops were largely northerners eager to return home, led a mutiny and killed Emperor Yang. In the following months Li Yuan proclaimed the Tang dynasty, while other regimes in Luoyang, Hebei, and the Yangtze claimed the succession. The Tang's founding and its consolidation of these rivals are the subject of Chapter 25; here it is enough to note that the Sui did not fall to a single dynastic rival, but dissolved into a field of competing regional forces, from which one, based in the northwestern heartland, prevailed.

The section's central point is that Sui failure was systemic. A state that had built its power on registering households and summoning labor exhausted the very capacities on which it relied. It is therefore misleading to explain the fall as merely the result of a "tyrant" or as an instance of a mechanical "dynastic cycle." The Sui's rise and fall was a process involving fiscal design, geography, social differences between the core in Guanzhong and the burdened eastern plain, elite politics, environment, and frontier conflict. Section 24.10 draws these threads together.

Section 24.10

Chapter Review and Sui Dynasty Checkpoint

This chapter has examined how a northwestern regime, drawing on the institutions of the Northern Dynasties, conquered the south in 589 and built a reunified empire with a reorganized administration, a registered population, a system of land allocation and taxation, and a canal network linking the major river basins. It has also examined how the same concentration of resources enabled construction and military programs that, especially after 605, placed severe burdens on households and regions, contributing to the rebellions that ended the dynasty in 618.

The core insights are as follows. Reunification was contingent, dependent on the Northern Zhou's conquest of the Northern Qi, the succession crisis of 578–581, and Yang Jian's political skill. The Sui was a product of the northwestern Guanlong aristocracy and did not restore the Han. Its administrative reforms, including the reduction of local jurisdictions, central appointment of officials, new law codes, and standardization, were extensions of Northern Dynasty practice as well as innovations. The Grand Canal was a linked system of enlarged and new channels, not a single new creation. Regional integration was real, but its costs were unevenly distributed. And the Sui's short life belongs alongside its long influence: its capital, laws, fiscal system, canal network, and lessons about overmobilization shaped the Tang and later dynasties.

Context: The annals of Emperor Yang record, year by year, the orders that established the Eastern Capital and the canal system. They were composed by historians serving the dynasty that had replaced the Sui, and they were meant both to preserve a record and to provide a warning to later rulers.

In 605, shortly after taking the throne, the emperor ordered the building of the Eastern Capital at Luoyang and, in the same period, the digging of a canal drawing on more than a million men and women from the commanderies of Henan, linking the Luo and Yellow rivers to the Huai. Additional laborers were called up from the Huai region to reopen the channel across to the Yangtze, and the emperor then traveled south by boat in a large flotilla.

Analysis: The passage documents a real and major engineering program and shows how the state relied on household registers to summon labor by region. It also reflects the historians' perspective: the large numbers and the juxtaposition of the labor call with the imperial procession serve a moral argument about the ruler's priorities. The annals say little about the laborers' own experience, about wages or provisioning, about how the labor was organized on the ground, or about how local officials met their quotas. The account also does not tell us what the canal accomplished economically, since that emerges from later evidence. Historians combine this record with archaeology of the channels and with treatises on taxation to assess the project.

  2. Who created it, and how might the Tang court's purposes have shaped its emphasis?

Historical Debate

Historical Debate: Why Did the Sui Collapse So Quickly Despite Strong Institutions?

The question: The Sui governed a reunified country with a large registered population, substantial granary reserves, and a functioning administration, yet it collapsed within thirty-seven years. Was the cause primarily the policies of Emperor Yang, or the structure of the state and society he ruled?

Interpretation A — Overmobilization and imperial overreach: Some historians emphasize that the concentration of construction and military projects between 605 and 614 exceeded what households and local officials could sustain. On this view, the canals, capital building, and Goguryeo campaigns were each defensible in isolation, but their scale, speed, and overlap ruined the peasant economy of the eastern plain and drove people into rebellion. Emperor Yang's personal decisions and his lack of restraint were central, but the underlying mechanism was overtaxation of labor.
Interpretation B — Structural fragility, regional division, and elite politics: Other historians emphasize weaknesses built into the regime: a narrow ruling network centered on Guanzhong with limited roots among eastern and southern elites; an administrative system that relied on registered households but had thin local reach; vulnerability to floods and famine; frontier pressure from the Turks; and the fracturing of the northwestern aristocracy, as shown by Yang Xuangan's revolt and Li Yuan's rise. On this view, a sudden succession of crises exposed a state that had been assembled quickly and had not yet acquired deep legitimacy.

Evidence: Both views draw on the Sui Shu treatises, the pattern of rebellion in Shandong, Hebei, and Henan, and the record of elite defections. Interpretation A relies heavily on descriptions of levies and campaign numbers, which may be exaggerated. Interpretation B relies on reading the biographies of defecting officials and on comparisons with the Tang, which inherited many Sui institutions yet survived, suggesting that institutions alone did not doom the Sui. A third consideration is source bias: because Tang historians had a stake in blaming the last Sui ruler, all interpretations must allow for the possibility that personal blame was overemphasized.

Why it matters: The debate concerns how historians explain state failure. A purely personal explanation risks reproducing the moral narrative of the sources; a purely structural one risks underestimating the effects of specific decisions. Most modern accounts combine them.

Student task: Using evidence from Sections 24.5–24.9, evaluate which interpretation better explains the speed of the collapse, or explain what additional evidence, such as local records of labor levies or harvest data, would help decide between them.

Multiple Perspectives

Multiple Perspectives: The Sui Mobilization

Note: these are analytical perspectives synthesized from patterns in the sources, not quotations from named individuals.

Court officials of the northwestern aristocracy: For many, the regime offered rank, land, and a role in a reunified empire, and large projects were expressions of state capacity. Some, like Yang Xuangan, later concluded that the emperor's demands threatened their own positions and turned against him; others remained loyal until the end.
Southern landholders and literati: The conquest ended the political autonomy of the south and reduced Jiankang's role, but it also opened access to a larger market and to the court. Some southerners served the Sui, and southern scholars and monks gained patronage, while others resented northern officials and the registration of households that had previously been beyond the state's reach.
Peasant households on the eastern plain: These households faced repeated labor and military drafts, the loss of adult workers during the growing season, and, in some years, flood or famine. For them the canal and the campaigns meant lost harvests and, in many cases, flight, banditry, or joining rebel bands. Women and boys took on field work and, in extreme years, were themselves drafted.
Boatmen, merchants, and townspeople along the canals: Some found opportunities in the new traffic and markets, though they also faced requisitions for imperial travel and official transport.
Soldiers and their commanders: Troops from Guanzhong were sent far from home on campaigns with high casualties, and many became disaffected, as illustrated by the northern guards who killed Emperor Yang at Jiangdu. Commanders such as Li Yuan weighed loyalty to the dynasty against the survival of their forces.
The Goguryeo court and people: For Goguryeo, the Sui invasions were an existential threat, and the successful defense at Salsu became a defining memory. The kingdom's own society bore the costs of war on its territory.

These perspectives were not equally powerful. The court and aristocracy set policy; peasants and laborers bore the consequences with few means of redress other than flight and rebellion.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Continuity and Change Over Time: Institutions From the Northern Dynasties to Sui and Tang

What Changed

  • A single government again ruled north and south, with central appointment of local officials and fewer jurisdictions
  • Military men were placed under civil administration and registration in 590, weakening hereditary military status, though command structures persisted in Guanzhong and Hedong
  • Central government took a form (three departments, six ministries) that would develop into the Tang system
  • Canals connected river basins into an integrated transport network
  • Recruitment began moving away from aristocratic grading toward central selection

What Stayed the Same

  • The equal-field system, household registration, and grain, cloth, and labor obligations inherited from the Northern Wei, Northern Qi, and Northern Zhou
  • Dominance of the northwestern aristocracy, closely tied by marriage and service
  • Reliance on local clerks and elites for actual administration
  • Buddhism as a landholding institution and source of imperial legitimacy, alongside Confucian and Daoist traditions
  • Regional differences between north and south in dialect, economy, and political memory

Who experienced what: Officials and aristocrats experienced continuity of status but changes in how appointments were made; southern elites experienced political loss; peasants in the eastern plain experienced sharp change in the form of heavier obligations. Why the pattern matters: The Sui shows that an empire's institutions can be largely inherited even when its political form is new, and that the character of a regime can change dramatically within a reign without any change in formal institutions.

Cause and Effect

Cause and Effect: The Collapse of the Sui

Long-term causes

A regime assembled rapidly from the institutions of the Northern Dynasties; a ruling network narrowly based in Guanzhong; a fiscal system tied to household registers and labor service; unresolved north–south and east–west differences; and a strategic environment shaped by the Turks and Goguryeo.

Short-term causes

Simultaneous programs of capital building, canal construction, and Goguryeo campaigns between 605 and 614; floods and famine in the eastern plain; Emperor Yang's long absences, isolation from advice, and departure for Jiangdu in 616; the Turkic threat.

The transformation

From 611 rebellions spread across Shandong, Hebei, Henan, and the Yangtze; the elite fractured (Yang Xuangan, 613); Emperor Yang was killed in 618; commanders and rebel leaders set up rival regimes, one of which, the Tang, prevailed.

Effects

The end of the Sui dynasty; a period of civil war (Chapter 25); the survival of Sui institutions under the Tang; and a Tang political vocabulary that treated the Sui as a warning against overextension and against neglecting the material welfare of the population.

Historical complexity: The collapse cannot be reduced to a tyrant's personality, to "corruption," or to a mechanical dynastic cycle. Fiscal design, geography, environment, elite politics, frontier relations, and specific decisions interacted, and different regions experienced the crisis at different times and in different ways.

Everyday Life and Social History

Living Under the Sui State

For a peasant household on the North China Plain in the 580s and 590s, the Sui state brought both relief and new obligations. Registration under the equal-field system meant a claim to land and a predictable set of taxes, and the reduction of labor service to twenty days a year and the establishment of granaries offered some protection from arbitrary demands and famine. A household typically consisted of a married couple, children, and sometimes elderly relatives or a married son's family, farming grain fields, growing mulberry trees, and weaving cloth, with women's textile work supplying the cloth tax as well as household needs. Households near Buddhist monasteries might attend festivals, receive charity, or lease land from the monastery.

After 605 daily life changed sharply for many. Men were called away for canal work or military service, often for months, and travel to the work site and back at their own expense was itself a burden. In some regions women and older boys were drawn into the work, and fields went untended. In the wake of floods and poor harvests, families sold assets, borrowed, or migrated. Escape, often into remote hills or marshes, meant the loss of legal status but might offer safety, and the bands that formed in these refuges became the core of rebellion. Along the canals, meanwhile, boatmen, porters, innkeepers, and local officials adapted to the new traffic, with some prospering from it.

Urban life in the two capitals reflected the empire's new scale. Daxingcheng and Luoyang held officials, soldiers, merchants, foreign envoys and traders, and Buddhist clergy, with markets under state supervision. Elite households in these cities lived in walled compounds, and their tombs and epitaphs reflect a culture of ritual and Buddhist piety. Historians must be careful not to treat the metropolitan elite as typical: for most people, the state appeared as the county official, the register, the tax collector, and the recruiter.

Regional and Frontier Context

Regions Within the Sui Empire

Guanzhong and the northwest were the political and military core, where the Sui's ruling network lived and where capital building and granaries were concentrated. The North China Plain (Henan, Hebei, Shandong) was the most populous and burdened region, the center of canal labor, conscription for Goguryeo, and the earliest and most intense rebellions. The Huai region was the corridor connecting north and south and a source of labor. The lower and middle Yangtze provided grain, commercial wealth, and cultural prestige, and experienced the 589 conquest, the 590–591 revolts, and Yang Guang's early governorship. Lingnan and the southeast coast were incorporated partly through local leaders such as Lady Xian. Sichuan was connected through the upper Yangtze and less affected by the eastern crisis.

Frontier zones were arenas of interaction, not merely of central-government control. Turkic khagans negotiated with the Sui and later played a role in its fall; the Tuyuhun were defeated and their lands partly occupied in 608–609, and garrisons and trade were established in the Gansu corridor; Goguryeo defended its own political order against invasion; the Khitan and other northeastern peoples were involved in the campaigns. Each had its own aims and made choices that shaped Sui policy, and frontier peoples should not be viewed solely through the perspective of the court.

China and the Wider World

The Sui in Eurasia and East Asia

The Sui was an active participant in a wider political world. It managed relations with the Turkic khaganates through diplomacy, marriage, and force; sent Pei Ju to organize contacts with Central Asian states at Zhangye in 609; sent an expedition against the kingdom of Linyi (Champa) in Southeast Asia in 605; and received embassies from Japan. In 607 a Japanese mission, sent by the court of Empress Suiko under the regent Prince Shotoku, delivered a letter that used the language of equality between rulers, which reportedly displeased Emperor Yang, but the Sui nevertheless sent an envoy in return, and subsequent Japanese missions studied Buddhism, law, and administration, prefiguring the larger Tang-era exchanges (Chapter 27). Korean states, Goguryeo, Baekje, and Silla, all sought to use Sui and Turkic power to their own ends; Goguryeo's resistance shaped the strategic geography of Northeast Asia for the following decades, and the Tang's later alliance with Silla would ultimately reshape the peninsula.

Buddhism linked the Sui to a Eurasian religious network, with monks traveling to and from India and Central Asia and Buddhist relics distributed throughout the empire. These were active choices by Chinese rulers, not developments imposed from outside. The Sui's brief history thus already displays the features of the Tang's international position: a center with links to steppe, oasis, Korean, Japanese, and Southeast Asian societies, each contributing to and affecting the empire.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that the Sui dynasty reunified a political world that had experienced nearly three centuries of sustained north–south division after the Western Jin, by drawing on institutions developed under the Northern Dynasties, conquering the south, rebuilding administration, reorganizing taxation and landholding, and mobilizing labor for transport and military projects. The same concentration of resources and ambition placed severe burdens on households and regions and contributed to rebellion and the dynasty's rapid fall.

The chapter began with the conditions of reunification. Northern Zhou's conquest of Northern Qi in 577 gave one state control of the whole north, but its ruling house was destabilized by succession crises, and Yang Jian, a leading member of the northwestern military aristocracy and father-in-law of a Zhou emperor, took power in 580–581. Reunification was contingent, not inevitable, and it emerged from a specific political world rather than from a general logic of Chinese history.

Emperor Wen's regime rested on the Guanlong aristocracy, whose members were linked by marriage and service, and on a combination of legitimating strategies: abdication rituals, a new capital at Daxingcheng, Buddhist patronage, Confucian ritual reform, and skilled diplomacy with the Turks. Empress Dugu was a significant political actor. The regime's early years brought the absorption of Later Liang and preparation for the southern campaign.

The conquest of the Chen in 588–589 ended the division between north and south, but integration required more than victory. Southern revolts in 590–591 showed resistance to northern officials and household registration, and the Sui relied on negotiation with local leaders such as Lady Xian, on patronage of southern culture and Buddhism, and on the destruction of Jiankang's status as capital. Reunification was a process, not an instant erasure of regional differences.

Administrative reconstruction and fiscal policy formed the institutional core. The Sui reduced jurisdictions, centralized appointment, issued new law codes, standardized coinage, and moved toward central selection of officials. It inherited the equal-field system and adapted it through registration drives, tax classification, the placing of military households under civil registration, and the building of granaries. These achievements, many of which were foundations of Tang government, gave the state exceptional ability to count, tax, and mobilize its population.

The Grand Canal exemplifies both achievement and cost. It was not a single new creation but a linked system of enlarged, repaired, and new channels, built between 584 and 610, that connected the Wei, Yellow, Huai, Yangtze, and lower Yangtze regions and northeastern supply routes. It allowed the state to draw on southern productivity and shaped the economic geography of later dynasties, but its construction depended on massive levies of labor from the plains of Henan, the Huai, and Hebei.

The Goguryeo campaigns and the rebellions that followed showed the limits of that capacity. Repeated mobilization overlapped with canal work, flood and famine in the eastern plain, frontier pressure from the Turks, and the isolation of the emperor. Rebellion began among the most burdened populations, spread through the elite, and left the empire divided among commanders and rebel leaders. The collapse reflected fiscal design, geography, environment, elite politics, and specific decisions, not merely the character of a single ruler or a mechanical cycle.

The Sui belongs in the larger history of Chinese civilization as a brief but pivotal empire. Its capital, legal codes, fiscal arrangements, and transport network survived into the Tang and beyond, while its collapse became a reference point for later rulers and historians. Students moving to Chapter 25 should understand that the Tang did not begin from nothing: it emerged from the same northwestern elite, inherited Sui structures, and defined its own governing style partly in response to the Sui's failure.

Chapter Reflection

The Sui matters because it shows how a divided political world could be reunified through the institutions of one of its parts, and how quickly such a reunification could be endangered by its own success. It did not restore an earlier empire. It built a new one from the northern regimes' registers, military organization, and legal practice, from the productivity of the Yangtze region, and from the ambitions of a small aristocratic network.

The Sui shaped later Chinese history by supplying much of the institutional and physical framework of the Tang: a capital plan, a central government structure, legal codes, a land and tax system, and a canal network. It also supplied a warning that early Tang rulers and historians turned into a political language about restraint. The Sui's fall, and the Tang's use of its lessons, illustrate a larger theme of this textbook: political power in China was repeatedly constructed, contested, and reconstructed through concrete fiscal, military, and social arrangements, not through a simple recurring cycle.

The chapter also connects China to a wider Asian world. Turkic khaganates, Goguryeo, the Tuyuhun, Central Asian oasis states, Champa, and Japan were active participants whose choices shaped Sui policy. The Sui was one power among several in Northeast Asia and Inner Asia, and its relations with them were negotiated, contested, and reciprocal.

Understanding the Sui helps students see Chinese history as diverse, interconnected, contested, and regionally varied. Reunification integrated regions without erasing their differences; achievements in engineering and administration came at real human cost; and the same state that built a lasting canal network collapsed within a generation. As students move to Chapter 25, they should carry with them the recognition that the Tang was both a new dynasty and an heir, and that the story of imperial China is one of unfinished construction as much as continuity.
