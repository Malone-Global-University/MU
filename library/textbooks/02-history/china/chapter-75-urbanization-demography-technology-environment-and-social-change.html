Urbanization, Demography, Technology, Environment, and Social Change

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

In 1990, most Chinese people lived in the countryside, few households owned a telephone, and a university education was out of reach for nearly everyone. By the mid-2020s, a majority lived in cities, most adults carried a smartphone, and the country was beginning to count fewer people each year than the year before. This chapter asks what those changes felt like, and who experienced them how.

Imagine a young woman from a village in inland Henan who, in the late 1990s, boards a long-distance bus to a factory town on the Pearl River Delta. She carries a temporary residence permit, a few clothes, and the telephone number of a cousin. Twenty-five years later, her daughter studies at a provincial university, pays for a train ticket with a phone, and worries less about whether she can leave the village than about whether she can afford an apartment and find a stable job. Between these two lives lie the transformations this chapter examines: urbanization, the institutions that sorted people into different categories of citizenship, a sharp fall in fertility, an expansion of schooling, a digital revolution, severe environmental stress, and the rebuilding of health and welfare systems.

Chapter 74 explained how China's economy grew: how enterprises were restructured, how exports and investment expanded, and how the country integrated into global production. This chapter turns from the mechanisms of growth to its consequences for households and communities. Its central subjects are not ministries or firms but migrants, women, children, students, teachers, workers, older people, patients, consumers, and the institutions that governed their access to housing, schools, hospitals, and pensions. The governing question is how rapid urbanization, demographic transition, educational expansion, and digital and environmental change reshaped everyday life, and why those changes brought new opportunities alongside persistent inequalities and institutional constraints.

What changed was extraordinary in scale. By the end of 2025, according to China's National Bureau of Statistics, the officially reported urban resident share of the population stood at 67.89 percent, and about 23 percent of the population was aged sixty or above. Roughly 301 million people were counted as migrant workers under the Bureau's definition, and the China Internet Network Information Center reported about 1.125 billion internet users. These figures are best read as indicators of scale, not as simple descriptions of lived experience: each depends on a definition, a year, and a method of counting, and each conceals large differences among regions, generations, and social groups.

What continued is equally important. The household registration system (hukou) continued to shape access to services even as people moved. Family obligations, especially between generations, remained central to how people cared for children and aging parents, even as families grew smaller and more dispersed. Regional inequalities between coastal and inland areas, and between cities and villages, persisted despite enormous investment. And the state continued to play a dominant role in setting the framework within which families and markets acted, from fertility policy to digital regulation to carbon targets.

The evidence for these developments is unusually varied. Historians and social scientists draw on population censuses (conducted in 1990, 2000, 2010, and 2020), the annual statistical communiqués and yearbooks of the National Bureau of Statistics, household and migrant surveys, laws and State Council documents, reports by UNESCO, the World Health Organization, and the World Bank, environmental monitoring data, corporate and platform records, journalism, ethnographic fieldwork, and oral testimony. Each has limits. Official statistics may change definitions between censuses; surveys capture only a sample; environmental and health data are shaped by what is measured and published; and for events within living memory, interpretation remains actively contested.

The chapter also connects China to wider histories. Urbanization and demographic aging occurred in many countries, though rarely so quickly; mobile payments, platform labor, and e-commerce raised questions that societies elsewhere also confronted; and China's choices about energy and climate affect the global environment. At the same time, the chapter avoids treating Chinese experience as either exceptional or merely a variation on a universal model of modernization. It is a history of a diverse society, and its aim is to give students the tools to ask who benefited, who bore the costs, and what evidence supports each answer.

Historical Context

The social transformations of 1990–2025 grew out of institutional arrangements built in the Mao era and partly dismantled in the reform era. In the 1950s, the state introduced a household registration system that classified people by place of residence and as agricultural or non-agricultural, restricting movement from countryside to city and tying access to rations, housing, and employment to registration status. Urban workers received welfare through work units (danwei), including housing, medical care, and pensions; rural residents relied on collectives, with far lower levels of provision (Chapters 67 and 68). The result was a sharply divided society in which the rural-urban boundary was a legal and economic boundary, not only a geographic one.

The reforms of the 1980s (Chapter 72) loosened parts of this system. The household responsibility system raised agricultural productivity and released rural labor; township and village enterprises and the Special Economic Zones created new demand for workers; and restrictions on movement were gradually relaxed so that people could seek work away from home, though without gaining full urban registration. Chapter 74 described how the 1990s accelerated market reform, restructured state-owned enterprises, and tied the economy to global supply chains. By the early 1990s, therefore, the conditions for mass migration, commercialized housing, and expanding private consumption were in place, while the older registration categories remained largely intact.

Geography shaped the pattern. The coastal provinces and major river deltas, the Yangtze River Delta around Shanghai and the Pearl River Delta around Guangzhou and Shenzhen, attracted export manufacturing and migrant labor; inland provinces such as Henan, Sichuan, Hunan, Anhui, and Guizhou supplied many migrants; the northwest faced water scarcity and fragile ecosystems; and frontier regions had distinct linguistic, religious, and economic profiles. Chapter 75 therefore treats "China" not as a uniform national experience but as a set of regionally varied histories linked by common institutions and policies. Questions of governance and territory in Tibet, Xinjiang, Hong Kong, and Taiwan are reserved for Chapter 76.

The demographic starting point was also distinctive. Mortality had fallen sharply in the decades after 1949, and fertility began to decline substantially in the 1970s under the "later, longer, fewer" campaign, before the strict one-child framework of 1980. The population was therefore young and growing in 1990, with a large cohort entering working age, a condition often described as a demographic dividend. That dividend, and its eventual reversal, forms the demographic backbone of this chapter. Students should keep in mind that "China" in this chapter means the People's Republic of China as an administrative unit with its own statistical conventions; figures usually refer to mainland China and typically exclude Hong Kong, Macao, and Taiwan unless stated otherwise.

Urbanization and the Transformation of China's Cities

How urbanization reorganized China's geography, housing, social relationships, and daily routines, and why residence in a city did not guarantee equal rights within it.

Section 75.1

From a Predominantly Rural Society to an Urban Nation

At the start of the reform era, roughly four in five Chinese lived in the countryside. By the early 2010s, the officially reported urban share crossed 50 percent, and by the end of 2025 it stood at 67.89 percent. In demographic terms, few societies have moved so many people into cities so quickly: hundreds of millions of people changed where they lived and how they earned a living within a single lifetime. The transformation was driven by factory employment, service-sector growth, the expansion of existing cities, the creation of new ones, and the reclassification of rural areas as urban.

That last point requires care. The urban population is a statistical category, and its definition has changed. China's statistical authorities count as urban residents those who have lived in urban areas for a given period (generally six months), regardless of registration. Boundaries of cities and towns were also redrawn, so that some of the recorded growth reflects the reclassification of villages and counties as urban districts rather than the movement of people alone. Comparisons across the 1990, 2000, 2010, and 2020 censuses must therefore take into account changing definitions and administrative boundaries.

The more consequential distinction is between residence and registration. A person could live and work in a city for years and still hold a rural hukou or a registration from another locality, which limited access to local schools, subsidized housing, and some social benefits (Section 75.6). The share of the population holding urban household registration has been lower than the share living in urban areas, and the gap, in the order of tens of percentage points in the mid-2010s, is a measure of the social distance between "living in the city" and "belonging to it."

Urbanization also took different forms. Some people moved to large coastal cities; many moved to provincial capitals and county seats closer to home; others stayed in place while their villages were absorbed into expanding towns. Historians of the period therefore distinguish between migration, in-place urbanization, and administrative reclassification, each of which has different social meanings.

The speed of the change is clearer when set against earlier decades. In 1978 only around one in six Chinese lived in cities, and during the Mao era the state had often tried to hold urban populations down, sending urban youth to the countryside and tying grain rations to registration (Chapters 67–70). The 2000 census recorded roughly a third of the population as urban, and the share passed one-half around 2011. The main drivers differed over time. In the 1980s, township and village enterprises and small towns absorbed rural labor; in the 1990s and 2000s, export factories and construction drew workers to large cities and coastal zones; in the 2010s, county seats and inland provincial capitals grew as industries relocated and as governments built new districts, universities, and transport hubs.

Urbanization was also a project of the state. Planners designated cities for particular roles, converted counties into municipalities, and set national plans for urban systems; local officials, whose careers depended partly on measurable growth, competed to expand their urban footprints. This means that urbanization reflected policy choices as well as household decisions, and that some new urban districts filled slowly or remained partly vacant. Analysts therefore ask not only how many people lived in cities but what kinds of cities they were: whether they offered stable jobs, whether residents had equal rights, and whether services kept pace.

Seen internationally, China's urban transition resembles earlier ones in Europe, the Americas, and Asia in its link to industrialization, but differs in its pace, its institutional restrictions on settlement, and its scale. In regional terms, it was never uniform: the coast urbanized earlier and more densely than the interior, and the western regions and frontier areas followed their own trajectories, with distinct settlement patterns among minority communities. Treating the national urban share as a single story would miss these differences.

Section 75.2

Megacities, Metropolitan Regions, and Urban Networks

China's urban landscape evolved into a hierarchy with several layers. At the top were national and global cities: Beijing, the political and cultural capital; Shanghai, the financial and commercial center; Guangzhou, the historic gateway of the south; and Shenzhen, which grew from a small border town in 1980 into a major technology and manufacturing city. Chongqing, a municipality administratively directly under the central government, serves as a large inland hub in the upper Yangtze region. Below these stood provincial capitals such as Chengdu, Wuhan, Xi'an, and Zhengzhou, then prefecture-level cities, county seats, and market towns.

Cities increasingly formed regional systems. The Yangtze River Delta linked Shanghai with Suzhou, Hangzhou, Nanjing, and Ningbo, building on a long history of commerce and handicraft production in Jiangnan (Chapters 32 and 43). The Pearl River Delta connected Guangzhou, Shenzhen, Dongguan, and Hong Kong and Macao across different administrative systems. The Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei region combined the capital with a port city and a surrounding industrial province, and its planning has been marked by efforts to relocate some functions away from Beijing. Dense high-speed rail and expressway networks tied these regions together and encouraged daily commuting across city boundaries.

Regional contrasts remained pronounced. Coastal cities generally had higher average incomes, greater access to foreign investment, and larger service sectors; interior cities developed more slowly in the 1990s and 2000s but grew rapidly afterward, as manufacturing and logistics moved inland and as governments invested in infrastructure. Smaller industrial and mining cities, especially in the northeast, faced decline as older industries contracted and populations moved elsewhere. Urbanization was thus uneven: some cities expanded, while others lost population.

Official planning increasingly described cities in terms of clusters. The central government designated the Greater Bay Area (linking Guangdong's Pearl River Delta cities with Hong Kong and Macao, in a plan issued in 2019), the Yangtze River Delta, and Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei as national priorities, and in 2017 announced the creation of Xiong'an New Area in Hebei to relieve pressure on Beijing. Pudong, across the river from Shanghai's old center, was opened for development in 1990 and became a symbol of the new financial city, while Shenzhen's growth from a special economic zone created in 1980 into a metropolis of well over ten million people became an emblem of reform-era urban history (Chapter 72).

Behind these showcase regions lay a more complex hierarchy. Chinese commentators informally rank cities in tiers, based on size, wealth, and commercial importance, while the state administers them through a system in which provincial capitals, sub-provincial cities, prefecture-level cities, and county-level cities hold different powers and budgets. Administrative rank affects access to investment and to resources such as hospitals and universities. A city's position in the hierarchy therefore shaped the opportunities of its residents, quite apart from their individual circumstances.

The urban map was not one of uninterrupted growth. Large parts of the northeast, with its older heavy industry and cold climate, lost population as state enterprises were restructured, and some resource-based cities declined as mines closed. Meanwhile, new inland metropolitan centers such as Chengdu–Chongqing, Wuhan, Zhengzhou, and Xi'an attracted both migrants and returning migrants. Scholars debate whether the largest cities should grow further or whether policy should favor medium-sized cities, a debate linked to hukou rules (Section 75.10).

Section 75.3

Housing, Neighborhoods, and the High-Rise City

Before the late 1990s, most urban residents obtained housing from their work units, at low rents and in modest quantities. The end of welfare housing allocation in 1998, together with mortgage lending and the sale of publicly owned apartments to occupants at favorable prices, created a housing market. Households that already held work-unit housing frequently became owners and, as prices rose, accumulated considerable wealth; those who entered the market later, including young adults and migrants, faced high prices relative to income.

The residential landscape changed. Low-rise courtyard houses, factory dormitories, and work-unit compounds were replaced by high-rise towers, often in gated communities with property-management companies and homeowners' associations. These arrangements gave many residents better sanitation, space, and amenities but changed neighborhood relations: where earlier residential units had tied neighbors together through shared workplaces, new complexes mixed strangers, and community life often shifted to online groups and management disputes.

Urban villages, former farming villages enveloped by expanding cities, offered a different form of housing. Village collectives and households built dense rental buildings that supplied inexpensive accommodation for migrants. They provided affordable access to the city but often lacked planning, services, and legal clarity, and became targets of redevelopment. Housing was thus a central mechanism of inequality: ownership conferred wealth, security, and, in some places, access to better schools, while renting limited both.

Housing policy had its roots in the 1980s and 1990s, when pilot sales of public housing began and urban rent reforms started, but the decisive moment came in 1998, when a State Council notice ended the system of physical allocation by work units and promoted commercial housing. Banks began to offer mortgages, and developers built at large scale. For households that had received work-unit apartments, privatization transferred substantial assets into private hands; for later entrants, buying meant large debt, often supported by parents and grandparents pooling savings. As a result, homeownership became very common by international standards, but wealth gaps between owners and non-owners, and between cities with rising and with stagnant prices, widened.

Housing prices became a central social issue. Policy shifted repeatedly between encouraging construction and restraining speculation: local governments introduced purchase restrictions in major cities; the Central Economic Work Conference of 2016 declared that housing was for living in, not for speculation; and in 2020 regulators imposed limits on developers' borrowing. Financial difficulties among large developers from 2021 and a downturn in the property market affected buyers who had paid for apartments not yet completed, as well as local government finances, suppliers, and construction workers, showing how deeply property had become embedded in household and public finances.

School and housing choices were tied together through catchment-area rules, so that apartments near well-regarded public schools commanded premiums. Public rental housing, shantytown redevelopment programs, and affordable-housing schemes aimed to broaden access, but they reached only part of those in need, and migrants without local registration were often excluded. The high-rise city thus offered comfort and investment opportunities to some, while others experienced it as a source of debt, insecurity, or exclusion.

Section 75.4

Urban Expansion, Land Conversion, and Displacement

Cities grew outward, and the land they needed was largely agricultural. Under the legal framework in force, urban land is state-owned, while rural land is collectively owned by village communities. Local governments could acquire collectively owned land, convert it to urban use, and lease use rights to developers; the revenue from these leases became a major source of local government income, particularly after the tax-sharing reforms of 1994 (Chapter 74) shifted a large share of tax revenue to the center while leaving local governments with extensive spending responsibilities.

The consequences were mixed. Land conversion financed roads, subways, industrial parks, and housing, and many villagers received compensation, apartments, or shares in collectively owned enterprises, and some gained substantial wealth. But compensation levels varied, and disputes were widespread. Residents complained of inadequate payment, coercive demolition, and lack of consultation; in some areas, protests, petitions, and violence occurred. Farmland loss also raised concerns about food security, leading central authorities to emphasize protection of a minimum area of arable land.

Relocated villagers faced difficult adjustments. Moving from a village house with a garden to an apartment tower could change livelihoods, social networks, and cost of living. Some became rentiers, collecting rent from migrant tenants; others, especially older farmers, lost their occupation. Historians use court records, petitions, government regulations, and ethnographic studies to examine these disputes, recognizing that sources reflect the perspectives of officials, developers, journalists, and aggrieved residents differently.

Legal and fiscal arrangements explain why land conversion proceeded so quickly. The Land Administration Law of 1998 strengthened state control over the conversion of farmland, and central policy sought to protect a minimum area of arable land, with a figure of 1.8 billion mu (about 120 million hectares) announced in 2006 as a limit that should not be breached. Yet local governments continued to find ways to expand urban construction land, and revenue from leasing it, together with borrowing against land through local government financing vehicles, supported public investment. When land prices fell after 2021, the vulnerability of this model became apparent.

The treatment of residents changed over time. Demolition and relocation disputes in the 2000s, including some that ended in suicides and violent confrontations, drew national attention and public criticism, and in 2011 new regulations on the expropriation of buildings on state-owned land replaced earlier rules, prohibiting violent eviction and requiring fair compensation, a public-interest purpose, and procedures for consultation. A 2019 revision of the Land Administration Law allowed some collectively owned construction land to be sold directly into the market, in principle giving village collectives more of the gains.

Experiences differed. Villagers in prosperous suburbs sometimes obtained multiple apartments and became relatively wealthy, while those in poorer or remote areas received less, and farmers who lost land but had little other work faced uncertain livelihoods. Women could be disadvantaged where village rules tied compensation to male household heads or to local membership, a subject of litigation and activism. These cases show how land conversion redistributed wealth and power, and why historians seek local records alongside official summaries.

Section 75.5

Public Transportation, Infrastructure, and Urban Everyday Life

Infrastructure changed the rhythm of daily life. Metro systems, which existed in only a few Chinese cities in the 1990s, were built in dozens of cities in the following decades, and China developed the world's largest urban rail network. High-speed rail, introduced in the late 2000s, shortened intercity travel from many hours to a few, making it possible to commute between neighboring cities or return home for festivals more easily. Expressways, airports, bridges, and ports expanded in parallel, and utilities such as piped water, electricity, and sewage treatment reached more households.

The benefits were substantial but unevenly experienced. Commuters in central districts enjoyed frequent service, while residents of peripheral districts and urban villages spent long hours traveling. Private car ownership expanded rapidly, bringing mobility and congestion, parking shortages, and air pollution. Cities built parks, plazas, and waterfront promenades, but access to quality public space varied. Elderly residents, people with disabilities, and migrants often experienced barriers: stairs without elevators, digital ticketing, and unfamiliar procedures.

These everyday arrangements show that "modernization" was not a single experience. A white-collar worker in Shanghai, a delivery rider in Guangzhou, and a retired resident in a county town encountered the same infrastructure with different constraints.

The first Chinese subway opened in Beijing in 1969, but for decades networks stayed small. From the 2000s, central approval and local financing led to rapid construction in dozens of cities, and by the 2020s China's metro systems were the longest in the world. High-speed rail began with the Beijing–Tianjin line in 2008 and grew into a national grid of tens of thousands of kilometers, changing the geography of business trips, tourism, family visits, and student travel. The railway was also a political and financial project, involving large debts and, at times, disputes about the safety and efficiency of rapid construction, such as the 2011 Wenzhou collision that fueled criticism on social media.

Everyday mobility changed in other ways: bicycles, once ubiquitous, gave way in many cities to cars and electric scooters, then returned in the form of shared bicycles introduced around 2016; bus rapid transit, ride-hailing, and digital navigation reshaped commuting. Car ownership rose from a rarity to a mass phenomenon in the 2000s and 2010s, with consequences for congestion, parking, and pollution, and for city planning, as some cities restricted purchases through license-plate lotteries and auctions.

Municipal services expanded: piped water, sewerage, natural gas, district heating in the north, and waste collection reached more neighborhoods, while garbage-sorting rules and city-beautification campaigns changed daily habits and the visibility of informal street vending. Enforcement of urban order (the work of urban management officers, or chengguan) produced friction with street vendors, who were often migrants. Public space, accessibility, and the quality of services depended heavily on a district's wealth and administrative status, and older people, people with disabilities, and migrants often described barriers that statistics on infrastructure did not capture.

Division II · Sections 75.6–75.11

Migration, Hukou, and Urban Inequality

Why China's urbanization cannot be understood through population figures alone: the legal and social position of migrants shaped who benefited fully from urban development.

Section 75.6

The Household Registration System and the Rural-Urban Divide

The household registration system has roots in older Chinese practices of registering households for taxation, labor, and security (Chapters 15 and 26). Its modern form took shape in the 1950s. Regulations adopted in 1958 required residents to register at their place of residence, distinguished between agricultural and non-agricultural status, and required permission for moving between rural and urban areas. In the Mao era, this system supported planned industrialization by limiting the growth of urban populations that the state was committed to supplying with grain and jobs, and it created a durable institutional boundary between city and countryside.

In the reform era, the system was relaxed but not abolished. From the 1980s, people could live and work away from their place of registration, but access to many public goods remained tied to local registration: enrollment in public schools, eligibility for certain social benefits, the right to buy property or a car in some cities, and sometimes the right to sit for entrance examinations locally. Registration also determined entitlements in the home village, such as use of collectively owned land. Many rural migrants hesitated to give up rural registration because it protected their land rights.

Understanding hukou requires attention to its changing purposes. Imperial and Republican governments used household registration for taxation, conscription, and social control, and the Communist state built on this tradition. After 1958, registration became a tool for allocating resources in a planned economy: urban hukou holders received subsidized grain, housing, and jobs, while rural residents were expected to remain in collectives, producing food and surplus for industrialization. During the famine that followed the Great Leap Forward, restrictions on movement were enforced strictly, and people who left villages could not obtain rations (Chapter 68). The result was a lasting hierarchy of status inscribed in documents.

The reform era changed the meaning without erasing the institution. Temporary residence permits introduced in the mid-1980s allowed rural workers to live in cities, yet made their stay conditional; local authorities used the permits for control as well as service. A custody-and-repatriation system allowed police to detain and return migrants lacking papers until 2003, when the death in custody of a young migrant, Sun Zhigang, in Guangzhou prompted public outcry and the system's abolition. In 1998, children were allowed to take the registration of either parent, ending a rule that had tied them to the mother's status.

The persistent question is why registration mattered after the planned economy ended. Part of the answer is fiscal: local governments finance schools, hospitals, and social programs largely from local resources, so expanding membership increases costs. Part is political: established urban residents often opposed sharing scarce resources. And part is that registration gave rural residents claims on land and village benefits, making it rational for some migrants to keep their rural status. The institution thus persisted because it served the interests, and reflected the constraints, of several groups at once.

Historical Vocabulary
Hukou, Residence, and Urban Registration

Hukou (户口) is the household registration system. A person's hukou records their registered place (locality) and has historically recorded an agricultural or non-agricultural classification. Residence refers to where a person actually lives, usually for six months or more in statistical definitions. A person may be an urban resident without holding an urban hukou. The distinction matters because it separates statistical urbanization from social inclusion. Later reforms removed the formal agricultural/non-agricultural labels in many places and introduced residence permits, but local rules continued to differ.

Historians debate how rigid the system was and how much it has changed, but agree that registration, together with local fiscal arrangements, helped sustain an unequal distribution of public goods between residents and migrants.

Section 75.7

Rural-to-Urban Migration and the Formation of Migrant Networks

From the early 1990s, tens of millions of rural residents moved to coastal factories, construction sites, and urban services each year. Migration followed networks of kinship and native-place ties: workers learned of jobs from relatives, traveled together, and often clustered in particular industries or neighborhoods according to place of origin. Labor recruiters, labor-dispatch agencies, and later online platforms also mediated migration. Many migrants intended to return home after saving money, a pattern often described as circular migration, and many did return, bringing savings and skills that supported new businesses or house construction.

Patterns changed over time. In the 1990s and 2000s, long-distance migration to coastal export zones dominated. In later years, more migrants worked in their home provinces as inland cities grew, and the average age of migrant workers increased as the first generation aged. A second generation, born in the 1980s and later, often had more schooling, less farming experience, and weaker attachments to the village, and was more likely to want to remain in cities.

The statistical category requires careful reading. The National Bureau of Statistics reported approximately 301 million migrant workers in 2025, under a definition that includes both workers employed away from their home townships and workers engaged in nonagricultural work locally. That total is not the same as the number of people currently living outside their home regions.

Historians and sociologists have described migration as a collective, network-based process. Migrants from the same county or village frequently worked in the same factory or building trade, shared housing, and relied on one another for information, loans, and protection. Native-place associations and informal enclaves, such as the migrant communities of Beijing's outskirts or the neighborhoods of Wenzhou entrepreneurs, echo earlier forms of sojourning in Chinese history, though under different legal conditions (Chapters 41, 49, 63).

The motives for migrating were mixed. Rural incomes from farming were low, and remittances paid for housing, weddings, schooling, and medical bills. Women migrants sometimes sought independence from family constraints as well as income. Migration also reshaped villages: houses were rebuilt with remittances, farmland was rented out or left fallow, and older people and children became the main residents of many villages. At the same time, returning migrants started small businesses, and local governments in inland provinces promoted "return home to start a business" policies.

Counting migrants raises measurement issues. The 2020 census reported approximately 375.82 million people in its defined “floating population,” excluding the additional approximately 116.94 million people whose residence and registration differed within the same municipal district. The combined population with residence separated from registration was approximately 492.76 million. Neither category is equivalent to the narrower employment-based NBS migrant-worker measure. Students should compare definitions before comparing numbers.

Section 75.8

Migrant Labor, Informal Employment, and Urban Precarity

Migrant workers labored in factories, construction, domestic service, retail, restaurants, cleaning, logistics, and, increasingly, delivery and ride-hailing. Working hours were often long, wages were sometimes delayed, and written contracts and social insurance coverage were frequently absent. Construction workers, in particular, faced wage arrears, a problem that prompted regulatory campaigns and enforcement efforts. Occupational hazards, from industrial accidents to respiratory disease, affected workers in mining, manufacturing, and construction.

Experiences differed by sector, gender, age, and region. Young women were prominent in electronics assembly and garment factories, where dormitory regimes and production quotas structured their lives; men predominated in construction; and domestic service, care work, and retail employed women of varied ages. Workers' strategies included changing jobs frequently, relying on kin networks, and, in some cases, collective action or petitions. Labor law reforms, notably the Labor Contract Law of 2008, strengthened formal protections, but enforcement varied and informal employment remained substantial.

The conditions of migrant work changed over time. In the 1990s, many factories relied on dormitory labor regimes with long overtime and limited oversight; in 2010, a series of suicides among workers at a large electronics contract manufacturer drew international attention to working conditions and prompted wage increases and some changes in management. After the global financial crisis of 2008, millions of migrant workers lost jobs and returned home, and in the following decade labor shortages in some regions and rising wages led scholars to debate whether China had reached a "turning point" at which surplus rural labor was exhausted.

Wage arrears, especially in construction, remained a persistent grievance. Government campaigns in the 2000s urged employers to pay back wages, and a State Council regulation on guaranteeing wage payment to migrant workers took effect in 2020, requiring wage accounts and deposits on construction projects. Enforcement relied on local labor bureaus, courts, and, in practice, workers' own protests and petitions, which sometimes drew on social media. Official trade unions, organized under the All-China Federation of Trade Unions, have had a limited role in representing migrants, and independent organizing faces legal restrictions, although labor NGOs operated in some places and periods.

Informal employment included street vending, recycling, small-scale services, and domestic work, much of it unprotected by contracts. Occupational health, particularly dust diseases among miners and construction workers and chemical exposure among factory workers, produced long-term harms, and compensation was often difficult to obtain. The evidence for these conditions comes from surveys, labor-dispute statistics, court records, journalism, and ethnographic fieldwork, each of which captures different groups and carries different biases.

Section 75.9

Migrant Families, Children, Schools, and Access to Services

Migration frequently divided families. Many parents left children with grandparents or other relatives in villages, giving rise to the category of "left-behind children," numbering in the tens of millions in some survey estimates. Studies document both the support provided by extended families and concerns about emotional well-being, educational attainment, and supervision. Other migrants brought children to the city, where access to public schools was often limited by registration, requiring documents, proof of employment and residence, or payment of fees; some children attended private schools for migrants, which varied in quality and were sometimes closed in redevelopment campaigns.

Education policy changed over time. From the early 2000s, central policy emphasized that cities receiving migrant children should enroll them in public schools, but implementation depended on local governments, which bore costs. In large cities, requirements remained stringent. Another barrier concerned the college entrance examination, which has generally been taken in the place of registration, so migrant children sometimes returned to their home provinces for high school. Healthcare, housing assistance, and social insurance similarly depended on local rules. As a result, family separation was often an adaptation to institutional constraints and costs, not simply a preference.

Left-behind children became a symbol of the social costs of migration. Estimates have varied widely, depending on definitions: the 2010 census-based counts of rural children left behind by one or both parents exceeded sixty million, while later official counts using narrower criteria (children whose parents both work away and who lack a guardian at home) were far smaller. The difference illustrates how definitions shape perceptions of a problem. In June 2015, the deaths of four siblings in Guizhou who had been left in the care of one another drew national attention and led to central policies requiring local governments to identify and assist vulnerable children, including a 2016 State Council opinion on strengthening care and protection of left-behind rural children.

Research on the effects of separation reports mixed results: some studies found negative outcomes for educational performance and emotional well-being, particularly where children lived without any adult caregiver, while others found that remittances improved nutrition and schooling opportunities, and that strong grandparent care mitigated harm. Boarding schools in rural areas, where children lived during the week, became an additional context of care. For parents, separation brought guilt as well as hopes of providing better futures.

For children who accompanied their parents, the main barriers were enrollment and cost. Some cities developed point-based admission systems or required multiple documents, such as employment contracts, social-insurance records, and proof of housing, which many informal workers could not provide. Others, under central pressure, increased the share of migrant children in public schools. Children who grew up in cities but could not take the entrance examination there faced the prospect of returning to unfamiliar home provinces, a source of tension known in Chinese discussions as the problem of examination access for migrants' children.

Section 75.10

Hukou Reform, Residence Permits, and Uneven Urban Integration

Reform efforts proceeded at both national and local levels. Some provinces began abolishing the agricultural/non-agricultural distinction in the early 2000s. In 2014, the State Council issued guidance on further promoting reform of the household registration system, which set out a national direction: establishing a unified system of registration, removing restrictions on settlement in small towns and small cities, easing them in medium-sized cities, setting conditions in larger cities, and strictly controlling the populations of the largest cities. It also called for a residence permit system to provide basic public services to residents without local registration. A related national urbanization plan set an ambitious target for the number of rural migrants to be granted urban status.

The results were uneven. In smaller cities, settlement became easy and in many places was effectively open, but migrants often preferred larger cities, where wages and services were better. In the largest cities, local governments continued to use points systems, education and employment criteria, and housing requirements to limit settlement, citing pressure on infrastructure, services, and land. Some rural residents also declined urban registration because they would lose entitlements linked to rural land and housing. Historians therefore distinguish policy announcements from outcomes, and attribute persistent differences to local fiscal incentives, central-local relations, and the cost of extending services.

The 2014 guidance built on earlier experiments. Cities such as Chongqing and Chengdu had tried to combine registration change with land-rights arrangements, offering rural residents urban status while protecting entitlements, and Shanghai, Beijing, and Guangzhou adopted points systems allocating registration according to education, skills, tax and insurance contributions, housing, and years of residence. In 2015, the State Council issued interim regulations on residence permits, effective in January 2016, to provide holders with basic public services and convenient access to some additional rights depending on years of residence and local conditions.

Later policy documents continued to adjust the framework. In 2019 the National Development and Reform Commission called for removing settlement restrictions in cities with populations below three million and easing them in larger ones, while keeping tight control on the largest cities. A 2024 national plan on people-centered urbanization emphasized granting public services to permanent residents regardless of registration. Observers noted that central announcements outpaced local practice, because local governments bear the costs, and that the share of the population with registration in the city where they lived rose more slowly than the urban resident share.

Interpretations differ. Some scholars stress progress, noting that many small and medium cities opened and that services such as basic schooling and pensions have been extended to migrants in many places. Others stress persistence, observing that the largest cities, where wages and amenities are highest, remain the hardest to enter. Both views can be supported with evidence, and the weight a historian gives depends on which cities and which services are considered.

Section 75.11

Urban Classes, Inequality, and the Geography of Opportunity

Urbanization created new social strata. Professionals, managers, entrepreneurs, civil servants, and property owners formed a growing middle class, though the term covers very different circumstances; state-sector and large-firm employees often enjoyed more security and benefits than those in small private firms. Industrial workers in state-owned enterprises saw their positions change dramatically after the restructuring of the 1990s (Chapter 74), when millions were laid off. Service employees, migrants, and informal workers formed lower-income groups with limited protection. At the upper end, the concentration of wealth, particularly through property and business ownership, increased.

Inequality has multiple dimensions: between urban and rural households, among regions, within cities, and between genders and generations. Official and independent estimates of income inequality have differed in method, and measures such as the Gini coefficient depend on data sources. Historians therefore compare multiple indicators: income, wealth, housing, education, and access to services.

The urban-rural income ratio is one of the most closely watched indicators of inequality. Official figures indicate that the ratio of average urban to rural per-capita disposable income was above three in the late 2000s and fell to roughly two and a half or lower in the early 2020s, partly through rising rural wages and transfers. At the same time, measures of overall inequality such as the Gini coefficient remained high by international standards in official and independent estimates, and wealth inequality, particularly through property and business assets, grew. The policy discourse shifted accordingly, from "letting some get rich first" to promoting "common prosperity" in the 2020s.

Class structure itself changed. A new professional and managerial middle class emerged, many with university degrees and with homes and cars; a business elite gained wealth in real estate, manufacturing, and technology; a large working class of factory, construction, and service workers had less security; and a stratum of public-sector employees enjoyed stable jobs and benefits. Scholars debate how cohesive these groups are and whether the term "middle class" fits people whose incomes depend on housing prices and employer decisions.

Geography of opportunity mattered. Residents of eastern coastal cities had higher incomes and better services than residents of western provinces, and within provinces, county towns and villages lagged behind provincial capitals. Policies such as the Western Development Strategy (2000), the Northeast Revitalization program, and the Central China Rise aimed to narrow regional gaps, with mixed results. In cities themselves, residential segregation by income and registration emerged, and the experience of belonging to a metropolis differed sharply among its residents.

Historical Debate

Has Urbanization Reduced or Reorganized Inequality?

The question: Did moving to cities narrow gaps between rural and urban households, or did it relocate inequality into the cities themselves?

Interpretation A — Urbanization reduced inequality: Migrants earned far more than they could have in agriculture, remittances raised rural incomes, access to schooling and services improved, and the rural-urban income ratio, while still large, narrowed in some later years. Poverty fell dramatically.
Interpretation B — Urbanization reorganized inequality: Migrants gained income but remained disadvantaged within cities because of registration, housing, and insecure employment, and wealth gaps widened through property appreciation. Inequality shifted from a geographic divide to a divide within urban society.

Evidence: Each side draws on household surveys, wage data, and censuses; they differ over which measure (income, consumption, wealth, services) is most meaningful and over how to treat the costs of urban living.

Why it matters: The debate affects how historians assess whether urbanization represented broad improvement and how they interpret policy reforms.

Population Policy, Demographic Change, Gender, and Family Life

China's demographic transformation as an interaction between public policy, economic modernization, education, gender relations, and changing family decisions.

Section 75.12

China's Demographic Transition and the Changing Pattern of Fertility

Demographers describe a demographic transition as a shift from high birth and death rates to low ones. China experienced this transition rapidly. Mortality declined markedly from the 1950s as public health campaigns, improved nutrition, and basic medical care spread, and life expectancy rose substantially over subsequent decades. Fertility, which had been high in the 1950s and 1960s, fell sharply in the 1970s, before the one-child framework, and continued to fall afterward. By the 1990s, the total fertility rate (the average number of children per woman over her lifetime) was near or below the replacement level of about 2.1, and by the 2020s it was among the lowest in the world, according to most estimates.

Explaining this decline involves multiple factors: rising female education and employment, urbanization, the increasing cost of raising and educating children, delayed marriage, access to contraception, and state policy. Comparative evidence helps: fertility also fell sharply in South Korea, Japan, Taiwan, Singapore, and Thailand, countries without China's coercive birth limits, suggesting that development and changing expectations were powerful forces. Accurate measurement of fertility in China has been complicated by under-reporting of births in some periods, particularly during the strictest enforcement, and by disagreement over census figures.

Understanding the demographic transition requires attention to the three components of population change: births, deaths, and migration. Mortality decline came first. Life expectancy at birth, only around thirty-five in 1949 by commonly cited estimates, rose to above seventy by the 1990s and above seventy-eight in the early 2020s according to official figures, reflecting improvements in nutrition, sanitation, vaccination, and healthcare. Infant and maternal mortality fell sharply, though regional gaps remained, with rural and western areas lagging.

Fertility decline began in the early 1970s, when the "later, longer, fewer" campaign of 1973 encouraged later marriage, longer spacing between births, and fewer children. Total fertility fell from around six children per woman in the late 1960s to under three by the end of the 1970s. This timing is central to arguments about the one-child policy, since much of the decline preceded it. By the 2000 census, fertility was well below replacement, and by the 2020 census it was around 1.3 children per woman, a level among the lowest in the world.

Decline varied by region and group. Fertility was lower in cities and in the northeast than in some western and rural areas; ethnic minorities generally faced looser limits and, in some areas, had higher fertility, though trends converged toward low levels over time. Education was a strong correlate: women with more schooling married later and had fewer children. The transition also shifted the age structure, producing a large working-age population around 2000–2010 and then a decline, which framed economic debates about growth and labor supply (Chapter 74).

Section 75.13

The One-Child Policy: Implementation and Social Consequences

In 1980, the Communist Party leadership urged couples to have only one child, citing concern that population growth would hinder economic development. The policy was never a single uniform rule. Exceptions evolved: many rural couples were permitted a second child if the first was a girl; ethnic minorities generally faced looser limits; and in some areas couples who were themselves only children could have two. Enforcement was carried out through local cadres, birth-planning commissions, and a system of quotas, rewards, and fines (the "social maintenance fee").

Enforcement practices varied and, in some places and periods, were coercive. Documented practices included heavy fines, loss of employment, demolition of houses, mandatory contraception and sterilization, and forced abortions, practices that generated resistance, evasion, and, at times, violence. Families responded in many ways: hiding births, registering children late or not at all, paying fines, or moving. Unregistered children (the "black children") faced difficulties in accessing services. Some families experienced the policy as burdensome and traumatic; others, particularly in cities, adopted small families willingly.

Scholars disagree about how many births the policy prevented, because fertility was already falling before 1980 and continued to fall in places with looser rules. Government sources have claimed hundreds of millions of births averted, while many demographers consider these estimates overstated. Social consequences include a generation of only children, in the cities especially; intensified parental investment in children; distorted sex ratios; and a smaller cohort of young adults to support aging parents.

The policy emerged from concerns of leaders in the late 1970s, including scientists in defense research who made projections of population growth, that large numbers would undermine plans for modernization. In September 1980, an open letter from the Party's Central Committee called on members to have one child, and the 1982 Constitution made family planning a duty of couples. A 1984 directive adjusted the policy, allowing many rural couples a second child under certain conditions, a pattern sometimes described as the 1.5-child policy. Provincial regulations varied, and the national Population and Family Planning Law was enacted only in 2001 (effective 2002).

Implementation relied on a system of targets. Local cadres' performance was evaluated partly on meeting birth quotas, and a veto on promotion could apply when targets were missed. This created incentives for coercion, as well as for under-reporting. The most severe periods of enforcement included campaigns in the early 1980s and the early 1990s. Documented cases include forced sterilization campaigns, late-term abortions, destruction of property, and detention of relatives; there were also cases in which local officials who engaged in abuses were punished, and activists who publicized abuses faced harassment or imprisonment. At the same time, many couples, especially in cities, accepted small families, and surveys show that preferences for fewer children grew independently.

The policy's consequences extended beyond fertility. Families with one child concentrated resources on that child, contributing to higher educational investment, and the "little emperor" stereotype in media debates reflected concern about only children, though research found mixed evidence. The loss of an only child created particular hardship, with "shidu" parents (those who lost their only child) lacking support in old age. Girls were affected through sex-selective practices (Section 75.17). Historians and demographers continue to debate the policy's net effects, and the debate is as much about ethical evaluation as about counting births.

Section 75.14

From the One-Child Policy to Two-Child and Three-Child Policies

Restrictions were gradually relaxed. In 2013, couples in which one spouse was an only child were permitted a second child. In late 2015, authorities announced the end of the one-child framework, and the amended Population and Family Planning Law took effect nationwide on January 1, 2016, allowing all couples two children. The expected surge in births was brief: births rose in 2016 and then declined in subsequent years. In 2021, amid evidence of very low fertility from the 2020 census, the government announced a three-child policy, and the law was amended later that year to permit three children and add supportive measures such as childcare services, education, housing, and employment protection.

The limited response shows that birth restrictions were no longer the main constraint on family size for most couples. Surveys and studies point to costs of housing and education, long working hours, childcare availability, gender inequality in domestic work, job insecurity for women who have children, and the preference of many young adults for later marriage or none.

The path from restriction to encouragement shows how policy followed demographic evidence. Researchers had warned for years that low fertility and aging would constrain growth, and some local pilots, such as in Jiangsu and Shanxi, allowed two children. The 2013 decision to permit a second child where one parent was an only child produced fewer applications than projected. The 2016 universal two-child policy was followed by a short rise in births: about 17.9 million births were recorded in 2016, higher than in the previous year, but births fell in following years, to around 12 million in 2020, according to official statistics.

The three-child policy was announced in May 2021, and supportive measures followed: the 2021 law amendment called for improved prenatal and childcare services, extended maternity leave in many provinces, tax deductions for child-rearing, and housing support, while local governments experimented with cash payments. In 2025, the central government introduced a national childcare subsidy for young children, among other measures. The effects of such measures are being studied, and comparative experience from Korea, Japan, and European countries suggests that incentives tend to produce limited and uncertain increases.

Women's accounts and surveys indicate that the principal obstacles were not legal limits but costs, time, and the unequal sharing of childcare, along with employer reluctance to hire or promote women of childbearing age. Another factor was educational competition, which raised parents' expected spending per child. The policy shift also highlighted a historical irony: a state that had spent decades limiting births now urged families to have more, yet found that its ability to shape such personal decisions had diminished.

Section 75.15

Population Peak, Declining Births, and the Beginning of Population Contraction

Population growth slowed steadily from the 1990s. Within the UNICEF/NBS series covering 1980–2017, annual births reached a high of approximately 25.29 million in 1987, as large cohorts born in the 1960s entered childbearing ages; this was not an all-time historical birth peak, since births had been higher in some earlier years. a smaller recent high occurred in 2016 after the two-child change, and births then fell sharply. In 2022, China recorded its first annual decline in total population in decades (the first since the famine years around 1960–1961), as deaths exceeded births, and the decline continued. China's 2025 statistical communiqué reports a population of approximately 1.405 billion, down 3.39 million from the previous year. India overtook China as the world's most populous country in the 2020s according to United Nations estimates.

Population forecasts vary widely depending on assumed fertility, mortality, and migration, and recorded changes should be distinguished from projections. The United Nations, Chinese demographers, and independent researchers have produced different projections of future population size; students should treat them as scenarios, not predictions.

The recorded numbers show a sharp turn. The 2020 census counted about 1.41 billion people and recorded some twelve million births in 2020. In 2022, according to the NBS, births were about 9.56 million and deaths about 10.41 million, producing a population decline of roughly 850,000; the population fell further in 2023 and 2024, and in 2025 the decline was 3.39 million. The rise in deaths reflects the aging of large cohorts as well as COVID-19, and some of the changes after 2022 were affected by the end of zero-COVID (Section 75.41). Fluctuations such as a modest rebound in births in 2024, a year associated in the lunar calendar with the dragon and considered auspicious, are a reminder that cultural calendars also shape timing.

Measurement is not simple. The census may under- or over-count particular groups, and some researchers argued that earlier censuses overstated or understated births. The reliability of registered births, the treatment of unregistered people, and revisions to previous estimates all complicate comparisons. The United Nations Population Division and independent demographers publish projections that differ from official ones; students should compare assumptions about fertility and mortality, and treat results as scenarios.

The significance of contraction lies partly in its consequences for the economy and society: a smaller number of young workers and consumers, fewer students, and shrinking enrollments in kindergartens and primary schools, with some kindergartens closing in the early 2020s, then primary schools later. Regions experienced it differently, as population loss began earlier in the northeast and in many rural areas than in the large coastal cities, where migration still added residents.

Historical Debate

What Explains China's Low Fertility?

The question: To what extent did state birth-limitation policy cause China's low fertility, and to what extent did social and economic change?

Interpretation A — Policy as a primary cause: Strict limits and enforcement reduced births far below what voluntary decisions would have produced, and the legacy of small families and a generation of only children persists even after limits ended.
Interpretation B — Development and changing expectations as primary causes: Fertility fell before 1980 and in comparable societies without such policies, and it stayed low after restrictions eased, implying that education, urbanization, costs, and values matter more than legal limits.

Evidence: Time series of fertility before and after 1980, comparisons with other East Asian societies, provincial variation in enforcement, survey data on desired family size, and the response to relaxation after 2016.

Why it matters: The debate bears on whether pro-birth incentives can raise fertility and on how historians assess the policy's human costs and demographic effects.

Section 75.16

Population Aging and the Changing Age Structure

Population aging describes a rising share of older people, driven by falling fertility and rising longevity. At the end of 2025, according to the statistical communiqué, about 23 percent of the population, or 323.38 million people, were aged sixty or older. The working-age population, which had grown for decades, began shrinking in the 2010s, reducing the demographic dividend that had supported labor-intensive growth. The old-age dependency ratio, the number of older people relative to working-age people, rose.

Aging is distinct from population decline: a population can age while growing, and China's aging would continue even if births stabilized. It is also uneven. Rural areas aged faster than cities because young adults left; some villages consist largely of older people and children. Urban retirees typically had pensions and medical coverage, while many rural older people relied on family support and modest basic pensions. Retirement ages, which had been relatively low, were raised by a gradual reform adopted in September 2024, with phased implementation beginning in January 2025, an example of policy responding to demographic change.

The older population is not homogeneous. The 2020 census recorded about 18.7 percent of the population aged sixty or above and about 13.5 percent aged sixty-five or above; by 2025 the sixty-and-above share had reached 23 percent. The very old (eighty and above) grew rapidly in absolute numbers. Differences in longevity by gender, region, and education mean that older women outnumber older men at the oldest ages, and many are widows with fewer assets.

Aging interacts with migration. Rural areas, which saw working-age adults leave, often had higher shares of older people, whose support depended on remittances, small pensions, and farming into old age. In cities, retirees in the state sector commonly had pensions that could be comparable to wages, while older workers in informal employment had fewer protections. Retirement ages, set in the 1950s at sixty for men and fifty or fifty-five for women, remained low for decades; a reform adopted in September 2024, with phased implementation beginning in January 2025, gradually raises them over some fifteen years, and the change has been discussed in terms of fairness, workforce supply, and pension finance.

For policymakers and families, the question is how to finance and provide support for a growing group of older people when fewer working adults are available. Official strategy has combined raising the retirement age, expanding pension coverage, developing a "silver economy" of products and services for older people, and promoting community care. Scholars note both the risks of strain and the opportunities offered by healthier, better-educated cohorts, and caution against treating older people only as dependents.

Section 75.17

Gender Imbalance, Sex Ratios, and Demographic Consequences

China's sex ratio at birth, normally around 105 boys per 100 girls, rose well above that level from the 1980s, reaching roughly 115–120 in the 2000s according to census and survey data before declining somewhat in later years. The imbalance reflected a combination of a long-standing cultural preference for sons, especially in patrilineal and agrarian settings where sons provided old-age support and continued the family line; small family size, which gave families fewer chances to have a son; and the availability of ultrasound and sex-selective abortion, which was illegal for non-medical reasons. Under-registration of girls also affected data.

Regional variation was marked, with higher ratios in some rural provinces and lower ratios in others and in many minority areas. The policy contributed in some places but cannot alone explain the pattern: comparable imbalances appeared in South Korea and parts of India with different policies. A consequence is a surplus of men in the marriage market, particularly in rural, poorer areas, contributing to higher bride prices, delayed marriage, and in some cases trafficking or cross-border marriage. Authorities have responded with campaigns against sex-selection and efforts to promote gender equality.

The history of the sex ratio shows how a cultural preference interacts with technology and policy. In patrilineal and agrarian societies, sons carried the family name, inherited property, and typically supported parents; daughters married into other households. When fertility fell, the pressure to have at least one son intensified. Ultrasound machines became widely available in the 1980s, and sex-selective abortion became possible; although laws and regulations prohibited non-medical sex determination, enforcement was difficult. The sex ratio at birth rose to around 120 boys per 100 girls in the mid-2000s, and then declined, reaching about 111 in the 2020 census, a fall linked to improved enforcement, rising female status, changing attitudes, and urbanization.

Regional patterns were distinctive. Imbalances were large in parts of the central and southern provinces and in some rural areas, but modest in the northeast and in many minority regions where restrictions were looser. Because some of the highest ratios occurred where couples were allowed a second child if the first was a girl, the pattern complicates single-policy explanations. Researchers also note that some girls were unregistered, so that apparent imbalances overstated or understated reality, and later "found" in census data.

Consequences included a surplus of young men in the marriage market. Studies suggest that men with low income or education, especially in rural areas, had difficulty marrying, and that marriage payments (bride price) rose in some areas. Cross-border marriages, with brides from neighboring countries, and instances of trafficking have been reported. Campaigns such as the "Care for Girls" program from 2003 sought to change attitudes, offering incentives and support to families with daughters. Scholars caution against portraying all families as sex-selecting: most Chinese families did not, and attitudes changed substantially among younger cohorts.

Section 75.18

Women, Education, Employment, Marriage, and Gender Relations

Women's educational attainment rose dramatically: the gender gap in basic schooling narrowed to near parity, and women made up a majority of university students in many fields by the 2010s. Female labor force participation, historically high in China, declined somewhat after the 1990s as state-sector employment contracted, but remained high by international standards. Women worked in factories, services, agriculture, and professions, but faced a gender pay gap, occupational segregation, and discrimination in hiring related to marriage and pregnancy. Retirement ages that differed by gender affected pensions.

Marriage and family expectations shifted. The age at first marriage rose, the number of registered marriages declined in the 2010s and early 2020s, and divorce became more common. The 2015 Anti-Domestic Violence Law, the 2020 Civil Code (which introduced a thirty-day cooling-off period for divorces by agreement, effective 2021), and legal changes on property and inheritance reflect changing norms. Public debates, including discussion of the "leftover women" label applied to unmarried urban women, "996" work schedules, and the unequal division of household work, show tensions between expectations of career achievement and family responsibility. Feminist activism and the digital spread of the #MeToo movement met with mixed responses, including restrictions on some forms of organizing.

Women's lives changed in education, work, and law. The 1992 Law on the Protection of Women's Rights and Interests, revised in 2005 and 2022, affirmed equality and strengthened provisions against employment discrimination and sexual harassment; the 2015 Anti-Domestic Violence Law was the first national law of its kind. Female enrollment in tertiary education surpassed male enrollment around 2009, and women became prominent in medicine, education, finance, and technology, though they remained underrepresented in senior positions in politics and business.

Employment patterns reflected both opportunity and constraint. Many young women worked in manufacturing and services, and women who were migrants sometimes obtained greater autonomy; at the same time, job advertisements specifying gender or age, questions about marriage plans, and unequal pay persisted. The privatization of services such as childcare after the dissolution of work-unit nurseries increased the burden on families, with women disproportionately affected. Surveys of time use show that women do a larger share of housework and childcare even when both partners work full-time.

Marriage practices changed. Dating websites, apps, and matchmaking events, including parent-organized marriage markets in some city parks, coexisted with traditional expectations. Registered marriages peaked at about 13.5 million in 2013 and then fell sharply, to roughly half that number in the early 2020s, while the average age at first marriage rose. Divorce rates rose in the 2000s and 2010s; a thirty-day cooling-off period introduced with the Civil Code in 2021 was followed by a drop in registered divorces, though scholars debate its effect. Feminist activism, including the detention in 2015 of five young activists, and later discussions on social media of harassment, domestic violence, and the "leftover women" label, show contested terrain where public debate expanded and was also constrained.

Section 75.19

Changing Families, Elder Care, and Intergenerational Relationships

Households became smaller on average, but multigenerational ties remained strong. Grandparents commonly provided childcare, especially for children of working or migrant parents; adult children provided financial support and care for older parents, in line with the long-standing ideal of filial responsibility, now enshrined in law (the Law on the Protection of the Rights and Interests of the Elderly requires adult children to support and visit parents). At the same time, geographic separation, small families, and the "4-2-1" structure, in which one child may eventually support two parents and four grandparents, strained the capacity of families.

The 2020 census recorded an average household size of about 2.6 persons, down from over three a decade earlier, reflecting smaller families, more single-person households, and migration. Yet households often extended across space: grandparents moved to cities to help with grandchildren, and young adults living in other cities sent money and visited at festivals. The phrase "empty-nest" described older couples or individuals living alone, particularly in villages, a phenomenon associated with migration and, in cities, with the preference of older and younger generations to live separately but near each other.

Law and custom shaped obligations. The Law on the Protection of the Rights and Interests of the Elderly, amended in 2012 (effective 2013), requires adult children to support parents and to visit them frequently, and some courts have heard cases enforcing these duties. Practices of caregiving rely heavily on daughters and daughters-in-law, though sons and, increasingly, paid caregivers also provide help. The cultural ideal of filial piety (xiao) draws on a long Confucian tradition (Chapters 11, 34), but the way people practice it adapts to modern conditions, including financial support, remote contact through messaging, and arranging professional care.

Intergenerational relations also involve money flowing downward: parents contribute to housing purchases, weddings, and grandchildren's education, sometimes at the expense of their own security. Survey research documents both strong solidarity and strains, such as conflict over caregiving, inheritance, and housing. These patterns help explain why public debates about aging, birth rates, and housing prices are interconnected.

Section 75.20

Compulsory Education, Literacy, and the Expansion of Schooling

The Compulsory Education Law of 1986 established nine years of schooling, and during the 1990s and 2000s the state worked to make that guarantee real. Government announcements declared that nine-year compulsory education had been basically universalized by around 2011, and primary and junior-secondary enrollment became nearly universal. Adult literacy rose to very high levels, though illiteracy remained concentrated among older women and in remote areas. Tuition and miscellaneous fees for compulsory schooling were abolished in rural areas in the mid-2000s and in cities later, reducing a major burden on poor families.

Challenges remained. Rural schools often had fewer qualified teachers, older facilities, and fewer resources; consolidation policies closed many village schools, sending children to larger schools farther away, sometimes in boarding arrangements. Funding responsibility was shifted upward from villages to counties and higher levels of government, but disparities persisted. Teachers, including rural teachers who were often underpaid and some hired on temporary contracts, were central actors, and national programs sought to improve their training and pay.

Expanding schooling required more than legislation. Between the 1990s and 2010s, the state built or renovated schools, trained teachers, supplied textbooks, and reduced fees; the Compulsory Education Law was revised in 2006 to guarantee free tuition and miscellaneous fees and to require equitable resource allocation. Government spending on education rose, and the long-standing target of spending four percent of GDP was reached in 2012. These investments mattered most for poor rural counties, where central transfers supported teacher salaries and school buildings.

School consolidation illustrates the tradeoffs. From around 2001, authorities merged small village schools into larger town and county schools to improve quality and efficiency. Children traveled farther or boarded, and in some places dropout rates rose and families bore transport and living costs; policy was adjusted from 2012 to slow closures and protect small rural schools. Teachers in village schools, including "substitute teachers" hired outside the formal system, were central actors whose status was gradually regularized.

Evidence for these changes comes from official enrollment data, national surveys such as the China Family Panel Studies, international tests in which Chinese urban students performed highly while rural comparisons were less often available, and local case studies. Historians should note that official completion rates may overstate quality: attendance and learning are different outcomes. The expansion nonetheless transformed expectations, making twelve years of schooling a common aspiration and a basis for later debates about educational quality and equity.

Section 75.21

Higher Education Expansion and the Transformation of Universities

In 1999, the government decided to expand university enrollment dramatically, motivated partly by the goal of building human capital and partly by the desire to delay entry into the labor market after the Asian financial crisis. New enrollments rose sharply year after year; the gross enrollment ratio in tertiary education, around one in ten in the late 1990s, passed fifty percent in the late 2010s, and exceeded sixty percent in the early 2020s according to official figures. UNESCO analyses confirm the exceptional pace of this expansion and the increasingly stratified nature of the system.

Universities were differentiated. Elite national institutions, supported by special programs such as Project 211, Project 985, and later "Double First-Class" initiatives, concentrated resources, research funding, and prestige. Provincial universities, private colleges, and vocational institutions absorbed much of the growth. Tuition, which was introduced in the 1990s, rose, and student loans and scholarships were developed to offset costs. Universities also became sites of research, with China's share of global scientific publications and patent applications increasing considerably.

Several motives lay behind the 1999 expansion. Leaders sought a skilled workforce for an economy moving up the value chain; economists urged stimulating domestic demand after the Asian financial crisis of 1997; and families, seeing university degrees as passports to urban employment, supported more places. Universities responded by building large new campuses, often in suburban "university towns," financed through bank loans and tuition, and by merging institutions into larger comprehensive universities. Private (minban) colleges and independent colleges affiliated with public universities took in many students, and graduate education expanded as well.

Government programs concentrated resources. Project 211, launched in the mid-1990s, and Project 985, announced in 1998 and named for the date of Peking University's centennial speech, provided funds to selected universities to reach world-class status; in 2015–2017 these were succeeded by the "Double First-Class" initiative. Such policies helped some universities rise in international rankings and research output, but also deepened differences between a small elite group and the many institutions that taught larger numbers with fewer resources. The result was a stratified system in which institutional prestige strongly shaped graduates' prospects.

Costs shifted to families. Until the 1990s, tuition was nominal, but tuition fees introduced in the 1990s and unified by the end of the decade made higher education a major household expense, partly offset by scholarships and national student loans, which began in 1999. Students from low-income families sometimes took work during their studies or chose lower-cost institutions. The overall effect was to open universities to many more young people, while linking their experiences to family finances and to the institutional hierarchy.

Section 75.22

The Gaokao, Educational Competition, and Household Strategies

The national college entrance examination, the gaokao (高考), resumed in 1977 after being suspended during the Cultural Revolution (Chapter 70), and has since been the central gateway to higher education. Its social weight echoes the imperial examinations (Chapters 26, 41), though the institutions differ profoundly: the modern examination is a state-run test of a mass school population, not a route to office in a bureaucracy. Admission is often determined by scores, province-level quotas, and the applicant's place of registration, so the same score may lead to different outcomes depending on province.

Competition shaped family life. Parents invested in tutoring, cram schools, and preparatory courses, and moved or purchased homes to secure access to well-regarded schools. Students endured long hours of study, with significant pressure and, in surveys, reports of stress and sleep deprivation. In 2021, the government issued "double reduction" measures restricting for-profit tutoring in core school subjects and reducing homework, aiming to relieve costs; analysts disagree on its results, as some tutoring moved into informal markets. Examination reforms have also sought to give students more subject choices and to curb cheating through measures including technology and criminal penalties.

The history of the gaokao reaches back to 1977, when the examination was restored after the Cultural Revolution; about five and a half million candidates took the exam that winter, and the admitted students, many of them older people who had been sent to the countryside, formed a celebrated cohort. In the 2020s, over thirteen million candidates sit the exam each year. The examination is held in June and covers core subjects of Chinese, mathematics, and a foreign language, along with other subjects depending on the provincial format; reforms from 2014, beginning in Shanghai and Zhejiang, allowed students to choose among elective subjects.

The examination's symbolic weight stems from the belief that it is comparatively impartial, a belief that echoes the legitimacy of the imperial examinations (Chapters 26, 41) but also from real stakes: scores strongly determine which university and major a student can enter. Provincial quotas for universities, tied to the location of the institution and registration of the candidate, mean that students of some provinces face more competitive conditions than others. Debates about "fairness" thus concern not only cheating, which authorities punish severely, but the distribution of quotas and the rules for migrants' children, some of whom were allowed from 2012 onward to take the exam in their city of residence under conditions.

Outside the classroom, an industry of cram schools, online tutoring, and test preparation developed. Parents' spending on out-of-school classes rose substantially, creating a race that some economists compared to an arms race: if one family invests, others must follow. The 2021 double-reduction rules sharply curtailed the for-profit sector, and enforcement varied. The social effects included less pressure for some families and a shift of tutoring to private arrangements, with wealthier households better able to pay. Historians evaluating the policy rely on surveys, school reports, and journalism, and should be cautious about generalizing from any single source.

Section 75.23

Vocational Education, Technical Training, and Changing Career Pathways

Vocational schools and technical colleges educate a large share of upper-secondary students. They have been promoted as a route to skilled employment in manufacturing, services, and technology, and the Vocational Education Law was revised in 2022 to strengthen their standing and encourage partnerships with employers. Yet vocational education carried lower prestige than academic study, partly because of the gaokao hierarchy, and many families regarded it as a second choice; students in vocational schools were disproportionately from rural and lower-income households.

Employers reported shortages of skilled technicians even as many graduates struggled to find jobs, a mismatch attributed to quality differences, training content, and wage structures. Apprenticeship-style programs, workplace training, and the growth of technology and service industries opened new pathways, and some regions, such as the Pearl River Delta, developed strong linkages between schools and firms.

Vocational education has older roots in specialized schools of the planned economy, which trained workers for state enterprises. After the restructuring of the late 1990s, many of these schools declined or were converted, while new vocational high schools and colleges grew with the economy. Law and policy documents, including the 1996 Vocational Education Law and a 2019 national plan for reform, committed the state to raising their quality and status, and by the 2020s secondary vocational schools enrolled a large minority of upper-secondary students.

Debates about vocational education reflect wider tensions. Policymakers wanted to expand it to supply skilled workers and to reduce pressure on academic routes; parents often saw academic high school as the better path, and in the early 2020s, a policy of roughly equal numbers entering academic and vocational tracks at the end of junior secondary school stirred public concern about "streaming." Vocational colleges, including higher vocational institutions that enrolled many students, offered degrees in fields such as nursing, manufacturing, logistics, and information technology, and the 2022 revision of the Vocational Education Law recognized "vocational undergraduate" programs.

Experiences varied greatly. Students in programs connected to employers, such as in advanced manufacturing or railways, often found jobs quickly, while others faced limited prospects, particularly where schools had weak links to industry or teachers lacked industry experience. Some programs placed students in internships at factories, raising concerns about whether these were educational experiences or sources of low-cost labor. The topic shows how educational structures reproduced social hierarchies and how attempts to reform them depended on changes in employers' wages and recognition of skills.

Section 75.24

Rural-Urban Educational Inequality and the Geography of Opportunity

Educational outcomes varied widely by location. Per-pupil funding, teacher quality, and facilities differed among provinces and between urban and rural areas. Admission quotas for universities favored the municipalities and provinces where elite universities were located, so that a student from Beijing or Shanghai historically had a much higher chance of entering a top university than a student from an inland province with the same score. Policies in later years expanded special recruitment quotas for students from poorer, rural, and western areas.

Minority and frontier regions had distinct circumstances. In areas where minority languages are spoken, questions concerning the language of instruction, bilingual education, and curriculum have been significant; discussion of specific policies in Tibet and Xinjiang belongs to Chapter 76. Migrant children faced the barriers described in Section 75.9. The geographic pattern is one of overlapping disadvantage: remoteness, low household income, and registration status combined to limit opportunities, while online education and national programs partly expanded access.

Inequality in education operates at several levels. At the school level, differences in facilities, class size, and teacher qualifications persisted between urban and rural schools and between key schools and ordinary schools, even after official policies abolished the formal "key school" designation for compulsory education. At the household level, parental education and income strongly predicted children's attainment, and at the regional level, local fiscal capacity shaped what schools could offer.

Admission to top universities illustrates geographic inequality. Beijing and Shanghai residents historically enjoyed substantially higher admission probabilities to leading universities than candidates from populous inland provinces, such as Henan, Shandong, or Hebei, because quotas were allocated by province partly in relation to the universities' locations and funding. Since 2012, special national and local programs reserved places at selective universities for students from poor rural counties, and the proportion of rural students at top institutions has been studied by researchers, who report both gains and continuing gaps.

Frontier and minority regions faced additional issues: schools in remote areas of Tibet, Xinjiang, Qinghai, Yunnan, and Inner Mongolia often serve multilingual communities, and policies on the language of instruction, bilingual education, and boarding schools have been subjects of discussion in China and abroad. This chapter notes the educational dimension, without treating it as a full account; Chapter 76 addresses the political questions. During the COVID-19 period, remote learning exposed inequalities in internet access and devices, and in households' ability to support children's study at home.

Section 75.25

University Graduates, Employment, Research, and Social Mobility

Annual graduating cohorts grew from under a million in the late 1990s to over ten million in the 2020s. Graduates entered a labor market in which the supply of degree holders rose faster than the supply of jobs perceived as desirable. Many pursued civil-service examinations, state-sector jobs, and large firms for security; others entered private firms, startups, or platform work; some pursued postgraduate study to defer entry or improve their prospects. Youth unemployment, as officially measured for ages sixteen to twenty-four, reached 21.3 percent in June 2023, after which publication was suspended and then resumed in January 2024 under a revised method, a reminder that statistics themselves are historical artifacts.

Overseas study expanded, with Chinese students forming one of the largest international student groups, though flows changed with geopolitical tensions and pandemic restrictions. Universities increased research output, and Chinese scientists played a growing role in global research. Cultural expressions of graduate anxiety, such as the online term for "involution" (neijuan, 内卷) and "lying flat" (tangping, 躺平), became part of public conversation.

The scale of graduating cohorts changed rapidly. About one million students graduated from university in 2001; by 2013 the number exceeded six million; and by the mid-2020s it exceeded twelve million, according to official announcements. Employers and local governments competed for talent in some fields, such as engineering and computer science, while graduates in other fields found fewer openings. The state encouraged entrepreneurship, grassroots service, military enlistment, and postgraduate study as alternatives, and the annual civil-service examination attracted millions of applicants for a small number of posts, creating a modern echo of the examination culture of earlier periods.

Youth unemployment, as officially measured for ages sixteen to twenty-four, reached 21.3 percent in June 2023, after which the National Bureau of Statistics suspended publication; it resumed in January 2024 using a revised method that excludes students. The episode shows how definitions shape discussion of a social problem, and that economists differ about the degree of underemployment among graduates, including those who accept jobs below their qualifications or work in platform jobs. Many graduates returned to their hometowns, entered family businesses, or combined part-time and project-based work.

Research and innovation grew with higher education. China became the world's leading producer of scientific publications in several fields, and its universities and institutes trained large numbers of engineers; students going abroad, once largely for graduate study, increasingly pursued undergraduate degrees, while many returned to China after studying abroad, encouraged by talent programs and domestic opportunities. Geopolitical tensions and visa restrictions in the late 2010s and 2020s affected some flows. Students' trajectories thus tied educational history to the economy, to international relations, and to family strategies.

Division V · Sections 75.26–75.31

Telecommunications, the Internet, and Digital Society

How China became a highly digitized society and how this changed communication, consumption, work, and the relationship between individuals and institutions.

Section 75.26

Telecommunications, Internet Infrastructure, and Mass Connectivity

In 1990, telephones were scarce and mostly in cities. Investment in fixed-line networks in the 1990s expanded service, and mobile phones spread rapidly after 2000, in many places leapfrogging fixed lines. China first connected to the global internet in the mid-1990s; the number of users grew from a few million at the end of the decade to hundreds of millions in the 2010s. State-owned operators built broadband and mobile networks, introduced 3G in 2009, 4G in 2013, and 5G commercially in 2019, and extended connectivity to villages through national programs. By the end of 2025, the China Internet Network Information Center reported about 1.125 billion internet users, or 80.1 percent of the population.

The chronology of connectivity can be divided into phases. In the 1990s, the main tasks were building fixed-line networks and introducing mobile telephony; telephone penetration rose from around one line per hundred people at the start of the decade to much higher levels by 2000. China joined the global internet in 1994, and the first survey by the China Internet Network Information Center in 1997 counted fewer than a million users. From the early 2000s, broadband and internet cafes extended access, particularly among young urban men; in the 2010s, smartphones and mobile data made internet use ubiquitous across age groups and regions.

State policy shaped infrastructure. Large state-owned telecommunications companies built networks under national plans, including "Broadband China" strategies, universal-service programs to connect villages, and the early rollout of 5G, with the number of 5G base stations exceeding one million in the early 2020s. Costs fell, and handsets produced by Chinese manufacturers became widely affordable. The gaps that persisted included lower speeds and fewer devices in remote areas, and higher costs relative to income for poorer households.

Telecommunications changed households and communities. Migrant workers, separated from families, used phone cards and later video calls to maintain relationships; rural entrepreneurs used mobile devices to market produce; and farmers used messaging groups to coordinate. The history of connectivity is thus a social history of communication as well as of infrastructure, and it forms the foundation for the digital developments in the following sections.

Historical Vocabulary
Platform Economy, Digital Divide, and Data Privacy

The platform economy consists of economic activity organized through digital platforms that match buyers and sellers, riders and customers, or workers and tasks, often through algorithmic management. The digital divide refers to unequal access to, or ability to use, digital technology, by age, income, region, or education. Data privacy concerns how personal information is collected, used, shared, and protected. These concepts are used analytically here; each has different meanings in law, business, and everyday speech.

Section 75.27

Social Media, Online Communication, and the Changing Public Sphere

Early internet forums, bulletin boards, and instant-messaging services in the late 1990s and 2000s created new spaces for discussion. Blogs and microblogging, especially Weibo from 2009, allowed citizens, journalists, and celebrities to share information and comment on events such as accidents, corruption cases, and consumer scandals, at times prompting official responses. WeChat, launched in 2011, became a multi-purpose application for messaging, social networking, payments, and services, with a user base spanning generations and an important role in family communication, including for migrants separated from their families.

Online expression has been shaped by regulation. Platforms are required to moderate content, real-name registration was introduced, and filtering and blocking restrict access to some foreign websites and topics, a system often called the "Great Firewall." Users developed coded language, humor, and memes, and online communities formed around interests, locality, and identity. Historians studying this period face the problem that deleted posts and restricted content leave gaps in the record, and that platform data are held by private firms.

Public discussion online passed through stages. Forums and bulletin-board systems such as Tianya hosted lively debates in the early 2000s; QQ, launched in 1999, popularized instant messaging; blogs enabled personal writing; and Weibo (2009) combined broadcasting and conversation in a way that allowed news and criticism to spread quickly. Events such as the 2011 Wenzhou high-speed rail accident showed the potential of social media to mobilize public attention, challenge official accounts, and press for responses. WeChat, launched in 2011, shifted communication into semi-private groups and "moments" feeds, and later added payments and mini-programs, making it a hub of daily life.

Regulation accompanied expansion. Platforms were made responsible for content on their services, and authorities developed rules for real-name registration, which were applied to microblogs in 2011–2012 and then broadly. A campaign in 2013 targeted influential users, sometimes described as "Big Vs," and public discussion became more cautious on political topics, while entertainment, consumer, and lifestyle content flourished on newer platforms such as Douyin (2016) and Bilibili. Users developed euphemisms and memes to talk about sensitive topics, and local campaigns, charitable fundraising, and mutual aid expanded online, as in the volunteer networks that formed during floods and the pandemic.

Scholars disagree on how to characterize these developments: some highlight spaces of public voice and deliberation that coexisted with limits; others emphasize the state's capacity to guide and shape online opinion. A historian must also note that content is often removed, so the archive of online speech is incomplete, and that platform algorithms influence what users see.

Section 75.28

E-Commerce, Digital Payments, and Changing Consumer Behavior

Online marketplaces such as Taobao (from 2003) and JD.com, followed by newer platforms, enabled small sellers to reach consumers nationwide. Alipay, developed to support online transactions, and WeChat Pay, popularized through digital red envelopes in 2014, spread mobile payments quickly; QR codes became common for small purchases from supermarkets to street vendors. The shift was aided by a relatively low rate of credit-card use, a large smartphone base, and delivery networks supported by low-cost labor. Rural e-commerce programs connected farmers and village entrepreneurs to national markets, giving rise to so-called "Taobao villages" specializing in particular products.

Digital commerce brought convenience, but also raised issues of market concentration, counterfeit goods, data collection, and exclusion of people without smartphones; some older adults and visitors found cash refused in places, prompting regulations requiring that cash remain accepted.

E-commerce developed in part because of gaps in older retail systems. In many cities, large modern retail chains were few in the early 2000s, and trust in distant sellers was low; Alipay's escrow system, introduced in 2004, addressed trust by holding funds until buyers confirmed receipt. Shopping festivals such as Singles' Day, which began in 2009, became annual events for promotions and record sales. Express delivery companies, mostly organized as networks of franchised couriers, handled over a hundred billion parcels per year by 2021, and logistics employed millions of workers.

Mobile payments diffused after smartphones spread and QR-code payment became simple. In 2014, WeChat's digital red-envelope function for the Lunar New Year gave millions of users a reason to link bank accounts, and by the late 2010s mobile payments accounted for a large share of retail transactions in cities. Government regulation, including the central bank's oversight of non-bank payment institutions, caps on certain fees, and later efforts to promote a digital yuan (e-CNY) pilot, shaped the system. Foreign visitors in the 2010s often remarked on the near disappearance of cash in some urban contexts, which in turn prompted rules to ensure that cash remained accepted.

Rural e-commerce changed incomes in some localities. "Taobao villages," where large shares of households sell goods online, appeared from around 2009 and multiplied across the country, producing items from furniture to clothing; live-stream sales promoted local produce. At the same time, small sellers depended on platform rules and fees, and gains concentrated in places with logistics and entrepreneurial networks. Consumers benefited from convenience and choice, yet concerns about counterfeit goods, data use, and platform market power led to regulatory actions in 2020–2021.

Section 75.29

Platform Employment, Delivery Services, and the Digital Labor Economy

Platforms for food delivery, ride-hailing, courier services, live-streaming, and freelance work created a large flexible labor market. Millions of people, including many migrants, worked as delivery riders, drivers, and couriers; surveys suggested that such work offered higher earnings and easier entry than many alternatives, along with scheduling flexibility. At the same time, riders were typically classified as independent contractors or placed with subcontracting agencies, with limited social insurance and little control over pay rates; algorithmic systems set delivery time limits and monitored performance, and public attention to rider safety grew after reports and investigative journalism in 2020.

The government responded with guidelines on protecting the rights of delivery workers, rules on algorithmic recommendation services, and pilot occupational injury insurance programs, while courts addressed disputes about employment status. Debates continue over whether platform workers should be treated as employees, and over how to extend social protections to flexible workers.

Platform employment emerged from the combination of consumer demand, venture capital, and a large pool of workers. Ride-hailing services such as Didi (founded in 2012) and food-delivery platforms such as Meituan and Ele.me hired or contracted millions of drivers and riders; live-streaming and short-video platforms supported a creator economy; and freelance platforms matched workers with short-term tasks. The All-China Federation of Trade Unions cited tens of millions of workers in "new forms of employment" (about 84 million in a figure reported by the federation in July 2023), though definitions vary.

The conditions of platform work became a public issue in September 2020, when a magazine article titled in translation "Delivery Riders, Trapped in the System" described how algorithmic time limits pressured riders into risky behavior. Platforms announced changes, and in 2021 a group of central agencies issued guidelines on protecting delivery workers' rights, including requirements on minimum pay standards, rest, and insurance, while a pilot program for occupational injury insurance for platform workers began in several provinces. Rules on algorithmic recommendation services, effective in 2022, required transparency and options for users, and courts in some cases held that platform workers had employment relationships.

Workers' own perspectives vary. Many riders, who include migrants and, increasingly, local residents and graduates, value flexibility and quick earnings and compare the work favorably with factory jobs; others emphasize dangers of traffic accidents, income insecurity, and the lack of pensions and medical insurance. Female workers are less represented but are present in some services. Studies by economists, sociologists, and journalists provide different evidence, and platform data are usually not publicly available, which limits independent analysis.

Section 75.30

Digital Regulation, Privacy, Surveillance, and Online Citizenship

China built a body of digital law. The Cybersecurity Law (effective 2017) set requirements on network security, real-name registration, and data localization for critical sectors; the Data Security Law and the Personal Information Protection Law (PIPL) both took effect in 2021. The PIPL, passed in August 2021 and effective November 1, 2021, set rules on consent, purposes of data processing, individual rights, and responsibilities of data handlers, and bears comparison with the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation. Regulators also acted against large platforms in 2020–2022, including antitrust penalties and restrictions on data practices, as well as measures on minors' gaming and recommendation algorithms.

The regulatory picture is double-sided. Citizens gained legal tools against commercial data misuse, and courts heard privacy cases. At the same time, state agencies have broad authority to access data for security and public-order purposes, extensive camera networks and identification systems exist in cities, and online speech is subject to content rules. Historians and scholars differ on how to characterize the system and how it is experienced by ordinary users, and available evidence includes laws, enforcement announcements, court cases, journalism, and research, each with limits.

Digital governance in China has three interacting dimensions. First, there is regulation of commercial data use, culminating in the Personal Information Protection Law of 2021. Second, there is state oversight of information and security, including the Cybersecurity Law (adopted in 2016 and effective in 2017), which requires network operators to cooperate with authorities and to store certain data within China, and the Data Security Law (effective September 2021), which establishes categories of data and security reviews. Third, there is the development of technologies of identification and monitoring, including facial recognition, large camera networks, and digital identity systems, justified by authorities as measures for security, fraud prevention, and efficient services.

Regulatory action against platforms in 2020–2022 reshaped industry. In April 2021, the market regulator fined Alibaba 18.2 billion yuan for antitrust violations, and in 2021 the cybersecurity agency opened reviews of several overseas-listed firms, including the ride-hailing company Didi. Rules on algorithmic recommendations, deep synthesis ("deepfake") technologies, and minors' use of games and apps followed. Some analysts saw these steps as protecting consumers and workers; others saw them as asserting political control over a powerful private sector.

The "social credit" concept is often misunderstood outside China. Rather than a single national score, it has comprised multiple, fragmented systems: financial credit reporting, blacklists of people who fail to comply with court orders, regulatory credit records for businesses, and local pilot programs with varying rewards and penalties. Scholars disagree on how far these systems have affected everyday behavior, and students should distinguish documented policies from popular exaggerations. Ordinary users encounter digital regulation in practical ways: real-name registration to obtain a mobile number, app-permission requests, and consent forms, and in the pandemic-era health codes, which showed how digital systems could structure movement.

Section 75.31

Digital Divides, Artificial Intelligence, and Contemporary Technological Change

Digital participation was uneven. Older adults, rural residents, and people with lower education or disabilities faced barriers, especially as services such as health codes, ticketing, and government applications moved online. National campaigns promoted "aging-friendly" interfaces and training. Regional gaps in broadband speed and device quality persisted, and gender and generational differences shaped types of use.

Artificial intelligence became a major theme in the 2020s: facial recognition, recommendation systems, voice assistants, and large language models were deployed in commerce, education, healthcare, and government, and Chinese firms and research institutions competed internationally. Regulations on generative AI services took effect in 2023, and the release of competitive Chinese models in 2025 drew global attention. Evidence on social impacts, such as effects on employment and education, is still emerging, and students should distinguish documented developments from speculation.

The digital divide in China is multidimensional. Internet users are overwhelmingly mobile users, and those who use only smartphones may have different capabilities than people with computers. Older adults were less likely to use the internet, and as services moved online, from hospital registration to transport tickets to payment, those without smartphones or skills encountered obstacles. In 2020, a State Council notice called on service providers to preserve non-digital options and to adapt to older users, and companies introduced simplified interfaces. Surveys show wide differences by age, education, and region, and between men and women in some places.

Artificial intelligence developed on strengths in data, computing, and an active research community. The government's 2017 New Generation Artificial Intelligence Development Plan set goals for leadership by 2030, and applications spread in facial recognition, logistics optimization, translation, industrial robots, and autonomous driving pilots. Regulations on generative AI services took effect in August 2023, requiring security assessments and content controls, and in 2025 the State Council promoted an "AI Plus" initiative to integrate AI into industries. The release of competitive Chinese language models in 2025 attracted international attention, and AI tools spread into education, customer service, and creative work.

The social effects are still unfolding. Questions concern jobs, with some roles automated and others created; schools, which issued rules on student use of AI; the quality and bias of automated decisions; and the allocation of benefits between firms, workers, and consumers. Historians writing in the mid-2020s must distinguish documented adoption from forecasts, and should treat claims by companies and governments as evidence of aspirations as much as of outcomes.

Section 75.32

Industrial Development and the Environmental Costs of Growth

Rapid industrialization and urbanization placed heavy demands on land, water, air, and energy. Heavy industry (steel, cement, chemicals, power generation), coal mining, and manufacturing produced emissions and waste; agricultural intensification increased use of fertilizers and pesticides; and cities generated waste, traffic, and construction dust. Local governments competing for investment and growth sometimes weakened enforcement of environmental rules, and information on pollution was limited in the early reform era.

The costs fell unevenly. Communities near mines, chemical plants, and waste sites bore health risks; some villages became known as "cancer villages," a term used in media and research for places with reported clusters of disease, though causal attribution requires careful study. Environmental awareness grew, expressed through journalism, nongovernmental organizations, local protests, and, later, social media. Official policy slowly shifted from prioritizing growth to emphasizing "ecological civilization" in the 2010s.

The environmental costs of growth emerged from a combination of technology, ownership, and governance. Heavy industry used coal-intensive processes; export manufacturing produced chemical and electronic waste; urban construction consumed sand, cement, and steel; and agricultural intensification increased fertilizer use and runoff. By the late 2000s, China had become the world's largest emitter of carbon dioxide and the largest consumer of coal, steel, and cement, and its pollution was a national and global concern.

A series of incidents made pollution politically salient. In 2005, an explosion at a chemical plant released benzene into the Songhua River in the northeast, interrupting water supply to the city of Harbin and affecting communities downstream; in 2007, an algal bloom in Lake Taihu cut off drinking water for Wuxi's residents. Protests against planned chemical plants, including at Xiamen in 2007, Dalian in 2011, and Maoming in 2014, showed local resistance, often organized through new media, to projects seen as hazardous. In March 2014, the premier declared a "war on pollution," and in 2015 a documentary about smog by a former television journalist was viewed by very large audiences before being removed from major platforms, illustrating both public concern and the limits on public mobilization.

The Environmental Protection Law, revised in 2014 and effective in January 2015, strengthened penalties, allowed public-interest lawsuits by designated organizations, and required officials to take responsibility, and later central environmental inspection teams visited provinces to examine enforcement. Scholars debate how much improvement came from regulation, how much from economic restructuring, and how much from technological change, and they note that some polluting activities relocated to inland areas or other countries.

Section 75.33

Air Pollution, Urban Health, and Clean-Air Policies

Coal combustion for heating and power, industrial emissions, and rising vehicle use created severe air pollution in many cities. A winter 2013 episode of dense smog across northern China, together with publicly accessible PM2.5 readings (from the U.S. embassy in Beijing and then national monitoring), turned air quality into a major public concern. In 2013, the State Council issued the Air Pollution Prevention and Control Action Plan, setting targets to reduce fine-particle concentrations in key regions by 2017, and measures including coal consumption control, industrial upgrading, vehicle standards, and the replacement of household coal heating in northern areas. A revised Environmental Protection Law took effect in 2015, strengthening penalties and enforcement.

Measured average concentrations of PM2.5 in many Chinese cities fell substantially in the following decade, according to both official monitoring and independent analyses, though levels in many areas remained above World Health Organization guidelines, ozone pollution became a growing problem, and some coal-to-gas conversion programs produced hardships when supplies fell short.

Air pollution has multiple sources: coal burning in power plants, industrial boilers, and household heating; vehicle emissions; construction dust; and agricultural burning. In north China, winter heating based on coal combined with stagnant weather produced severe smog episodes. Measuring fine particulate matter (PM2.5) became standard after the national air quality standard of 2012 was applied to cities in stages from 2013, and mobile applications gave residents real-time readings, which changed public understanding. Health studies linked pollution to respiratory and cardiovascular disease and to reduced life expectancy.

The 2013 plan was followed by further policies: the 2018 three-year action plan to win the "blue sky defense battle," bans on new coal-fired boilers in key areas, closures of small polluting factories, tighter fuel standards, and a campaign to replace household coal heating with gas or electricity in northern villages. Some of these measures caused difficulties, as in the winter of 2017–2018 when households in some northern areas lacked heat because gas supplies and new equipment were not ready, an episode that led authorities to adjust the pace of conversion.

Monitoring data show substantial improvements in major cities: annual average PM2.5 in Beijing fell by roughly two-thirds between 2013 and the early 2020s, though it remained above the stricter guideline set by the World Health Organization in 2021. Ozone pollution, which is harder to control, became more prominent, and some regions continued to face winter episodes. Experience of clean-air policy varied: urban middle-class residents benefited from cleaner air, while workers in closed factories and rural households affected by heating changes bore costs, and regions such as Hebei, with large steel industries, were particularly affected.

Section 75.34

Water Scarcity, River Systems, and Water Pollution

Water resources are unevenly distributed: the south, especially the Yangtze basin and areas south of it, has abundant water, while the north has far less per person, though it has large populations and important agricultural and industrial regions. The Yellow River, which supplies much of the north, has been heavily withdrawn for irrigation, industry, and cities, and in the 1990s sections of its lower reaches repeatedly ran dry before reaching the sea; subsequent management of withdrawals has improved flows. Groundwater depletion in the North China Plain created land subsidence and falling water tables.

Pollution compounded scarcity: industrial wastewater, urban sewage, and agricultural runoff contaminated rivers, lakes, and groundwater. Responses included wastewater-treatment expansion, the "river chief" system assigning officials responsibility for river sections, stricter standards, and major engineering projects such as the South–North Water Transfer Project, whose eastern and middle routes began delivering water in the 2010s. These projects raised questions about costs, displacement of residents along the routes, and the effects on source regions.

The geography of water in China is a long-standing theme (Chapter 2). The Yellow River carries large amounts of sediment and has caused floods throughout history, yet in the late twentieth century heavy withdrawals contributed to episodes in which the lower reaches ran dry for part of the year; central authorities introduced unified water-allocation management in 1999, and flows to the sea were maintained thereafter. The Yellow River Protection Law, effective in April 2023, set rules on allocation, ecological protection, and pollution control, and a Yangtze River Protection Law took effect in 2021, together with a ten-year fishing ban in the main stem and major tributaries, aimed at restoring fish populations.

Water pollution was extensive in the 2000s, with significant portions of monitored rivers and lakes classified as unsuitable for drinking, and rural groundwater in some areas contaminated by industrial or agricultural sources. The 2015 Water Pollution Prevention and Control Action Plan ("Water Ten") set targets for improving surface-water quality, and official data in the following years reported increases in the proportion of monitored sites reaching good quality. The river chief system, begun in Jiangsu in 2007 after the Taihu bloom and extended nationwide by 2018, made local officials accountable for specific rivers.

Engineering responses included the South–North Water Transfer Project, conceived in the 1950s and implemented in stages: the eastern route began delivering water in 2013 and the middle route from the Danjiangkou Reservoir to Beijing and Tianjin in 2014, with relocation of hundreds of thousands of people near the reservoir. Cities in the north used the water to relieve groundwater overuse; farmers in some source regions lost water and land. The experience illustrates trade-offs between regional development and the interests of residents who bear costs of large infrastructure, and parallels earlier state-led water projects in Chinese history.

Section 75.35

Land Degradation, Soil Pollution, Biodiversity, and Ecological Protection

Soil contamination from heavy metals, industrial sites, and agricultural chemicals affected some farmland; a national survey published in 2014 reported that 16.1 percent of monitoring points exceeded national standards (a figure for sampled points, not for the share of China's total land area), and a Soil Pollution Prevention and Control Law took effect in 2019. Erosion on the Loess Plateau, with its long history of settlement and agriculture (Chapters 2 and 4), and desertification in northern and western regions threatened livelihoods. Wetland loss and habitat fragmentation affected biodiversity.

Large state-led ecological programs sought to reverse some damage: the Three-North Shelterbelt program begun in 1978, the Grain for Green program from 1999 that paid farmers to convert steep farmland to forest or grass, nationwide logging restrictions after the 1998 floods, and the establishment of national parks and protected areas. Forest cover increased according to official data, though scholars note that plantations differ ecologically from natural forests. Rural communities experienced these programs differently, receiving payments in some cases and losing farmland or grazing rights in others.

Land degradation has a long history in China, with deforestation, erosion, and overgrazing affecting the Loess Plateau and northern grasslands for centuries (Chapters 4, 43, 49). In the modern period, the extension of cultivation, mining, urban expansion, and irrigation placed new pressures on soils and ecosystems. A national soil survey, whose results were released in 2014, reported that 16.1 percent of monitoring points exceeded national standards; this refers to sampled points and not to the share of China's total land area, and cultivated land and areas near industrial sites showed higher rates. The 2019 Soil Pollution Prevention and Control Law set up classification of farmland and obligations for polluters.

State ecological programs were large in scale. The Three-North Shelterbelt program, begun in 1978, planted trees across northern China to curb desertification, with mixed ecological results where species were poorly matched to the climate. After catastrophic floods on the Yangtze in 1998, authorities banned commercial logging in natural forests and in 1999 launched the Grain for Green program, paying farmers to retire steep farmland. Official reports indicate rising forest cover, and satellite studies show greening in many areas, although scientists note differences between plantation monocultures and natural forests, and water-use implications of tree planting in dry regions.

Biodiversity pressures persist. The Yangtze river dolphin (baiji) was declared functionally extinct in the 2000s, while conservation successes included the recovery of populations of some species such as the giant panda. In 2021, China designated its first group of national parks, including Sanjiangyuan (the Three-River-Source), the Giant Panda park, the Northeast China Tiger and Leopard park, Hainan Tropical Rainforest, and Wuyishan. Conservation policy, including "ecological redlines" that limit development, has implications for pastoralists, farmers, and villagers within protected areas, some of whom were resettled or compensated. Evidence comes from satellite data, government reports, ecological studies, and community accounts.

Section 75.36

Coal, Hydropower, Electricity, and the Changing Energy System

Coal powered China's industrialization and still supplied the largest single share of primary energy at the end of the period covered here, although its share fell from the very high levels of earlier decades. Electricity demand grew with industry, urban growth, air conditioning, and appliances, and rural electrification reached nearly all households. Concerns about energy security, since China imports large amounts of oil and gas, shaped policy and reinforced the role of domestic coal.

Hydropower expanded greatly: the Three Gorges Dam on the Yangtze, completed in the 2000s, provided electricity, flood control, and navigation improvements but required the relocation of more than a million people and altered ecosystems, and was debated domestically and internationally. Dams on rivers in the southwest, including in Yunnan and Sichuan, supplied power to eastern cities through long-distance transmission, raising issues of displacement of local and minority communities and ecological effects. Power shortages in 2021 and 2022, caused by coal supply problems, extreme weather, and demand, underscored the tension between reliability and decarbonization.

The energy system expanded quickly. Electricity generation grew many times over between 1990 and the 2020s; coal accounted for around seventy percent of primary energy in the early 2010s and a bit more than half by the 2020s, according to official statistics, while oil and gas imports rose. Large coal bases in Shanxi, Inner Mongolia, and Shaanxi supplied power plants and heavy industry, and coal-mining regions saw both prosperity and environmental harm, including subsidence and water depletion. Mine safety improved markedly after campaigns in the 2000s and 2010s, although accidents still occurred.

Hydropower projects redistributed benefits and costs. The Three Gorges project on the Yangtze, approved in 1992, built between 1994 and 2006, and fully operational in 2012, relocated over a million people and flooded historic sites and farmland, with supporters pointing to flood control and clean power and critics to sedimentation, landslides, and displacement. In the southwest, cascades of dams on the Jinsha, Lancang (Mekong), Nu (Salween), and Yalong rivers delivered power east and raised concerns about downstream countries, ecosystems, and minority communities.

Reliability concerns shaped policy. In late 2021, electricity rationing in several provinces, especially in the northeast, resulted from coal shortages, price controls, and demand surges; in summer 2022, a drought and heat wave reduced hydropower in Sichuan and forced factory closures and curbs on electricity use in cities. These events strengthened arguments for coal capacity as backup, and also for grid upgrades, storage, and more diversified generation. The dilemmas of the energy system thus connected environmental goals to household and industrial reliance on stable power.

Section 75.37

Solar, Wind, Electric Vehicles, and the Energy Transition

Government support, manufacturing capacity, and large domestic demand turned China into the leading producer of solar panels, wind turbines, and batteries, and the largest installer of solar and wind capacity. Policies included subsidies, feed-in tariffs, targets, and industrial planning, and led to a steep fall in global prices for solar equipment. Electric vehicles (new-energy vehicles) grew rapidly from the 2010s, supported by purchase incentives, charging infrastructure, and city licence-plate policies, and by the mid-2020s they accounted for a large and rising share of passenger-car sales (the category includes plug-in hybrids as well as battery-electric vehicles; see below for the 2025 retail share according to industry data).

The transition faced obstacles: grid integration, curtailment of wind and solar power when transmission was inadequate, energy storage, regional imbalances between generation in the west and demand in the east, and continued approval of new coal plants for reliability. Manufacturers also experienced overcapacity and price competition, and international trade disputes arose over clean-technology exports.

China's role in clean energy resulted from policy and market forces. Early support came from feed-in tariffs and subsidies for wind and solar in the 2000s and 2010s; local governments financed solar factories; and manufacturers scaled up production, driving prices down. By the 2020s, China manufactured the large majority of the world's solar modules and a large share of batteries, and it was installing more solar and wind capacity annually than any other country. Large solar and wind bases were built in the northwest and north, and ultra-high-voltage lines transmitted power to eastern cities.

The electric vehicle story combined industrial policy and consumer choice. Pilot subsidies for new-energy vehicles began around 2009; purchase subsidies and license-plate privileges for electric cars in cities with restrictions encouraged adoption; and charging infrastructure expanded. The category of new-energy vehicles includes both battery-electric and plug-in hybrid vehicles, and in 2025 they accounted for approximately 53.9 percent of domestic passenger-vehicle retail sales according to the China Passenger Car Association, while battery-only electric vehicles made up a smaller share. This retail passenger-car share must not be confused with the approximately 47.9 percent share of all new vehicle sales recorded by the China Association of Automobile Manufacturers. Chinese firms, including BYD and newer companies, expanded exports, leading to trade disputes with the European Union and the United States over subsidies and tariffs.

Challenges remain. Power grids needed upgrades to handle variable generation, and in some years a portion of wind and solar output was curtailed. Mining and processing of materials for batteries raised environmental and labor issues, domestically and abroad. Overcapacity and price wars in solar and automotive industries led, in 2025, to official calls to curb "involution-style" competition. Moreover, fossil-fuel plants continued to be built, and the transition's pace and ultimate effect on emissions depended on how the new capacity displaced coal.

Section 75.38

Climate Policy, Carbon Commitments, and Environmental Challenges

China took part in international climate negotiations and, in 2015, committed under the Paris Agreement to peak carbon dioxide emissions around 2030. In September 2020, the leadership announced that China would aim to peak emissions before 2030 and reach carbon neutrality before 2060, goals elaborated in policy documents in 2021 that established a "1+N" framework, including guidance on peaking and neutrality and sector plans. In September 2025, China announced a new national contribution that included a target of reducing net greenhouse gas emissions by 7–10 percent from peak levels by 2035. These are targets and pledges; they are not achieved outcomes, and the timing of the peak depends on economic growth, industrial structure, energy use, and policy implementation.

Adaptation concerns grew as extreme weather, floods, droughts, and heat waves affected agriculture and cities, and sea-level rise threatened coastal regions. Climate policy intersects with other goals, including employment in coal regions, local government revenue, food and energy security, and international competitiveness.

China's climate policy evolved from emphasizing development rights to accepting commitments. Before 2009 it resisted binding targets, arguing that industrialized countries bore historical responsibility; at the Copenhagen conference in 2009 it announced an intensity target for carbon dioxide per unit of GDP; the 2014 China–United States joint announcement and the 2015 Paris Agreement marked a turn toward a peaking commitment. In 2020, the pledge to reach carbon neutrality before 2060 broadened the framework, and in July 2021 a national emissions-trading system began operating for power generators, among the largest in the world by volume of emissions covered.

The September 2025 national contribution announced a target of reducing net greenhouse gas emissions by 7–10 percent from peak levels by 2035, which analysts viewed as modest by some comparisons and as significant by others, given the scale of China's emissions. Emissions data for the mid-2020s, as reported by independent analysts, suggested that growth in emissions had slowed or plateaued in some periods as clean-energy additions expanded, but whether a durable peak has been reached is a question that students should treat as unresolved in the sources available to this textbook.

Climate effects are already present. Heat waves, floods such as those of 2021 in Henan, and droughts affected agriculture, cities, and infrastructure; rising sea levels threaten coastal cities such as Shanghai and Guangzhou; glacier retreat on the Tibetan Plateau alters river flows for downstream populations. Adaptation policy included "sponge city" programs to manage urban flooding, but capacities varied with local resources. Climate policy therefore involves distribution of costs and benefits across regions, such as coal-dependent provinces versus industrial centers and wealthy coasts.

Public Health, Healthcare, and Social Welfare

How economic development, demographic aging, and public health crises transformed the institutions supporting people's health and welfare.

Section 75.39

Public Health and Healthcare After the Reform Era

In the Mao era, urban workers received medical care through work units and government insurance, while rural residents relied on cooperative medical systems and the "barefoot doctors," village health workers trained to provide basic care and public health measures (Chapters 66–68). These arrangements achieved large gains in life expectancy and infant survival at low cost, though quality and coverage varied. During the reform era, rural collectives dissolved and state-owned enterprises restructured, undermining many of these arrangements. Hospitals, financed increasingly by patient charges and drug sales, became market-oriented, and out-of-pocket spending rose, leaving many uninsured, especially in rural areas. "Difficult and expensive access to care" became a widespread complaint.

From the late 1990s, the state began constructing new insurance systems, discussed in Section 75.42, and from 2009 launched a major healthcare reform that increased public funding for primary care, essential medicines, and insurance. Hospitals, especially large urban public hospitals, remained crowded as patients bypassed local clinics, and tensions between patients and medical staff, including incidents of violence against doctors, reflected strains in trust and resources.

The Mao-era health system was impressive in its reach but limited in resources. Cooperative medical schemes and barefoot doctors extended basic care to most villages by the 1970s, public health campaigns tackled schistosomiasis, malaria, and other infectious diseases, and life expectancy rose significantly. The reform era's dissolution of communes ended the financial base of the rural cooperatives, and coverage dropped to a small minority of villages by the 1990s; state hospitals and clinics were told to raise revenue through fees and drug sales, producing incentives to prescribe expensive tests and medicines.

The results included inequities and public dissatisfaction. Studies reported that large shares of rural households faced catastrophic health spending, and patients who could not pay were sometimes turned away. Doctors, whose official salaries were low, often relied on bonuses linked to revenue, contributing to mistrust. From 2009, reform measures aimed at "basic healthcare for all" included expanding insurance, strengthening township and community clinics, creating an essential medicines list, and from 2017 ending the markup on drugs in public hospitals. The Healthy China 2030 plan (2016) set longer-term goals for prevention and chronic-disease control.

Challenges remain in primary care. Many patients prefer large urban hospitals, which are crowded, while community clinics are underused; staff shortages in rural areas persist; and private hospitals and online consultation services grew in the 2010s and 2020s. Public health institutions, including disease control centers, were rebuilt after SARS but faced uneven funding. For historians, sources include health yearbooks, surveys, hospital records, and the testimony of patients and medical workers, each reflecting different parts of the system.

Section 75.40

The SARS Epidemic of 2002–2003 and Public Health Reform

Severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS) emerged in Guangdong Province in late 2002. Early cases were not publicly reported promptly, information was restricted, and the disease spread to other provinces and countries. In spring 2003, public statements that minimized the scale of the outbreak were challenged by a retired military physician in Beijing, and international attention intensified. On April 20, 2003, the government removed the health minister and Beijing's mayor, acknowledged the epidemic's scale, and mobilized large containment measures, including isolation, quarantine, hospital construction, and travel controls. The World Health Organization declared the global outbreak contained in July 2003.

The crisis prompted reforms: a national web-based system for direct reporting of infectious diseases, a revised Law on the Prevention and Control of Infectious Diseases, investment in public health institutions and the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention, and enhanced cooperation with the WHO. It also reshaped public expectations about information and official accountability. Historians note that SARS was a smaller epidemic in China than COVID-19 and that its lessons were applied unevenly later.

The course of SARS illustrates the role of information in epidemics. Cases in Foshan and Guangzhou in late 2002 were handled as an atypical pneumonia, and local health authorities reported to higher levels but information was not made widely public; a medical worker traveling to Hong Kong in February 2003 seeded outbreaks that spread internationally. In April 2003, a retired military surgeon in Beijing, Jiang Yanyong, wrote that officials were understating the number of cases in the capital, and after international pressure and internal debate, the government acknowledged the problem; on April 20 the health minister and Beijing's mayor were removed, and daily reporting began.

The response was sweeping. Authorities cancelled the May Day holiday, closed schools in some cities, set up fever checkpoints, quarantined buildings and hospitals, and built a dedicated hospital in Beijing's Xiaotangshan area in about a week. Internationally, around eight thousand cases and some 770 deaths were recorded in about thirty countries, with the largest numbers in mainland China, Hong Kong, Taiwan, Singapore, and Canada. WHO declared the outbreak contained in July 2003. The epidemic affected tourism, retail, and transport, and had short-term economic effects.

Institutional reforms followed: a national online disease reporting system (launched in 2004), a revised Law on Prevention and Control of Infectious Diseases (2004), investments in hospitals and laboratories, and emergency-response plans. SARS also changed public expectations of transparency, and the expansion of the internet allowed information to circulate in ways that would shape the response to later crises. Historians debate how far the reforms addressed underlying problems, such as incentives for local officials to avoid reporting bad news, and how the SARS experience influenced the design of COVID-19 controls.

Section 75.41

COVID-19, Public Health Restrictions, and the End of Zero-COVID

COVID-19 was first identified in Wuhan in late 2019. Local authorities initially acknowledged cases slowly, and a Wuhan doctor, Li Wenliang, who had warned colleagues, was reprimanded by police and later died of the disease, becoming a focus of public grief and debate. On January 23, 2020, authorities locked down Wuhan and, soon afterward, much of Hubei Province, and national measures followed, including community-level controls, mass testing, quarantine facilities, travel restrictions, and mobile-phone health codes that determined access to public spaces. Domestic transmission was brought low in much of 2020–2021, and the economy recovered in part, but a "dynamic zero-COVID" strategy meant that outbreaks of the more transmissible Omicron variant triggered lockdowns in 2022, including a prolonged lockdown of Shanghai in spring 2022.

Experiences varied: residents of locked-down cities faced difficulties in obtaining food and medical care; migrant workers, small businesses, and students were affected; some communities praised protective measures, especially early in the epidemic, while others criticized disruptions. In late November 2022, protests occurred in several cities. On December 7, 2022, the government announced a set of measures substantially relaxing testing, quarantine, and lockdown requirements, and a large wave of infection followed. Official reports of deaths covered mainly hospital deaths with COVID diagnoses, while independent researchers estimated substantially higher excess mortality; estimates differ, and students should compare methods and definitions rather than treat any one number as definitive.

Early evidence on COVID-19 comes from clinical reports, genomic studies, and official statements. Chinese authorities notified the World Health Organization of cases of pneumonia of unknown cause on December 31, 2019, and shared the virus's genetic sequence in January 2020. Wuhan's lockdown beginning January 23 affected some eleven million people; some 42,000 medical staff from other provinces were dispatched to Hubei, and temporary "Fangcang" shelter hospitals were built to isolate mild cases. Li Wenliang, an ophthalmologist who had warned colleagues, died on February 7, and public reaction showed deep concern about information control. The central government later emphasized the achievements of strict control, and domestic spread was largely contained by spring 2020.

From 2021, the strategy of "dynamic zero" aimed to detect and eliminate clusters of cases through mass testing, contact tracing, quarantine centers, and area lockdowns. Domestic vaccines, including inactivated vaccines, were widely administered, though vaccination rates among the oldest age groups were lower than in younger groups. Outbreaks of the Omicron variant in 2022 strained the approach. In Shanghai, a large-scale lockdown from late March to early June, affecting about twenty-five million residents, disrupted food supply, medical access, and incomes, and prompted both mutual-aid efforts and complaints. In late November 2022, after a fatal apartment fire in Urumqi in which many believed that controls had hampered rescue (officials disputed this), protests took place in several cities.

The end of zero-COVID was abrupt. After the December 7 measures, infections surged across the country in December 2022 and January 2023, stressing hospitals, crematoria, and pharmacies. In February 2023, the National Health Commission reported nearly sixty thousand deaths in hospitals from December 8 to early February; epidemiological studies estimated much larger numbers of excess deaths, with ranges that vary by method, and WHO urged China to share more data. Students should compare these sources, noting differences in definitions, coverage, and assumptions. The pandemic's wider effects included disruptions in education, employment, and travel, changes in consumption, and heightened attention to health and resilience.

Section 75.42

Healthcare Insurance, Hospitals, and Access to Treatment

China built a nearly universal basic insurance structure in about two decades. Basic medical insurance for urban employees was established in 1998; the New Rural Cooperative Medical Scheme, launched in 2003, offered voluntary, subsidized coverage for rural residents; insurance for non-employed urban residents followed in 2007. Policy later merged the urban resident and rural schemes in many places to reduce disparities, and official statistics report basic insurance coverage of the great majority of the population. Public subsidies for resident insurance increased, and serious-illness insurance and medical assistance were added.

Coverage does not equal access. Reimbursement rates vary among schemes and regions; patients often pay deductibles and uncovered costs; high-quality hospitals are concentrated in large cities and in the east; rural clinics face shortages of qualified staff; and migrants have faced complications when seeking reimbursement away from where they are insured. Policy innovations include centralized procurement to lower drug prices and efforts to build tiered care with family doctors and community health centers, with mixed results so far.

The design of the insurance system reflected China's divided social structure. Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance, established in 1998, covered formal-sector workers with contributions from employers and employees; the New Rural Cooperative Medical Scheme (2003) and Urban Resident Basic Medical Insurance (2007) provided subsidized, voluntary coverage for others. A 2016 State Council opinion directed the merger of the urban resident and rural schemes, and in 2018 a National Healthcare Security Administration was created to manage insurance and negotiate prices. By the late 2010s, basic insurance covered more than 95 percent of the population, according to official statistics.

Despite broad coverage, benefits differ: employee insurance typically reimburses a higher share of costs than the resident insurance, with higher ceilings, and patients often pay for services outside the reimbursement list. The out-of-pocket share of total health spending fell from around sixty percent in 2001 to under thirty percent in the 2020s, a notable change, but families with serious illness, particularly cancer or chronic kidney disease, still faced large expenses, and informal payments and gifts were reported. Cross-regional settlement of hospital bills for patients treated away from home improved in the late 2010s, benefiting migrants and retirees.

The government attempted to lower prices through centralized procurement of drugs and medical consumables, beginning with pilot programs in 2018 that negotiated large price cuts, and by incorporating new medicines into the insurance catalog through annual negotiations. Observers note benefits in cost reduction and access, alongside concerns about supply and the effects on pharmaceutical firms. Hospital systems, led by large public hospitals, retain dominant roles, and reforms to payment methods are still in progress.

Section 75.43

Social Assistance, Pensions, and the Expansion of Welfare Protection

As work-unit welfare declined, new national programs emerged. A unified basic pension for urban employees took shape in the late 1990s; unemployment insurance became more formal; and the urban minimum livelihood guarantee (dibao, 低保) was extended nationwide around 1999, followed by a rural counterpart in 2007. A New Rural Pension Scheme from 2009 and a residents' scheme merged in 2014 gave rural and non-employed urban residents modest basic pensions. The 2011 Social Insurance Law codified the framework, and the government announced in 2020 that it had eliminated extreme poverty as defined by its standard, a claim based on a specific poverty line and criteria.

Social insurance was constructed in stages. Reforms in the late 1990s created a pension system with a social pool and individual accounts for urban employees, though many individual accounts were "empty," with funds used to pay current retirees. Unemployment insurance (1999) and work-injury and maternity insurance (2000s) were added; in 2014, the rural and urban resident pensions were merged into a unified scheme; and in 2015, civil-service and public-institution pensions were brought into a unified system. National pooling of pension funds began in 2022, to shift resources from regions with younger workforces to those with older ones, and private personal pension pilots started in the same year.

Public assistance changed too. Dibao started in Shanghai in 1993 and extended nationwide in 1999 for cities and 2007 for the countryside, with local governments setting thresholds. Beneficiaries numbered tens of millions in the late 2010s, and applicants faced assessments of income and assets that could be subjective. The targeted poverty alleviation campaign launched in 2013 combined cash transfers, relocation of villagers, investments in infrastructure, and industrial support, and in 2020 the government announced that extreme rural poverty, defined by a line of about 2,300 yuan per year at 2010 prices, had been eliminated, with nearly 100 million rural residents having been lifted from poverty since 2012 according to official figures. International institutions use higher poverty lines, and the poverty line choice affects conclusions.

The system's weaknesses include fragmentation, uneven benefit levels, and participation gaps among migrants and platform workers, who may not be enrolled where they work. Rural basic pensions have been small relative to living costs, though they increased in the 2020s, and many older people continue to work. Historians connecting the history of welfare to earlier periods may note that the idea of state granaries and relief in imperial times (Chapters 15, 26, 41) is different from modern entitlements, though both involve state obligations to subjects.

Historical Vocabulary
Social Insurance, Dibao, and Public Health

Social insurance (社会保险) refers to contributory or subsidized programs covering pensions, medical care, unemployment, work injury, and maternity. Dibao (低保) is the minimum livelihood guarantee, a means-tested cash assistance program for households below locally set thresholds. Public health refers to population-level measures such as disease surveillance, vaccination, sanitation, and health education, distinct from individual medical treatment.

Limits persisted. Levels of benefits differed widely: urban employees' pensions were much higher than rural residents' basic pensions; local governments financed much of the system, producing regional differences; and informal and migrant workers had low participation. Dibao thresholds and payments varied locally, and eligibility procedures sometimes excluded needy households. The pension system faced pressure from aging and a shrinking workforce, as fewer contributors supported more retirees.

Section 75.44

Elder Care, Disability, Chronic Illness, and the Future of Social Provision

As the older population grew, so did demand for care. Most older people lived at home with family; public policy emphasized "home-based, community-supported, institution-supplemented" care. Nursing homes and community services expanded, but supply, quality, and affordability varied, and rural areas lacked services as young adults left. Long-term care insurance was piloted in selected cities from 2016 and was expanded. Dementia, chronic diseases such as hypertension and diabetes, and cancers became leading health burdens, replacing infectious diseases as the main causes of mortality.

Services for people with disabilities expanded through law, rehabilitation programs, and accessibility requirements, though families bore much of the burden. Mental health services were limited relative to need, despite the Mental Health Law of 2012, and stigma discouraged help-seeking. The scale of these demands shows that the future of social provision involves choices about financing, the role of families, markets, and the state, and the labor of caregivers, who are disproportionately women, including migrant domestic workers.

Elder care in practice depends on family, community, and market. Community programs include day-care centers and meal services in cities, "mutual aid" arrangements in villages, and volunteer visits; private and nonprofit nursing homes expanded, though affordability and quality are uneven, and many older people prefer to stay at home. Long-term care insurance pilots, which began in fifteen cities in 2016 and extended to dozens more by 2020, provide payments or services for those needing help with daily living, but a nationwide system had not been established by the mid-2020s. Cultural attitudes toward institutional care have changed gradually, with stigma declining in some places.

Chronic diseases and disability form a large part of the health burden. Cardiovascular diseases, cancers, diabetes, and respiratory conditions account for most deaths, and risk factors include smoking, diet, air pollution, and aging. China has more than eighty million people with disabilities according to national surveys; laws and the ratification of the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities in 2008 established principles of accessibility and equal participation, while practical services in education, employment, and rehabilitation remain uneven. Mental health care expanded under the 2012 Mental Health Law, yet psychiatrists and counselors are few relative to population, and stigma hinders treatment.

These issues point to a larger question about how a society allocates responsibility for care among families, markets, and the state. Women, who provide most unpaid care, and migrant domestic workers, who provide much paid care, carry heavy burdens; policies such as raising pensions, supporting caregivers, and extending insurance would change this balance. The historical record to date shows both rapid institutional growth and a gap between needs and provision.

Culture, Consumption, and Changing Everyday Life

Restoring the human dimension to the preceding institutional and statistical histories, and showing what modernization meant to different people.

Section 75.45

Consumer Culture, Leisure, and the Changing Meaning of Prosperity

Rising incomes transformed consumption. In the 1980s, desirable goods were bicycles, watches, and sewing machines; in the 1990s, televisions, refrigerators, and washing machines; in the 2000s, apartments, cars, and mobile phones; and later, travel, dining, education, and services. Shopping malls, supermarkets, chain restaurants, and brands expanded, and international and domestic tourism boomed, with tens or hundreds of millions of domestic trips during holiday periods. The "Golden Week" holidays introduced in 1999 organized national travel rhythms.

Consumption differed sharply among groups. Higher-income urban households purchased luxury goods and overseas travel; rural households spent a larger share of income on food and basic needs and often limited spending to save for housing, education, and health. Prosperity acquired changing meanings, from having enough to eat, to material comfort, to quality of life and personal fulfillment. Concerns grew about debt, waste, and the pressure to display status, and policy sought to boost domestic consumption to rebalance an economy long reliant on investment and exports (Chapter 74).

Consumer culture developed in distinct stages. The 1980s saw the first wave of durable goods; the 1990s introduced department stores and foreign brands; the 2000s brought housing, automobiles, and large-scale tourism; and the 2010s added online shopping, travel abroad, coffee shops, and fitness. Household consumption as a share of GDP remained comparatively low, partly because of high savings motivated by uncertainty about health, education, and old-age costs, and partly because of the high share of income going to investment and firms. Rebalancing toward consumption was a repeated policy goal.

Leisure also changed. The introduction of week-long holidays around May Day, National Day, and the Spring Festival in 1999 created peak travel periods; the 2008 reform restored traditional festivals such as Qingming and the Mid-Autumn Festival as public holidays. Domestic tourism reached about six billion trips in 2019, and outbound trips exceeded 150 million before the pandemic, with Chinese travelers becoming important in global tourism. After COVID-19 restrictions ended, "revenge travel" surged, along with new patterns such as short trips to small cities made popular through social media.

Class and values shaped consumption. Wealthier consumers sought luxury goods and experiences, while many households emphasized education and health expenditures; young people embraced trends in fashion, cosmetics, and collectibles, and "guochao" (national-trend) products promoting Chinese designs gained popularity. At the same time, minimalist or frugal lifestyles, second-hand markets, and "rational consumption" appeared among those weary of consumerism or facing financial pressures. Consumption thus reveals social identities and aspirations, and should not be reduced to national totals.

Section 75.46

Literature, Film, Television, and Popular Music

Cultural production became both commercial and regulated. Publishing, film, and television expanded; Chinese cinema grew into one of the world's largest box-office markets, while domestic television dramas, variety shows, and the annual Spring Festival Gala reached vast audiences. Novelists, filmmakers, and musicians addressed urbanization, migration, family change, history, and memory; popular music evolved from Hong Kong and Taiwan pop through mainland pop, rock, and hip-hop to digital-era genres. Foreign works circulated in translation, though quotas and censorship limited imports.

The state sets rules for content through licensing, review, and guidelines on themes considered sensitive, while also promoting patriotic and "mainstream" works and supporting cultural industries. Creators negotiated these constraints in different ways, through genre, allegory, humor, or self-censorship, and some works reached international audiences, as with animated and fantasy films and, in the 2020s, video games. Students should avoid treating cultural output as either simple propaganda or simple resistance.

Cultural industries expanded alongside commercialization. Publishing houses and bookstores multiplied in the 1990s and 2000s; television channels increased, with provincial satellite channels competing with China Central Television; and since 1983 the CCTV Spring Festival Gala has become a shared annual ritual. The film industry, reformed in the 1990s, grew rapidly after 2002, building thousands of multiplex screens; Chinese-language films reached record box-office totals in the 2010s, such as the 2017 action film Wolf Warrior 2, and in 2025 the animated film Ne Zha 2 became the highest-grossing animated film worldwide, according to contemporaneous box-office reporting and Guinness World Records. Its exceptional commercial success was concentrated in the Chinese domestic market; published worldwide grosses and their dollar conversions vary by reporting date and methodology.

Literary life included both established and experimental writers. Mo Yan received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 2012, and Liu Cixin's science-fiction novel The Three-Body Problem, translated into English, won the Hugo Award in 2015, drawing international attention to Chinese science fiction. Writers and filmmakers addressed topics from rural life and migration to family conflict and historical memory, though censorship, funding, and market demand shaped what was produced. Film regulators set approval processes, quotas for imported films increased after China's entry into the WTO (Chapter 74), and cultural policy encouraged works promoting national themes alongside commercial entertainment.

Popular music extended from Hong Kong and Taiwan pop to mainland rock, folk, hip-hop, and online songs, with talent shows and streaming platforms creating new routes to fame. Regional languages, such as Cantonese and Sichuanese, appear in music and film, despite the promotion of Mandarin. For historians, the major issue is how to read cultural products as evidence: they reveal ideas and aspirations, the preferences of audiences, and the limits imposed by institutions, but they are not transparent windows on society.

Section 75.47

Online Entertainment, Gaming, and New Cultural Communities

Short video, live streaming, online literature, streaming series, and mobile games became central to leisure. Social-media celebrities and live-stream sellers built large followings, rural creators showcased village life, and fan communities organized around music, television, and games. Online novels, a major source of stories for film and television, developed a vast readership and a distinctive industry of paid serialization.

These communities brought economic opportunities, including for small-town creators, and spaces for identity and friendship, but also raised regulatory and social concerns: youth gaming limits introduced in 2021, restrictions on celebrity "fan economy" practices, content rules, and debates over addiction and screen time. Online communities also hosted nationalist sentiment, feminist and anti-feminist arguments, and subcultures, showing that digital culture is not monolithic.

The platform era multiplied forms of entertainment. Online literature websites such as Qidian, founded in 2002, pioneered a model of paid serialization that produced huge readerships for fantasy, romance, and historical fiction, and these stories were adapted into television series, comics, and games. Video platforms and short-video apps provided vast quantities of content, and live-streaming allowed performers, gamers, and sellers to interact with audiences in real time. The mobile game market grew to be one of the largest in the world, and in 2024 a Chinese-developed action role-playing game based on Journey to the West, Black Myth: Wukong, drew global attention to Chinese game development.

The social meanings were varied. Fan communities organized around celebrities, sports teams, and online novels developed their own norms, charitable activities, and conflicts. Young people formed communities around hobbies such as hanfu (traditional-style clothing), anime, and e-sports; older people used short-video apps and square-dance groups, expanding online and offline sociability. Rural creators gained audiences by showing farm life, and some became influential sellers of regional produce.

Regulators intervened in several domains: in 2021, rules limited minors' online gaming to about one hour on certain days; campaigns against disorderly "fan circle" practices targeted online ranking contests and celebrity scandals; and platforms were required to implement youth modes and content controls. Supporters emphasized protection of children and public order; critics, including some gamers and industry observers, pointed to the economic costs and the limits on expression. The evidence for effects comes from surveys, company reports, and observation, with debate over their reliability.

Section 75.48

Cultural Heritage, Religion, Local Traditions, and Community Life

Religious and community life revived and diversified after the Cultural Revolution (Chapter 70). The state officially recognizes five religions (Buddhism, Daoism, Islam, Catholicism, and Protestantism) under state-supervised associations, within a legal framework regulating religious activity; alongside these, popular religion, ancestor veneration, temple festivals, lineage halls, and local deities have reappeared widely, especially in southeastern villages (Chapters 28, 50). Christianity grew among both registered and unregistered congregations; Islam is practiced by communities including Hui and Uyghur peoples, among others; and Tibetan Buddhism is central in Tibetan and many Mongol communities. Policy toward religion has varied by period and place and has become more restrictive in some respects in recent years; specific policies in Tibet and Xinjiang are examined in Chapter 76.

Heritage preservation became a prominent field: historic districts, museums, and intangible cultural heritage listings, including UNESCO inscriptions, helped sustain crafts, festivals, and performing arts, even as redevelopment demolished older neighborhoods. Festivals such as the Spring Festival, Qingming, and Mid-Autumn Festival continued as occasions for family travel and ritual, and the Spring Festival migration, one of the largest annual movements of people in the world, linked cities and villages. Regional diversity in dialect, cuisine, ritual, and custom persisted alongside the spread of standard Mandarin through schooling and media.

Religious revival after 1978 took many forms. The state recognizes five religions, with official associations, and religious activities are restricted to registered venues; it has also developed policy on the "sinicization" of religion, emphasizing adaptation to Chinese conditions and the leadership of the Party, and a revised set of Regulations on Religious Affairs took effect in 2018. Buddhist and Daoist temples were rebuilt and attracted pilgrims and tourists, some temple festivals became large events, and in southeastern provinces such as Fujian and Zhejiang, local gods like Mazu and community temple associations organized processions, theater, and charity. Protestant and Catholic communities include registered churches and unregistered house churches, and Muslim communities include Hui, Uyghur, and others with differing practices and relations to state institutions.

Heritage and local tradition were reorganized by tourism, policy, and local initiative. Historic towns, such as Pingyao or Lijiang, were developed for visitors; intangible cultural heritage programs have recognized folk crafts, performing arts, and festivals; and a national list of heritage items was complemented by UNESCO inscriptions, including, in 2024, the Spring Festival social practices. The commercialization of heritage raised questions about authenticity and the displacement of residents, while many communities continued practices on their own terms. Local lineage halls, rebuilt in some villages, hosted ancestor rituals and genealogies, reflecting the continuity of lineage organization in some regions (Chapters 34, 41, 49).

Language policy and identity also shifted. The promotion of standard Mandarin (putonghua) through schools, television, and migration reduced the daily use of some dialects among younger urban generations; protests in Guangzhou in 2010 against a proposal to reduce Cantonese on local television illustrated attachment to regional language. Minority languages are used in education and media to varying degrees. The picture is one of coexisting standardization and persistence of local identity, with variation by region, generation, and community.

Section 75.49

Young People, Social Expectations, and the Diversity of Contemporary Chinese Life

Young Chinese adults of the 2010s and 2020s grew up in a society of greater material comfort, smaller families, and more choices than their parents, but also of intense competition. Many described pressure to succeed in education, find stable jobs, buy housing, marry, and care for aging parents. Long working hours in some sectors, the "996" schedule, drew public criticism. Marriage registrations declined sharply from their 2010s peak, birth rates fell, and the vocabulary of "lying flat" and "involution" expressed fatigue and skepticism about relentless competition, though most young people continued to work and study hard. Others pursued entrepreneurship, rural revitalization, volunteer work, and travel.

Diversity is essential here: a university graduate in Shanghai, an elderly resident in a rural county, a migrant delivery worker in Guangzhou, a herder in Inner Mongolia, and a household in an inland city have different experiences, shaped by income, region, gender, age, ethnicity, and registration. Generalizations about "young Chinese" or "Chinese society" therefore require caution.

Young people's attitudes cannot be summarized in one slogan. Surveys show strong pride in national achievements among many, along with anxiety about careers and housing. Terms such as "involution" (neijuan), popularized around 2020 to describe pointless competition, and "lying flat" (tangping), which spread after a 2021 online post advocating a minimalist life, captured widespread fatigue; the "996" work schedule (9 a.m. to 9 p.m., six days a week) was criticized in an online campaign in 2019, and labor regulators later stated that excessive overtime schedules violated the law. These expressions reflect the pressure on some, though they do not imply that all young people disengaged.

Family expectations remained influential. Parents often expect adult children to marry, buy a home, and have children, and bride-price demands in some regions drew criticism and official campaigns for simpler weddings. Single-person households and individuals who choose not to marry or have children increased, particularly in cities; some young people described "slow" or flexible approaches to adulthood. The pressures were gendered: women faced scrutiny about marriage age and employment, while men in rural areas faced demands for housing and bride payments.

The diversity of experience includes young herders in Inner Mongolia combining pastoralism with online selling; migrant youth in factory towns and delivery services; students at elite universities competing for graduate study or civil-service positions; rural returnees who start businesses in home counties; and young people in minority regions balancing local language, religion, and national schooling. What they share are some common media and institutions, but their prospects depend on region, family resources, and registration, which is the central lesson of the chapter.

Continuity and Change Over Time

Chinese Family and Community Life Across Three Generations

What Changed

  • Grandparents' generation (born 1940s–1950s): large families, rural collective or work-unit life, limited schooling, little mobility.
  • Parents' generation (born 1960s–1970s): reform-era work, migration or state-sector jobs, smaller families, new housing markets, schooling for children as a priority.
  • Grandchildren's generation (born 1990s–2000s): urban or university experience, digital life, delayed marriage, uncertain labor markets.
  • Family size, fertility, residence, education, communication technology, and material consumption changed profoundly.

What Stayed the Same

  • The family as the central unit of economic and emotional support, including intergenerational exchange of care and money.
  • Ritual and festival calendars, ancestral commemoration, and regional customs, often adapted rather than abandoned.
  • High value on education and concern for children's futures.
  • Rural-urban and regional inequalities, in changed forms.

Who experienced change most directly? Women who gained education and employment but carried care burdens; migrants who moved between worlds; and rural older people who stayed behind. Why it matters: The pattern shows that continuity and change coexisted and that modernization was experienced differently by gender, generation, and place.

Section 75.50

Chapter Review and Contemporary Social Change Checkpoint

This chapter has traced social transformation from the 1990s to the mid-2020s. The principal synthesis question is: How did China's social transformation from the 1990s to the 2020s create new opportunities in education, mobility, communication, health, and consumption while also producing demographic pressures, environmental costs, institutional inequalities, and competing expectations about the future? Students should be able to distinguish modernization from equal social improvement, and to identify which changes resulted chiefly from public policy (for example, birth limits, hukou rules, the 1999 university expansion, and clean-air regulation), which emerged through family and individual decisions (for example, delayed marriage, migration strategies, and educational investment), and which reflected interactions among economic, political, environmental, and technological factors (for example, platform labor and urban housing).

The chapter also reinforces several analytical distinctions: urban residence versus urban registration; fertility policy versus fertility outcomes; aging versus population decline; digital growth versus universal access; environmental targets versus environmental results; and insurance coverage versus access to care. Each distinction protects against reading national totals as if they described everyone's experience.

The relationships among the chapter's themes can be summarized as a set of linked processes. Urbanization and migration changed where people lived, but registration and housing shaped what they could obtain. Fertility decline and aging altered the family and the labor force, and shaped demands on education and welfare. Education and digital technology opened new routes for mobility and income, while creating competition and new forms of dependency. Environmental damage prompted regulation and technological change, with benefits and costs distributed unevenly. Health crises exposed weaknesses and prompted reform. And culture and everyday life reflected these changes in diverse ways. Students should be able to explain each of these relationships with specific evidence, and to identify the limits of that evidence.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that China's rapid economic development between about 1990 and 2025 was accompanied by fundamental transformations in how people lived, worked, learned, communicated, cared for one another, and experienced their environment. The country moved from a predominantly rural society to one in which, by the end of 2025, more than two-thirds of residents were counted as urban. The scale of change is real, but the chapter has stressed that it is best understood through distinctions, such as that between living in a city and holding its household registration, and through the varied experiences of migrants, established urban residents, relocated villagers, women, older people, and young graduates.

Urbanization reorganized geography, housing, and everyday routines. Metropolitan regions grew, high-rise housing replaced earlier forms, and infrastructure from metro lines to high-speed rail changed mobility. Land conversion financed growth but also produced displacement and disputes. Migration connected rural households to expanding cities, yet the hukou system and local fiscal arrangements limited migrants' access to services, producing family separation, precarious employment, and a form of inequality located inside cities.

Demographic change was a second major axis. Fertility fell sharply, through a combination of state policy and wide social and economic forces, and the transition from the one-child framework to two- and three-child policies did not restore higher birth rates. By 2022, population began to decline, and population aging, a distinct process, reduced the working-age population and increased demands on families and welfare. Sex-ratio imbalances, changing marriage patterns, and evolving gender roles reshaped family life, with women's rising education and persistent inequalities in work and domestic responsibility.

Educational expansion opened pathways for mobility, particularly after the 1999 university expansion, but competition for prestigious institutions, differences between regions and between rural and urban schools, and graduate underemployment showed that expansion and equal opportunity are not the same. Households responded with heavy investment in schooling, shaping decisions about housing, work, and family size.

Digital transformation changed communication, commerce, and work. Mobile payments, e-commerce, social media, and platform labor brought convenience, new livelihoods, and cultural communities, while raising issues of dependence, surveillance, privacy, algorithmic management, and exclusion of those with limited access or skills. Digital law, including the Personal Information Protection Law, shows both efforts to protect individuals from commercial misuse and the broad scope of state authority over data and content.

Environmental pressures, from air and water pollution to soil and ecological damage, accompanied industrial growth. Public concern and policy responses, including the 2013 air action plan, produced measurable improvements in some indicators, while coal remained central and regional stresses persisted. Renewable-energy and electric-vehicle manufacturing made China a leading actor in the energy transition, and climate pledges set long-term goals, which, as the chapter emphasized, remain targets, not achievements.

Health and welfare institutions were rebuilt after the decline of work-unit and collective provision. SARS exposed weaknesses in information and surveillance and led to reform; COVID-19 brought broader restrictions on daily life and a sudden policy reversal in December 2022, with outcomes that remain debated and depend on the sources used. Insurance and pension systems expanded coverage, but disparities in benefits, access, and quality remained, and aging raised demands for elder care and chronic-disease management.

Culture and everyday life diversified. Consumer culture, entertainment, online communities, religion, heritage, and local traditions evolved under market forces and regulation, and young people faced new aspirations and pressures. The chapter's evidence shows that modernization was not a single experience, and that generalizations must be tested against region, gender, age, ethnicity, and registration status.

For the larger history of Chinese civilization, the chapter demonstrates that the social consequences of economic growth were as significant as the growth itself, and that they were shaped by institutions inherited from earlier eras as well as by new policies and technologies. It also shows how historians work with recent evidence: with attention to definitions, years, methods, and the limits of official and unofficial sources. Chapter 76 turns to governance, territory, and China's global role, building on this social foundation.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows that the history of contemporary China is also a history of households. Behind the figures on urbanization, internet use, and population are people who moved, studied, married or did not, cared for children and parents, and adapted to institutions and technologies that changed faster than most earlier generations had experienced. Understanding these experiences is necessary for understanding the economic and political developments discussed in Chapters 74 and 76.

The chapter also reveals continuities. Registration systems that trace back to earlier eras of Chinese governance still shaped access to services; the family remained central; education retained its prestige as a route to advancement; and the state remained a powerful actor in the economy and society. At the same time, new elements, including mass digital life, platform work, low fertility, and an aging society, indicate that earlier patterns cannot simply be assumed to repeat.

China's social change also belongs to Asian and global history. Demographic aging, urbanization, and environmental stress appear in many societies, and Chinese firms and policies influence global technology and climate. Treating China as part of these wider processes, without minimizing its distinctive institutions, helps students see it as an interconnected society, not an isolated case.

Finally, the chapter illustrates the book's broader claim that Chinese history is diverse, contested, adaptive, regionally varied, and unfinished. The questions about aging, inequality, environment, and technology that students have examined remain open, and historians' interpretations will change as new evidence appears. Chapter 76 turns to governance, territory, and China's global role, where many of these social questions meet political ones.
