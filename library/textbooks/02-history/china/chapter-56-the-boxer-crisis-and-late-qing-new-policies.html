Part IX — Nineteenth-Century Crisis and the End of the Qing
56

The Boxer Crisis and Late Qing New Policies

China: A History of a Civilization

Chapter Opening

How did an imperial government that survived one of the worst crises in its history embark on extensive reforms that transformed Chinese society and government, yet fail to secure the long-term survival of the dynasty itself?

In the summer of 1900, the people of Beijing watched a city they thought they knew turn into something unrecognizable. Young men in red sashes drilled in the streets and claimed that spirits could make their bodies proof against bullets. Altars were set up in the courtyards of princely mansions. Rumors ran through the markets: that Christians had poisoned wells, that foreign soldiers were about to seize the city, that heaven itself was calling believers to cleanse the land. Within a few weeks, Chinese converts had been hunted down in the capital and the countryside around it, the foreign legations were under siege, and the Qing court had issued orders that put its own armies into conflict with eleven foreign governments. By mid-August, foreign troops were marching through the Forbidden City's outer gates, and the Empress Dowager Cixi was fleeing west in a farm cart, dressed as a peasant woman.

This chapter studies two connected episodes in the final dozen years of the Qing dynasty. The first is the Boxer Crisis of 1899–1901: the rise of a rural movement in Shandong and Zhili province, the violence against Chinese Christians and foreigners, the decisions of the Qing court, the siege of the legations and of Beitang Cathedral, the intervention of the Eight-Nation Alliance, and the punitive settlement of 1901. The second is the program of institutional change that followed, usually called the New Policies (xinzheng, 1901–1911). These reforms reorganized the army, abolished the examination system that had selected officials for more than a thousand years, rebuilt ministries, courts, and police forces, encouraged commerce and railways, and, from 1906, committed the dynasty to preparing a constitutional monarchy.

The chapter matters because it complicates two familiar stories. One holds that the Qing court was simply too conservative to change and was swept away in 1911 because it resisted modernization to the end. The other treats the Boxers as either a blind explosion of superstition or an early, unified national resistance to imperialism. The historical record supports neither. The Boxers were a diverse and changing set of local movements, shaped by drought, flood, hardship, local religion, and quarrels between villagers. The Qing government that followed them into war was divided, and many of its most powerful provincial governors refused to follow. And the government that emerged from defeat did more to rebuild itself between 1901 and 1911 than at any time since the Taiping wars, though in ways that created new political actors and new fiscal burdens that the dynasty could not control.

Much changed in this decade. The treaty system tightened around the Qing: a heavy indemnity, foreign garrisons, and razed forts. The examination system, which had shaped elite life, family strategy, and local society, was abolished in 1905. A generation of students studied in Japan, Europe, and the United States, and new professional groups, chambers of commerce, and provincial assemblies appeared. A new army, trained in modern drill and led by officers educated in military academies, became a political force in its own right. Meanwhile, other things continued: the dynasty remained a Manchu-led imperial house; Cixi dominated the court until 1908; provincial officials and local gentry retained great influence; and the rural majority continued to farm, pay taxes, and, increasingly, bear the cost of reforms designed in distant cities.

The people affected were many and unequal. Chinese Christians, both Catholic and Protestant, suffered tens of thousands of deaths. Missionaries, merchants, and diplomats were killed or besieged. Villagers in Shandong and Zhili endured famine, looting, and reprisals from several directions. Officials were executed on the court's orders, either for opposing the Boxers or for supporting them. Merchants in Shanghai, Nanjing, and Wuhan feared the loss of trade. After 1901, students, teachers, soldiers, police recruits, chamber-of-commerce members, women entering new schools, and taxpayers all lived through the reforms in different ways.

Historians draw on a broad range of evidence: Boxer placards and handbills, missionary and consular reports, diaries kept inside the legation quarter and Beitang, Chinese official memorials and telegrams, the text of the Boxer Protocol, Qing edicts, newspapers, memoirs, oral histories collected in Shandong in the twentieth century, and the statistical and regulatory record of the New Policies. Each source has its own bias, and the chapter's features teach students to read them critically.

Finally, the chapter connects China to wider histories. The Boxer War was a global event, in which armies from Japan, Russia, Britain, the United States, France, Germany, Italy, and Austria-Hungary fought together and competed in North China. The New Policies drew heavily on Japanese models, which themselves reflected Japan's adaptation of European institutions, and on Chinese students' travels. The chapter ends at the threshold of Chapter 57, which examines how revolutionary nationalism, provincial politics, and military mutiny brought the dynasty to an end in 1911–1912. Chapter 56 explains why the dynasty reached that threshold having changed so much, and why those changes did not secure its survival.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students should be able to:

  1. Explain the environmental, economic, religious, and political conditions in Shandong and Zhili that produced the Boxer movement.
  2. Describe the diversity of Boxer groups, beliefs, practices, and organization, and avoid treating them as a single unified movement.
  3. Analyze the causes of anti-Christian violence and the experiences of Chinese Christians as historical actors rather than extensions of foreign missions.
  4. Evaluate competing explanations for the Qing court's decision to support, tolerate, or oppose the Boxers.
  5. Trace the military and diplomatic sequence of 1900, from Tianjin and the Dagu forts through the sieges and the capture of Beijing.
  6. Distinguish the relief of besieged people from the looting, reprisals, and imperial rivalries of the Allied occupation.
  7. Interpret the Boxer Protocol and assess its effects on Qing finances, sovereignty, and politics.
  8. Explain why the Qing government undertook the New Policies, and compare them with the Hundred Days Reform of 1898.
  9. Assess reforms in the military, education, administration, law, economy, and constitutional government, including their achievements and limits.
  10. Analyze how reform created new political groups, regional tensions, and fiscal burdens that undermined the dynasty even as they strengthened state institutions.
  11. Contextualize the Boxer War and the New Policies within Asian and global history, including Japan's role as model and competitor.

Guiding Historical Questions

Questions for Historical Inquiry

  1. Why did a movement of martial-arts and spirit-possession groups spread so quickly through the villages of North China in 1898–1900?
  2. How should historians describe the Boxers without reducing them either to irrational superstition or to a coherent nationalist army?
  3. Why did the Qing court turn from suppressing the Boxers to supporting them, and why did leading provincial officials refuse to follow?
  4. How did Chinese Christians experience the crisis, and what does their presence in the legation quarter and Beitang reveal about the conflict?
  5. What distinguished the relief of the legations from the occupation and reprisals that followed?
  6. How did the Boxer Protocol change the Qing state's finances and sovereignty?
  7. Why did a court that had crushed the Hundred Days Reform in 1898 launch broader reforms in 1901?
  8. How did the abolition of the examination system change Chinese society, and what continued despite it?
  9. Did the New Policies strengthen the Qing state, weaken it, or both?
  10. What does the history of the New Policies suggest about the limits of reform inside an imperial political order?

Historical Context

Students entering this chapter should bring the background established in Chapters 51 through 55. After the Opium Wars, the Treaty of Nanjing and later treaties opened ports, granted extraterritoriality, and, from 1860, gave Christian missionaries the right to reside and own property in the interior. The Taiping and other mid-century rebellions (Chapter 53) had led the Qing to rely on regional armies and provincial governors-general such as Zeng Guofan, Li Hongzhang, and Zhang Zhidong, and had shifted fiscal and military power toward the provinces. The Self-Strengthening Movement (Chapter 54) built arsenals, shipyards, telegraphs, and a modern navy, but its institutions were fragmented across provinces and unevenly funded. The defeat by Japan in 1894–1895 (Chapter 55) shattered confidence in these efforts, brought the cession of Taiwan and a large indemnity, and set off a scramble for concessions, leased territories, and spheres of influence: Germany at Jiaozhou Bay in Shandong, Russia at Lüshun (Port Arthur), Britain at Weihaiwei and the New Territories, and France at Guangzhouwan.

In 1898, the Guangxu Emperor, influenced by reformers such as Kang Youwei and Liang Qichao, issued a flood of reform edicts over about one hundred days. In September, Cixi and conservative officials, backed by the military commander Ronglu, ended the experiment, confined the emperor, and executed or exiled several reformers. Kang and Liang escaped to Japan. This history matters for the present chapter because it shaped the court's suspicion of reform, its distrust of foreign powers that sheltered the reformers, and its anxiety that the foreign powers might support the removal of Cixi.

The geographic scene of the Boxer Crisis was the northern plain of Shandong and southern Zhili (now Hebei), densely populated agricultural counties watered unevenly by the Yellow River, the Grand Canal, and smaller streams. This was an area of small-holding farmers growing wheat, millet, sorghum, cotton, and peanuts, with few large cities and a strong tradition of local martial arts, village associations, temple festivals, and secret religious sects. It was also an area prone to flood and drought. The Yellow River broke its banks in Shandong in 1898, and drought followed in 1899–1900. Meanwhile, the region was entering a new economic order. Machine-made cotton goods imported through treaty ports undercut some handicraft spinning. Steamship traffic and, in the north, new railways began to reroute trade and displace boatmen and cart-drivers. The telegraph line and the railway were also new physical symbols of foreign power running through farmland.

It is crucial to remember that the Qing state in 1900 was not a modern centralized government. The Zongli Yamen, created in 1861, handled foreign affairs; the Grand Council advised the throne; provincial governors and governors-general commanded armies and raised revenue largely on their own authority. The Qing imperial house was Manchu, and the dynasty ruled through a Manchu–Chinese partnership that was by this date strained by fiscal crisis and by growing Han nationalist critiques. Cixi, the Empress Dowager, held decisive power at court, though the Guangxu Emperor remained on the throne. Reading the events of 1900 requires attention to this fragmented structure: in many ways, the court at Beijing was one power among several in China.

Finally, "China" in 1900 was a territory defined by the Qing empire, including Manchuria, Mongolia, Xinjiang, and Tibet, not by the later Republic or People's Republic. The Boxer Crisis played out mostly in North China, but Russian operations in Manchuria and reactions in the Yangtze valley, Guangdong, and the Southeast show that its effects were spread unevenly across that larger space.

Historical Evidence

The sources for this chapter are abundant but uneven, and each type must be read with attention to its author, audience, and purpose.

Boxer sources. The Boxers left few written records of their own. Surviving evidence includes placards, handbills, spells, and songs, many of which were collected by missionaries, officials, and later scholars; this material reveals Boxer beliefs but was often recorded by hostile observers. Oral histories gathered in Shandong in the 1960s, notably by teams from Shandong University, preserved memories of the movement, but they were collected under political conditions that emphasized its anti-imperialist character, and they were recorded decades after the events. Historians such as Joseph Esherick and Paul Cohen have shown how to combine such sources critically with missionary reports, county records, and local gazetteers.

Foreign accounts. Diaries, letters, and memoirs of besieged diplomats, missionaries, and soldiers describe the sieges and the Allied campaign in vivid detail. They are indispensable for the experience of the legations, but they tend to emphasize foreign suffering and heroism, often misunderstand Chinese politics, and rarely report events outside the compounds. Records of Chinese Christians are less numerous but exist in mission archives and in later testimonies.

Qing documents. Edicts, memorials, and telegrams between the court and provincial officials, compiled in archival collections in Beijing and Taipei, show the formal record of decisions. Some documents were later edited or rewritten; the question of forged or altered edicts remains difficult. The diaries and memoirs of officials, written after the fact, tend to place blame on others.

Treaties and diplomatic records. The Protocol and the correspondence of the powers show the settlement's terms and the divisions among the powers. They reflect diplomatic priorities and say little of the experience of Chinese civilians.

New Policies sources. Regulations, budgets, school statistics, newspapers such as Shen bao, journals by students abroad, gazetteers, provincial assembly proceedings, photographs, and memoirs document the reforms. Official statistics often reflect intentions more than outcomes, and they may overstate progress; newspapers and memoirs provide context but are shaped by political positions.

Photographs and visual sources. Photographs of the occupation, of the legations, and of new schools and armies are valuable but staged or selected by photographers who often worked for foreign or official clients. Students should ask who took each image and why.

Section 56.1

North China and the Crisis of the Late 1890s

The Boxer movement began in the countryside of northwest Shandong and the adjoining counties of Zhili, a densely populated, poor, and ecologically fragile region. Historians who have studied the movement in detail, notably Joseph Esherick, emphasize that it did not arise from a single cause, and that its origins lay in a convergence of environmental disaster, economic dislocation, religious conflict, and local politics. Understanding that convergence is the first step in understanding why the movement spread.

Environment and economy. The Yellow River, which had shifted its course to the north of the Shandong peninsula in 1855, broke its dikes repeatedly in the late nineteenth century. A major flood in 1898 inundated dozens of counties, destroying crops, homes, and the livelihoods of tens of thousands of families. In 1899 and 1900, drought hit the same region. Farmers who already worked small plots with narrow margins faced hunger, debt, and migration. Some of the region's sandy, poorly drained soils supported cotton, peanuts, and sorghum for local and regional markets, but harvests were unstable. Handicraft spinning and weaving, an important source of household income, faced competition from imported yarn and cloth. Boatmen, cart-drivers, and porters feared loss of work as steamships and railways began to carry freight. These economic pressures were uneven and should not be exaggerated; many villages were not directly affected by imports. But insecurity was common and rumor traveled quickly.

Foreign presence and missionary privilege. After 1860, Catholic and Protestant missions expanded across Shandong and Zhili. Catholic missions, especially German Society of the Divine Word priests in southern Shandong, French Jesuits in Zhili, and others, claimed the protection of treaty rights, and they sometimes intervened in lawsuits on behalf of converts. Local people often resented the practice. Chinese converts included the poor and marginal, orphans and widows who received support from missions, but also families seeking legal protection in village disputes. In disputes over land, temple taxes, theatrical festivals, or irrigation, a convert backed by a missionary could be seen as having an unfair advantage. Villagers who refused to contribute to local festivals dedicated to traditional gods, on the grounds that these were idolatrous, were criticized by neighbors for breaking communal obligations.

In November 1897, two German Catholic missionaries were killed in Juye county, Shandong, by a local group. Germany responded by seizing Jiaozhou Bay (Qingdao) and obtaining a lease, railway, and mining rights in Shandong. This was the clearest example of the connection between missionary incidents and foreign territorial demands, and it intensified fear that the foreign presence was expanding. The construction of the Shandong railway under German direction and the arrival of German surveyors and engineers further deepened local anxiety about the land and its spiritual protections, including questions of geomancy (fengshui).

Local government and politics. Qing county magistrates, outnumbered and under-resourced, depended on local gentry, village heads, and militia organizations to maintain order. Villages in the region had long organized themselves for crop protection, self-defense, and martial training, often under the cover of festivals. The Qing court itself had encouraged militia (tuanlian) organization in the past, and in the late 1890s some officials saw such groups as a possible local defense force. The line between legal militia and illicit sect was blurred, an ambiguity that mattered for how officials responded to the Boxers.

The result was a society primed for conflict but not destined for the specific events of 1900. Many other regions of China faced floods, imports, and missionary presence without producing a Boxer movement. The movement took shape because particular local conditions, networks, and political decisions came together at a particular moment, a point that students should keep in mind when evaluating simple explanations.

Section 56.2

Origins, Beliefs, and Organization of the Boxer Movement

The groups that foreigners called "Boxers" called themselves by various names, among them the Yihequan (Righteous and Harmonious Fists) and later Yihetuan (Righteous and Harmonious Militia). They grew from older traditions of martial-arts boxing and from sects such as the Eight Trigrams networks, which had a long history in North China, including participation in rebellions in 1813 and elsewhere. Some of the earliest groups that became part of the movement, like the Big Sword Society in southwest Shandong, were primarily protective associations that defended villages against bandits, and in some cases clashed with Christians. Others emerged from boxing associations (quan) in the northwest of the province. The movement was a collection of local networks that sometimes overlapped, and which shared a repertoire of rituals more than a command structure.

Yihetuan (義和團) and fu Qing mie yang (扶清滅洋)

Yihetuan means "Righteous and Harmonious Militia" (or "Fists"). The term tuan (militia, or group) also appeared in the names of officially recognized local defense associations, and this resemblance helped Boxer leaders and sympathetic officials argue that the movement was a lawful local defense force. "Boxers" is an English label coined by missionaries and foreigners.

Fu Qing mie yang is commonly translated "Support the Qing, destroy the foreign." This slogan became prominent in 1899–1900, but earlier Boxer placards and some groups used different formulas, including calls to overthrow the Qing, and the change in slogan marked a political bargain with officials and the court rather than a fixed ideology. Historians read the slogan as evidence of the movement's adaptability, not as proof that all Boxers shared a single program.

Ritual and belief. The central ritual practice of the Boxers was spirit possession. A participant, often a young man, would burn incense, recite spells, kowtow toward the southeast, and then fall into a trance in which a god or hero from popular drama and fiction entered his body. The spirits were drawn from the popular religious and literary world of North China: Guandi (the deified general Guan Yu), characters from the novel Journey to the West such as the Monkey King, and heroes of opera and storytelling. A possessed fighter, believers held, was invulnerable to swords and to bullets. This belief was reinforced by demonstrations and by the claim that the power could fail if rules about ritual purity, or contact with women or foreign objects, were violated. Followers also believed that the dead in the spirit world, angered by foreign-and-Christian pollution, were assisting the living, and that drought and other disasters were signs of divine displeasure. Boxer placards and handbills circulated in verse, rumor, and songs rather than treatises, and they attacked Christianity as heterodox and foreigners as demonic.

Social composition. Most Boxers were young rural men, including farmers, laborers, boatmen, peddlers, and unemployed workers, and the movement often attracted teenagers. In the Tianjin area, groups of young women called the Hongdengzhao (Red Lanterns) emerged, believed to possess special powers against foreign weapons and to be able to burn foreign property from afar. Their existence shows that gender roles in the movement were complex: women's ritual purity was both a source of power and, in some Boxer rules, a source of danger. Some local gentry and former soldiers joined or led Boxer groups; others condemned them. In Beijing, the movement drew also on urban poor, former soldiers, and bannermen families.

Organization. The Boxers did not have a national organization. Local altars (tan) functioned independently, often under a leader who claimed ritual authority, and who might take the name of a god or hero. Because the movement was decentralized, its behavior differed from place to place: in some counties Boxers acted mainly as defenders of villages; in others they extorted payments, killed suspected Christians or "secondary foreign devils" (a label applied to Chinese suspected of foreign contact, even sometimes for owning foreign goods); and in others they had little contact with Christians at all. The movement's rhetoric of loyalty to the dynasty and its decentralized, locally varied practice make it difficult to describe as a single political movement with a single aim.

Relations with authority. Boxer groups initially clashed with local officials who tried to suppress them. In 1898, in Shandong, some prefectural and county officials drew a distinction between criminal sects and legitimate militia, and the shift of the slogan toward supporting the dynasty helped some Boxer groups gain official tolerance. Yu Xian, who became governor of Shandong in 1899, was sympathetic and allowed Boxer groups to operate as militia. Under pressure from foreign ministers, who demanded stronger action, he was replaced in December 1899 by Yuan Shikai, who moved troops against the movement. Suppression in Shandong was effective, but it pushed Boxer groups across the provincial boundary into Zhili, where they found fewer and less determined opponents.

Section 56.3

Anti-Christian Violence and Local Religious Conflict

Anti-Christian violence was not new in 1900. Throughout the nineteenth century, so-called missionary cases (jiaoan) had occurred in many parts of China: riots, lawsuits, destruction of churches, and killings, which often led to diplomatic demands for compensation and punishment. The Tianjin Massacre of 1870, in which a mob killed French nuns, priests, and other foreigners amid rumors about orphanages, was one well-known example. What changed in 1899–1900 was the scale, the speed, and the involvement of a movement that claimed religious and political legitimacy.

Sources of conflict. Villagers disliked Christianity for various reasons. Some viewed the refusal of converts to join in community rituals, including temple festivals, ancestor-related ceremonies, and processions for rain, as a rejection of shared obligations. When drought threatened crops, these refusals could be seen as a danger to the whole village because they offended the gods who controlled rain. Rumors that missionaries mutilated children, used body parts for medicine, or poisoned wells were old and recurring in Qing society. Legal conflict was also serious: when a Christian was involved in a land or debt dispute, county magistrates might fear the diplomatic consequences of ruling against him, and missionaries sometimes intervened directly, a practice many local people saw as favoritism. At the same time, many conflicts were between neighbors and families, with religion becoming the language through which older disputes were expressed.

Chinese Christians. By 1900, Catholic communities in North China numbered several hundred thousand, and Protestants far fewer. They included families that had been Catholic for generations, particularly in parts of Zhili and Shanxi, as well as recent converts. Some joined for spiritual reasons; others for access to education, medical care, famine relief, or protection. Most were ordinary farmers who lived in villages alongside non-Christian neighbors and relatives. They were Chinese people embedded in kinship networks and local communities, not agents of foreign powers. In some cases, Christian villages armed themselves and defended against Boxers; in others, communities sought refuge in walled mission compounds; in many, converts were killed or forced to renounce faith. Estimates of deaths among Chinese Christians are uncertain but are commonly given in the tens of thousands, with far larger proportional loss among Catholics than among Protestants, simply because of their greater numbers in the affected regions.

Escalation. In 1899–1900, Boxer attacks on Christian villages, churches, and homes escalated. In Zhili, hundreds of churches and thousands of homes were burned. In the summer of 1900, as the court shifted toward tolerance and then support, violence spread to Shanxi, Inner Mongolia, Manchuria, and elsewhere. In Taiyuan, Shanxi, the governor Yuxian, who strongly opposed foreign missions, presided over the killing of dozens of foreign missionaries and children in July 1900, along with Chinese Christians. Not every official followed this course: in some areas, magistrates protected Christians, helped them escape, or refused to enforce hostile orders.

Section 56.4

The Spread of the Boxers into Zhili and Beijing

In spring 1900, the Boxer movement moved rapidly through Zhili. With Yuan Shikai's forces pressing in Shandong, many groups crossed into the province that surrounded the capital. There they met an administration headed by the governor-general Yu Lu, who was ambivalent and, in the early months, tried both to restrain and to co-opt the movement. Boxer altars appeared in villages and in towns along the Beijing–Tianjin corridor; in some places, officials and military officers participated, supplying weapons, grain, or permission.

Attacks on infrastructure. In May and June 1900, Boxers began tearing up railway track, burning stations, and cutting telegraph lines. The Beijing–Tianjin and Beijing–Hankou lines, built with foreign capital and engineering, symbolized foreign intrusion, and also threatened the livelihoods of carters and boatmen. Belgian and French engineers working on the Beijing–Hankou line fled or were attacked. The destruction of the railway and telegraph cut Beijing's communications with the coast and made it harder for foreign diplomats to obtain information or to move troops.

Mobilization of the capital. By early June, Boxers were entering Beijing in large numbers, and by mid-June they were present throughout the city. Officials and princes hosted them; some bannermen and soldiers joined. The Boxers attacked Chinese Christians in the city, burned churches and foreign-related shops, and killed many converts. Some commercial districts were destroyed by fire. Because the movement had no central discipline, the city was effectively unsettled: residents lived under the rule of armed groups claiming divine protection, while the court debated how to respond.

Foreign alarm. Foreign ministers requested troops to guard the legations, and the first small detachments arrived on May 31 and June 1. In early June, reinforcements were stopped when the railway was cut. On June 11, the chancellor of the Japanese legation, Sugiyama Akira, was killed by soldiers from the Gansu Army outside the city; on June 20, the German minister, Clemens von Ketteler, was shot while on his way to the Zongli Yamen. These killings, and the threat to the legations, convinced many foreign governments that they must act together.

The spread of the Boxers into the capital region is a reminder that the movement's extent was geographic and uneven. It was concentrated in Shandong, Zhili, Shanxi, and parts of Manchuria and Mongolia, not spread across the empire. In the south and southeast, there were rumors and scattered incidents, but the movement did not take root. This unevenness would become central to the political choices of provincial governors in the weeks that followed.

Section 56.5

The Qing Court and the Boxers

The decisions of the Qing court in the spring and summer of 1900 are among the most debated in modern Chinese history. The court was not a unified body. It contained officials who wanted to suppress the Boxers, officials who wanted to use them, and officials who changed their minds as the situation shifted. The Guangxu Emperor, confined since 1898, had little independent influence. Cixi was the pivotal figure, but she acted amid rival counselors and uncertain information.

Factions and individuals. Prince Duan (Zaiyi), a Manchu imperial clansman whose son Pujun had been named heir in January 1900 as a step toward replacing Guangxu, was strongly anti-foreign and pro-Boxer; he led the Boxers' supporters in the Zongli Yamen and the Imperial Household. Gangyi, a Grand Councillor, was similarly conservative. Ronglu, the commander of the capital's most modern forces, was cautious and sought to restrain violence, though he also carried out orders against foreigners. Prince Qing (Yikuang), head of the Zongli Yamen, and a number of Han officials, such as Xu Jingcheng, Yuan Chang, and Lishan, argued for suppression and diplomacy; several were later executed on Cixi's orders. Provincial governors-general such as Liu Kunyi and Zhang Zhidong urged restraint. Yuan Shikai, in Shandong, followed his own course, protecting foreign missionaries and avoiding open conflict.

The court's turns. In January 1900, edicts distinguished between bad and good groups, in effect allowing tolerance of militia. As Boxers advanced, the court sent Ronglu and other officials to assess the movement, and in late May and early June they reported varying conclusions. The foreign ministers' demands that the Boxers be suppressed, along with rumors of an alleged foreign plan to force Cixi to return power to Guangxu, hardened opinion at court. On June 16–19, the court held several conferences; Cixi was told that the foreign powers had demanded surrender of the Dagu forts, and she ordered resistance. On June 21, an imperial edict was issued that effectively declared war on the powers. Soon after, the court offered rewards for foreign heads, encouraged Boxers, and allowed regular troops to fire on the legations. At the same time, the court also sent mixed signals: it provided food to the legations, ordered protection of foreigners, and permitted ceasefires.

Information was poor, and the sequence of orders is not always clear. Some edicts were circulated selectively, and some provincial officials later claimed that a forged edict had been issued. Court proceedings were based on rumor, fear, and perceptions of foreign threat, including the belief that foreign powers wanted to dismember China.

Historical Debate

Was Qing Support for the Boxers a Rational Political Strategy?

The question: Why did the Qing court, which had signed treaties with the powers and had no realistic hope of defeating combined foreign armies, choose to support the Boxers and engage in war? Was this a calculated gamble, a failure of judgment, or the product of fragmented decision-making?

Interpretation A — The court's decision was a plausible, if risky, response to real threats: Some historians argue that the court faced foreign pressure that appeared to threaten its survival: leased territories, railway concessions, and rumors that the powers wished to depose Cixi. The Boxers had mass support in the capital region, and suppressing them risked internal revolt. Siding with them, and presenting them as loyal militia, offered a way to channel popular energy against foreign threats and to preserve the dynasty's legitimacy. Cixi's earlier experience with foreign pressure, and the example of successful popular mobilization in some earlier cases, may have made the gamble seem reasonable.
Interpretation B — The decision reflected miscalculation, factional politics, and poor information: Others stress that the court misjudged the military balance and the powers' unity, believed claims about invulnerability, or was captured by a conservative faction led by Prince Duan, whose personal ambitions were tied to the succession question. The rapid sequence of events, with rumors of an ultimatum, left little time for careful deliberation, and the court's inability to control events once the Boxers were in the capital turned a political gamble into a catastrophe. The refusal of the southeastern governors to follow the war order suggests that many senior officials did not see the policy as rational.

Evidence: Both views rely on Qing court records, memorials, diaries by participating officials, and foreign accounts, all of which are incomplete, written after the event, or shaped by later political purposes. Historians such as Joseph Esherick and Paul Cohen, in different ways, have emphasized local origins and experience of the movement; scholars of court politics have reached different conclusions about the court's motives.

Why it matters: The debate shapes how historians assess Qing decision-making, the nature of late imperial politics, and the degree to which the dynasty was capable of responding to crisis. It also affects how the Boxers are remembered: as victims of manipulation, as patriotic resisters, or as a diverse movement with its own agency.

Section 56.6

The Crisis Escalates: Tianjin, the Seymour Expedition, and the Dagu Forts

While the court deliberated, military events moved quickly along the corridor between the sea and the capital. The treaty port of Tianjin, about 120 kilometers southeast of Beijing, was the gateway: foreign concessions lay along the Hai River, and the Dagu forts guarded the river's mouth, about 50 kilometers downstream.

The Seymour Expedition. On June 10, 1900, Admiral Edward Seymour of the British Royal Navy led a force of roughly two thousand men from several nations, with sailors from Britain, Germany, Russia, France, the United States, Japan, Italy, and Austria-Hungary, by rail from Tianjin toward Beijing to relieve the legations. Boxers had damaged the track, and the expedition had to stop repeatedly to repair it. It met resistance not only from Boxers but also from Qing regular troops, who had begun to join the fighting. By mid-June the column was low on supplies, short of ammunition, and cut off, and it retreated toward Tianjin; it was rescued by a Russian force near Xigu arsenal on June 25. The failure showed that the foreign forces in the region were too few and the supply lines too long.

The Dagu forts. Meanwhile, foreign naval commanders in the Gulf of Zhili feared that the Dagu forts, which controlled access to Tianjin, would be used against them. On June 16, they issued an ultimatum demanding surrender of the forts by the following morning. Before the deadline, Qing forces opened fire on the foreign ships, and on June 17 the Allied force captured the forts. This act, which Cixi's court regarded as an act of war, was a major factor in the decision of June 19–21 to confront the powers. Historians continue to ask whether the ultimatum and the seizure made war unavoidable.

Tianjin. From mid-June, Tianjin became a battlefield. Boxers and Qing troops, including the forces of Nie Shicheng, a general who had earlier been ordered to suppress Boxers and who died fighting the Allies in July, besieged the foreign concessions. Chinese Christians, foreign residents, and soldiers defended the concession areas. Civilians in the walled Chinese city, which was shelled, suffered heavily.

Section 56.7

The Siege of the Foreign Legations and Beitang Cathedral

The siege of the foreign legations in Beijing lasted from about June 20 to August 14, 1900, and has often been told as a story of foreign endurance. A fuller account must include the Chinese Christians who made up much of the besieged population, the Chinese soldiers and Boxers who attacked, and the civilians of the city.

The Legation Quarter. The Legation Quarter, a compound of embassies and residences, held roughly 470 foreign civilians, about 400 foreign soldiers and marines, and a large number of Chinese Christians, estimated at around 3,000, who had fled there. A defensive perimeter was organized under the British minister Claude MacDonald, with military leadership from several nations. Chinese Christians contributed essential labor: they built barricades, dug trenches, carried water, and performed other tasks. Chinese converts also served as fighters in some sections, notably in the compound of the Fu (a princely mansion) defended by Japanese troops and Chinese Christians. The quarter was bombarded and attacked; food, ammunition, and sanitation became serious problems; disease and wounds killed dozens. Chinese Christians, who received smaller rations than foreigners, suffered in particular from hunger.

Beitang. Beitang, the Catholic North Cathedral, held a separate siege. It sheltered more than 3,000 Chinese Catholics, along with a few dozen French and Italian marines and priests, under Bishop Alphonse Favier. The defenders were outnumbered and short of food, and the compound was mined and attacked repeatedly. Hundreds of Chinese Christians died of wounds, starvation, and disease before relief arrived on August 16. Because Beitang's story was recorded mainly by the clergy, it presents a particular viewpoint, but its scale shows that the majority of the besieged were Chinese.

The attackers. The attacks combined Boxers and units of Qing regular troops, such as the Gansu Army under Dong Fuxiang, whose soldiers included many Hui Muslim troops. At times, shelling was intense; at other times, there were ceasefires, notably from mid-July, when Qing officials sent food, such as fruit and vegetables, to the legations. Historians dispute the reasons: some stress Ronglu's attempts to restrain attackers or the court's wish to keep open diplomatic options, while others emphasize logistical limits or a split command. The fact that the legations were not overrun despite weeks of attack suggests that, at least at times, the Qing command did not employ its full force.

Beyond the walls. Inside Beijing, Chinese Christians outside the two compounds were hunted down, and thousands were killed. Several Qing officials who had urged restraint were executed, including Xu Jingcheng and Yuan Chang in late July. Residents of the city faced fires, shortages, and fear. Accounts by besieged foreigners, such as diaries and later memoirs, are rich and dramatic but represent only one side; Chinese sources from the besieging forces, and from the Christians inside, are less numerous, though they exist and have been used increasingly by historians.

Section 56.8

Provincial Governments and the Southeast Mutual Protection Agreement

A striking feature of the Boxer War is that much of the Qing empire stayed out of it. By 1900 the Qing government remained formally centralized under the throne, but decades of regional military organization and fiscal decentralization had given powerful provincial governors and governors-general substantial autonomy in practice, and those who controlled the Yangtze valley and the south used that autonomy to refuse an order they considered disastrous.

The agreements. After receiving the June 21 edict (or reports of it), Liu Kunyi, governor-general at Nanjing, and Zhang Zhidong, governor-general at Wuchang, together with other officials, treated the order as a product of coercion or confusion and did not carry it out. With the help of the merchant-official Sheng Xuanhuai, they negotiated with foreign consuls in Shanghai. On June 26, 1900, a set of arrangements, known as the Southeast Mutual Protection (Dongnan huding), committed the officials to maintain order and protect foreign lives and property in their regions, while the foreign powers agreed not to send troops to the Yangtze valley and to avoid attacking those provinces. Yuan Shikai in Shandong and other officials in the southeast, such as in Zhejiang and Fujian, took similar positions. Li Hongzhang, the veteran statesman then in Guangdong, delayed obeying summons to the north and soon became the court's chief negotiator.

Motives. Provincial leaders were motivated by several concerns. They feared that war would ruin commerce at ports such as Shanghai and Hankou, on which provincial revenue depended. They distrusted the Boxers, whose movement threatened social order, and they judged that the dynasty could not win. They also wished to preserve their own authority and army. The agreements reflected the reality that the power to protect Qing territory against foreign invasion, and against domestic unrest, rested substantially with the provinces.

Significance. The southeastern arrangements limited the war's geographic spread, kept Yangtze commerce moving, and gave the powers a reason to treat the Qing as divisible between a "rebellious" north and a "loyal" south, which later affected the settlement. They also set a precedent of provincial autonomy against central authority that would recur in 1911. Historians sometimes describe them as evidence that the court had lost control over its officials; others note that officials justified their position as loyalty to the dynasty's true interests, claiming that the court's orders did not express its genuine will.

Section 56.9

The Eight-Nation Alliance and the Campaign for Beijing

The Eight-Nation Alliance was not a formal alliance but a loose coalition of powers with different interests. Its members were Japan, Russia, Britain, the United States, France, Germany, Italy, and Austria-Hungary. By August 1900, Japan had contributed the largest single contingent to the relief column that marched on Beijing, and Russia the second largest, followed by Britain (including Indian troops), the United States, and France. The column numbered roughly 20,000 men, with Japanese troops making up about half; over the whole intervention Japan eventually sent about 20,000 soldiers to North China and Russia many more, mostly for Manchuria. Numbers varied through the campaign and sources give different figures.

Divergent interests. The powers agreed on rescuing their nationals but not on aims. Russia hoped to extend its influence in Manchuria and used the crisis to occupy the region, bringing its forces into conflict with Chinese residents. A massacre of Chinese residents at Blagoveshchensk on the Amur River in July 1900 killed or drove into the river several thousand people. Japan wished to show it was a responsible power and to prevent Russian dominance. Britain was concerned with its Yangtze interests and with Russian expansion. The United States promoted the "Open Door" policy of equal commercial access, first set out in notes of September 1899 and reaffirmed in a July 1900 note that stressed China's territorial and administrative integrity, and opposed partition of China. Germany, whose minister had been killed, demanded vengeance; Kaiser Wilhelm II's July 1900 speech urging his troops to act without mercy was widely quoted. France protected its Catholic missions.

The campaign. Troops gathered at Tianjin through June and July. After fierce fighting, the walled city fell on July 14, with heavy civilian casualties and looting. The expedition to Beijing set out in early August, about 20,000 men under the senior officer present, the British General Alfred Gaselee. It advanced along the Hai River and the road to Beijing, fought battles at Beicang and Yangcun, and reached Tongzhou on August 12. The Allies fought Qing regular troops and Boxers, whose spirit-protection proved ineffective against modern weapons. Heat, disease, and supply problems affected all armies. On August 14, Japanese, Russian, British, and American units entered the city from different gates, in a race partly driven by national rivalry. The Legation Quarter was relieved the same day and Beitang two days later.

The coalition showed both cooperation and competition. Allied forces coordinated, but each also sought advantage, and their treatment of the Chinese population varied. In the months afterward, command was assigned to the German Field Marshal Alfred von Waldersee, who arrived in October, after the fighting for Beijing was over.

Section 56.10

The Fall of Beijing, the Flight of the Court, and Allied Reprisals

On August 15, 1900, the day after the Allied entry, Cixi, the Guangxu Emperor, and a small entourage left the Forbidden City in disguise and traveled west. The journey, through villages affected by drought and warfare, took weeks; the court reached Taiyuan and then Xi'an in Shaanxi, where it settled in October. Princes and officials who had supported the Boxers fled or committed suicide; some, such as the Grand Secretary Gangyi, died during the retreat. Cixi appointed Prince Qing and Li Hongzhang, who was recalled to the north, to negotiate with the powers.

Occupation. Beijing was divided into occupation zones among the powers, and the Forbidden City was briefly occupied by Allied troops. Looting was extensive, by soldiers of several nations, diplomats, missionaries, and civilians. Private houses, shops, temples, palaces, and government treasuries were stripped of valuables. Records and artworks, including parts of the great Yongle dadian encyclopedia held in the Hanlin Academy library, which was damaged by fire during the siege, were lost, and objects entered museum and private collections abroad. Civilians suffered rape, killings, and the destruction of property; suicides of women, particularly among families of officials and Manchu bannermen, were reported. The occupation also brought some order: foreign authorities organized police patrols, food distribution, and sanitation in their zones, and some residents sought their protection.

Punitive expeditions. From September 1900 to early 1901, Allied forces mounted dozens of expeditions into Zhili and beyond, ostensibly to suppress Boxers and punish those responsible for attacks. Some were targeted, but many were broader reprisals: villages were burned, suspected Boxers executed, and local communities fined or looted. German, French, British, Russian, and Japanese columns operated in different areas, and their conduct varied. Chinese Christians, missionaries, and some Chinese officials supplied information or demanded punishment; in some places, missionaries collected indemnities from villagers on behalf of their converts, a practice that sparked controversy even among foreigners. Some missionaries criticized the severity and urged moderation. At the same time, Qing authorities and local gentry sometimes cooperated with Allied commanders to avoid destruction, while Boxers and bandits continued to operate in the countryside.

Historians must therefore separate several things that accounts often merge. The relief of besieged people was a legitimate act of rescue for those inside the compounds. The campaign that followed, however, included looting, collective punishment, and the pursuit of national advantage. The occupation also served as the basis for the diplomatic demands of 1901. A critical account must recognize both the suffering of the besieged and the violence inflicted on Chinese civilians by foreign troops.

Section 56.11

The Boxer Protocol of 1901

Negotiations between the powers and the Qing representatives, Prince Qing and Li Hongzhang, took months. The powers disagreed among themselves on how harsh the terms should be: Russia and the United States, for different reasons, favored moderation on some points; Germany and Britain demanded punishment of officials; Japan sought influence and a share of the indemnity. A joint note presented in December 1900 became the framework. The final Protocol was signed on September 7, 1901, by the Qing and representatives of eleven states (the eight powers plus Belgium, the Netherlands, and Spain). Li Hongzhang died on November 7, 1901, two months after signing.

Main provisions. The Protocol required the Qing to send missions of apology to Germany and Japan for the killings of von Ketteler and Sugiyama and to erect memorials; to punish officials responsible, including the execution of some and the exile or forced suicide of others; to suspend civil service examinations for five years in cities where foreigners had been killed or mistreated; to prohibit imports of arms and munitions for two years; to pay an indemnity of 450 million Haikwan taels; to permit the powers to station permanent guards in the Legation Quarter and to hold it as a foreign-controlled area; to raze the Dagu forts and other fortifications; to allow the powers to occupy points along the railway from Beijing to the sea; and to reform the Zongli Yamen into a Ministry of Foreign Affairs ranked above the other ministries, which took place in 1901. Of the punishments, those of the two best-known princes illustrate the range: Prince Zhuang (Zaixun) was ordered to commit suicide, which he did in February 1901, while Prince Duan (Zaiyi) was sentenced to death, a sentence commuted to exile in Xinjiang.

The indemnity. The sum of 450 million taels, roughly a symbolic one tael for each person of the empire's population as estimated at the time, was to be paid over 39 years at 4 percent interest, with a total cost above 980 million taels. It was secured against customs revenue, the salt tax, and other income, much of which was already pledged to earlier loans. This arrangement greatly increased foreign control over Qing finance, especially through the Imperial Maritime Customs, and forced the court to raise additional taxes from the provinces. Several powers later returned portions of their shares: the United States, beginning in 1908–1909, reduced its claim and used the remitted funds for scholarships and the founding of Tsinghua College for Chinese students.

Section 56.12

Consequences of the Boxer Crisis

The consequences of the Boxer Crisis were far-reaching, uneven, and in some cases long-lasting.

Human and material costs. The numbers are uncertain. Foreign sources record the deaths of more than two hundred Protestant and Catholic missionaries and their children. Chinese Christians died in far greater numbers, commonly estimated at tens of thousands, including many Catholics in Zhili and Shanxi. Boxers, Qing soldiers, and civilians killed in battle, executions, and reprisals numbered many thousands more. Tianjin, Beijing, and the countryside of Zhili suffered destruction, famine, and displacement. Commercial networks were disrupted, and many families lost homes and savings.

Experiences differed by place and community. North China, especially Zhili and Shanxi, bore the heaviest damage. In the Yangtze valley and the southeast, daily life continued, and merchants benefited from the agreements that kept trade open. Manchuria experienced both Boxer violence and Russian occupation, which would lead to the Russo-Japanese War of 1904–1905 fought on Chinese territory. Christian communities that survived often emerged stronger in some respects, with missions securing compensation and legal protection, and in others stigmatized. The Hui Muslim troops of the Gansu Army, who had served in the siege, became associated in foreign accounts with the war, and their position at the court and in the northwest changed with the new settlement.

Foreign perceptions and policy. In the West, the crisis produced lasting stereotypes of Chinese xenophobia and cruelty, alongside real fears for besieged civilians; popular books and films later embedded these images. Within China and among Chinese overseas, memories varied: some intellectuals criticized the Boxers' superstition and the court's folly, while others later celebrated them as early anti-imperialist fighters, a reading that gained influence in the twentieth century. Foreign policy shifted too: the powers decided not to partition China, reaffirmed the Open Door principle announced in 1899, and relied on the Qing government to collect the indemnity, an arrangement that bound them to the dynasty's survival.

Political recognition of reform. The most important consequence for the Qing government was the realization among almost all officials, including Cixi, that continuing without institutional change was impossible. The court had lost its capital, its authority, and a large share of its revenue. The provinces had shown their independence, the foreign powers had demonstrated their reach, and the examination system and bureaucracy had proved unable to prevent catastrophe. The conditions for the New Policies were in place, as the next section explains.

Section 56.13

The New Policies: Reform After Catastrophe

On January 29, 1901, while the court was still in Xi'an and negotiations with the powers were under way, an edict issued in the Guangxu Emperor's name, but reflecting Cixi's decision, ordered senior officials at the capital and in the provinces to propose reforms in government, education, finance, and the military. Within two months they were to submit memorials on how to "take the best from Chinese and Western practice." This edict opened what became known as the New Policies (xinzheng). It was a decisive moment: the same Empress Dowager who had crushed the Hundred Days Reform three years earlier now authorized reforms that went further.

Historical Vocabulary
Xinzheng (新政), the "New Policies" or "New Governance"

Xinzheng refers to the program of reforms from 1901 to 1911 covering the military, education, administration, law, commerce, police, and, after 1906, constitutional preparation. Contemporaries and later historians have used the term differently: some limit it to the years 1901–1905, before the constitutional movement; others include the whole decade. The term was also used by officials to avoid the more radical associations of "reform" (bianfa), the word used for the 1898 movement. Because the New Policies were adopted unevenly across provinces, they should be understood as a set of overlapping initiatives rather than a single plan.

Leadership. The edict was followed in April 1901 by the creation of a Bureau of Government Affairs (Duban zhengwu chu) to coordinate reform, headed by Prince Qing, with Ronglu, Li Hongzhang (until his death), Liu Kunyi, Zhang Zhidong, and Yuan Shikai playing important parts. In July 1901, Liu Kunyi and Zhang Zhidong submitted three memorials proposing reforms in education, administration, and the adoption of Western laws and techniques. Yuan Shikai, who succeeded Li Hongzhang as governor-general of Zhili in late 1901, became the most powerful implementer of the New Policies, building the Beiyang Army, police, schools, and industries in his jurisdiction. Cixi returned to Beijing in January 1902, accompanied by the emperor, and the court resumed work in a capital marked by foreign occupation and ruin.

Comparison with 1898. The New Policies differed from the Hundred Days in several respects. They were prepared through consultation with senior officials rather than imposed by a small group of young advisers around the emperor, and they were backed by the authority of Cixi, the court's conservative core, and powerful governors-general. They were also broader in practice: the 1898 reformers had proposed new schools, a modern army, and railways, but many of the edicts remained unimplemented. After 1901, those and other measures were actually carried out. Yet the New Policies kept limits: the 1901 edict condemned the 1898 radicals, in particular Kang Youwei, who remained a wanted man, and insisted on the fundamental principles of the Confucian order. Ideological caution coexisted with institutional change.

Cause and Effect

Why the Boxer Crisis Led to Reform

Long-term causes

Fiscal weakness and dependence on provincial revenue; the failure of Self-Strengthening initiatives to produce an integrated modern state; the defeat of 1895; growing exposure to foreign models, especially Japan's; an expanding reform-minded literati and merchant elite in the treaty ports and provinces.

Short-term causes

The occupation of Beijing; the Protocol's indemnity and punishments; the flight of the court; the leverage of governors-general who had stayed out of the war; Cixi's need to restore the dynasty's authority and win foreign confidence, including the powers' concern that she be treated as a legitimate ruler.

Immediate effects

Edicts and bureaus coordinating reform; new schools and army units; abolition of outdated institutions; legal and administrative reorganization; heavier fiscal demands.

Long-term effects

A stronger, but more costly and politically contested, state; new social groups with expectations of participation; military and provincial power that could act independently; a growing gap between constitutional promises and actual control.

Historical complexity: The Boxer defeat did not by itself force reform. Powerful officials had advocated reform for years; Cixi had her own reasons, including the dynasty's survival, her personal position, and the desire to reduce the powers' hostility. Reform was a political strategy as well as a response to pressure, and it served different people's interests in different ways.

Imperial Edict Announcing Reform, January 29, 1901

Creator: Issued in the Guangxu Emperor's name from Xi'an, drafted by court officials under Cixi's direction · Date: January 29, 1901 (lunar date, 11th day of the 12th month, 26th year of Guangxu) · Type: Imperial edict (published in English translation in several early twentieth-century collections)

Context: The edict was issued during exile, as the powers pressed their demands. It was addressed to senior officials and called on them to propose reforms. Its language is the formal prose of imperial documents, which blends moral reflection, admonition, and administrative instruction.

Paraphrase: The edict states that the fundamental moral principles of the dynasty and the Confucian order do not change, but that laws and methods of government have always been adjusted to circumstances. It criticizes officials who cling to old routines and also those who pursue foreign ideas superficially, blames the radical reformers of 1898 for rashness, and orders ministers and provincial officials to examine how to reform administration, education, the examination system, finance, and the military, and to submit their proposals within two months.

Analysis: The edict is revealing for how the Qing leadership explained reform to itself and to the public. It frames change as consistent with tradition and avoids acknowledging the court's responsibility for the war; it allocates blame to officials and to the 1898 reformers rather than to the decision to support the Boxers. Its claim that reform follows the changing times echoes arguments made by earlier officials. As evidence, it shows the official justification, not the full range of motives, and it says nothing about how reforms would be financed or how conflicts between court and provinces would be resolved. Missing perspectives include those of the Han gentry, merchants, and women, who would be affected by the reforms, and of those who had suffered in the war.

Questions for students:

  1. What does the edict say about the relationship between unchanging principles and changing institutions?
  2. Who created the document, and what audiences might they have had in mind, domestic and foreign?
  3. What can historians learn from it about elite arguments for reform?
  4. What can they not learn from it about the Boxer crisis or the real motives behind reform?
Section 56.14

Military Reconstruction and the New Army

The Qing military in 1900 was a patchwork. The Manchu, Mongol, and Chinese Banners had declined as fighting forces; the Green Standard Army was largely a police force; and the regional armies that had defeated the Taiping, such as the Hunan Army and the Huai Army, had been partly modernized but remained tied to provincial commanders. The most modern units were the foreign-trained forces of Yuan Shikai's Newly Created Army, established after 1895, and the forces under Zhang Zhidong in Hubei. The defeat of 1900 convinced the court that a standardized, modern army was essential.

Organization. In 1901 the military examinations, which had tested archery and strength for centuries, were abolished, ending an old route to military office. In 1903, a Bureau of Army Reorganization was created under Prince Qing, Yuan Shikai, and the Manchu official Tieliang, and in 1904 a plan envisioned thirty-six divisions of a standard type, to be raised by the provinces, with uniform training, organization, and equipment. A Ministry of the Army (Lujunbu) was created in 1906 to centralize planning. Military academies were founded at Baoding, Wuchang, Nanjing, and elsewhere, and officers were sent to study in Japan, whose Army Officers' Academy trained hundreds of Chinese cadets, some of whom became senior commanders and some revolutionaries. Foreign instructors, especially Japanese and German, assisted.

Results. By 1906–1907, the Beiyang Army under Yuan Shikai consisted of six modern divisions. Provinces such as Hubei, Jiangsu, Yunnan, and Guangdong raised their own "New Army" units. By 1911, the New Army included roughly fourteen divisions and a number of mixed brigades, in total perhaps a quarter of a million men, although estimates vary and many units were under strength. Soldiers were increasingly literate, were recruited from the respectable poor and sometimes from educated families, and received pay, uniforms, and drill. They represented a break from older attitudes that held soldiering to be a low-status occupation.

Political consequences. The New Army strengthened Qing military capacity but also created problems. It was expensive and was financed largely by provincial revenues, adding to local tax burdens. Its loyalty was often to individual commanders rather than to the court: Beiyang officers owed their positions to Yuan Shikai. Many young officers trained in Japan or in provincial academies were exposed to nationalist ideas, and revolutionary groups such as the Tongmenghui recruited among them, a development explored in Chapter 57. In 1906, Manchu court leaders, worried about Yuan's power, transferred four of the Beiyang divisions to central control, and in 1907–1909 sought to bring the military under imperial princes, culminating in the dismissal of Yuan in January 1909. The result was that the army, while larger and better trained, was neither fully centralized nor reliably dynastic.

Section 56.15

Educational Reform and the Abolition of the Examination System

Education reform was the most socially consequential element of the New Policies. For centuries, the civil service examinations had tested candidates on the Confucian classics and the "eight-legged essay," and success conferred degrees, status, and eligibility for office. Reformers, from Kang Youwei to Zhang Zhidong, argued that this system produced officials ignorant of the modern world.

New schools. In 1901, the court ordered the eight-legged essay replaced in examinations with essays on policy and the classics, and in the same year it ordered the establishment of modern schools in provincial capitals and counties. The Imperial University of Peking, founded in 1898, was reopened and reorganized in 1902, and regulations issued in 1902 and 1904 created a national school system, with primary, secondary, normal, technical, and higher education, patterned on Japan's. Curricula added mathematics, science, geography, history, foreign languages, and physical training, while retaining Confucian classics and moral instruction. In late 1905, a Ministry of Education was created. Provincial governments, gentry, merchants, and temples converted academies and temples into schools and raised local funds, often through surcharges. By 1909, according to commonly cited official statistics, there were more than 50,000 modern schools and over one and a half million students, though quality varied greatly and the figures are likely incomplete.

Study abroad. Government and private funds sent Chinese students overseas, especially to Japan, where the number peaked around 1905–1906, in estimates of eight thousand or more. Students studied law, education, the military, and science, formed associations, published journals, and debated nationalism and revolution. Others went to Europe and the United States; after 1908–1909, the American remission of indemnity funds financed scholarships. Their experiences produced a generation familiar with foreign institutions, but also at times disillusioned with Qing rule.

Abolition of the examinations. Because modern schools could not compete with an exam system that still offered the surest road to official position, reformers argued that the examinations must end. In 1903 the court had announced a plan to phase them out gradually over three examination cycles. In September 1905, after memorials by Yuan Shikai, Zhang Zhidong, and other governors, an edict abolished the examinations at all levels beginning with the next cycle. The last metropolitan examination had been held in 1904.

Continuity and Change Over Time

The End of the Examination System

What Changed

  • A route to office and status that had operated, with changes, for more than a thousand years ended in 1905
  • New degrees and diplomas from modern schools, including those earned abroad, became credentials, and the state organized special examinations for returned students
  • Young men from gentry families had to choose between traditional study and new schools; many families diversified, sending some sons to each
  • Local gentry lost a predictable career ladder, and their community leadership increasingly rested on wealth, school management, and participation in local self-government

What Stayed the Same

  • The belief that formal education should be the basis for official selection and social status
  • Classical learning and moral instruction continued in the new curricula, and elite families still valued literacy and scholarly culture
  • Existing degree-holders kept their titles and privileges, and many held office or local influence for decades
  • Most rural families, who had never produced examination graduates, were affected mainly through taxes for schools rather than access to them

Who experienced change most directly: Aspiring candidates in the middle of their studies, families who had invested in examination success, and local gentry. Who experienced continuity: Many successful degree-holders, officials, and the rural majority outside the examination system. Why it matters: The abolition removed an institution that tied local elites to the court's culture and the central state; later historians see this as a factor weakening the ties of loyalty that had linked gentry to the dynasty, though other factors, such as fiscal pressure and new political opportunities, were also at work.

The Protocol's provision suspending examinations in cities where foreigners had been killed was thus largely overtaken by the larger abolition. Many contemporaries mourned the end of the examinations, but many welcomed it, particularly those who saw modern education as a path to national strength.

Section 56.16

Administrative, Legal, Judicial, and Police Reform

Government reorganization. In 1901 the Zongli Yamen became the Ministry of Foreign Affairs (Waiwubu), and was ranked first among the ministries. In 1903 a Ministry of Commerce was established. A major reorganization in 1906 replaced the traditional Six Boards and other offices with a group of eleven ministries, including Civil Affairs, Finance, Education, the Army, Justice, Agriculture, Industry and Commerce, Posts and Communications, and Dependencies. A new Supreme Court (Daliyuan) was created, along with an Audit Bureau. Though the Grand Council remained and the emperor retained the final word, the reorganization introduced functional specialization, written regulations, and budgets. Critics noted that the new ministries were dominated by Manchu princes and officials, and that the number of positions held by Manchus remained disproportionate; in 1907 and after, commissions studied reforms to the Banner system and the elimination of Manchu–Han distinctions. As early as 1902, an edict had lifted the ban on Manchu–Han intermarriage and urged an end to footbinding, though change in practice was slow.

Law. In 1902, Shen Jiaben, an experienced legal official, and Wu Tingfang, a Western-trained diplomat and lawyer, were charged with revising the legal code. A Law Codification Commission worked from 1904, studying European and Japanese codes, with Japanese legal advisers. Some punishments judged cruel by foreign and reformist critics, such as slow slicing (lingchi), the display of heads, and branding, were abolished in 1905. A commercial law and company law were issued in 1904; a new criminal code, the Great Qing New Criminal Code, was promulgated provisionally in 1910, along with draft civil and procedural codes that were not fully completed before 1911. The legal changes had several motives: improving justice, modernizing the state, and removing a justification for foreign extraterritoriality, since the powers had indicated they might reconsider extraterritorial rights when Chinese laws and courts met their standards (a promise that was not fulfilled in this period).

Courts and police. The reforms envisioned separating judicial from administrative functions, creating a hierarchy of courts, and training professional judges and lawyers. Pilot courts were set up in Beijing and some provinces, but in most counties the magistrate continued to act as judge, administrator, and tax collector, because there were neither funds nor trained personnel for a separate judiciary. Modern police forces were established first in Tianjin under Yuan Shikai, then in Beijing (1905) and other cities, and a Ministry of Civil Affairs supervised them. Police patrolled streets, registered households, enforced sanitation and traffic rules, and suppressed disorder; they also monitored dissidents and publications. Many people saw police as an intrusion, particularly when they enforced new regulations on markets, religious festivals, or public behavior. In 1906, the court launched a ten-year campaign to suppress opium cultivation and use, and negotiations with Britain during 1907 culminated in the Anglo-Chinese opium agreement accepted in January 1908, providing for progressive reductions in Indian opium exports alongside Chinese suppression measures, a policy with significant effects in some provinces.

The central difficulty was the gap between design and implementation. Regulations were drafted in Beijing and often copied from foreign models, while the practical work depended on county officials, local gentry, and underpaid clerks. Reform therefore operated through existing local power structures, and its results varied dramatically: Zhili, Hubei, Jiangsu, and Guangdong saw the most extensive changes, while remote provinces such as Guizhou, Gansu, and Yunnan experienced slower and thinner change.

Section 56.17

Economic Reform, State Finance, and Infrastructure

Finance was the New Policies' most difficult problem. The Qing's fiscal system had been decentralized since the mid-nineteenth century. The land tax was fixed at low rates; the central government depended on customs duties, the salt tax, and lijin (a transit tax on goods), much of it collected by provinces, and on provincial remittances. After the Boxer indemnity, annual obligations to foreign powers rose by tens of millions of taels, on top of earlier indemnities and loans. Meanwhile, the New Policies themselves required large expenditures for armies, schools, police, and administrative reorganization.

Raising revenue. The government and provinces responded by increasing existing taxes and imposing new ones: surcharges on land tax, higher salt and liquor taxes, levies on tobacco, sugar, and other goods, stamp duties, and local "contributions" (juan) for schools and police. Some provinces sold official titles. Foreign loans, secured by customs and other revenues, covered railways and military purposes. In 1908–1911, the court began to prepare national budgets, a Ministry of Finance, and a centralized fiscal system, but implementation was slow, and provincial governors resisted giving up revenue. The result was a tax burden that fell heavily on peasants and small traders, producing tax resistance and riots in several provinces by 1909–1911.

Encouraging commerce and industry. The Ministry of Commerce, established in 1903, promoted company law (1904), chambers of commerce, trade exhibitions, and technical schools. Chambers of commerce, organized in Shanghai, Tianjin, Guangzhou, and other cities, gave merchants a legal institution and a voice in local government and public affairs. In 1905, merchants in several cities, led by those in Shanghai and Guangzhou, organized a boycott of American goods to protest the American exclusion of Chinese immigrants, a sign of growing political consciousness among the commercial elite. New banks were created, including the Bank of the Board of Revenue (1905), renamed the Great Qing Government Bank in 1908, and the Bank of Communications (1908). Modern factories, including cotton mills, flour mills, and match factories, grew in the treaty ports, run by Chinese and foreign owners, especially in Shanghai, Tianjin, Wuhan, and Guangzhou.

Railways and rights recovery. The Beijing–Hankou railway, financed by a Belgian-French syndicate, opened in 1906; the Beijing–Zhangjiakou line, designed and built by Chinese engineer Zhan Tianyou with Chinese capital, opened in 1909. Provincial elites, concerned about foreign control, launched a "rights recovery movement" (shouhui liquan yundong) to buy back concessions for railways and mines and to build lines with Chinese capital. Provincial railway companies sold shares to local investors, from wealthy merchants to small shareholders, but most struggled with mismanagement, shortages of capital, and technical problems. The conflict between provincial investors and the central government's efforts to nationalize trunk lines, which came to a head in 1911, is the subject of Chapter 57. For now, the key point is that railway policy joined questions of finance, foreign loans, and local autonomy.

Section 56.18

Society and Everyday Life During the New Policies

The New Policies were not only decrees from Beijing; they changed how people studied, worked, paid taxes, and imagined their futures, and these changes were experienced very differently across regions, classes, and genders.

Students and professionals. A new category of educated people emerged: students in modern schools and colleges, teachers, journalists, doctors, engineers, lawyers, and officers. Many lived in cities, read newspapers and journals, and joined associations. Younger people from gentry and merchant families who might once have prepared for the examinations now attended school or went abroad. For some, these changes meant opportunity; for others, uncertainty, since schooling was expensive and positions for graduates were not guaranteed.

Women. Educational reform opened new, though limited, opportunities for women. Private girls' schools had been founded by missionaries and by Chinese reformers in the 1890s, and in 1907 the court issued regulations for primary and normal schools for girls, giving official recognition. Women teachers, students, and writers appeared in treaty ports, and some traveled abroad to Japan. The anti-footbinding movement, supported by reformers, Christian missionaries, and local societies, and encouraged by the 1902 edict, spread in many cities, though in the countryside footbinding remained common for years. Some women joined revolutionary circles; the poet and educator Qiu Jin, who studied in Japan and became involved in a failed uprising, was executed in 1907 and became a symbol of women's participation in politics. At the same time, women's work in the home, in agriculture, and in textile production, which continued to be central to household economies, was little affected by official policy, and women from poor rural families rarely entered new schools.

Local gentry and rural communities. For rural communities, the New Policies often appeared first as new taxes, school levies, and police fees. Temples, ancestral halls, and academies were sometimes seized for schools or offices, and village festivals were restricted by officials who regarded popular religion as superstition. These measures angered villagers, who sometimes responded by smashing the new schools or attacking officials and gentry who managed them. In 1909 regulations for local self-government created councils in towns and townships, giving local elites formal roles in managing schools, police, and public works, but also tying them to unpopular levies.

Everyday Life and Social History

The Effects of Reform on Ordinary Life

A county in Zhili. In a county seat in Zhili, reform might mean a police station with uniformed officers, a primary school in a converted temple, a telegraph office, and a newly dug drainage channel. Households were registered and sometimes charged fees for street lighting or sanitation. Families who had lost kin in the Boxer disturbances paid taxes to cover the indemnity and rebuild churches, a source of lingering bitterness.

A merchant in Shanghai. A cloth merchant could join the chamber of commerce, support a local school, invest in a railway company, and take part in boycotts. The merchant's social standing rose, and with it expectations of consultation in government.

A peasant household in an interior province. A farming family in a less affected region might see few direct benefits. They paid a surcharge on land tax, faced a new levy for the police, and in some provinces were pressed to grow less opium, which had been a cash crop, without a clear replacement. Resistance, when it occurred, took the form of riots over rice prices and taxes, as in Changsha in 1910.

A student returned from Japan. A young man who studied law or military science in Tokyo might find a post in a provincial school or army, or become involved in political associations and journals. His education set him apart from older elites, and his ideas about nation and citizenship often clashed with court policy.

Evidence for these experiences includes local gazetteers, newspapers, school records, memoirs, petitions, and later oral histories; but most surviving sources reflect the perspective of literate urban people, and rural women and laborers are least well documented.

Section 56.19

Constitutional Reform and the Search for a New Political Order

The idea of constitutional government had circulated in China since the 1890s, in the writings of Liang Qichao, Kang Youwei, and other reformers who studied Western and Japanese politics. After 1900, it moved from the margins to the center of political discussion, in large part because of Japan.

The Japanese example. Japan had adopted a constitution in 1889 and had defeated China in 1895. In 1904–1905, Japan defeated Russia in a war fought largely on Chinese soil in Manchuria. Many Chinese officials and intellectuals interpreted the outcome as a victory of a constitutional state over an autocracy, and argued that constitutional government mobilized national energies and strengthened the state. This interpretation was widely shared, though historians note that Japan's constitution preserved strong imperial authority and military autonomy, features that were as important to Qing reformers as popular representation.

Investigation missions. In 1905, the court sent two groups of officials, including Prince Zaize, Duanfang, Dai Hongci, and Xu Shichang, to study constitutional systems in Japan, the United States, Britain, France, Germany, and other countries. The departure of the first party was nearly derailed when a revolutionary set off a bomb at the Beijing railway station in September 1905. The missions returned in 1906 and recommended preparing a constitutional monarchy on the Japanese model, arguing that it would preserve imperial authority, reduce internal discontent, and win foreign respect. On September 1, 1906, the court issued an edict promising preparation for constitutional government, with a gradual timetable.

The 1908 Outline. In August 1908, the court issued the Outline of a Constitution (Qinding xianfa dagang), modeled on Japan's, and a nine-year program of preparatory steps, running from 1908 to 1916, which envisioned completing the principal preparatory measures by 1916, with a fully operative constitutional system and parliament projected for 1916–1917 according to contemporary descriptions of the timetable. The Outline described the emperor as sovereign, sacred, and inviolable, with extensive powers over the military, foreign relations, and legislation, while listing limited rights and duties for subjects. A parliament would be created, but with restricted authority. The program scheduled the establishment of provincial assemblies, local self-government, census, budgets, and a national assembly, step by step.

Motives and interpretations. Historians debate what the court intended. Some stress that Cixi and the Manchu leadership regarded constitutionalism as a way to strengthen the dynasty by adopting institutions that had helped Japan; others emphasize its role as a response to pressure from the provincial elite and returned students; still others see it as a delaying tactic. In November 1908, within a day of one another, the Guangxu Emperor and Cixi died. A two-year-old child, Puyi (the Xuantong Emperor), took the throne under the regency of his father, Zaifeng (Prince Chun), with the Empress Dowager Longyu, Guangxu's consort, holding the formal position as senior woman of the court. The new regent dismissed Yuan Shikai in January 1909, citing illness, and sought to restore the authority of imperial princes.

Section 56.20

Provincial Assemblies and the Expansion of Political Participation

The first concrete step in the constitutional program was the creation of provincial assemblies. In 1907 the court ordered their establishment, and in 1908 it issued regulations; elections were held in 1909, and the assemblies opened in October of that year in twenty-one provinces. The same year, regulations were issued for local self-government councils in cities, towns, and townships.

Historical Vocabulary
Ziyiju (諮議局), the provincial "consultative bureau" or assembly

The ziyiju was a provincial deliberative body whose name, "bureau for consultation," emphasized that it advised rather than governed. Its members were chosen through limited elections: voters had to be adult men who met requirements of education, property, or official background, a small share of the population, commonly estimated at well under one percent. The assemblies could debate provincial budgets, local regulations, and matters referred by the governor, and could question officials, but the governor-general could dissolve them and the court could overrule them. The word chosen by the court reflected a view that representation should support, not limit, imperial authority; many members, however, saw the assemblies as the beginning of real self-government.

Who participated. Members were mostly gentry and merchants, many with degrees, some with modern education, and many with experience managing schools, charities, and railway companies. Leading figures included Zhang Jian, a Jiangsu industrialist and former examination graduate who chaired the Jiangsu assembly, and Tan Yankai in Hunan. Constitutionalist organizations, such as the Association for Preparing Constitutionalism in Shanghai and groups linked to Liang Qichao, built networks across provinces, published newspapers, and organized petition campaigns. Supporters of Kang Youwei, in exile, and Liang Qichao advocated constitutional monarchy, while others, influenced by Sun Yat-sen's followers, sought a republic, a division explored in Chapter 57.

The National Assembly and petitions. In October 1910, a National Assembly (Zizhengyuan) opened in Beijing, with half its members appointed by the court and half chosen by the provincial assemblies. It was an advisory body, with limited powers. Meanwhile, provincial delegates, constitutionalists, and others organized three petition campaigns between January and October 1910, demanding the immediate opening of a parliament rather than waiting for the end of the nine-year program in 1916–1917. The court shortened the timetable to 1913 in November 1910 and dispersed the petitioners, in some cases with police action and arrests. The assemblies, which had been intended as safe outlets for elite opinion, quickly became forums for criticism of the court's finances, foreign loans, and appointments, and the National Assembly, in early 1911, challenged the government's budget and ministers.

The expansion of political participation was real, but narrow. Women, the poor, soldiers, and most rural inhabitants had no vote; non-Han populations in frontier regions, such as Mongolia, Tibet, and Xinjiang, were largely outside the system. Yet the assemblies, newspapers, and associations created a political culture and personnel that would operate in 1911–1912 and beyond.

Section 56.21

Achievements, Contradictions, and Limits of Late Qing Reform

Achievements. By 1910, the Qing state had a more modern army, a school system, police and judicial institutions, ministries with specialized functions, a new commercial code and banking institutions, railways, a legal code, and representative bodies at the local and provincial level. A new generation of educated professionals took part in public life. Opium cultivation in several provinces declined sharply. China's educational and legal institutions, many established in these years, shaped the Republic that followed. The New Policies are best understood as a substantial attempt at state-building, not a mere gesture.

Contradictions. The reforms nonetheless produced tensions that the dynasty could not manage.

Fiscal. Reforms cost money, and the indemnity and loans absorbed much of the government's revenue. Taxes and levies, imposed by both the center and provinces, provoked resistance from peasants and urban consumers. Foreign loans increased dependence on the powers.

Central versus provincial power. The Qing court sought to centralize authority, reclaim military control, and nationalize railways, while provincial elites, who had benefited from the decentralization of the post-Taiping era and from new assemblies, resisted. The creation of provincial assemblies gave regional interests a legal platform for opposing the center.

Manchu–Han tensions. The dominance of Manchu princes in the cabinet and ministries, culminating in the "Imperial Kinsmen Cabinet" of May 1911, in which most posts went to Manchus and imperial relatives, confirmed to many Han officials and gentry that the dynasty would not share power, even as it promised constitutional government. Revolutionary writers also used anti-Manchu rhetoric.

Expectations versus authority. The 1908 Outline preserved the emperor's sovereignty while the assemblies and press raised expectations of representation. The court's slow timetable, restrictions on assemblies, and suppression of petitions widened the gap between promise and practice. After 1908, the court also lacked a strong political leader: the regent Zaifeng was inexperienced and distrusted, and the child emperor could not command authority.

Education and the army. The new schools and military academies created people who could staff a modern state but whose careers and loyalties were not secured by the dynasty. Some became revolutionaries; others, constitutionalists; others, officials of the Republic.

Chapter 57 turns from these developments to the revolutionary movements, the railway crisis, the Wuchang Uprising, and the end of the dynasty. Students should be careful not to read the whole decade as a simple prelude to 1911: in 1910, many contemporaries, including foreign observers, expected the Qing to survive in some reformed form.

Historical Debate

Could Late Qing Constitutional Reform Have Succeeded?

The question: Did the New Policies and constitutional program offer a viable path to preserving the dynasty as a constitutional monarchy, or were they too late, too contradictory, or too constrained to succeed?

Interpretation A — The reforms were a plausible path that failed because of contingent events: Scholars who emphasize the reforms' achievements argue that the Qing was modernizing effectively, that constitutionalists such as Zhang Jian were willing to work within the dynasty, and that the death of Cixi in 1908, the weakness of the regency, and the 1911 railway crisis were contingent mistakes. Had leadership been steadier, the court might have accommodated provincial elites by speeding the parliament and sharing power, producing a Japanese- or German-style constitutional monarchy.
Interpretation B — The reforms generated structural contradictions that made survival unlikely: Other scholars argue that the court could not reconcile its aims: it wanted to centralize and preserve Manchu privilege while empowering the provincial elite on whom it relied; it needed revenue that its own policies made politically impossible to raise; and it trained armies and students whose loyalties it could not control. In this view, even a more skillful regency would have faced the same structural problems, and some form of political rupture was likely, though its exact form was not predetermined.

Evidence: Both views draw on provincial assembly records, memorials, budgets, newspapers, and memoirs. Interpretation A stresses the continuing support for constitutional monarchy among elite reformers in 1909–1910; Interpretation B points to fiscal records, tax riots, the Imperial Kinsmen Cabinet, and the rapid defection of provinces in late 1911.

Why it matters: The debate bears on how historians understand the causes of the 1911 Revolution: as the result of revolutionary organization, of reform-induced strains, of fiscal and political contingency, or of a combination. It also raises a comparative question about why some monarchies adapted to constitutional change and others did not.

Student task: What evidence would help distinguish a contingent failure from a structural one? How does the fact that historians know the outcome affect the way they assess the earlier possibilities?

Regional and Frontier Context

Events in this chapter unfolded differently across the Qing empire, and a student who studies only Beijing will misunderstand the period.

Shandong and Zhili. The core of the Boxer movement, with severe violence, famine, and reprisals, followed by the heaviest burden of reconstruction. Shandong also hosted the German leasehold at Qingdao, which became a model of foreign colonial administration and a site of railway and port development.

Shanxi and the northwest. Shanxi suffered serious anti-Christian killings under Governor Yuxian. The northwest, including Gansu, was the base of the Gansu Army, whose Hui Muslim troops played a conspicuous role in 1900; the court's route of flight via Taiyuan to Xi'an passed through regions where the war and drought had left villages impoverished, and the exile temporarily made Xi'an a political center.

The Yangtze valley and the southeast. Protected by the Southeast Mutual Protection, these regions escaped the war and became the base for reform. Zhang Zhidong in Hubei built schools, factories, and military units; Liu Kunyi's successors in Jiangsu supported modern schools and industries; Shanghai's merchants developed chambers of commerce and press institutions, and Jiangsu and Zhejiang gentry led the constitutional movement.

Guangdong and Hong Kong. Close to overseas Chinese communities and the colonial port of Hong Kong, this region produced reformers and revolutionaries, including Sun Yat-sen's circle, and was active in boycotts and rights-recovery movements.

Manchuria. Russian forces occupied the region after the Boxer War and remained after the Protocol; Boxer violence in Manchuria was followed by Russian military operations and violence against Chinese residents. The Russo-Japanese War (1904–1905) was fought there, and in 1907 the Qing created provinces in Manchuria (Fengtian, Jilin, Heilongjiang) in an effort to integrate the northeast more closely, encouraging Han migration and strengthening administration.

Mongolia, Tibet, and Xinjiang. These regions, governed through distinct institutions, experienced the New Policies as new demands for colonization, taxation, and military reorganization, especially in Xinjiang, which became a province in 1884, and in Mongolia, where Qing "new administration" policies from 1906 encouraged Han settlement and provoked resentment among Mongol princes and herders. In Tibet, the British expedition of 1903–1904 and Qing efforts after 1905 to strengthen control through military action in Kham, led by Zhao Erfeng, altered relations with the Dalai Lama's government and other Tibetan authorities. These developments, which would shape twentieth-century questions of sovereignty, should be understood in their own context, including the perspectives of Mongol and Tibetan leaders, who sought to preserve their own institutions and autonomy.

China and the Wider World

The Boxer War and the New Policies took place within a global context of imperial rivalry, nationalism, and reform, and Chinese actors were active participants rather than passive objects.

The intervention as a global event. The Eight-Nation Alliance was one of the first major multinational military operations of the modern era, drawing on armies from Europe, Asia, and North America, with Indian, Vietnamese, and other colonial troops serving under British and French command. Reports and photographs circulated globally, shaping images of China and of the powers' own national character. The conflict also shaped the diplomacy of the Open Door policy: the United States had first articulated it in notes of September 1899, and its July 1900 note, issued during the crisis, stressed the preservation of China's territorial and administrative integrity. The crisis also sharpened Russian and Japanese ambitions in Manchuria and contributed to the Anglo-Japanese Alliance of 1902.

Japan as model and rival. Japan's role was dual: a participant in the intervention, a competitor for influence, and the primary source of models for Qing reform. Thousands of Chinese students studied in Japan, and Japanese advisers worked in Chinese schools, police, military, and legal institutions. Japanese translations of Western works became the route through which many modern concepts, such as "constitution," "society," and "nation," entered Chinese.

Chinese diplomacy. Qing officials such as Li Hongzhang, Prince Qing, and Wu Tingfang negotiated skillfully within severe constraints, helping to avoid partition and moderate some demands. The court's missions of apology to Germany and Japan, the participation of Chinese delegates in international conferences, and the creation of a modern foreign ministry show the dynasty adapting to international diplomacy. Chinese merchants and overseas communities, including the boycott of American goods in 1905, also used international economic pressure.

Global exchange. The indemnity tied China's finances to global capital markets; railway loans linked Chinese construction to European banks; students' travels carried ideas between East Asian capitals, Europe, and North America; and the opium suppression campaign of 1906 and the Anglo-Chinese agreement of January 1908 connected Chinese policy to British Indian revenue and to a growing international anti-opium movement.

Section 56.22

Chapter Review and Late Qing Reform Checkpoint

This chapter has argued that the Boxer Crisis and the New Policies must be studied together. The Boxer movement arose from specific regional conditions: flood, drought, economic insecurity, religious conflict, missionary privilege, and the pressures of foreign encroachment. It was a varied set of local movements, not a unified army, and its violence fell heavily on Chinese Christians. The Qing court's decision to support the movement and to enter a war with the powers was shaped by factional politics, incomplete information, fear for the dynasty's survival, and the actions of the powers themselves. The war was limited in geography by provincial officials who refused to follow the court, and it ended with foreign occupation, reprisals, and a harsh settlement.

The reforms that followed were a serious effort to rebuild state capacity: a modern army, a school system, new ministries, legal codification, police, commercial institutions, railways, and representative assemblies. They also altered social life, from the lives of students and women to the burdens of taxpayers. But the reforms intensified fiscal strain, enlarged the power of provincial elites and military commanders, widened Manchu–Han distrust, and raised expectations that the court was unwilling to satisfy. The result was a state that had changed profoundly yet was less secure.

The central question remains the one posed at the start: how did a government that survived one of the worst crises in its history embark on extensive reforms that transformed Chinese society and government, yet fail to secure the dynasty's long-term survival? Students should be able to answer it with specific evidence, avoiding both the claim that the Qing merely resisted change and the claim that reform alone could have saved it.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has examined two linked episodes that framed the last decade of Qing rule: the Boxer Crisis of 1899–1901 and the reform program of 1901–1911. Its central argument is that the Qing government did not simply resist modernization until it collapsed. After a disastrous war, it undertook institutional changes that strengthened some parts of the state and weakened its political and social foundations at the same time.

The Boxer movement arose in northwest Shandong and southern Zhili from a convergence of flood, drought, economic insecurity, religious conflict, missionary privilege, and foreign encroachment, including Germany's seizure of Jiaozhou Bay in 1897. The Boxers were a collection of local networks sharing rituals of spirit possession and invulnerability, not a unified organization with one program. Their composition, behavior, and relations with officials differed from place to place. The slogan "support the Qing, destroy the foreign" reflected a political bargain with local officials and, later, the court.

Anti-Christian violence was central to the crisis. Chinese Christians, who were villagers embedded in local communities and not extensions of foreign missions, suffered tens of thousands of deaths. Many missionaries were also killed, and Chinese Christians formed the majority of those besieged at Beitang and a large part of the Legation Quarter's population. Violence arose from older village conflicts, rumors, legal disputes, ritual obligations, and fear during drought, as well as from the Boxers' ideology.

The Qing court's decisions were made amid factional division and poor information. Prince Duan and conservative officials favored the Boxers; others urged suppression and diplomacy; Cixi was the pivotal decision-maker. After the Dagu forts were seized on June 17, 1900, the court issued orders that effectively declared war. Historians continue to debate whether this was a calculated gamble or a failure of judgment, and the evidence permits more than one reading.

The war's geography was limited by provincial governors who refused to follow the court. The Southeast Mutual Protection agreements preserved order and commerce in the Yangtze valley and southeast, demonstrating the extent of provincial autonomy and offering a precedent for later events. The sieges of the Legation Quarter and Beitang, and the campaign of the Eight-Nation Alliance, which relieved them in August 1900, must be distinguished from the looting, collective punishment, and imperial rivalry that accompanied the occupation, as well as from Russia's operations in Manchuria.

The Boxer Protocol of September 1901 imposed an indemnity of 450 million taels, punished officials, authorized foreign guards in Beijing, razed forts, and tied Qing finances more closely to foreign control, while the powers, unable to agree on alternatives, preserved the dynasty. The consequences were uneven: heavy destruction in the north, relative continuity in the south, and lasting stereotypes and memories in China and abroad.

The New Policies began with the edict of January 29, 1901. They reorganized the army into a New Army, established a school system, sent students abroad, abolished the examination system in 1905, created ministries, codified laws, built police forces, promoted commerce and railways, and, from 1906, set the empire on a path toward constitutional monarchy through the 1908 Outline, provincial assemblies, and a National Assembly. These were genuine achievements with lasting institutional effects, and they were broader than the Hundred Days Reform of 1898.

The reforms were also contradictory. They were expensive, financed by new taxes that provoked resistance; they strengthened provincial elites and military commanders whose loyalty to the dynasty was conditional; they sharpened Manchu–Han tensions through the dominance of imperial princes; and they raised expectations of political participation that the court limited. The deaths of Guangxu and Cixi in 1908 left a child emperor and an inexperienced regency.

Regional variation was marked. Zhili, Hubei, Jiangsu, and Guangdong saw intensive change, while interior provinces experienced thin implementation; in Manchuria, Mongolia, Tibet, and Xinjiang, the New Policies meant attempts at closer integration that met resistance from local leaders and communities. Japan played a double role as occupier, competitor, and model for reform, and Chinese officials, merchants, students, and diplomats acted as agents in international exchange.

For the larger history of Chinese civilization, the chapter shows that late imperial China was neither static nor simply doomed. It was a complex state whose leaders adapted, sometimes boldly, under intense pressure, and whose reforms reshaped Chinese society in ways that outlasted the dynasty. Chapter 57 turns to how revolutionary nationalism, the railway crisis, military uprising, and provincial secession ended the Qing in 1911–1912.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it shows a state at its moment of greatest humiliation choosing to rebuild itself. The Boxer Crisis revealed the fragility of the Qing political order, the depth of social and religious conflict in the countryside, and the reach of foreign power. The New Policies revealed that the same order was capable of ambitious institutional change. Together they form one of the clearest examples in Chinese history of how crisis and reform are linked, and of how reform can transform a society without guaranteeing the survival of the regime that begins it.

The chapter shaped later Chinese history in many ways. The New Army, the school system, the legal codes, the ministries, the police, the chambers of commerce, and the provincial assemblies became building blocks for the Republic. The abolition of the examinations changed the social basis of elite life. Provincial autonomy, military power, and a politically active educated class would define the politics of the 1910s and 1920s. The memory of the Boxer War and the indemnity fed twentieth-century nationalism, and debates over how to interpret the Boxers continued throughout the century.

The chapter also connects China to wider histories: of imperial rivalry, of Japan's rise, of global debates over constitutional government, of missionary encounters, and of the movement of students and ideas across Asia. It reminds students that Chinese officials, merchants, villagers, Christians, and students were actors in these processes, with choices and constraints of their own.

Finally, the chapter helps students see Chinese history as diverse, interconnected, contested, adaptive, regionally varied, politically changing, and unfinished. Neither the Boxers nor the Qing reformers, neither the besieged nor the besiegers, can be understood through a single story. The questions of who speaks for a nation, how a state reforms itself, and who bears the cost of change continued into the next chapter and into the history of the twentieth century.
