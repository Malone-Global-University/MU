Vietnamese Nationalism before the Communist Ascendancy

Southeast Asian History: Crossroads of the Indian and Pacific Oceans

Chapter Opening

Vietnamese nationalism did not begin with communism, and its earlier movements did not simply fail until communism supplied the answer. Between 1885 and 1930 Vietnamese scholars, princes, teachers, journalists, students, soldiers, women's writers, and religious leaders advanced competing programs for ending French rule and deciding what kind of country should follow it.

In April 1926 a very large funeral procession moved through Saigon. The man being buried, Phan Châu Trinh, had died on 24 March after years of imprisonment, exile in France, and illness. Many accounts describe crowds in the tens of thousands, with students prominent among the mourners and schools affected by strikes and boycotts in the following days. The authorities noticed. A scholar-official who had never led an army and never headed a mass party had become a symbol around which a politically aware public could gather, and it did so in a city whose newspapers, lawyers, and secondary schools had created a new space for public argument.

At almost the same moment, another famous patriot was living under guarded house arrest in Huế. Phan Bội Châu, who had spent two decades as a revolutionary and exile, had been arrested in Shanghai in 1925, tried in Hanoi, and then spared prison by a political decision that followed a public outcry. The two men had disagreed deeply about strategy, and neither had rejected the other's commitment to Vietnam. In the same years, Saigon constitutionalists were seeking representation within the colonial system, a young journalist named Nguyễn An Ninh was testing the limits of press freedom, a Hanoi-based circle was forming what would become the Việt Nam Quốc Dân Đảng, and a young Nguyễn Ái Quốc was building a different kind of political network, which Chapter 128 examines.

This chapter studies the noncommunist and pre-communist political landscape of colonial Vietnam, concentrating on the years from 1885 to 1930. Its central argument is that this landscape was diverse, organizationally inventive, and unsettled. Restorationists, reformers, republicans, constitutionalists, religious communities, and revolutionary conspirators differed on whether to keep or abolish the monarchy, whether to seek foreign help, whether to cooperate with France in order to reform society, whether violence was necessary or counterproductive, and whether the nation was to be defined by the educated elite or by the broad population. Historians reading backward from 1945 have sometimes treated these debates as preliminary rehearsals for a communist outcome. That reading narrows the evidence and makes the choices of historical actors appear inevitable. This chapter treats them as real alternatives.

The chapter also insists on geography. French rule divided the territory that is now Vietnam into the colony of Cochinchina and the protectorates of Annam and Tonkin, each with different legal arrangements, different newspapers, different police environments, and different political opportunities. A chapter written only from Hanoi would miss the Saigon press. One written only from Saigon would miss the central Vietnamese tax revolts. The movements examined here also reached beyond Vietnam, to Japan, China, Siam, and France, and the chapter distinguishes carefully between actual journeys, correspondence, and aspirations.

The evidence is unusually varied and unusually difficult. Phan Bội Châu wrote in classical Chinese and in Vietnamese. Reformers in the Saigon press wrote in French and quốc ngữ. The French Sûreté produced large quantities of police reports, interrogations, and political summaries. Colonial courts generated trial records. Later memoirs and commemorations, written under very different regimes in North and South Vietnam and abroad, reshaped the memory of these figures. Each type of source has strengths and distortions, and no single category can be used neutrally. Police reports in particular recorded what the colonial state feared and believed, not simply what activists did.

This chapter builds on Chapter 124's account of schools, newspapers, and new publics and on Chapter 125's treatment of religious reform, and it parallels Chapter 126's study of Indonesian nationalism. The comparison is useful: Vietnamese and Indonesian activists faced colonial regimes that both tolerated some forms of political expression and repressed others severely. Chapter 128 takes up Hồ Chí Minh and the communist movement. Here, communist ideas appear only as part of an increasingly crowded political field.

The chapter follows five transformations. Division I examines scholar-gentry resistance and the turn to international revolutionary organizing. Division II studies reformist education and the 1908 protests. Division III follows the shift to republicanism, the Saigon public sphere, and wartime disappointment. Division IV examines mass mobilization in 1925–1926 and the rise and defeat of the VNQDD. Division V turns to women, religion, surveillance, imprisonment, and the competing political futures visible in 1930.

Historical Context

The Nguyễn dynasty, established by Gia Long after the Tây Sơn wars (Chapters 98–99), ruled a territory stretching from the northern Red River delta to the Mekong delta. French conquest (Chapter 110) ended that unity. France took Cochinchina in stages between 1862 and 1867 and governed it as a colony. Treaties in 1883 and 1884 placed Annam and Tonkin under French "protection," and in 1887 these territories joined Cambodia in the Indochinese Union, to which Laos was added later (Chapter 111). Hanoi became the Union's capital in 1902. The monarchy remained at Huế, but its authority was confined by French residents and by a political structure the court could not control.

The economy changed alongside the political order (Chapter 112). Cochinchina became a major rice-exporting region built on canals, large landholdings, and tenancy. Taxation under Governor-General Paul Doumer relied heavily on state monopolies in salt, alcohol, and opium, and on head taxes and labor obligations that fell especially hard on rural households. Plantations, mines, and port cities drew migrants and wage workers. These changes created new social groups, including a French-educated indigenous elite, urban teachers and clerks, students, and wage laborers, who would later furnish the audiences and leaders of nationalist politics (Chapter 124).

The educated elite of the older order was transformed too. The traditional examination system, which had produced the scholar-officials who staffed the Nguyễn state, was phased out in the early twentieth century, in Tonkin in 1915 and in Annam by the final court examinations at Huế in 1919. Chinese characters (chữ Hán) were displaced by the Romanized script quốc ngữ, first promoted by missionaries and then adopted by French administrators and Vietnamese journalists. French language instruction expanded, though access remained limited. This transition meant that many literate men who had been trained for office needed new roles, and some found them in reform and revolution.

International models also entered Vietnamese discussion. Chinese reformers such as Liang Qichao and Kang Youwei offered writings on national decline and renewal, which circulated through Japanese and Chinese publications. Japan's modernization and its 1905 victory over Russia suggested that an Asian state could adopt Western methods and resist Western domination. The 1911 Chinese Revolution presented a republican alternative to monarchy, and the French Third Republic's proclaimed ideals of liberty and equality could be turned against the colonial order that claimed to represent them.

Finally, the memory of earlier resistance mattered. The Cần Vương movement, proclaimed in 1885 in the name of the boy emperor Hàm Nghi, had united royal loyalty with scholar-gentry activism and had ended in military defeat. Its leaders' deaths or capture, culminating in the death of Phan Đình Phùng in 1895 and the slow eclipse of the Yên Thế movement under Hoàng Hoa Thám, left later generations with a question: if armed dynastic restoration could not succeed, what could?

Section 127.1

French Colonial Vietnam and the Problem of National Sovereignty

The first problem facing Vietnamese nationalists was that there was no single colonial state to oppose. French rule had divided Vietnamese territory into three jurisdictions with different legal foundations. Cochinchina, taken between 1862 and 1867, was a colony in the strict sense. It was governed directly by French officials, subject to French law as modified for the colony, and represented, by and for French citizens, in the French parliament. Annam and Tonkin were protectorates, with a nominally sovereign emperor at Huế. In practice, the French Résident supérieur in Huế held the real authority in Annam, and in Tonkin, after the abolition of the imperial viceroy's office in the 1890s, French residents ran the administration with Vietnamese mandarins serving as subordinates.

This arrangement created a deep ambiguity. The monarchy symbolized national continuity, and many scholars and villagers still thought of loyalty to the dynasty as a moral obligation. Yet the court served at French pleasure. The emperors deposed in the late nineteenth century, including Hàm Nghi (exiled in 1888) and later Thành Thái (removed in 1907), had shown the limits of royal independence. A patriot in Huế might ask whether to defend a dynasty that had accommodated French power, to replace it, or to bypass it entirely. A patriot in Saigon, living under direct colonial law, faced a different question: whether French republican institutions could be turned to Vietnamese advantage.

Differences in administration shaped political opportunity. Cochinchina had a Colonial Council with some indigenous members elected by a narrow electorate, a relatively open Saigon press, and a French-educated landlord and professional class. Annam and Tonkin had tighter restrictions on associations and printing, and the old examination elite had fewer outlets. Governor-General Paul Doumer's fiscal reforms from 1897 added monopolies on salt, alcohol, and opium and expanded labor and tax demands, which increased rural grievances throughout the Union. Differences in social structure also mattered: Cochinchina's landlord-tenant society differed sharply from the small-holding villages of Tonkin and the land-poor provinces of central Vietnam.

French labels reinforced the divisions. Colonial officials often used "Annamite" for all Vietnamese, while Vietnamese writers increasingly used Việt Nam, a term that Phan Bội Châu and others deliberately popularized as a national name. The geography of resistance followed these divisions: Tonkin and Annam produced scholar-led movements, Cochinchina produced press-based constitutionalism, and activists in all three zones sought to overcome the barriers between them. The chapter's national story must therefore be read as a story of regional variation and of efforts to build a common political space.

The language of protection concealed a deep asymmetry. France recognized the continued existence of the Nguyễn court while transferring control of diplomacy, security, finances, and crucial appointments to colonial officials. Local Vietnamese intermediaries retained substantial responsibility for village administration, yet their room for maneuver depended on a shifting combination of imperial regulations, provincial residents, and customary relationships. A village notary, a provincial mandarin, and a French civil servant might each claim to represent legitimate authority, but none could be treated as interchangeable. Petitioners learned to address different offices according to the remedy sought. Nationalists later drew on precisely this experience of divided authority when demanding a single Vietnamese public with a voice in governing its territory.

The status of Cochinchina needs particular attention because it gave legally trained Vietnamese access to arguments less usable elsewhere. Limited electoral institutions, a somewhat more active press, and greater exposure to French commercial law did not give the colony equal rights; French citizens and privileged residents possessed advantages unavailable to most Vietnamese. Still, Vietnamese lawyers and journalists could invoke metropolitan republican principles while challenging discrimination in appointments, education, and property. Northern and central reformers, meanwhile, operated around a surviving monarchy whose symbolism could unite people even as its practical authority contracted. These were different political environments within a territory that nationalist writers increasingly insisted should be considered together rather than governed as permanently separate units.

National sovereignty therefore had several possible meanings. For a loyalist it might mean restoring effective authority to a Vietnamese monarch; for a constitutionalist it could mean protection of civic rights through a reformed association with France; for a republican it meant a government responsible to the people rather than a dynasty; for a revolutionary it could require forcibly displacing the colonial state. These positions were not yet arranged into permanent political parties. Families, schools, religious institutions, trade networks, and scholarly correspondence could sustain sympathies across organizational lines. The history of nationalism is partly the history of converting these scattered forms of political dissatisfaction into competing claims about a common nation and its legitimate institutions.

Section 127.2

The Cần Vương Legacy and the Transformation of Scholar-Gentry Resistance

In July 1885, after a failed attack on the French garrison at Huế led by the regent Tôn Thất Thuyết, the young Hàm Nghi left the capital and issued the Cần Vương edict, calling on officials and subjects to "aid the king." The edict mobilized a broad response, particularly among scholars and local gentry in the central provinces, and the movement spread across Annam and parts of Tonkin. It combined loyalty to a dynasty with resistance to a foreign occupation, and the two were not the same thing. Some mandarins fought for the Nguyễn house; others fought for a country they had been trained to identify with the dynasty. The movement also had a darker side: the Văn Thân (scholar) forces in several provinces carried out massacres of Vietnamese Catholics, whom they associated with the French, leaving a legacy of communal bitterness that later politics could not ignore.

The French captured Hàm Nghi in 1888 and exiled him to Algeria, but resistance continued. Phan Đình Phùng's Hương Khê campaign in Hà Tĩnh, noted for its organized weapons production and its ties to local communities, held out until his death in 1895. In the northern midlands, Hoàng Hoa Thám's Yên Thế base combined armed resistance with periods of negotiated accommodation and survived until his assassination in 1913. These movements were serious military efforts, and the later image of Vietnamese resistance as fragmented or amateur does not match their record. But they depended on rural networks, fortified bases, and the legitimacy of a dynastic cause, and French firepower, intelligence, and negotiation gradually wore them down.

The defeat produced intellectual as well as military consequences. A younger generation of scholars, among them Phan Bội Châu and Phan Châu Trinh, had grown up with the Cần Vương memory and with stories of fathers, teachers, and neighbors who had fought. They also saw that the old methods had not worked. They began reading translations and commentaries from China and Japan, including works by Liang Qichao, Kang Youwei, and translations of European thinkers such as Rousseau and Montesquieu, which reached Vietnam through Chinese-language books and journals that circulated despite French censorship. These texts offered an explanation for national weakness based on political and educational backwardness, and they proposed that nations survived by developing the capacities of their peoples.

The shift was gradual, not absolute. Many 1900s activists still wished to restore a Vietnamese monarchy, and they kept using the language of loyalty. But they added new priorities: schools, associations, commercial enterprise, foreign alliances, and a conception of "the people" as participants rather than merely subjects. The transformation of scholar-gentry resistance created the setting in which the two Phans, though their conclusions diverged, shared a common starting point.

The Cần Vương appeal was not uniformly received, and its geography reflected the uneven reach of the Nguyễn monarchy and local social relationships. A scholar who organized recruits and provisions in a central highland district occupied a different position from a village elder seeking to protect the harvest, a Catholic family threatened by anti-Christian violence, or an official who stayed in government service. Some communities supplied armed bands; others negotiated with French officers or avoided both sides. Such variation mattered because later nationalists sometimes selected a simplified tradition of unbroken patriotic resistance. A complete history must recognize both the substantial sacrifice involved and the conflicts within Vietnamese society that resistance could aggravate.

The younger scholar generation inherited institutions in crisis. The classical examinations had once linked the village school to the capital and provided a recognizable route into office. When that route narrowed, mastery of the classics did not guarantee employment, and the prestige of scholars could no longer rest solely on service to the court. Yet knowledge of classical Chinese also opened an intellectual world of translated European political thought circulating through China and Japan. Scholars could read arguments about parliaments, armies, national education, and political economy without being fluent in French. Traditional learning thus helped transmit modern nationalist ideas even as reformers attacked the conservatism of the old examination order.

The unresolved question was what should replace imperial loyalty as a source of political obligation. Some writers preserved the moral vocabulary of service and sacrifice while redirecting it toward a population increasingly called the nation. Others argued that the people must become citizens capable of judging rulers rather than subjects required to obey them. Neither transformation occurred overnight. A village could continue to honor an emperor while younger residents organized a modern school; an activist could seek Japanese military assistance and simultaneously argue for constitutional reform. Recognizing this overlap prevents the false impression that Vietnam crossed a clean boundary between an entirely traditional nineteenth century and an entirely modern twentieth century.

Cause and Effect

The Decline of Dynastic Resistance and the Emergence of New Political Strategies

Long-term causes

Scholar-gentry identification with the dynasty; rural networks that supported local resistance; but also the technological gap in weapons and French control of the delta and railways.

Short-term causes

Capture of Hàm Nghi (1888); death of Phan Đình Phùng (1895); French pacification campaigns; amnesties and accommodations that divided local leaders.

Immediate trigger

The disappearance of viable armed bases around 1895–1900, just as new ideas from China and Japan arrived and examination-trained scholars faced uncertain careers.

Transformation

A turn from rural insurgency to organizations, publications, schools, and foreign contacts.

Immediate effects

The Duy Tân Hội (1904), the Đông Du (1905–1909), and reformist schools (1907).

Long-term consequences

Politicization of education and print; party-style organization; deeper tension between restoration and republic.

Historical complexity: Military defeat alone did not produce the new politics. Intellectual imports, economic change, and a new reading public were equally necessary.

Section 127.3

Phan Bội Châu and the Formation of the Duy Tân Hội

Phan Bội Châu was born in 1867 in Nghệ An, into a poor scholar family, and grew up during the Cần Vương years. He earned the top place in the regional examination at Nghệ An in 1900, a distinction that gave him prestige and access to scholarly networks but not an official career, since he turned instead to organizing resistance. He traveled through central and southern Vietnam, meeting scholars, secret-society members, and local leaders, and he was drawn to the Cần Vương inheritance and also impressed by the fall of other Asian states as described in Chinese-language writings.

In 1904, in Quảng Nam, Phan Bội Châu and a small group of associates founded the Duy Tân Hội (Modernization Society), commonly dated to that year, though details of the founding meeting vary between accounts. The society's aim was to expel the French and restore Vietnamese independence under a constitutional monarchy, and its members chose Prince Cường Để, a descendant of Gia Long's eldest son, as its nominal head. The choice was strategic: a Nguyễn prince could claim legitimate loyalty, unite traditional supporters, and appeal to the many officials and villagers who still identified the dynasty with the nation. Cường Để himself, a young man in his early twenties at the time, spent most of his remaining life in exile in Japan and became a political figure of long if uneven influence.

Phan Bội Châu's writing was central to his political method. He wrote in classical Chinese, for an audience of literate scholars, and in verse and prose adapted to broader readers. His 1905 Việt Nam Vong Quốc Sử (History of the Loss of Vietnam), published in Yokohama with the encouragement of Liang Qichao, told the story of French conquest and the failures of Vietnamese officials in a manner meant to shame and to rouse the literate elite. His 1906 Hải Ngoại Huyết Thư (Letter Written in Blood from Overseas) continued these themes. These works circulated in Vietnam in manuscript and printed copies, and they show how revolutionary organizing relied on texts as much as on conspiracy.

The Duy Tân Hội's early structure was thin. It depended on personal ties among scholars, on wealthy supporters such as landowners in the south, and on exiles abroad. Its program assumed that Japan, as a successful Asian modernizer, could provide military and educational assistance. Its leaders could not foresee that Japan's own diplomatic interests would soon intervene. The society was therefore both an organization and an aspiration: it linked scholars across regions around a program that blended monarchist loyalty, anti-French nationalism, and the borrowing of modern techniques.

The appeal to Cường Để illustrates both the resources and the limitations of Phan Bội Châu's early strategy. A prince with an identifiable dynastic claim helped reassure donors for whom the destruction of monarchy was unthinkable; the same symbolism could limit cooperation with activists who blamed the Nguyễn court for national decline. Phan treated the prince less as an absolute ruler than as a rallying point for a projected national renewal. But the society did not possess the finances, cadres, or military formations needed to translate allegiance into effective control. Its connections depended heavily on regional intermediaries, personal promises, and the movement of correspondence through routes vulnerable to interception.

Material support came from more than the scholar-official milieu. Southern supporters able to finance travel or provide introductions could be indispensable, especially because overseas education was expensive. Merchants, local notables, and people involved in the developing newspaper economy supplied money and practical assistance. This southern dimension corrects the impression that the Duy Tân Hội consisted solely of northern or central literati. At the same time, the uneven ability to contribute produced inequalities within the movement itself: those who could donate large sums might have different expectations from young students volunteering to risk expatriation. Political unity was claimed in public writing but constantly negotiated in the actual administration of the network.

Phan's writings also deserve to be read as arguments intended to create an audience, not only as transparent accounts of national suffering. He selected incidents of conquest, collaboration, resistance, and administrative failure to persuade readers that national destruction resulted partly from remediable political weakness. Shame, sacrifice, education, and example operated together in his rhetorical method. The comparison with Japan was especially powerful after its victory over Russia in 1905, but it did not mean that Phan understood the Japanese state as benevolent or that Japan had promised to liberate Vietnam. His political challenge was to convert admiration for foreign transformation into resources controlled by Vietnamese activists themselves.

Section 127.4

The Đông Du Movement and the Search for International Support

In early 1905 Phan Bội Châu left Vietnam for Japan, traveling via Hong Kong and southern China. In Yokohama he met Liang Qichao, who advised him that Vietnam's first need was not weapons but educated people, and that Japanese officials would be more receptive to a movement that emphasized education and modernization than to one that sought arms. He also met Japanese politicians sympathetic to Asian causes. The advice shaped the Đông Du (Travel to the East) movement, which sent young Vietnamese to Japan to study.

The first students arrived in 1905–1906, and Cường Để joined them. Over the next three years the number grew, with scholars' estimates usually placing the peak at around two hundred, though the figures vary and depend on whether short-term and temporary participants are counted. The students came from all three regions, with a notably large proportion from Cochinchina, where wealthy landlords and merchants contributed funds. Among the southern supporters, the journalist and entrepreneur Gilbert Trần Chánh Chiếu helped organize the Minh Tân movement, which linked commercial enterprise, publishing, and support for the overseas students. Chiếu was arrested by the French in 1908. The movement thus drew on both traditional scholarly networks and a new commercial class.

The students attended Japanese schools, and a few entered military training connected to the Shinbu Gakko, a school for Chinese cadets, though many attended private academies and language programs. They studied language, science, and political ideas, and they lived in modest conditions funded by remittances from home. Their presence in Japan also gave Vietnamese activists a place to meet, publish, and debate, and a few produced pamphlets and newspapers for circulation in Vietnam.

The movement's weakness was its dependence on Japanese goodwill. After Japan's 1907 agreements with France, Japanese authorities came under French diplomatic pressure to restrict Vietnamese activity. In late 1908 and early 1909 the Japanese government ordered the students and leaders to leave. Phan Bội Châu departed in 1909, Cường Để eventually left Japan, and many students dispersed to China, Siam, or home. For Phan Bội Châu the expulsion was a lesson: Japan was a model, but not a reliable ally. For the French, it removed a base without removing the ideas.

The Đông Du therefore illustrates both the new possibilities and the limits of transnational anticolonial activism. It created networks of educated Vietnamese who had seen another Asian society and who brought back political and organizational experience. Some of its alumni later joined the Quang Phục Hội, and others returned to quieter lives or to administrative service. Its failure did not end Phan Bội Châu's search for support, but it moved his base to China, where the Chinese revolution soon opened new possibilities.

The practical experience of overseas study was narrower than the movement's ambitious name might suggest. Students needed funds for passage and maintenance, contacts with sympathetic associations, and an educational placement they could actually enter. Some pursued language and preparatory instruction rather than advanced military training. Others became deeply involved with exiled political circles and learned more about contemporary Asian debates than they could have learned at home. Their presence in Japan was itself politically charged: a colonial subject obtaining new education outside the French system might return as a teacher or professional, but colonial officers feared that he could instead become a courier, recruiter, or conspirator. The categories of student and revolutionary consequently overlapped without being identical.

Japan's relationship with the French empire governed the limits of this opportunity. Japanese public sympathy, support from individual politicians, and contacts with organizations favoring Asian cooperation did not amount to a state commitment to Vietnamese independence. Tokyo was building its own empire and safeguarding diplomatic relationships, including arrangements with France. Vietnamese activists therefore discovered that an admired Asian model could impose a policy fundamentally at odds with their hopes. Diplomatic pressure eventually narrowed their room for operation and brought expulsions in 1908 and 1909. The movement's supporters in Vietnam faced arrests, financial loss, and fears that their involvement had exposed families and associates to danger.

The end of Đông Du should nevertheless not be equated with the disappearance of its political effects. Participants had acquired new languages, contacts, organizational habits, and a sharper understanding of international power. Some sought alternatives in China or returned to reform activities at home; others withdrew from politics under pressure. The failed venture raised questions that survived throughout the nationalist movement: could any government be trusted to support Vietnamese independence, should education serve immediate revolution or longer-term civic strengthening, and how could a dispersed network communicate securely? Those questions would recur after the Chinese Revolution of 1911 and during the later activities of republican parties.

Section 127.5

Phan Châu Trinh and the Philosophy of Civic Reform

Phan Châu Trinh was born in 1872 in Quảng Nam and earned the lower doctoral degree (phó bảng) in 1901. He served briefly in the Ministry of Rites in Huế, then resigned in disillusionment with the court and the examination-trained bureaucracy. His critique was unusual for a graduate of the old system: he argued that Vietnam's decline owed less to French strength than to indigenous weaknesses, including an autocratic monarchy, a corrupt and servile officialdom, outdated education, and a population denied the rights and knowledge needed to act as citizens. He summarized the needed program in a famous triad, commonly rendered as raising the people's knowledge, strengthening the people's spirit, and improving the people's livelihood.

His assumptions differed from Phan Bội Châu's in several ways. He distrusted monarchy as a form of government, believing that Vietnam's future required popular rights, representative institutions, and the rule of law. He saw independence as a long-term goal that could not be achieved before the population was educated and organized. And he distrusted violence and foreign military assistance, arguing that reliance on Japan would simply exchange one dominator for another and that armed uprisings would be crushed and discredit the movement. In 1906 he traveled to Japan, met Phan Bội Châu, and the two argued over these questions without reconciling them; they remained personally respectful.

Phan Châu Trinh's relationship to France was complex. He admired the principles of the French Revolution and the Third Republic and hoped that France could be pressed to live up to them, a hope he expressed in appeals to colonial officials. Critics, then and later, accused him of accommodation. His own writings, however, criticized French abuses, and the colonial authorities treated him as a dangerous agitator, imprisoning him after the 1908 events. His position is best understood as a strategy: using the colonial regime's professed ideals and legal mechanisms to build a Vietnamese civil society that could eventually demand independence from a position of strength. It is not accurate to describe him as a supporter of colonial rule as such.

The contrast between the two Phans should not be reduced to revolution versus reform, or patriotism versus collaboration. They differed on sequencing (independence first, or civic transformation first), on the political form of the future state (restored or constitutional monarchy, republic), on tactics (violence and foreign aid, or education and legal agitation), and on the role of the dynasty. Both feared that Vietnam would vanish as a nation if nothing changed. Their disagreement was therefore a debate within nationalism.

Phan Châu Trinh's argument was radical in its criticism of Vietnamese political society even when its immediate institutional proposals appeared moderate. He questioned whether a monarchy sustained by obedience, examinations, and administrative patronage could produce people capable of defending their own interests. His call for opening minds, renewing public spirit, and improving material conditions joined educational reform to political ethics. Popular enlightenment was not simply acquiring Western information: it required the capacity to recognize arbitrary power, challenge corrupt officials, and organize public institutions. His attacks on old customs often provoked resistance because they touched family authority and village standing as well as court politics.

The appeal to French authorities is frequently misunderstood when judged only by later demands for full independence. Phan sought to expose contradictions between France's professed civilizing and republican principles and the conduct of officials in Indochina. He hoped that a reduction in official abuses and a broader sphere for education and civic organization would enable Vietnamese society to strengthen itself. This position exposed him to severe criticism from revolutionaries who regarded the colonial state as structurally incapable of granting genuine liberty. Yet his own experience of arrests and exile gave little basis for treating his reformism as an endorsement of arbitrary colonial domination. He was disputing the route to national renewal, not rejecting the national problem.

The debates between Phan Châu Trinh and Phan Bội Châu were especially consequential because they showed that both violence and peaceful reform carried risks. Revolutionary action could invite overwhelming retaliation before an organization had acquired broad support. Reform through colonial channels could be closed whenever officials saw education or association as politically dangerous. Their strategies were not pure opposites: both valued schools, print, travel, and wider public participation, while differing over monarchy, foreign aid, and the order in which political transformation should occur. Students should compare their texts at specific dates, since each man's views changed through travel, repression, and reflection.

Section 127.6

The Đông Kinh Nghĩa Thục and the Revolution in Education

The Đông Kinh Nghĩa Thục (Tonkin Free School), founded in Hanoi in March 1907, was the most visible institutional expression of the reformist movement in the north. Its leaders included Lương Văn Can, a respected elderly scholar who served as head of the school, along with Nguyễn Quyền, Lê Đại, Phan Tuấn Phong, and other northern scholars. The school was inspired in part by Japan's Keio academy and by the reformist ideas then circulating through Chinese and Japanese writings. Phan Châu Trinh and other central Vietnamese reformers had contacts with its founders.

The school's methods were novel. It offered free or low-cost instruction, relying on donations and volunteer teachers. It taught quốc ngữ, Chinese characters, and French, together with geography, history, mathematics, and sciences. Its textbooks and public lectures promoted modern knowledge, civic responsibility, and national consciousness, and its curriculum included materials such as the Văn Minh Tân Học Sách, a text associated with the school that urged educational and social reform. The school also supported commercial ventures and encouraged cultural changes such as cutting men's long hair and wearing practical clothing, which symbolized rejection of old customs.

Sources describe rapid growth, with classes expanding quickly and enrollments said to reach several hundred, and some accounts higher; students should be cautious about the figures, since they come from commemorative and official sources with different purposes. What is clear is that the school attracted both adults and young people and held public lectures that drew audiences. It also sponsored the writing and circulation of poems and songs in quốc ngữ, which carried reformist messages to readers who could not read classical Chinese.

The French authorities soon grew suspicious. They saw in the school a center of political agitation. It was closed by the end of 1907 or the very start of 1908, depending on the account, within a year of its opening, and in the following months, particularly after the Hanoi poisoning plot of June 1908, many of its leaders were arrested or exiled, a number of them to Côn Đảo. The brevity of its existence has led some historians to see it as a failure. A better view is that it demonstrated the political significance of education. It put quốc ngữ to the service of national awakening, linked reformist ideas to institutional practice, and created a model that later schools and associations imitated.

The school's story also illustrates the limits of colonial tolerance. French policy encouraged Franco-Vietnamese schools that would train auxiliaries, but a privately run school that taught civic ideals and national identity crossed an invisible line. This ambivalence, with education promoted as a "civilizing" instrument and feared as a political one, recurs throughout Chapter 124's account of schools and newspapers.

The Tonkin Free School made an argument through its daily practices. A classroom that used quốc ngữ to explain geography, practical economics, and the condition of other countries implicitly challenged the assumption that political knowledge belonged only to examination scholars or colonial specialists. Public lectures brought material beyond the students formally enrolled, while the circulation of pamphlets extended the project into household and village discussion. Rather than discarding classical learning outright, instructors selectively reorganized what counted as useful knowledge. The goal was to produce readers able to compare societies, discuss public affairs, and act together, even though French officials might interpret such education as preparing political opposition.

Accounts of the school's organizational scale are difficult to reconcile because they variously count enrolled pupils, attenders at lectures, affiliated classes, or readers reached by published material. It is therefore preferable to describe its documented activities and regional connections than to claim an exact membership or enrollment unsupported by a named report. Lương Văn Can and associated educators were not merely symbolic leaders: their fundraising, classroom management, selection of texts, and public communications illustrate how cultural reform required administrative labor. The school also intersected with commercial networks that could support educational ventures financially. The connection between practical enterprise and schooling suggests that reformers envisioned a stronger society across economic as well as intellectual dimensions.

Its closure revealed the boundaries of tolerated modernization. French administrators favored limited technical and vernacular instruction where it could supply productive colonial subjects; they feared forms of instruction that invited students to measure colonial rule against the political institutions of independent nations. The same page discussing the geography of Asia could become evidence of patriotic agitation depending on its authorship and setting. Suppression did not eliminate the intellectual demand the school had expressed, but it deprived participants of a visible institutional center. Later journalists and political organizers inherited its conviction that language, texts, and educational practice could create a politically informed public even without a formal political party.

Historical Vocabulary I
Civic reform, popular enlightenment, quốc ngữ, and modernization

Civic reform refers to changes meant to give ordinary people rights, knowledge, and responsibilities within public life. Popular enlightenment (khai dân trí) meant raising the people's knowledge through schools, lectures, and printed materials. Quốc ngữ ("national language") is the Romanized Vietnamese script, developed by seventeenth-century missionaries, later used by the French administration and adopted by reformers as a tool for mass literacy. Modernization (duy tân, "renew"), here, meant adopting selected foreign techniques and institutions while claiming to preserve national identity.

Potential misunderstanding: These terms do not mean "Westernization" in a simple sense. Reformers drew on Chinese, Japanese, and Western sources, and many wished to retain Confucian ethics while changing political and educational structures.

The Duy Tân Reform Movement in Central Vietnam

While the Đông Kinh Nghĩa Thục operated in Hanoi, the heart of the Duy Tân (Modernization) reform movement lay in central Vietnam, especially Quảng Nam and neighboring provinces. This was the homeland of Phan Châu Trinh, Huỳnh Thúc Kháng, Trần Quý Cáp, and others. Huỳnh Thúc Kháng, born in 1876, was a degree-holder who became one of the movement's principal organizers and later a prolific journalist. Trần Quý Cáp, a scholar-official, taught and wrote in the same spirit. Together with Phan Châu Trinh they formed a core group that combined scholarly prestige with practical programs.

The movement's activities were local and concrete. Reformers opened schools that taught quốc ngữ and modern subjects, sometimes in private homes or rented buildings. They organized commercial associations and cooperative enterprises, aiming to strengthen Vietnamese economic life and reduce dependence on Chinese and French merchants; one prominent example was a commercial association (thương hội) in Quảng Nam that traded in goods and sought to model collective enterprise. They encouraged agricultural improvement, local manufacture, and reading groups. They urged changes in customs, including shorter hair for men, simpler clothing, and the abandonment of costly rituals and superstitions.

These cultural changes had political weight. In a society where hairstyle and dress marked status and loyalty, cutting one's hair was a visible break with the old order, and it later became an identifying mark that French and Vietnamese officials used to find reformers and protest participants. The reformers' message was that national survival required changes in everyday life, and that these changes should be undertaken voluntarily by communities rather than imposed from above.

The movement was not simply scholars teaching peasants. It also relied on local notables, merchants, and village leaders who saw practical benefits in schools and associations. Some officials in Annam sympathized, though the court's loyalty to the protectorate system limited what they could do. The movement's strength lay in the combination of respected leaders, local institutions, and a clear program. Its weakness was exposure: it operated openly, and open organization could be monitored and suppressed.

In Quảng Nam the reform movement acquired a distinctly local institutional character. Advocates promoted new schools, cooperative enterprises, commercial associations, and the study of subjects meant to strengthen livelihoods. They also encouraged changes in clothing and hairstyles that made the contrast between reform and inherited convention immediately visible. Cutting a scholar's traditional hair was not equivalent to joining a revolutionary conspiracy, yet it could signal support for a new public ideal and provoke hostility from conservative elites or authorities. Reform thus operated through the everyday habits of commerce, family life, and education as well as through formal manifestos.

The movement was never uniform across the central provinces. Local wealth, the cooperation of village notables, access to teachers, and the willingness of authorities to tolerate educational activity all varied. A market town with merchants able to finance a school enjoyed opportunities unavailable to a poor agricultural district. Reformist networks could spread along routes linking Quảng Nam to other provincial centers, but their political influence depended on whether local people interpreted proposals as practical assistance, cultural attack, or threats to existing hierarchies. Huỳnh Thúc Kháng and Trần Quý Cáp are essential individual examples because their later treatment by the colonial state demonstrates the high cost of public reform and its imperfect separation from anticolonial resistance.

The colonial government's changing response is particularly revealing. Many of the practices advocated by reformers—literacy, commercial efficiency, regular schooling—resembled measures colonial officials also associated with modernization. But French administrators claimed the right to determine who could organize them, what political meaning they carried, and whether independent institutions could survive. Suspicion intensified when taxation protests spread in 1908, and officials interpreted the overlap of people and ideas as evidence of a coordinated conspiracy. The inference should be investigated rather than simply accepted. Reformist organization and rural protest were connected, but local grievances could generate action for reasons different from those supplied by the scholars' political programs.

Section 127.8

The Antitax Demonstrations of 1908 and Colonial Repression

In March 1908, crowds began gathering in Quảng Nam to demand relief from taxes and labor obligations. The movement spread to neighboring provinces, including Quảng Ngãi, Bình Định, and Thừa Thiên, and reached as far north as parts of Nghệ An and Hà Tĩnh. Villagers marched on district and provincial offices, petitioned mandarins, and in some places confronted officials directly. They protested the head tax, the heavy burden of corvée (compulsory labor on roads and public works), and the exactions of local officials. Rural households in central Vietnam were especially vulnerable: land was scarce, harvests were uncertain, and colonial taxes were collected in cash from households with little access to markets.

The relationship between the protests and the reformers is a central historical question. Reformers such as Phan Châu Trinh and Huỳnh Thúc Kháng had advocated peaceful reform and had criticized abusive taxation and corvée. Some local reformers supported or advised protesters, and in several places protest leaders had ties to Duy Tân schools and associations. The protests also shared visible features with the movement, notably the cutting of hair, which gave the demonstrations their popular name in some accounts, the "short-hair" movement. But the grievances of villagers were distinct. They concerned immediate burdens, and they did not necessarily include commitment to civic reform, constitutional rights, or the end of the colonial order. Participants differed from place to place, and their motives included local resentments against officials and sometimes against wealthier villagers.

The colonial and court response was severe. Authorities used soldiers and police to disperse crowds, arrested hundreds of participants and reformist leaders, and tried many before provincial and colonial courts. Trần Quý Cáp was executed in March 1908 in Khánh Hòa. Phan Châu Trinh and Huỳnh Thúc Kháng were arrested and sentenced to death, and these sentences were commuted to imprisonment on Côn Đảo, along with many others. Estimates of the numbers arrested, imprisoned, and killed differ widely, and students should be wary of precise totals, which often come from retrospective or partisan accounts. The wider crackdown coincided with a separate event in Hanoi, the poisoning plot against French soldiers in June 1908, which alarmed French officials and added to their suspicion of political networks, including the Đông Kinh Nghĩa Thục.

The repression reshaped reformist politics. It destroyed or exiled much of the Duy Tân leadership in central Vietnam, discredited the idea that open legal reform could proceed safely, and convinced many Vietnamese that colonial authorities would not tolerate even peaceful organization. It also fed the argument, associated with Phan Bội Châu, that only organized revolution could succeed. Phan Châu Trinh survived on Côn Đảo until 1911, when appeals in France, supported by the Ligue des Droits de l'Homme and French sympathizers, secured his release into exile in France.

The movement spread through districts where smallholders and laboring households experienced obligations in immediate, concrete ways. Head taxes required cash regardless of harvest success, while corvée demands removed time from farming or household work. Taxes on commodities and the rising costs of everyday necessities also shaped perceptions of official injustice. Demonstrators did not necessarily interpret these demands through the concepts of republican citizenship that occupied elite writers. Some appealed for relief from particular burdens or abusive officials; others placed their grievances within a wider criticism of the foreign-protected bureaucracy. The historian must reconstruct these motivations from petitions, local testimony, and reports that were often collected by officials eager to discover political instigators.

The protest's geography challenges a simple top-down account. News moved between villages and district centers through markets, kinship ties, schools, and religious gatherings. Large groups could assemble around officials even when no national committee directed their action. Some demonstrations sought to present petitions or force public negotiations; elsewhere confrontation escalated. Women and poorer residents could participate without leaving personal narratives that survived in archives. The absence of those accounts should be acknowledged rather than replaced by assumptions about what all villagers wanted. A movement capable of mobilizing many households did not automatically possess the organizational structure to sustain prolonged resistance once armed forces intervened.

The colonial reaction conflated public agitation with reformist leadership to a degree that requires careful evidentiary testing. Phan Châu Trinh was imprisoned and later exiled; Huỳnh Thúc Kháng and other activists were sentenced to long terms, often served on Côn Đảo; Trần Quý Cáp was executed. Administrative investigations treated reformist texts, school networks, and acquaintance with protesters as potentially incriminating. The repression broke much of the reform movement's public institutional presence while ensuring that its leaders became examples of injustice for a later generation. This experience gave reformers a powerful lesson: even where an educational project expressly rejected armed revolt, the colonial state might interpret independent mass organization as an unacceptable challenge to authority.

From Dynastic Restoration to the Việt Nam Quang Phục Hội

After leaving Japan in 1909, Phan Bội Châu spent time in Siam and southern China, supporting a scattered network of exiles and sympathizers. The collapse of the Đông Du had left him without a secure base and with fewer resources, and French pressure on neighboring governments made organizing difficult. He continued to write and to plan, and he took advantage of any opportunity for foreign sympathy. In 1911 an opportunity of a different sort appeared: the Chinese Revolution overthrew the Qing dynasty and led to the establishment of the Republic of China in 1912.

The Chinese example changed his thinking. For years he had accepted a constitutional monarchy as the target of the Vietnamese struggle, partly because of Prince Cường Để's symbolic role and partly because of the reformist and Japanese models he admired. The Chinese Revolution demonstrated that a Asian monarchy could be replaced by a republic and that republican activism could draw broad support. In 1912, in Guangzhou, Phan Bội Châu and his associates dissolved the Duy Tân Hội and founded the Việt Nam Quang Phục Hội (Vietnam Restoration League). Its program called for expelling the French and establishing a republic. Cường Để was retained as a nominal figure in the organization, an indication that the transition was gradual and that monarchist loyalties had not vanished. The shift should not be treated as a simple abandonment of monarchy, since Phan Bội Châu and his colleagues continued to work with royalist networks in Vietnam for years.

The Quang Phục Hội adopted a more militant approach. It created a structure of leaders and military and civil functions, and it issued banknotes and proclamations intended to demonstrate state-like legitimacy. It also authorized armed action. In 1913 its operatives were linked to attacks in Hanoi, including a bombing at a hotel that killed French officers, which prompted a harsh crackdown, as well as to assassination attempts on Vietnamese officials seen as collaborators. French intelligence tracked the organization closely. In late 1913 Chinese authorities in Guangdong, under pressure from France and the local military governor, arrested Phan Bội Châu, and he remained in custody until 1917.

The Quang Phục Hội therefore demonstrates both the ambitions and the vulnerabilities of exile politics. It gave Vietnamese nationalists a republican program, a vocabulary of citizenship, and an organizational model, but it lacked a secure base, reliable funding, and a mass following. It also exposed the movement to infiltration, since a network of couriers and sympathizers stretching across borders was difficult to protect. What remained for later activists was the idea that Vietnam should be a republic, an idea that would shape both the VNQDD and, from a different ideological direction, communist movements.

Phan Bội Châu's turn toward republicanism followed an international transformation that seemed to reopen political possibilities. The fall of the Qing dynasty encouraged exiled activists to think a monarchy could be displaced and a republic constructed through political organization and armed revolt. Guangzhou offered contact with Chinese republican politicians, publishing networks, and military circles, but these connections did not guarantee material assistance. As in Japan, Vietnamese activists had to negotiate their aims within the priorities and rivalries of their hosts. A republican title could help recruit students excited by Chinese events, while at the same time alienating monarchist supporters who associated sovereignty with the Nguyễn house.

The Việt Nam Quang Phục Hội pursued organization across borders, sought funds, and associated its message with the possibility of simultaneous action in Vietnam. The use of violence created new dangers. Bomb attacks attributed to its networks in 1913 intensified French surveillance and retaliation; the circumstances and responsibility of particular actions must be differentiated from organizational rhetoric. An exiled leadership could issue proclamations or acquire limited weapons without having reliable authority over dispersed groups inside the country. Reports from colonial police and later memoirs often disagree about the strength and coherence of the organization. Neither should be treated as a transparent enumeration of active revolutionaries.

Republicanism did not erase earlier connections or solve the problem of popular participation. The symbolic prestige of Phan Bội Châu remained closely tied to scholar networks, while financial and communication systems continued to rely on trusted intermediaries. The new program placed greater stress on a government representing a national people, but activists still disputed how such a government would be chosen and what rights ordinary residents would exercise. These unresolved questions anticipate later divisions among constitutionalists, radical journalists, republican parties, and communist organizers. The organization represents a genuine ideological transformation, not the final stage of an inevitable march from dynasty to republic to a single revolutionary party.

The Duy Tân Emperor and the Failed Royalist Uprising of 1916

The most dramatic episode of the First World War years in Vietnam involved the young emperor himself. Duy Tân had been placed on the throne in 1907, at the age of about seven, after the French deposed his father, Thành Thái, who had shown signs of independence and was declared unfit to rule. As Duy Tân grew older he displayed a resentful awareness of his powerlessness, and Vietnamese patriots in central Vietnam saw in him a possible symbol of resistance. The Quang Phục Hội's central Vietnamese organizers, including Trần Cao Vân, a scholar and former prisoner, and Thái Phiên, built a network of supporters and approached the emperor.

The plot reflected the wartime context. Since 1914 France had drawn heavily on Indochina for soldiers, laborers, and resources, and rumors circulated that more recruitment would follow. Many Vietnamese feared conscription for the European war, and the demand for rice, taxes, and labor had risen. Activists hoped that disaffection among the population and among Vietnamese troops would help an uprising. The plan envisaged a revolt in Huế and surrounding provinces, timed for early May 1916, with the emperor joining the revolt and proclaiming a restored independent government.

The conspiracy was discovered. French and court officials learned of the plan, partly through informants and partly through the rumors that had spread, and the revolt collapsed before it could begin in earnest. On the night of 3 May 1916 Duy Tân left the palace and was captured shortly afterward. Trần Cao Vân, Thái Phiên, and others were tried, and several leaders, including those two, were executed later that month. The French deposed Duy Tân and exiled him to Réunion Island, where he spent decades; he later served with the Free French forces and died in an aircraft accident in 1945. The French replaced him with Khải Định, a ruler who was more compliant and who reigned until 1925.

The episode left several legacies. It showed that a young emperor could, at least briefly, align himself with an anticolonial conspiracy, and it supported a view among French officials that the monarchy remained a potential rallying point and therefore needed close control. For Vietnamese activists it produced martyrs and a story of royal patriotism. It also showed the limits of conspiracy: the uprising's secrecy was compromised by its dependence on wide networks and on rumors that outran organization. In the same period, other unrest occurred, such as the 1917 Thái Nguyên mutiny, in which Vietnamese soldiers and prisoners seized the town for several days, a revolt linked to Quang Phục Hội networks and ended by French troops; its prisoner-leader Lương Ngọc Quyến, a former Đông Du student, was killed.

The royalist plot of 1916 grew out of an especially contradictory wartime situation. France demanded soldiers, laborers, revenue, and loyalty from its colonial possessions while European governments claimed to be fighting for national survival. Local military units and officials were expected to enforce colonial order, yet discontent could circulate among conscripts and workers whose family obligations were disrupted. Conspirators sought to exploit this vulnerability by linking an imperial figurehead to military action and local networks of activists. The emperor Duy Tân had already been associated with a court whose freedom of action was tightly constrained; his participation could give the rising a recognizable claim of legitimacy even without an effective army under his command.

Planning depended on contacts among central Vietnamese officials, soldiers, and sympathizers who could pass instructions before surveillance intervened. Communication difficulties and the limited number of forces prepared to act meant the organizers were vulnerable once the scheme became known. The French response separated what it considered a disloyal emperor from the institutions of government, exiling Duy Tân to Réunion and executing prominent organizers including Trần Cao Vân and Thái Phiên. The punishment reinforced the colonial state's control over the court. For some Vietnamese observers the affair confirmed that even royal authority survived only on the condition of obedience to French power; for others it demonstrated the continued emotional influence of the monarchy.

The wartime chronology also includes the Thái Nguyên mutiny of 1917, which should be distinguished from the earlier imperial plot rather than merged into a single royalist movement. The combination of military grievances, political prisoners, and armed rebellion at Thái Nguyên exposed vulnerabilities in the colonial security system, though the authorities ultimately restored control. Both episodes revealed the appeal of armed action and its severe organizational difficulties. Neither can be understood solely through the ideology of the individual leaders: local conditions, access to weapons, the loyalty of military units, and the speed of French countermeasures determined the possibilities of success or failure.

Section 127.11

World War I, Overseas Vietnamese, and Postwar Political Expectations

The First World War affected Vietnamese politics far from the front. France recruited tens of thousands of Vietnamese soldiers and laborers for service in Europe, commonly estimated at around ninety to one hundred thousand, though figures vary by source and category (soldiers, workers, and others). Many worked in factories, arsenals, and on the front-support lines. Their experience in France, living among French workers, seeing French society and its divisions, and sometimes encountering political ideas, differed from the colonial image of French power. Some returned with new expectations and a more critical view, though the extent of political radicalization among them varied greatly, and many returned simply to ordinary life.

Within Vietnam the war brought heavier taxes, requisitions, inflation in some commodities, and fear of conscription, which fed the unrest of 1916–1917. Colonial authorities increased surveillance and political policing. The Sûreté Générale was reorganized in 1917 under Louis Marty, who directed political intelligence for the Union and shaped the French understanding of nationalist movements. The war also created political openings. French officials, needing support, promised rewards and reforms, and Vietnamese commentators noted the gap between wartime rhetoric about liberty and the realities of colonial control.

Vietnamese communities abroad also grew. In Paris, a small group of intellectuals and activists, among them Phan Châu Trinh (who had been released to France in 1911 and arrested in 1914 on suspicion of plotting), the lawyer Phan Văn Trường, and others, formed a circle that debated colonial policy and published criticisms. After the 1918 armistice, the language of self-determination, associated with U.S. President Woodrow Wilson and with revolutionary Russia, reached Asian activists. In June 1919 a petition containing demands for Vietnamese rights, including legal equality, press and assembly freedoms, and representation, was presented to the Allied delegations in Paris. It circulated under the name Nguyễn Ái Quốc and reflected the collaboration of several members of the Paris circle; the detailed activities and political development of Nguyễn Ái Quốc belong to Chapter 128. The Paris Peace Conference did not address colonial claims, and the petition had no effect on the colonial order.

The war's end thus left a mixed legacy. It had raised expectations without delivering concessions, it had given a few thousand Vietnamese direct experience of France, and it had introduced new political vocabularies. The French response, sometimes called the policy of "collaboration" or association, offered limited reforms, such as modest expansion of education and indigenous representation, without altering the basic distribution of power. In this setting, a new kind of politics developed, grounded in newspapers, associations, and legal argument.

Recruitment for the European war had direct consequences for Vietnamese households as well as for the men who went overseas. Soldiers faced military discipline, illness, unfamiliar weather, and in some cases combat; civilian workers served in factories, transport, and other labor operations. Their reasons for enlisting or accepting recruitment varied, and some decisions were shaped by pressure from officials rather than unconstrained individual choice. Scholarly research identifies 48,922 soldiers and 48,254 workers recruited for service in France between 1915 and 1919, giving a combined 97,176 in that particular accounting. These categories must not be collapsed into a single figure for combat troops, nor should recruitment be equated with actual overseas deployment in every case.

Life in France could expose recruits to new experiences of political hierarchy and racial discrimination. Some discovered higher wages or unfamiliar social liberties; others encountered abusive treatment or restrictions that belied French promises of equality. Letters, returning veterans, and workers provided information about the metropolis, although most people in Vietnam learned of the war indirectly through tax demands, news, and family disruptions. The colonial government expected wartime service to reinforce loyalty to France. Yet the contradiction between service to a republican empire and limited rights at home offered material for criticism by later nationalists. The war helped generate new questions rather than producing one uniform ideological response among those who served.

The armistice and the Paris peace settlements raised hopes among some educated activists that international language about self-determination might open a diplomatic route toward reform. The Vietnamese petition presented in Paris in 1919 demanded civil liberties and administrative protections, rather than immediate recognition of an independent Vietnamese republic. Its wider significance lies in placing colonial grievances in the idiom of rights and representation before an international audience. Nguyễn Ái Quốc's development through these debates belongs to the next chapter, but the petition was part of a larger climate in which constitutionalists, revolutionaries, overseas students, and returned workers reconsidered which political claims could be made to France and which would have to be pursued independently.

Political Journalism, Constitutionalism, and the Saigon Public Sphere

By the early 1920s Saigon had developed a distinctive political environment. As a colony, Cochinchina was governed by French law, with a press regime that, though restrictive, allowed more open discussion than in the protectorates. The city had a French-educated indigenous elite, many of them landlords' sons, lawyers, doctors, and journalists, and a growing readership of quốc ngữ and French-language papers. Philippe Peycam's study of the period shows how newspapers created a political sphere in which Vietnamese writers could debate rights, representation, and the nature of colonial rule, and how journalists became political actors.

The Constitutionalist Party, formed in 1923 and associated with Bùi Quang Chiêu and Nguyễn Phan Long, was the leading organized political group. Bùi Quang Chiêu, trained as an agricultural engineer in France, had earlier edited a paper called La Tribune Indigène, and Nguyễn Phan Long edited L'Écho Annamite. The party sought reforms within the existing system: greater indigenous representation in the Colonial Council and other bodies, press and association freedoms, equality in the civil service, and an end to economic discrimination. Many of its leaders were propertied and cautious, and they competed in colonial elections with a limited franchise. It is a mistake to equate all constitutionalists with collaborators. They pursued a strategy of reform through legal channels, and they often criticized abuses, though they did not seek immediate independence and were criticized by more radical Vietnamese for accepting colonial limits.

A more radical voice came from Nguyễn An Ninh. Born in 1900 and educated in law in Paris, he returned to Saigon in 1922 and became a leading public speaker. In 1923 he delivered a widely noted lecture on the aspirations of Vietnamese youth, and in December of that year he launched La Cloche Fêlée (The Cracked Bell), a French-language newspaper, with Phan Văn Trường as a collaborator. The paper criticized colonial injustice, called for intellectual and political emancipation, and challenged the constitutionalists' caution. Authorities prosecuted Nguyễn An Ninh repeatedly, and he was imprisoned in the mid-1920s and again later, dying in custody on Côn Đảo in 1943.

Saigon's press debates were conducted in French and quốc ngữ, and each audience had distinct characteristics. French-language papers reached French officials, metropolitan opinion, and the educated elite; quốc ngữ papers reached a wider, less wealthy readership. Newspapers were also constrained by censorship, fines, and the threat of prosecution. In Hanoi and Huế, journalists such as Nguyễn Văn Vĩnh and Phạm Quỳnh worked in a more restricted environment and often defended cultural cooperation with France and constitutional monarchy under protection, giving the northern and central press a different character. The contrast illustrates the way colonial jurisdiction shaped political expression.

The contrast between the Constitutionalist Party and Nguyễn An Ninh was not merely that one favored moderation and the other outspoken protest. Constitutionalists sought practical leverage within the institutions that existed, including elected offices available to narrowly qualified voters, commercial associations, and the prestige of French republican law. Their supporters could believe that improved representation would protect Vietnamese property and educational interests. Critics replied that these institutions gave colonial elites a stake in a system that denied broader popular sovereignty. There were also tensions within the constitutionalist camp over how far to press demands when officials threatened to close newspapers or curtail opportunities for participation.

Nguyễn An Ninh's journalistic intervention raised a different question: who had the right to speak for Vietnamese society? By using French, he addressed officials and educated readers in a language that claimed the principles of liberty and equality as common standards of judgment. In the early issues of La Cloche Fêlée he presented a newspaper produced by Vietnamese writers as a means of confronting injustice and preparing a more independent public culture. Issue 3 of 24 December 1923 included an article about a gathering directed against colonial capitalist interests. The existence of an identifiable article permits analysis of the paper's political priorities without pretending that a general summary of the journal is a quotation from a particular issue.

The newspaper world was also a business and a network of personal risks. Editors needed subscriptions, printing facilities, advertising or patrons, contributors able to write under pressure, and a readership willing to risk association with controversial positions. Censorship and prosecution could exhaust finances without formally outlawing a political movement. Readers, in turn, did not have identical expectations: a merchant might seek fair access to credit, a student might demand political rights, and a provincial reader might value a defense of cultural dignity. The Saigon public sphere widened discussion but remained socially unequal, vulnerable to the police, and internally divided over constitutional, cultural, and revolutionary alternatives.

Phan Bội Châu's Arrest and the Political Mobilizations of 1925–1926

On 30 June 1925, French agents seized Phan Bội Châu at the railway station in the French Concession of Shanghai and transported him to Vietnam. The circumstances of his arrest remain contested. Some accounts hold that Lâm Đức Thụ, a Vietnamese who worked with revolutionary circles in southern China and was later identified as a French police informant, helped bring about the arrest, and that Thụ's connections included contact with Nguyễn Ái Quốc's group; the details, including how much blame attaches to whom, are treated in Chapter 128 and remain debated. What is clear is that the French had long wanted to detain Phan Bội Châu and that his seizure alarmed Vietnamese patriots.

The authorities brought him before a criminal court in Hanoi in November 1925. The trial attracted large public attention. Newspapers, petitions, and protest meetings called for leniency, and a widespread sense developed that executing or imprisoning a revered elder scholar would damage French standing. The court sentenced him to hard labor for life, but the newly arrived Governor-General, Alexandre Varenne, intervened, and the sentence was commuted to confinement under guard in Huế, at a residence at Bến Ngự. There he lived, closely watched but receiving visitors and writing, until his death on 29 October 1940.

Phan Châu Trinh returned to Saigon from France in 1925, in poor health. In late 1925 he delivered public lectures criticizing autocracy and calling for a democratic order, including a well-known address contrasting rule by a monarch with rule by the people, and he had earlier, in 1922, publicly criticized Emperor Khải Định's conduct in a letter, often called the "seven points" letter, written while the emperor was visiting France. He died on 24 March 1926. His funeral on 4 April drew a very large turnout, often described as tens of thousands, and was accompanied by mourning observances, student strikes and boycotts in schools in Saigon and elsewhere, and expulsions of students by school authorities. The precise numbers vary by source and should be treated with caution.

These events were turning points because they showed that symbolic leadership and public commemoration could mobilize large numbers without a single party. Elder patriots, one in captivity and one in death, became rallying points for young people who had grown up in a world of schools, newspapers, and associations. The French authorities, for their part, learned that heavy-handed treatment of respected figures could backfire. The mobilizations also coincided with the accession of the young Bảo Đại, who was formally enthroned in 1926 but remained in France for his education, and with the death of Khải Định, which left the monarchy a weak political presence.

Phan Bội Châu's seizure in Shanghai marked the vulnerability of an exile movement dependent on foreign jurisdictions and covert travel. The French authorities could not ordinarily arrest Vietnamese activists everywhere abroad by their own officers' direct powers; they relied on political intelligence, contacts with local authorities, and the interception or betrayal of networks. Once in colonial custody, Phan was useful to the government as a prisoner through whom it could demonstrate that resistance abroad would not escape punishment. But his age, reputation, and decades of visible patriotic sacrifice made him difficult to treat as an obscure conspirator. Newspapers, students, and civic groups turned the case into a public argument about whether the colonial state could claim political legitimacy while criminalizing a widely revered nationalist figure.

The response was not simply a reflex of personal affection. Campaigns for clemency revealed an organizational public created by the changes of the preceding two decades. Petitions circulated through different towns, and journalists commented on events before a wide readership. Participants did not necessarily endorse Phan's historical willingness to employ violence; they could still object to his treatment or honor his commitment to national independence. The decision to commute the punishment and restrict him to Huế created a continuing public symbol. Visitors, letters, and later recollections helped sustain his presence in national political memory, although police restrictions sharply limited what he could organize directly.

Phan Châu Trinh's death in March 1926 produced another form of mobilization, at once civic, emotional, and explicitly political. Funeral committees, school groups, and associations organized ceremonies in the south and commemorations elsewhere; students sometimes confronted disciplinary measures for taking part. The widespread mourning demonstrated that a reformer who had opposed armed uprising could command respect among people committed to several political programs. Contemporary reports of crowd size vary and should not be converted into precise counts without a named source. More important than a single estimate is the evidence of organized participation, interrupted schooling, and colonial concern. The campaigns of 1925–1926 showed that public memory and symbolic leadership could mobilize people even when established political organizations were weak.

Section 127.14

Students, Workers, Youth Associations, and Popular Political Activism

By the mid-1920s, a new generation had come of age in colonial schools. Hue-Tam Ho Tai's study of radicalism in this period emphasizes that young, educated Vietnamese, many of them from modest backgrounds, were disillusioned by blocked careers, racial discrimination in employment, and the gap between the promises of French education and the reality of colonial society. They read newspapers and pamphlets, formed study circles and reading groups, and debated ideas from China, Japan, France, and Russia. Their sense of grievance was educational as well as political: school rules, discriminatory exams, and the dismissal of student protesters became causes of agitation.

Student and youth organizations multiplied. In central Vietnam, the Phục Việt Hội, which later became the Tân Việt Cách Mạng Đảng (Revolutionary Party of New Vietnam), drew teachers and students and sought a path between older nationalism and newer radical ideas, and it later split as members moved toward other organizations. In Saigon, groups influenced by Nguyễn An Ninh's circle formed secret societies and discussion groups. Publishing houses and bookstores, such as the Nam Đồng publishing circle in Hanoi, circulated political literature under the cover of ordinary business. Associations of mutual aid, sports clubs, and alumni groups supplied meeting places that the police found hard to ban outright.

Workers also began to organize. Saigon's Ba Son shipyard saw a strike in August 1925 in which workers resisted an attempt to send French ships to China and demanded better conditions; the incident is often cited as a sign of emerging labor consciousness, though it also belongs to the story of communist organizing examined in Chapter 128. Plantation and mine laborers, port workers, and urban artisans faced harsh conditions, and strikes remained relatively small in this period. The relationship between intellectual leaders and workers was uneven. Many nationalist intellectuals regarded workers as potential supporters but had few direct connections, while labor activism grew mostly through other channels.

Mass political culture also developed through public lectures, theatrical performances, songs, and popular literature in quốc ngữ. Cafés, bookshops, and schools served as informal political spaces. The authorities responded with surveillance, informants, and censorship. The result was a political environment in which a wide range of organizations, from loosely structured youth groups to tightly organized secret parties, competed for the allegiance of young Vietnamese, and in which no organization held a monopoly.

The emerging student public crossed institutional and regional boundaries, but did not consist of a unified political organization. Students in Hanoi, Huế, and Saigon encountered different curricula, censorship regimes, and expectations from their families. Some belonged to households invested in colonial education as a route to professional employment; others experienced discrimination and restricted opportunities as evidence that the system could not provide the future it promised. Strikes and boycotts following the commemorations of 1926 made schools into contested political spaces. The act of leaving class could be understood by officials as disobedience, by participants as an expression of civic dignity, and by parents as a threat to their children's careers. These different meanings are part of the history of mobilization.

Urban labor organization followed a different but intersecting trajectory. Workers faced wage disputes, discipline, dangerous conditions, and the uncertainty of employment in shipyards, transport, factories, and plantations. The August 1925 strike at the Ba Son shipyard in Saigon is often connected in later revolutionary histories with opposition to French naval activity involving China. The strike itself and workplace grievances are historical events; the assertion that all participants shared a unified international revolutionary motive is harder to establish. Political activists attempted to interpret labor action within larger nationalist frameworks, while many workers understandably focused on immediate bargaining demands. Both kinds of motivation could coexist without being identical.

The distinction between elite organizing and popular participation is crucial here. A newspaper could present students as representatives of the nation, but a woman employed in a workshop or a rural laborer arriving in town might relate to national politics through different experiences of coercion and opportunity. Political meetings, reading circles, mutual assistance, kinship, and commercial contacts allowed new ideas to move between social groups without producing equal access to leadership. The colonial police often treated these connections as evidence of conspiracy, while political memoirs later converted complex relationships into coherent organizational lineages. Historians should reconstruct concrete actions—who met, who distributed texts, who paid dues, who struck, and who faced punishment—before assuming a fully national political consciousness among participants.

Everyday Life and Social History

Student Activism, Workers, Urban Meetings, and Popular Political Culture

For a student in a colonial secondary school in the 1920s, political life began in small things: access to newspapers, borrowing banned pamphlets, copying poems, joining a study circle, or deciding whether to join a strike or a mourning ceremony. Parents could be anxious, since expulsion could end a career, and teachers were often caught between sympathy and the risk of dismissal. Urban workers lived in crowded quarters, worked long hours for wages that rose slowly, and saw political talk as an affair of the educated, until strikes or evening classes brought them into contact with organizers. Women and girls, who had less access to school, took part in mourning and reading circles but were less visible in official records.

Historians reconstruct these experiences from memoirs, police reports, and newspapers, each of which has biases: memoirs written decades later often shape memory of the past, and police reports reflect what the colonial state feared. Students should ask, in every case, whose voice is recorded and whose is absent.

The Việt Nam Quốc Dân Đảng and Revolutionary Republican Nationalism

The Việt Nam Quốc Dân Đảng (Vietnamese Nationalist Party, VNQDD) emerged from the Hanoi publishing circle called Nam Đồng Thư Xã, founded in 1927 by young teachers and students including Nguyễn Thái Học and Phạm Tuấn Tài, together with others such as Nguyễn Khắc Nhu and Phạm Tuấn Lâm. The group began by publishing and distributing books and journals, and it gradually became a political party. On 25 December 1927 the VNQDD was formally established. Its model was the Chinese Kuomintang and Sun Yat-sen's ideas of nationalism, people's rights, and livelihood, and its leaders adopted organizational methods used in Chinese revolutionary parties.

Nguyễn Thái Học, born in 1902 in Vĩnh Yên province, was a former student at the Hanoi commercial school and an energetic organizer. The party's objective was to overthrow French rule and establish a republic, and it described a program with several phases, including preparatory organization, revolutionary action, and constitution of a new government, though accounts of its precise doctrinal documents vary. It recruited teachers, students, clerks, small traders, and, importantly, Vietnamese soldiers (tirailleurs) in the colonial army, whose military skills and access to arms were essential for any armed uprising. Its structure comprised cells organized hierarchically, with branches across Tonkin, strong in the delta provinces and the northern midlands, and some presence in Annam and Cochinchina. Membership numbers are uncertain: French estimates and later nationalist claims differ widely, and this chapter does not adopt one figure.

The VNQDD differed from earlier movements in its emphasis on party discipline, secrecy, and military preparation, and from the Constitutionalists in rejecting accommodation. It differed from the exile movement of Phan Bội Châu in being based inside Vietnam and drawn mainly from a younger generation. It had links with Chinese republican circles, and some party members traveled to southern China for contacts and training. It was also not simply a failed predecessor of communism: it had its own republican program, social base, and strategy, and it competed with communist organizations for recruits in the late 1920s.

Women were present in the party's ranks, notably Nguyễn Thị Giang, Nguyễn Thái Học's fiancée, who became a prominent member. The party was also internally divided over timing and tactics, with some leaders urging caution, while others pressed for action as French repression increased. These divisions, combined with a recruiting strategy that expanded rapidly and brought in members whose commitment and discretion were uneven, left the party vulnerable. Scholars such as Nguyễn Văn Khánh and Christopher Goscha identify the VNQDD as a significant organized challenge to colonial authority, and its history after 1930, including its survival in exile and later reconstitution, shows that the party did not disappear with Yên Bái.

The party's republican identity mattered because it proposed an alternative to both the restored monarchy and a gradual enlargement of rights within French institutions. Its early cadres drew on teachers, students, clerks, small businesspeople, and activists already familiar with political newspapers. Organization in cells promised secrecy, but secrecy was expensive: members had difficulty confirming the reliability of distant branches, obtaining money, and verifying instructions purportedly issued by leaders. The Nam Đồng publishing circle helped provide a political language and contacts, while Chinese republican developments supplied examples of how a clandestine movement might imagine a national army and elected government. These influences should not be confused with direct command by a foreign party.

The VNQDD faced practical questions that its rhetoric could not settle. Should it recruit openly among students or quietly among soldiers? Was success possible through a dramatic strike at military garrisons, or did revolution require years of rural organization? Could branches coordinate action across Tonkin while members traveled under police observation and depended upon uncertain correspondence? Intelligence reports reveal organizational vulnerability, but they also reflect authorities who saw conspiracy in many types of social contact. The party's surviving programs, memoirs, and judicial records should therefore be compared rather than treated as mutually confirming accounts of the same chain of command.

Women's participation exposes further limits of a leader-centered account. Nguyễn Thị Giang, known as Cô Giang, was a VNQDD activist and the fiancée of Nguyễn Thái Học, not his sister. Her association with him is historically important but does not exhaust her role, which included political commitment and activity within networks where women often carried messages, helped maintain contacts, and faced their own risks. Her sister Nguyễn Thị Bắc was also connected to the party. Later commemorations of Cô Giang's death sometimes foreground devotion to a male revolutionary rather than the independent choices women made. A fuller institutional history must therefore distinguish direct documentary evidence about responsibilities from romanticized retrospective narratives and identify women as participants in political organization rather than only relatives of men.

Section 127.16

The Yên Bái Uprising of 1930 and the Defeat of the VNQDD

In February 1929, Alfred François Bazin, a private labor recruiter who recruited workers for plantations in southern Indochina and who was resented by many Vietnamese for his methods, was assassinated in Hanoi. French investigators attributed the killing to the VNQDD, and many historians accept that members of the party were involved, though they note that responsibility within the organization, and whether party leaders authorized it, remain unclear, and that French authorities initially considered other groups as well. The distinction matters: historians should not assume that the central leadership approved every violent act attributed to the party. The French response was swift. Police arrested large numbers of suspected members, seized documents, and established a special criminal commission to try them, which badly damaged the party's structure.

Facing mass arrests and the loss of organization, VNQDD leaders debated whether to wait or to launch an uprising before the party was dismantled. Nguyễn Thái Học and others concluded that the time had come to act, planning a coordinated revolt across the delta and midlands, to be centered on mutinies by Vietnamese troops at several garrisons. The plan assumed that mutinies in French-officered colonial regiments would trigger wider rebellion. Preparations were rushed, communication among regions was poor, and the French Sûreté had informants inside the network.

On the night of 9–10 February 1930, the uprising began. At Yên Bái, Vietnamese soldiers and VNQDD members attacked the garrison and killed several French officers and noncommissioned officers, but the mutiny was quickly suppressed. Attacks and attempted actions occurred in other places, including Phú Thọ, Hưng Hóa, Lâm Thao, and Phả Lại, and a group around Hải Dương and Kiến An fought for a few days, but coordination failed and French reinforcements restored control within a short time. In the following days French aircraft bombed the village of Cổ Am, associated with the rebels. Nguyễn Thái Học was arrested later in February.

The repression was extensive. Colonial courts tried many accused, handing down numerous sentences of death, imprisonment, and transportation, with a number of death sentences later commuted, and on 17 June 1930 thirteen were executed at Yên Bái, among them Nguyễn Thái Học and Phó Đức Chính. Figures for total arrests and imprisonments vary widely across sources, and the chapter therefore presents them only as ranges where documentary support exists. The defeat should be explained by specific conditions: rushed planning under police pressure, French intelligence penetration, weak regional coordination, and the narrow military base of the plot. It does not show that noncommunist nationalism was inherently incapable of achieving independence. The party survived in reduced form, but its organized strength in Tonkin was broken, and the executed leaders became martyrs in a commemorative tradition that later movements, northern and southern, claimed in different ways.

Alfred François Bazin's assassination illustrates the danger of equating a violent event with a single organization's approved policy. Bazin operated a private labor-recruitment enterprise implicated in the coercive conditions of the colonial plantation economy; he was not simply a government official. His death in February 1929 became a point of political crisis because police suspected connections between the attackers and the VNQDD, and because the case exposed grievances about recruitment and exploitation. Scholarly studies of the murder emphasize the need to distinguish the affiliations of individuals, the interests of the authorities investigating them, and evidence that a central party committee had authorized a particular act. Once police arrests began, the government could use the case to justify broader intervention against suspected revolutionary networks.

The final preparations for the uprising occurred under mounting pressure. Leaders debated the danger of acting before sufficient arms and supporters were available against the danger of postponement while associates were arrested. Military recruitment presented an opportunity because Vietnamese soldiers had weapons and inside knowledge of garrisons; it also created an acute dependency on the willingness of particular troops to mutiny at a specified moment. Local organizers could not necessarily guarantee that separate units would rise together. The events of February 1930 therefore unfolded across distinct places, not as a single coordinated battle. Rebels attacked or attempted action in areas including Yên Bái, parts of Phú Thọ, and districts farther east; the subsequent bombing of Cổ Am belongs to this wider geography of repression.

The trial and execution record offers unusually concrete evidence of how the colonial state responded. The Vietnamese National Archives summarize records from the French overseas archives, including a report dated 18 June 1930 by the chief of political police in Tonkin describing the execution of thirteen prisoners in the early morning of 17 June near the indigenous guards' barracks at Yên Bái. The report can establish administrative procedure, custody, and the official recording of death; it cannot by itself tell historians how all accused persons understood the insurrection or whether every allegation made in court was true. The survival of different names and spellings in the files further demonstrates the need for careful transcription.

The party's defeat was decisive for its ability to conduct open revolutionary activity in Tonkin, but the meaning of the outcome remained contested. Colonial authorities portrayed suppression as the restoration of order; nationalist commemorations treated the condemned as evidence of an unjust political system. Survivors, sympathizers, and exiles carried organizational memories forward even when the original leadership structure collapsed. The history of the uprising must hold these perspectives apart: the particular strategic mistakes that made military failure likely, the often severe consequences for civilians and prisoners, and the subsequent symbolic life of an event whose participants did not anticipate the later political uses of their deaths.

Section 127.17

Women, Gender Reform, and the Vietnamese National Community

Debates about the nation were also debates about the family. Reformers from Phan Châu Trinh onward had criticized customs that, in their view, kept Vietnamese society weak, and the status of women entered discussion as part of that critique. In the colonial period, a small but growing number of women gained access to schooling, and educated women began to write and speak publicly. Women's own voices appeared in print early: Nữ Giới Chung (Women's Bell), published in Saigon in 1918 under the editorship of the poet Sương Nguyệt Anh, is usually described as among the first Vietnamese women's newspapers. In Hanoi, the writer and activist Đạm Phương promoted women's education and social work.

The most important forum came in 1929 with the Saigon weekly Phụ Nữ Tân Văn (New Women's News), founded by Nguyễn Đức Nhuận and his wife and drawing contributors from among prominent writers. It addressed women's education, marriage, economic independence, motherhood, and the changing family. It also carried advertisements, fashion, and literature, since it needed to attract a paying readership. Its debates ranged from the criticism of arranged marriage and polygamy to arguments about women's work outside the home, and they provoked responses from conservatives and from women themselves. Hue-Tam Ho Tai and David Marr discuss how such debates were part of a wider reexamination of Confucian family ethics in the 1920s and 1930s.

Women also took part in political organizations. Nguyễn Thị Giang, a VNQDD member, was one of the most visible, and she took her own life after the 1930 defeat, a death later commemorated by nationalists. Other women helped as couriers, teachers, relatives of prisoners, and organizers of mourning and relief. Historians should avoid describing women solely as helpers of male activists. Women pursued their own agendas, including access to education and control over marriage, and their involvement was shaped by class, region, and family status: urban educated women in Saigon had options unavailable to rural women in central Vietnam.

The relationship between women's emancipation and national liberation was disputed. Some reformers argued that national strength required educated, independent women, while others saw feminist demands as a distraction from the struggle against colonial rule, or as a Western import that threatened family cohesion. Colonial authorities contributed to the debate by presenting modern education as a French gift. These tensions left the question unresolved in 1930 and shaped later political movements.

Reformist discussions about women's education were also disagreements over who could define the nation and its future. Advocates argued that an educated mother and professional woman could contribute to economic prosperity, civic competence, and the upbringing of a new generation. Yet this apparently supportive argument could preserve a restricted conception of women's roles, making education valuable primarily because it served family or national objectives. Other writers challenged the legal and cultural arrangements through which women were subordinated in marriage, employment, and inheritance. These debates appeared in different kinds of publications, and participants did not always agree about whether reform should be led by state officials, male scholars, women themselves, or independent associations.

The history of newspapers provides concrete institutional evidence. Nữ Giới Chung, first issued in 1918, offered an early newspaper devoted to women's concerns, while Phụ Nữ Tân Văn began publication in Saigon on 2 May 1929. The latter's cover represented women from the three regions of Vietnam reading a shared publication, thereby connecting gender reform to the imagined unity of a divided country. Its contributors discussed education, marriage, labor, travel, literary culture, and public affairs. Historical issues are essential here because the titles of columns and the voices of individual contributors help distinguish the actual priorities of the newspaper from later generalizations about Vietnamese feminism. Women should be recognized as editors, entrepreneurs, donors, writers, readers, and organizers rather than merely the intended recipients of elite instruction.

Political organizations presented more immediate risks. Women could transport correspondence, hide printed material, assist prisoners' families, or take part in revolutionary circles, but their work was less consistently recorded than that of prominent male leaders. Nguyễn Thị Giang's activities in the VNQDD provide one case; they should not stand for all women or be reduced to the circumstances of her death. Women's different experiences of schooling, marriage arrangements, domestic authority, and wage labor also meant that national independence was not always the sole or first demand they advanced. Historians should ask when the language of emancipation widened participation and when nationalist leadership expected women to defer their own priorities until political liberation was achieved.

Gender reform also differed by jurisdiction and social position. Urban Saigon journalists worked amid the comparatively active commercial press of Cochinchina; provincial teachers and families in central or northern Vietnam encountered other institutional constraints. A literacy campaign supported by a wealthy household might open a school to girls without challenging domestic authority, whereas a woman writing publicly against coerced marriage could face personal or professional sanctions. Treating these varied histories together shows why the nation was not merely an object for which men struggled: arguments about the definition of a modern Vietnamese community were taking place inside families, classrooms, workplaces, religious institutions, and newspapers.

Section 127.18

Religious, Regional, and Constitutional Visions of Political Community

Vietnamese national identity was understood in different ways by different communities. Vietnamese Catholics, a significant minority concentrated in parts of the Red River delta, Nghệ An, and the south, had a complicated relationship with nationalism. The 1885 Văn Thân massacres had left deep scars, the church hierarchy generally worked within the colonial order, and many Catholics distrusted movements associated with violence against their communities. At the same time, Catholic intellectuals and ordinary lay people were Vietnamese by language and family, and in later decades they participated in many political currents. Treating Catholics as outside the nation would repeat a colonial and, at times, a nationalist error.

Buddhist reform in the late 1920s and 1930s sought to revive and modernize Buddhism, with associations, journals, and schools formed in several regions, and monks and lay Buddhists debated their place in national life (Chapter 125 treats the movement's institutional history). The Cao Đài religion, formally established in Tây Ninh in 1926, built a large following in Cochinchina by combining elements of several traditions. Its relationship to politics was ambivalent. It created a major community organization that colonial authorities watched carefully, and its leaders often sought accommodation while many followers held nationalist sympathies. These cases show that religious loyalty and national identity could overlap, compete, or coexist.

Regional identities also mattered. Saigon constitutionalists thought in terms of Cochinchina's colonial status and southern interests; Huế's court officials considered Annam's role as the seat of the dynasty; Hanoi's reformers and journalists debated the northern delta's cultural leadership. Phạm Quỳnh, editor of Nam Phong, argued for cultural nationalism and a constitutional monarchy within the protectorate framework, a position criticized as accommodationist by radicals but defended as realistic by its advocates. The monarchy itself, weakened by Khải Định's reputation for subservience and Bảo Đại's absence in France, remained a symbol with uncertain political value.

These overlapping visions meant that, by the late 1920s, Vietnamese political community could be imagined as a restored monarchy, a republic, an autonomous territory within a French constitutional order, a religious community, or a federation of regions. None had won general assent. The question was not only how to end colonial rule but also who belonged to the nation and what its institutions should be.

The idea of a unified Vietnamese political people confronted existing commitments that could not simply be erased. Catholic communities carried memories of persecution by nineteenth-century resistance movements as well as experiences of French protection that varied sharply by locality. A Vietnamese Catholic could object to colonial discrimination while fearing programs that treated the church as foreign. Buddhist communities included monks and lay associations with their own educational and charitable priorities. New religious institutions in Cochinchina, including Cao Đài after 1926, drew supporters into organizational structures that did not necessarily coincide with constitutionalist or republican party networks. To label any one of these communities uniformly nationalist or loyalist would conceal important divisions among their members.

Regional politics complicated the question further. Saigon constitutionalists could point to limited representative institutions and the commercial power of a southern educated elite. Reformers in Huế negotiated the continuing symbolism of the Nguyễn court, while northern revolutionaries considered clandestine parties and the recruitment of soldiers. The political significance of a monarchy could differ even among its critics: some saw it as evidence of collective historical continuity, others as an institution that denied the political agency of ordinary people. Phạm Quỳnh's advocacy of cultural preservation and monarchical constitutionalism therefore should not be flattened into simple personal loyalty to France. His position was a program that others disputed, not a transparent measure of all Vietnamese opinions.

Religion and constitutional reform also framed the problem of minorities and political membership. Vietnamese nationalists could invoke a shared history, language, and territory, yet the Indochinese Union included Cambodian and Lao populations whose political futures could not be assumed to belong within a Vietnamese state. The presence of Chinese merchants, French residents, and other migrant communities raised questions about citizenship, legal equality, and representation that few early organizations resolved fully. A movement might demand national rights while reproducing existing exclusions concerning ethnicity, gender, or class. The historian should recover these limitations without assuming that any group possessed a fully specified constitutional blueprint for independence.

These competing visions produced genuine alternatives. Religious reformers could prioritize freedom to educate and manage their institutions; constitutionalists might seek protections within French structures; republicans demanded a different locus of sovereignty; royalists could retain faith in dynastic legitimacy. The same person might move between positions as events changed. Later political victories and defeats altered which voices received public commemoration, making retrospective accounts selective. A balanced interpretation considers what each project proposed at the time, what resources it controlled, which communities it could reach, and what it left unresolved.

Section 127.19

Colonial Surveillance, Political Trials, Prisons, and Exile

The colonial state built an extensive apparatus to monitor and suppress political activity. The Sûreté Générale Indochinoise, reorganized in 1917 under Louis Marty, collected intelligence through informants, mail interception, border and port controls, and the infiltration of organizations. Censorship laws controlled printing and importation, and authorities could suspend newspapers, fine editors, and ban books. Law distinguished the three jurisdictions: in Cochinchina ordinary French courts operated, though political cases could be treated harshly, while in Annam and Tonkin the protectorate courts and special tribunals allowed administrative detention and punishments without full trial. After 1929 special criminal commissions tried political defendants with restricted procedures.

Imprisonment was a principal tool. Côn Đảo, an island prison off the southern coast, held ordinary and political prisoners in harsh conditions, and Hanoi's central prison, the Maison Centrale (later called Hỏa Lò), held many political detainees. Others were confined in northern and central prisons and sent to remote areas. Phan Châu Trinh, Huỳnh Thúc Kháng, and many 1908 participants went to Côn Đảo, and Nguyễn An Ninh died there. Exile, internal and external, also served to isolate leaders: Hàm Nghi to Algeria, Duy Tân to Réunion, and Phan Bội Châu to house arrest in Huế.

Peter Zinoman's study of colonial prisons shows that prisons were also arenas of political exchange. Political prisoners who might otherwise have been scattered across the country were brought together, and they discussed ideas, taught one another, circulated texts, and built networks that survived release. This did not make prison pleasant: mortality, disease, forced labor, and abuse were serious features of the system, and prisons also broke some prisoners and divided others. But the colonial attempt to isolate and punish dissent produced unintended consequences, as imprisonment became a shared experience, a source of political legitimacy, and a school for the next generation of organizers, including communists, whose organizational history belongs in Chapter 128.

Historians must therefore read surveillance records with care. Police reports offer valuable detail on organizations, meetings, and personnel, but they were written to serve colonial control and often exaggerated conspiracy, relied on informants with their own motives, and used racialized categories. They should be compared with nationalist writings, memoirs, newspapers, trial records, and other sources. No single archive tells the whole story.

Political policing was an institution rather than a collection of isolated arrests. The Sûreté and related administrative offices gathered dossiers on students, newspapers, association leaders, travelers, and suspected revolutionaries. Reports were assembled from informants, intercepted communications, surveillance at public meetings, and statements made under conditions that could include intimidation. Names, aliases, kinship relationships, and organizational affiliations were recorded because such details helped officials anticipate meetings or build prosecutions. These files are invaluable for historians precisely because many clandestine groups left sparse archives of their own. Their detail does not make them neutral, however: an official attempting to demonstrate the discovery of a conspiracy had incentives to emphasize networks, motives, and imminent threats.

Legal differences among Cochinchina, Annam, and Tonkin influenced the routes through which repression operated. A defendant prosecuted for a press offense might encounter a different court from a person accused of conspiring to subvert the protectorate's administration. Military jurisdiction, special commissions, and administrative orders could restrict procedural safeguards available in ordinary civil cases. Students should distinguish a sentence handed down after a trial from detention imposed by executive authority and from exile enforced through diplomatic arrangements. The case of Phan Bội Châu in 1925, for example, combined international intelligence and a public trial with a subsequent decision to restrict him in Huế. These stages were politically connected but legally distinct.

The physical and social experience of confinement also varied. Prisoners could suffer overcrowding, poor health, forced labor, and separation from family, while political detainees sometimes found occasions to teach, debate, and organize with one another. Côn Đảo acquired particular significance because participants in different waves of protest were detained there, transmitting stories, skills, and political commitments across generations. Peter Zinoman's interpretation of prisons as sites of political exchange should not be sentimentalized: prisoners frequently experienced severe coercion and not all emerged as stronger activists. Nonetheless, the creation of concentrated prison populations could frustrate the state's intention to isolate political opponents completely.

Public trials became another contested arena of political authority. Colonial prosecutors presented imprisonment and execution as the lawful suppression of criminal conspiracy. Defense lawyers, supporters, and journalists could turn proceedings into arguments about the legitimacy of French sovereignty. Newspapers that reported prison sentences or printed names of the executed helped connect distant readers to events they had not witnessed. The official record is indispensable for establishing dates and legal acts, while letters, memoirs, and organizational publications reveal perspectives usually absent from the judgment. A sound account compares these sources rather than allowing either police accusation or patriotic commemoration to determine historical fact in advance.

Sûreté was the French political police and intelligence service in Indochina. Political exile means banishment or confinement of political figures, abroad or within the colony. Section 127.20

Vietnamese Nationalism at the Turning Point of 1930

By 1930 Vietnamese anticolonial politics had changed dramatically from 1885. Armed dynastic resistance had been replaced by reformist education, exile organization, journalism, mass commemorations, and secret parties. Restorationists had given way, in large part, to republicans, though monarchist sentiment survived. Reformers like Phan Châu Trinh had shown that education and civic rights were political projects. Constitutionalists had built a legal political presence in Saigon. The VNQDD had demonstrated that a modern party could attract soldiers and teachers, but its defeat showed the cost of rushed revolution under surveillance.

French repression had been consistently severe: executions in 1908, 1916, and 1930; imprisonment on Côn Đảo; closures of schools and newspapers; and trials of elders. It frequently shaped outcomes by destroying leaders, but it also generated martyrs and networks. Unresolved divisions persisted over monarchy and republic, violence and legality, cooperation and rupture, northern and southern priorities, elite and mass leadership, and religious pluralism. The question of social revolution, including land and labor, had barely been addressed by most noncommunist groups and was becoming central.

At the same time new currents were emerging. Communist organizations, which had formed in 1929–1930 and which Chapter 128 examines, offered a program that combined national independence with social revolution and an international network. Their rise did not mean the earlier currents had vanished: the VNQDD survived in exile and underground, constitutionalists remained active in Saigon, religious organizations grew, and the monarchy persisted. In 1930 the outcome of the struggle against French colonial rule remained uncertain. The Great Depression (Chapter 133) would soon intensify economic distress and political radicalization, and the wartime upheavals of Chapters 134–139 would reorder the field again.

Vietnamese nationalism before 1930 was intellectually diverse, organizationally innovative, geographically uneven, and politically contested. Its historical importance lies in the independent programs it developed and the transformations it produced, and not merely in its relation to the communist movements that followed.

The failure of individual campaigns should be compared by the particular goals their participants held. The Duy Tân Hội sought a restorationist rallying point and external assistance; Đông Kinh Nghĩa Thục prioritized civic knowledge and national strengthening; constitutionalists in Saigon sought legal participation and protections; the VNQDD attempted clandestine organization and armed republican revolt. These were not interchangeable instruments for an identical policy. They disagreed over who belonged to the political nation, what kind of state would be legitimate, and whether ordinary people should first become educated citizens or immediately be mobilized for insurrection. The differences shaped recruitment, organizational durability, and how the French authorities could respond.

The contrast between elite and popular participation also resists any simple account of development. Scholar-gentry networks could circulate manifestos, fund overseas journeys, and inspire schools, but their reach was restricted by literacy and social status. Rural tax demonstrators acted on immediate burdens that did not always fit reformist programs. Students and urban workers gained organizational experience through schools, newspapers, and strikes, although they rarely controlled the central decisions of prestigious organizations. Women wrote publicly about reform, contributed to associations, and participated in revolutionary circles while confronting constraints that male nationalist memoirs often minimized. National politics therefore developed through unequal relationships among leaders, local communities, and institutions.

Colonial authorities helped shape this field by the selective use of law, censorship, funding, and force. Allowing some schools while closing others, maintaining a restricted constitutional sphere in Saigon, prosecuting journalists, and using severe penalties against conspirators produced different incentives for political activity. A movement that appeared to fail because its program lacked support might instead have lost leaders through imprisonment or been denied legal means of organization. Conversely, repression alone cannot explain unsuccessful revolutionary strategy. Insufficient coordination, scarce funds, contested leadership, and reliance on uncertain foreign allies also limited particular movements. Historical explanation requires both institutional pressure and Vietnamese political choices.

The events of 1930 did not settle the future organization of national life. Republican activists remembered Yên Bái, constitutionalists continued to press for rights, religious associations grew, and communist networks were developing programs that combined independence and social transformation. Questions of landholding, rural debt, industrial employment, and social inequality remained only partially addressed by many earlier nationalist projects. The next chapter examines communist organization without treating its subsequent successes as predetermined. The significance of this chapter is the record of genuine political experimentation: Vietnamese people formed distinct institutions and argued publicly about sovereignty before any one movement could claim to embody the nation's future.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has argued that Vietnamese nationalism before the communist ascendancy was a plural, evolving field of political thought and organization, and not a prelude waiting for a single outcome. Between 1885 and 1930, Vietnamese activists confronted a colonial order that divided their country into three jurisdictions, preserved a subordinate monarchy, and taxed and policed a rural and urban population.

The defeat of the Cần Vương movement, ending with Phan Đình Phùng's death in 1895 and later the end of Yên Thế, closed the era of dynastic armed resistance. Scholars trained in the old system turned to new texts from China and Japan and to new institutions. Phan Bội Châu founded the Duy Tân Hội in 1904, organized the Đông Du, and, after the Chinese Revolution, helped found the republican Quang Phục Hội in 1912.

Phan Châu Trinh offered a different path. He criticized monarchy and autocracy, prioritized popular education and civic rights, and distrusted foreign military help. The two Phans disagreed on sequence, method, and political form, but both were anticolonial. Their contrast frames the whole period and should not be reduced to patriotism versus collaboration.

The Đông Kinh Nghĩa Thục and central Vietnamese Duy Tân movement made education into political action. The 1908 antitax protests mixed the aims of reformers with the grievances of villagers, and severe repression followed, destroying much of the reformist leadership while strengthening arguments for revolution.

During and after the First World War, plots such as the 1916 Duy Tân revolt and the 1917 Thái Nguyên mutiny failed, and the Versailles petition of 1919 introduced self-determination as a political language. In Saigon, the Constitutionalist Party and radical journalists like Nguyễn An Ninh built a public sphere shaped by Cochinchina's colonial status.

Phan Bội Châu's 1925 arrest and Phan Châu Trinh's 1926 death produced mass mobilization led by symbols, students, and associations. The VNQDD, formed in 1927, created a structured republican party with a recruitment base among teachers and soldiers. Its rushed uprising at Yên Bái in February 1930 was defeated through French intelligence, poor coordination, and repression, and its executed leaders became martyrs.

Women, religious communities, and regional groups complicated any single definition of the nation. Women's journalism and participation raised questions about the family and the nation, and Catholic, Buddhist, Cao Đài, southern, and monarchist actors had different stakes. Surveillance, trials, and prisons suppressed dissent but also connected political prisoners and spread ideas.

In 1930 the outcome remained open. Communist organizations were emerging (Chapter 128), but constitutionalists, republicans, religious movements, and the monarchy persisted. The period's importance lies in the programs, institutions, and political practices it created, which shaped everything that followed.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it restores the choices of historical actors. Reading Vietnamese history from the August Revolution backward makes earlier movements look like stages. Reading forward shows men and women trying different answers to the same problem under conditions they did not control.

Its larger processes recur across the region: scholar elites turning from armed resistance to education and print, exile networks, press publics, student mobilization, and the interplay of colonial repression and political creativity. Comparison with Chapter 126 on Indonesia and Chapter 129 on Burma shows both similarities and the distinct effects of Vietnam's three-part colonial division.

Continuities extended beyond 1930. Republican ideas, the commemoration of martyrs, the use of print and education, debates over women and the family, and the memory of prison all shaped later politics. Chapter 128 turns to the communist movement that would draw on, compete with, and eventually displace many of these currents, and Chapters 139 and 150 follow the wartime and revolutionary transformations that followed.
