Chapter Opening

Long before any European ship entered Philippine waters, people on these islands had made treaties, settled debts before officials, built plank boats lashed with fibre, traded gold for porcelain, and argued over who should lead. The chapter that follows studies those worlds on their own terms.

In the late 1980s, a thin copper plate surfaced after dredging in the Lumbang River area near Laguna de Bay on Luzon. Its provenance was subsequently investigated by the scholar Antoon Postma, but it had not come from a controlled archaeological excavation. Its surface bore lines of script unfamiliar to its first examiners. After decipherment, Postma concluded that the text was a legal document dated, by the Indian-derived Saka calendar, to the equivalent of 21 April 900 CE. It records a release of Namwaran and his descendants from a debt, under the authority of officials and in the presence of named dignitaries from several places. The object is now known as the Laguna Copperplate Inscription. It is one of the very few surviving written documents from the Philippine archipelago before the sixteenth century, and it is the central documentary artifact of this chapter.

The plate matters not only for its contents but for the chronology it implies. Textbooks of an older kind began Philippine history in 1521 with Ferdinand Magellan, or in 1565 with Miguel López de Legazpi. Such a starting point makes Spain the author of the archipelago's past. The copper plate belongs to a long earlier history. Seven centuries before Legazpi, people in the Manila Bay and Laguna de Bay region were using a script derived from Indian models, a language containing Old Malay, Sanskrit, and local elements, and legal and commercial categories shared with other parts of maritime Southeast Asia. Archaeological evidence from other regions, notably the excavated Butuan boats and associated archaeological remains in Mindanao, the porcelain-filled graves of Luzon and the Visayas, and the gold ornaments recovered from many sites, tells a similar story of settled communities participating in wide networks of exchange.

This chapter reconstructs the political, economic, social, religious, and cultural worlds of the Philippine archipelago before sustained Spanish colonization began in 1565. Its governing question is how the diverse societies of the archipelago organized political authority, commerce, labor, social hierarchy, religion, and everyday life, and how they were connected to the wider maritime worlds of Southeast and East Asia. The word "Philippines" is used here as a geographic designation. It was coined by Spaniards in the 1540s, and no unified Philippine state or national community existed before Spanish colonization. The chapter therefore studies many societies: chiefly trading communities on the Pasig River and in the Visayas, the river-port polity of Butuan, the Muslim polities of Sulu and Maguindanao, and the farming and foraging communities of highlands and interior valleys.

The evidence is uneven, and the chapter says so repeatedly. It draws on archaeology, a handful of inscriptions, Chinese records of trade and diplomacy, oral traditions and genealogies recorded later, and the accounts of Europeans. Some of the richest descriptions of preconquest society were written after Spanish settlement began, by missionaries and officials with their own purposes, and they cannot be treated as unchanged snapshots of earlier centuries. Equally, the absence of a written record does not show that a community lacked institutions, beliefs, history, or regional connections. Much of the writing that existed was done on bamboo, leaves, and bark, which rarely survive in a tropical climate.

Two opposite distortions are avoided. One imagines the preconquest archipelago as a scatter of small, politically undeveloped villages awaiting organization. The other treats every major port as the capital of a centralized kingdom comparable to later territorial states. The evidence supports neither. Political authority commonly rested on kinship, followers, marriage alliances, control of prestige goods and boats, and success in trade and war, rather than on bounded territory and bureaucracy. Variation among regions was large, and this chapter tries to keep that variation visible.

The chapter follows from earlier treatments of the Austronesian expansion (Chapter 14), of Indian and Chinese maritime trade and localization (Chapters 18 and 19), of the Javanese and Sumatran polities (Chapters 28 to 32), and of Islam and the northern maritime networks (Chapters 62 to 66). It stands beside Chapter 71 on the Spice Islands as a study of an indigenous maritime world that Iberian powers entered rather than created. It prepares for Chapter 73 on the Spanish conquest, Chapter 74 on Manila and the galleon trade, Chapter 75 on Christianity and colonial society, and Chapter 76 on the Moro-Spanish frontiers. It does not narrate those later developments, but it establishes the indigenous foundations on which all of them depended.

  6. Contextualize Tondo, Maynila, Cebu, Butuan, Sulu, and Maguindanao as distinct political and commercial centers.

  8. Evaluate the social categories of rank, dependency, and slavery, including regional differences and degrees of coercion.
  9. Assess what Juan de Plasencia's 1589 account can and cannot reveal about earlier Tagalog society, marriage, property, and law.

  11. Synthesize the political, economic, social, and cultural conditions the Spanish encountered after 1565.

Historical Context

The earliest human presence in the archipelago is far older than any state. Chapters 6 and 7 examined the evidence for archaic hominins, including Homo luzonensis, on Luzon, and Chapter 8 the arrival of modern humans. Chapter 14 traced the Austronesian expansion from Taiwan through the northern Philippines, beginning roughly four thousand years ago in the conventional reconstruction, bringing farming, pottery, domesticated animals, and seafaring knowledge. These populations met and mixed with earlier hunter-gatherers whose descendants include groups today often called Negrito peoples. During the first millennium CE, different communities across the archipelago practiced agriculture, fishing, maritime exchange, and metalworking, leaving varied burial and settlement evidence.

The region around the Philippines was meanwhile changing. Chapters 18 and 19 explained how Indian Ocean and South China Sea exchange brought Sanskrit vocabulary, Indic religious ideas, and Indian-derived scripts to the courts of Sumatra, Java, and the mainland, where they were selectively adopted and transformed. The Chinese Song, Yuan, and Ming courts took varying interest in the southern seas, and Chinese merchants from the Fujian coast traded with islands to their south. Chapters 62 to 66 described the spread of Islam through Southeast Asian ports from the thirteenth century onward, including the sultanate of Brunei. The Philippine societies lay on the northeastern edge of this commercial world. Their relationship to it was neither isolation nor dependence: they supplied gold, forest and sea products, and slaves, and they used imported goods in their own politics.

Volume V begins at 1500, but this chapter reaches further back, because understanding the encounter between Spain and the archipelago requires understanding what was already there. Its principal period is c. 900 to 1565, with particular emphasis on c. 1300 to 1565, the centuries for which archaeological, Chinese, and early European evidence is most abundant. Chapter 71 has shown how Iberian powers entered another indigenous commercial world, in Maluku, and how local rulers used those newcomers for their own purposes. A similar pattern, though with important differences, occurred in the Philippines.

Historical Vocabulary
Barangay

Definition. In the sixteenth century, a barangay was a small community of households, commonly reported by Spanish observers as tens to a few hundred families, organized around a leading family and bound by kinship, obligation, and loyalty to a chief. The word is widely connected to balangay, the plank boat, and many scholars see in this a memory of migration and maritime life, though the etymology is explained as a likely connection rather than a certainty.

Historical context. The preconquest barangay was a social and political group, not a bounded territorial district. Larger political relationships were built by alliances among barangay leaders. The Spanish later adopted the word for tribute and administrative units under colonial government (Chapters 73 to 75).

Potential misunderstanding. The modern Philippine barangay is an official administrative subdivision of cities and municipalities, established in its current legal form in the twentieth century. It should not be read back into the sixteenth century, and the preconquest barangay did not have uniform size, structure, or autonomy across the archipelago.

Historical Vocabulary
Datu

Definition. Datu (also written dato or datto) was a widely used title for a chief in many Philippine languages, especially in the Visayas and Mindanao, designating a leading person who headed a following of kin and dependents. Other titles existed in other regions, including lakan in Tondo, rajah in some coastal polities with Malay and Indic connections, and sultan in Muslim polities.

Historical context. A datu's standing depended on ancestry, the loyalty of followers, skill in war, success in trade, generosity at feasts, and the ability to arrange advantageous marriages. Some datu were subordinate to others; some commanded several barangay.

Potential misunderstanding. European writers often translated datu as "king" or "lord." Such translations can mislead, because a datu usually did not rule a clearly bounded territory, collect regular taxes through a bureaucracy, or hold absolute power. The title is better understood as describing leadership within networks of personal obligation.

Section 72.1

Reconstructing the Philippines before Spanish Conquest

Historians of the preconquest Philippines work with five broad kinds of evidence, none of which is complete. The first is archaeology. Excavations at Tabon Cave in Palawan, the Butuan boat and burial sites in Mindanao, the cemeteries of Calatagan in Batangas, and sites in the Pasig, Laguna, and Visayan regions have recovered pottery, imported ceramics, ironwork, gold, beads, boats, and human remains. Archaeology offers the only direct evidence for most of the archipelago's past and the only evidence for people who left no writing, but it shows material remains rather than names, laws, or beliefs, and many sites have been disturbed by looting and development.

The second kind is the small body of local inscriptions. The Laguna Copperplate Inscription of 900 CE (72.3) is the best known. Writing was evidently done more widely on perishable materials such as bamboo and leaves, and these have not survived. The third kind is Chinese documentary evidence. Song, Yuan, and Ming records, including Zhao Rukuo's Zhu Fan Zhi of about 1225, Wang Dayuan's Daoyi Zhilüe of about 1349, and the Ming court records, describe trade with islands to China's south and record diplomatic missions. They were written by officials and compilers who rarely visited the places they described, used names that are difficult to match with modern locations, and cared mainly about trade, tribute, and piracy.

The fourth kind is indigenous oral and genealogical tradition. Epics such as the Maranao Darangen and the Ifugao hudhud chants, and the Muslim genealogies known as tarsila (or salsilah), preserve memory of the past, but they were transmitted and often recorded long after the events they describe, and they serve the identities of the communities that kept them. The fifth kind is European writing. Antonio Pigafetta's account of Magellan's voyage in 1521 is the earliest detailed eyewitness description of Visayan communities. After 1565, Spanish officers, missionaries, and judges produced far fuller accounts, among them Miguel de Loarca's relation of the 1580s, Juan de Plasencia's of 1589, and later works by Pedro Chirino and Antonio de Morga. These are indispensable, but they were composed by people engaged in conquest and conversion, who interpreted what they saw through European categories, relied on selected informants, and described societies already changing under Spanish pressure.

The name itself is part of the story. "Las Islas Filipinas" was applied by Ruy López de Villalobos in 1543 in honor of the Spanish prince Philip, and the name spread only gradually from the central islands to the whole archipelago. Before that, communities used their own names for places and peoples, and Chinese, Malay, and Arab writers used others. This chapter uses "Philippine archipelago" as a geographic term for the islands that are now the Philippines, and it does not suggest that their inhabitants shared a common political identity.

A methodological rule runs through the chapter: where a description first appears in a post-1565 source, it is treated as evidence for the late sixteenth century and as a possible, not certain, guide to earlier periods. Where archaeology and early Chinese records support a practice, the case for its antiquity is stronger. Where sources are silent, the proper conclusion is uncertainty rather than absence.

Section 72.2

Geography, Languages, Settlement, and Regional Diversity

The archipelago consists of more than seven thousand islands, most of them small, spread across about fifteen degrees of latitude between the Bashi Channel near Taiwan in the north and the Sulu and Celebes Seas in the south. Three great island groups are conventionally distinguished: Luzon in the north, the Visayan islands in the center (Panay, Negros, Cebu, Bohol, Leyte, and Samar among them), and Mindanao in the south, together with the long island of Palawan to the west and the Sulu archipelago, a chain of islands extending toward Borneo. To the far north lie the Batanes islands, closer to Taiwan than to Manila.

Coastlines are long and complex, and most of the population historically lived on coasts, river mouths, and river valleys. Major rivers include the Cagayan in northern Luzon, the Pampanga and Agno in the central Luzon plain, the Pasig linking Laguna de Bay to Manila Bay, the Agusan in northeastern Mindanao, and the Pulangi, or Mindanao River, in the south. Laguna de Bay in Luzon and Lake Lanao in Mindanao were important inland waterways. Volcanic soils supported intensive farming in some regions, while mountain ranges, most notably the Cordillera of northern Luzon and the Sierra Madre, formed interior zones with distinctive agricultural and social patterns. The seasonal monsoons, the northeast amihan and the southwest habagat, shaped sailing routes, trading seasons, and the timing of raiding. Typhoons from the Pacific were a recurrent hazard. These conditions favored movement by water, but geography did not determine politics: neighboring river valleys could develop quite different political arrangements.

Language reflects the archipelago's diversity. Most Philippine languages belong to the Austronesian family, and linguists identify well over a hundred distinct languages, including Tagalog, Kapampangan, Pangasinan, Ilokano, Bikol, the several Visayan languages (Cebuano, Hiligaynon, and Waray among them), Tausug, Sama, Maguindanaon, and Maranao. Speakers of some languages also include groups of foraging or mixed foraging-farming peoples, often called Negrito in the scholarly literature, whose ancestry includes populations present before the Austronesian arrival. These categories are modern linguistic and scholarly classifications. People in the sixteenth century more often identified themselves by river, bay, kin group, or leader than by language.

The archipelago's connections reached in all directions. Its Austronesian-speaking communities were related by language and ancestry to communities in Taiwan, Borneo, Sulawesi, and beyond. Batanes had long-standing contacts with Taiwan. Sulu and Palawan lay along routes to Borneo and Brunei. Mindanao and the Visayas connected to Sulawesi and Maluku, the source of spices (Chapter 71). Luzon and Mindoro were linked to the Fujian coast of China. Sama-Bajau and other sea peoples maintained mobile communities of boat dwellers across the southern seas. These were settled societies with extensive mobility, not remote or isolated places.

Section 72.3

The Laguna Copperplate Inscription and the Political World of 900 CE

The Laguna Copperplate Inscription (LCI) surfaced after late-1980s dredging near the Lumbang River in Laguna and was brought to the National Museum of the Philippines; its precise original depositional context remains unknown. Postma deciphered it in 1990 and published an edition and commentary in 1992. The plate is a small sheet of copper inscribed on one side. The date given in its first line, in the Saka era used widely in Java, corresponds to Monday, 21 April 900 CE.

The script is Kawi, the Old Javanese script derived ultimately from Indian Brahmi-based scripts. It must be distinguished from baybayin, the family of syllabic scripts recorded in Spanish-period sources from the sixteenth century, which are related to Indic models but are distinct, later, and used for languages of the archipelago. The language of the inscription is mainly Old Malay, with Sanskrit terms, some Old Javanese, and a few words that Postma associated with Old Tagalog. The mixture reflects a world of overlapping communicative traditions, in which a legal text could be composed in a language and script used in courts across maritime Southeast Asia.

The content is a certificate of release from debt. In Postma's reading, Lady Angkatan and her relative Bukah, connected to Namwaran, received a formal declaration clearing Namwaran and his descendants of a debt denominated in gold. The document names a chief and commander of Tundun acting on behalf of Jayadewa, identified in the translation with Pailah, and names other dignitaries associated with Puliran, Pailah, and Binwangan. The exact administrative relationships are difficult to reconstruct from a single inscription. Place names include Tundun, which Postma identified with Tondo, along with Pailah, Binwangan, and Puliran, for which he proposed identifications with places in Luzon. Another name, Medang, has been linked by some readers to the Javanese kingdom of that name (Chapters 30 to 32). Some identifications are more persuasive than others, and Postma himself regarded some as proposals.

The inscription's value lies in the relationships it presupposes. A debt, an amount of gold, officials with titles, formal witnesses, a written certificate on durable metal, and an agreed calendar all indicate a society in which law and obligation were formalized, and in which participants understood Indic and Malay political vocabulary. The use of Kawi suggests connections with Java and the wider Malay world, though it does not by itself demonstrate migration, conquest, or political dependence. The plate also shows that debt was a legal and social fact in the tenth century, a matter taken up in 72.14.

Its limits are equally important. It is a single document. It does not show that Tondo, Pailah, and the other places formed a kingdom, or that the officials were subordinate to one ruler. It does not tell us the size of the communities involved, the nature of the debt's origin, or whether such documents were common. Attempts to locate every place name with confidence, or to infer a unified Luzon polity, outrun the evidence. The responsible conclusion is that the inscription documents a literate, legally active, internationally connected political world in the Laguna and Pasig region at the beginning of the tenth century.

Editorial paraphrase (not a verbatim excerpt):

The document records the formal debt release involving Lady Angkatan, her relative Bukah, and Namwaran, extending to his descendants, in a document issued by the chief and commander of Tundun, acting for Jayadewa, whom Postma identifies with the leadership of Pailah. Dignitaries associated with Puliran, Pailah, and Binwangan are named; the surviving text affirms a release for Namwaran and his descendants, while the final warning breaks off before its conclusion.

Analysis: The text shows a legal procedure with officials, witnesses, and a written certificate. It implies that gold was a unit of account and that creditors and debtors could be bound by obligations that extended to family members.

Limitations: It does not establish a centralized monarchy, the territorial extent of any polity, or the frequency of such documents. Place identifications are partly conjectural. The plate has no excavated context.

Perspective: The document represents the perspective of officials and the beneficiary family. Creditors' interests, ordinary debtors' experiences, and non-literate communities are not represented.

Section 72.4

Maritime Trade, Boats, Gold, Ceramics, and Asian Commercial Networks

Archaeological evidence for trade accumulates strongly from the tenth century. Chinese ceramics of the Tang, Song, Yuan, and Ming periods appear in burials and settlements across Luzon, Mindoro, the Visayas, and Mindanao. The cemetery at Calatagan, Batangas, contained many burials with imported ceramics in the fourteenth to sixteenth centuries, and similar finds come from the Laguna and Pasig regions and from Cebu. Ceramics were not merely useful vessels: they marked wealth and rank, were displayed at feasts, and were buried with the dead. Thailand, Vietnam, and other parts of Southeast Asia also exported ceramics found in Philippine sites.

Chinese texts describe the other side of the exchange. Zhao Rukuo's Zhu Fan Zhi (c. 1225) describes traders at Ma-i, probably a region including Mindoro and parts of Luzon, who came to meet Chinese ships and bartered local goods, including wax, cotton cloth, pearls, tortoiseshell, and betel nut, for ceramics, iron, and beads. The same work describes Pisheye, probably in the Visayas, whose people raided coastal China, a reminder that the seas also carried violence. Gold, though produced in several regions, especially in Butuan and Surigao and in the Cordillera, was an important exchange good, and goldworking reached a high level, as surviving ornaments show. Connections with Java and Borneo are shown by the language of the LCI and by Bornean ceramics and trade goods in Philippine sites.

Boats made these networks possible. At Butuan, excavations from the 1970s onward recovered several large plank boats, called balangay in modern Philippine usage. Their planks were edged with projecting lugs and joined by lashing fibre through the lugs and by wooden dowels, a technique known as lashed-lug construction found across the Austronesian world. Published radiocarbon determinations for particular Butuan boats range from the early centuries CE into the second millennium, with commonly reported examples near 320, 990, and 1250 CE; these are dates for individual samples and vessels, not a single construction date for the entire site. The finds demonstrate the scale of shipbuilding and the existence of river and sea transport in northeastern Mindanao. They do not tell us how far any particular boat sailed, and the term balangay in the Spanish documents referred to boats of varied kinds.

The political implications of this trade should be stated carefully. Imported goods did not carry Chinese or other foreign sovereignty. Chinese records describe trading and tribute missions, in which local leaders sometimes presented gifts to Chinese courts in return for recognition and valuable gifts; such tribute exchanges served the interests of both parties and are not evidence of political control. Within the archipelago, chiefs used access to imported goods to attract followers, reward allies, and display rank. Laura Lee Junker's archaeological studies of Philippine chiefdoms argue that this "prestige-goods" economy and the control of labor and followers, including through trade, feasting, and raiding, were fundamental to chiefly power.

Political Communities, Coastal Centers, and Regional Authority

How chiefly authority, kinship, and trade created diverse political arrangements.

Section 72.5

The Datu, Barangay, Kinship, and Political Authority

William Henry Scott's Barangay remains the central synthesis of sixteenth-century Philippine social and political institutions. Its basic picture, supported by Junker's archaeological work, is of societies organized into small communities led by chiefs, linked into larger networks by marriage, tribute, alliance, and warfare. A barangay in Tagalog regions, according to Spanish accounts, might comprise from thirty to a hundred households or more. The leader, called datu in the Visayas and Mindanao and by other titles elsewhere, usually came from a prominent family, but ancestry alone was not sufficient. A chief needed to prove ability by success in war, trade, and the distribution of wealth, and he held authority only so long as followers remained loyal.

Followers included relatives, free dependents who attached themselves to a chief for protection, and dependents who owed labor (72.13 and 72.14). Their obligations might include rendering service in building, fishing, and farming, paying shares of produce, joining raids, and supporting feasts. In return a chief provided protection, arbitrated disputes, performed ritual functions, and supplied access to trade goods. The relationship between chiefs and followers was personal rather than territorial, and followers could sometimes transfer loyalty. A chief of high rank might have a number of subordinate datu who owed him respect and military support, as the leader of Cebu seems to have done in 1521, but such overlordship was usually unstable and was reasserted through marriage, gifts, and campaigns.

Disputes were settled by customary law administered by chiefs and elders, with penalties often expressed in payments of gold, goods, or labor. Spanish observers described ordeals, oaths, and arbitration among kin groups. Warfare was frequent, and raiding for captives, plunder, and prestige, known in the Visayas as mangayaw, was a central political activity. Leaders gained reputation, wealth, and dependents by raiding and by successfully defending their communities. Succession to chiefly status generally passed within a lineage, but selection among kinsmen depended on ability and support, and conflicts over succession could be intense.

The sixteenth-century Spanish found Southeast Asian terms of rank in use. Some leaders, especially in Maynila and the Muslim south, carried titles such as rajah and sultan, drawn from Indic and Islamic traditions and tied to Brunei and Malay court culture, which conveyed prestige in external relations. This does not mean that these leaders governed large territorial states. Where evidence allows, Tondo and Maynila, Cebu, Butuan, Sulu, and Maguindanao are best understood as polities of differing scale, in which a paramount leader exercised influence through a network of alliances and obligations. Variation was real: Scott distinguishes Tagalog, Visayan, Mindanao, central Luzon, and northern Luzon patterns, and the more egalitarian highland communities discussed in 72.12 had no hereditary chiefs of the lowland type.

To understand how such communities operated, students should distinguish chiefly prerogative from a permanent office with uniform jurisdiction. A leader's ability to organize a canoe expedition, settle a dispute, or attract an allied settlement depended on the willingness of followers to contribute labor and accept reciprocal obligations. Gifts at feasts made power visible, but they also created expectations that wealth would circulate rather than remain entirely in chiefly households. An ambitious datu could build a larger following; the resulting coalition might diminish after defeat or succession. This was political organization, not the absence of it, and its instability was a condition of competition among leaders.

Section 72.6

Tondo, Maynila, and the Pasig–Laguna Political World

The Pasig River flows about twenty-five kilometres from Laguna de Bay to Manila Bay, linking the lake's farming communities with the sea. Settlements at its mouth, notably Tondo on the north bank and Maynila on the south, controlled access between the bay and the interior. Lowland communities around Laguna de Bay and rice-growing districts of Pampanga and Bulacan supplied food and goods to the river-mouth settlements, while ships bringing Chinese and Southeast Asian trade goods arrived through the bay. The Laguna Copperplate Inscription indicates that a place named Tundun, probably Tondo, was part of an interacting political world in 900 CE.

Evidence from the ninth and tenth centuries and evidence from the sixteenth belong to different layers and should not be fused. The LCI shows that Tondo's name was known in the Laguna region and that the area was integrated into regional legal networks, but it does not show continuous political institutions across the six hundred years that followed. For the sixteenth century, Spanish records from the early 1570s describe two distinct leadership groups: Tondo, where the ruler Lakandula was a major figure, and Maynila, where Rajah Matanda and Rajah Sulayman held authority. They were related by kinship and engaged in alliance and rivalry. Tondo and Maynila were not the same political institution simply because they were neighboring settlements, and the Spanish later dealt with each separately (Chapter 73).

Maynila's leaders had links with Brunei. Spanish accounts describe marriage and kinship ties with the Bruneian court, the presence of Muslim practices and leaders in Maynila, and the possession of bronze cannon, called lantaka, of a type produced in Brunei and the wider Malay world. The sultanate of Brunei at its sixteenth-century height (Chapter 66) was a major commercial power on the route between the South China Sea and the Sulu Sea, and ties to it brought prestige, trade access, and military technology to its Philippine partners. Chinese merchants came regularly, and Chinese goods were abundant in these markets. The Pasig region should therefore be seen as a commercial and political crossroads in which chiefs made use of external connections, rather than as a dependency of Brunei or China.

The agricultural and social base of the region is less visible in the sources, but it clearly mattered. Rice, fishing, and lake and river commerce supported dense settlement. Tondo's elite included families with large numbers of dependents. Political authority remained personal and negotiated: Spanish sources show that the chiefs of Tondo and Maynila, though powerful, needed to consult and cooperate with other datu in the surrounding region.

Section 72.7

Cebu, Panay, Bohol, and the Visayan Maritime Centers

The central islands were home to Visayan-speaking communities whose settlements lay along coasts and river mouths. Spanish writers called them Pintados, "painted ones," because many adult men were extensively tattooed, a mark of rank and martial achievement. Chinese texts of the thirteenth century already refer to Pisheye, possibly the Visayas, as a source of raiders, and the Visayan practice of seaborne raiding, mangayaw, continued into the sixteenth century. Visayan communities also traded, grew rice and root crops, fished, wove textiles of fibre and cotton, and worked gold.

Cebu was a major trading port by 1521. Pigafetta found there a ruler, Rajah Humabon, who received trading ships and had contact with visiting merchants, including a merchant from Siam (72.11). Archaeological work in Cebu and at other central Visayan sites such as Tanjay in Negros has found imported ceramics, gold, and burial goods that point to participation in long-distance trade. Junker's regional studies argue that Visayan chiefdoms were competitive polities in which leaders built power through trade, feasting, raiding, and control of followers. Cebu was important, but its importance came from its position within a network of rival centers rather than from superiority over a single Visayan "kingdom."

Panay, Bohol, Leyte, and Samar had their own leaders and communities. Evidence for them before 1565 is thinner than for Cebu. A well-known account of ten Bornean datus who settled Panay in the thirteenth century comes from the Maragtas, a text published in 1907 and based on a compilation of oral traditions and presumed older sources, which historians regard as unreliable for medieval chronology. It illustrates how later communities remember origins, but it is not contemporary evidence. For Bohol, Leyte, and Samar, the main detailed sources are Spanish accounts from the 1560s and later, which describe communities led by datu, divided by rivalry, and engaged in trade and raiding.

Visayan polities were thus locally rooted and diverse, with ties to Mindanao, Luzon, Borneo, and China. They were not united, and Spanish authors recorded constant hostilities among neighboring leaders. Cebu's historical significance before Magellan's arrival rests on these commercial ties and on its capacity to attract merchants, not on the later colonial role it assumed as Spain's first settlement (Chapter 73).

The evidence for the central islands also reveals different scales of interaction. An exchange between two coastal settlements might involve marriage and access to fishing grounds, while a longer voyage could bring porcelain, cloth, or metalwork to a chief who redistributed those goods among supporters. Local traders and boat crews connected these scales. Spanish descriptions of sixteenth-century Visayan settlements, especially as synthesized by Scott, offer useful institutional detail, but cannot simply be projected unchanged into Panay or Bohol two centuries earlier. Geography and chronology both matter when converting a regional comparison into a historical claim.

Section 72.8

Butuan and the Agusan River Commercial World

Butuan lies near the mouth of the Agusan River in northeastern Mindanao. The Agusan flows north through a wide basin between mountain ranges toward Butuan Bay and the Bohol Sea. The river provided a route from the interior to the coast, and the basin's gold-bearing deposits, forest products, and agricultural land made the river-mouth settlement a natural commercial hub. Interior peoples could bring gold and forest goods downriver, and coastal traders could send imported ceramics, beads, and iron upriver, making Butuan a connection between the Agusan basin and long-distance maritime exchange.

The archaeological evidence is extensive. The Libertad site and other localities have yielded the balangay boats described in 72.4, along with burials in wooden coffins, imported Chinese, Southeast Asian ceramics, and gold ornaments. Butuan's gold goods, including ornaments and, from nearby areas, funerary gold objects, show high craft skill. A gold image of a figure usually identified as a Hindu-Buddhist deity, known as the Agusan image or golden Tara and dated to about the thirteenth or fourteenth century, indicates contact with Indic religious iconography, though what it meant for local belief is uncertain. UNESCO's description of Butuan's archaeological heritage emphasizes the boats, burials, and ceramics as evidence of maritime trade.

Chinese documentation corroborates the picture. Song records mention missions from Butuan in the early eleventh century, beginning in 1001, and these accounts, though brief, indicate a ruler who dispatched envoys to the Chinese court, probably to secure trading advantages. Pigafetta, over five hundred years later, described rulers associated with Mazaua and Butuan as related and referred to Butuan as a place associated with gold. The encounters and Easter observance described at Mazaua should not be transferred to Butuan merely because one ruler had connections there. The narrative indicates continued awareness of Butuan without establishing its precise political reach. The sources do not allow a continuous political history, and the scale and duration of Butuan's authority are uncertain.

Butuan's importance should be assessed through its trade, shipbuilding, and craft production, not reduced to later debates over the location of the first Catholic Mass in the Philippines in 1521, which has been contested between Butuan and Limasawa. Whatever the answer to that question, Butuan's significance as an indigenous maritime and riverine polity rests on evidence that predates the Spanish by centuries.

The Agusan corridor should also be understood as a set of relationships rather than a river controlled continuously from mouth to headwaters by one ruler. Coastal merchants needed guides, forest suppliers, and inland exchange partners. Upland producers had choices about which intermediaries to approach and what goods to accept in payment. The archaeology of the boats makes the practical demands of this exchange visible: obtaining timber, organizing skilled builders, coordinating crews, and maintaining routes all required sustained cooperation. None of those activities proves a single centralized authority, but together they reveal considerable technical expertise and collective capacity.

Division III · Sections 72.9–72.12

Sulu, Maguindanao, Regional Diversity, and Early European Contact

Muslim polities, non-Muslim coastal centers, and upland societies followed different trajectories.

Section 72.9

Sulu, Maritime Commerce, and the Formation of Muslim Political Authority

The Sulu archipelago stretches southwest from Mindanao toward Borneo, forming a chain of islands that separates the Sulu Sea from the Celebes Sea. Jolo, the principal island, became a political and commercial center. The islands' inhabitants included the Tausug, whose name means "people of the current," and various Sama-speaking communities, among them the Sama-Bajau sea peoples who lived largely on boats. These groups fished, farmed, traded, and collected pearls and sea products, and their position between Borneo, Mindanao, and the Visayas made them important intermediaries.

Chinese sources give some of the best contemporary evidence. Wang Dayuan's account of about 1349 describes Sulu as a trading place and notes its pearls. In 1417, according to Ming records, three rulers of Sulu with a large entourage visited the Ming court. The senior one, called the Eastern King, died on the return journey in China, where he was buried at Dezhou in Shandong, and members of his family remained in China. These records establish that Sulu leaders were diplomatically active in the early fifteenth century, even if they do not tell us about the internal structure of their polity or whether the rulers were Muslim at that time.

For the development of Islam and of the sultanate in Sulu, the main sources are later. Muslim genealogies, the tarsila, describe a series of arrivals: first teachers and traders, such as a figure called Tuan Masha'ika or Makdum, remembered in connection with Simunul island in the Tawi-Tawi group, and later Sharif Abu Bakr, an Arab-descended or Malay-connected scholar who is said to have married into the local ruling family and founded the sultanate. Cesar Adib Majul placed the establishment of the sultanate in the middle of the fifteenth century, with Islam arriving earlier through merchants and teachers. Najeeb Saleeby collected and published Sulu genealogies in 1905, but his compilation is a later record of traditions and documents, not a contemporary account of fifteenth-century events, and the dates in the genealogies cannot be treated as independently established.

What can be said securely is that by the sixteenth century Sulu's rulers belonged to an Islamic commercial and political world connected to Brunei, Borneo, and the Malay world, and that Muslim titles and legal ideas had been adopted. The Sulu sultanate combined a ruler of sacred lineage with a council of datu, an arrangement that tied older chiefly structures to new religious legitimacy. The sultan's authority remained dependent on the cooperation of datu who controlled their own followers. The better-documented eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Sulu state, with its extensive slave raiding and trade, belongs to a later period and is outside this chapter.

Sulu's maritime position encouraged a political order based on negotiation across islands and languages. A Jolo-centered ruler could benefit from Tausug-speaking agricultural and trading communities while depending on Sama-speaking navigators, fishers, and boat crews for access to particular waters. Such cooperation could produce wealth without placing every island under identical institutions. The Ming records' references to several Sulu rulers may reflect that plurality rather than a single court duplicated under different names. Historians therefore need to keep separate the documented diplomatic visit of 1417, later accounts of religious founders, and the more elaborate sultanate institutions known from much later centuries.

Section 72.10

Maguindanao, the Pulangi Basin, and the Emergence of Muslim Polities

The Pulangi River, also known as the Mindanao or Rio Grande de Mindanao, flows through a broad floodplain in southwestern Mindanao to the sea at the Cotabato region. The floodplain supported rice and sago, and the river connected the interior to coastal ports. Political authority in the basin developed along the river's course. Two principal groupings were recognized in Muslim tradition and Spanish records: Maguindanao near the coast, from a name often interpreted as "people of the flooded plain," and Buayan upriver. Each had its own datu, and they were allies and rivals. Neighboring communities of Iranun and Maranao, around Lake Lanao and other areas, also developed distinct polities.

The central tradition about the formation of Muslim authority in the basin concerns Sharif Kabungsuwan, said to have come from Johor in the Malay world, arrived by ship, married into local families, and established an Islamic ruling line. The tradition is preserved in genealogies and later compilations, and Majul's study uses it carefully. Scholars generally place his activity in the early sixteenth century, but the dates are not securely established, and the story cannot be treated as an exact chronology. Nor was it likely that Islam appeared suddenly in a single event. Muslim traders and teachers probably reached coastal communities earlier through links with Brunei, Sulu, and the Malay world, and the tradition reflects how later rulers expressed legitimacy by tracing descent from a foreign sharif.

The river system organized the polity. Coastal datu controlled river mouths and trade, while upriver leaders had access to interior forest and agricultural products and followers. Alliances through marriage, tribute, and exchange of goods linked the two, and interior non-Muslim peoples also traded with and sometimes served the riverine rulers. Authority was personal and contested. The Maguindanao and Buayan polities were distinct, and only later, in the seventeenth century under leaders such as Dipatuan Kudarat, did Maguindanao assume the prominent regional role described in Chapter 76. For the pre-1565 period, evidence is limited, and what we can say is that Islam had begun to organize political legitimacy in southern Mindanao and that riverine networks structured political relationships.

The distinction between a coastal seat and an upriver following also complicates the word 'conversion.' Participation in Muslim commerce or recognition of a sharif's prestige did not mean every community along the Pulangi adopted identical religious practices or accepted the same ruler. A marriage could secure cooperation between a trading family and an inland leading house, yet that alliance might be renegotiated when succession or the balance of trade changed. The genealogies preserve claims about legitimate descent and the diffusion of Islam; archaeological and later written evidence is needed to test the chronology. This limitation is especially important when contrasting Maguindanao with the separately developing Buayan polity.

Section 72.11

The Encounter at Cebu in 1521: Indigenous Authority through Pigafetta's Account

Magellan, a Portuguese navigator in Spanish service, left Spain in 1519 seeking a western route to the Spice Islands. After crossing the Pacific, his fleet reached the central Philippines in mid-March 1521. Antonio Pigafetta, a Venetian-born member of the expedition, kept a journal. At Homonhon and later at Limasawa, the Spaniards met local leaders. At Mazaua, which Pigafetta describes as the domain of a ruler named Colambu, they were received with gifts and ceremonial exchange, and Colambu agreed to guide them to Cebu, which he described as a major trading port. The Spaniards' interpreter, Enrique, a Malay-speaking slave whom Magellan had acquired in Southeast Asia, could communicate with local people, a sign of the reach of Malay as a trade language.

The fleet reached Cebu on 7 April 1521. Pigafetta describes Rajah Humabon as the ruler of Cebu, who was initially cautious and who, according to the account, expected foreign traders to pay a customary harbor tribute, while a merchant from Siam present in the port advised him about the visitors. Negotiations followed, with gifts exchanged and an agreement of friendship. Humabon and many of his followers were baptized on 14 April, an event Pigafetta describes as a mass conversion, though the political motivations of the Cebuano leaders, including a desire for allies and trade advantage, are evident. Humabon is shown ordering other chiefs to support the Spaniards and to supply food; some obeyed, and others did not.

One who did not was the leader of Mactan, called Cilapulapu in Pigafetta's spelling and known in later Philippine memory as Lapulapu. Another Mactan leader, Zula, according to the account, sought Spanish help against him. Magellan chose to attack Mactan with a small force, and on 27 April 1521 he was killed. The episode reveals competition among local leaders, whose interests differed. Humabon was not the ruler of all the central islands; Mactan's leaders acted independently, and each used the Spaniards in their rivalries. After Magellan's death, the survivors were invited to a feast on Cebu on about 1 May, where several of them were killed, and the fleet left. Pigafetta's account of the reasons is partial; it does not show what decisions were made in Cebuano councils.

The account must be read critically. Pigafetta did not understand most local languages and relied on interpreters, he saw only the places the fleet visited, and he wrote to praise Magellan and to interest European patrons. His descriptions of "kings" and "kingdoms" applied European categories to leaders whose authority was personal and negotiated. The account is valuable because it shows existing political relationships, trade, hospitality, ritual exchange, and local agency in the moment before Spanish settlement, and because it shows that the encounter was shaped by local leaders' choices as much as by the Spaniards' actions. It is not the beginning of Philippine history; it is a European record of one episode within it.

Section 72.12

Upland, Inland, and Non-Coastal Societies

Much of the archipelago's population lived away from the coast. In northern Luzon, the Cordillera highlands are home to peoples including the Ifugao, Bontoc, Kalinga, Isneg, Ibaloy, and Kankanaey. Spanish later used the general term Igorot, meaning people of the mountains, for several of them, a label that obscured their diversity. These communities cultivated rice, sweet potato in later centuries, and other crops, hunted, kept pigs and water buffalo, and organized society through kin groups and wealthy households rather than hereditary chiefs of the lowland type. Feasting, ritual specialists, and exchange determined status, and communities negotiated peace and conflict through customary arrangements.

These highland societies were not isolated. Gold from the Cordillera, especially the Benguet area, circulated to lowland Pangasinan and Ilocos coasts, and highlanders traded for salt, cloth, ceramics, and iron. William Henry Scott's studies of the Cordillera stress these exchanges. Highlanders and lowlanders shared boundaries, intermarried, and traded, and the contrast between "civilized lowlanders" and "primitive highlanders" in later colonial writing does not match the evidence.

The Ifugao rice terraces illustrate the need for care. They have been widely presented as built two thousand years ago, a claim now unsupported. Stephen Acabado's archaeological work argues that major wet-rice terracing developed much more recently, from about the sixteenth or seventeenth century, as highland communities adapted to Spanish presence and to population movement and to lowland pressures, and that Ifugao history, by collaborative Indigenous archaeology, shows innovation and political agency rather than timeless continuity. The research continues, and the terrace chronology remains under study, but it is a reminder that highland societies changed.

Other inland communities include Negrito foraging peoples, such as the Agta and Aeta of the Sierra Madre and Zambales mountains, who exchanged forest goods with farmers and fishing communities; interior Mindanao peoples such as the Manobo, who traded along the Agusan and other river systems; and communities around Lake Lanao. Modern collective terms such as Lumad for non-Muslim, non-Christian peoples of Mindanao are twentieth-century political labels. These societies had their own political authority, often in the form of ritual and kin-based leaders, and they interacted with coastal polities as trading partners, rivals, and sources of labor. They were not remnants of an earlier age but communities with their own histories, adapting to changing circumstances.

Section 72.13

Social Rank, Political Status, and Relations of Dependency

Spanish writers described preconquest societies as divided into ranks, and later research, notably Scott's, has refined their accounts by recognizing regional differences. In general, a small elite of chiefly families, a middle group of free people, and various categories of dependents can be identified, but the labels and the boundaries differ across the archipelago.

Among the Tagalogs, accounts of the late sixteenth century distinguish a nobility, including datu and their kin, from freemen sometimes called maharlika in Plasencia's account, and from dependents called alipin. Among the Visayans, the nobility, called tumao or datu, were distinguished from a class called timawa, free warriors and commoners who owed military service but not the heavier labor of dependents and who could change allegiance, and from the dependent oripun. The Spanish sometimes assumed that these categories were the same everywhere, and the later nationalist adoption of terms such as maharlika and timawa has obscured their original and varied meanings. A timawa in the Visayas was not the same as a Tagalog maharlika, and neither equaled a modern "noble" or "commoner."

Dependency was the organizing relationship of this world. Chiefs gained power by accumulating followers and dependents, and poor families attached themselves to powerful ones for protection, food, and security. Rank was inherited but could change through wealth, marriage, war, debt, or misfortune. The system's flexibility should not be exaggerated: those at the bottom had few rights, and chiefs' power over dependents could be harsh. The details of dependency, and of the forms of bondage discussed in 72.14, were central to economic life.

The distinction between free following and dependent labor had practical consequences. A Visayan timawa could be expected to accompany a datu on voyages or in battle, gaining honor and a share of rewards; such service did not make that person an oripun. A dependent household could owe agricultural work or a share of harvest while retaining a dwelling and, in some categories, possessions. Those relationships cannot be accurately represented by a single ladder in which everyone beneath a chief had the same obligations. Sixteenth-century writers sometimes translated unfamiliar ranks into Spanish social and legal labels, and modern historians must reconstruct what specific services and freedoms those labels concealed.

Status was also relational. An individual might belong to a high-ranking household through birth, yet lack influence if allies withdrew support; another might gain standing through marriage, trading success, or military service without acquiring every privilege of an inherited rank. Women and men could enter dependency through different circumstances, and children inherited status according to rules that varied by community and the parents' relative position. A study of hierarchy must therefore ask who could marry whom, who could move residence, who could transfer allegiance, and who had authority to settle a grievance. Rank shaped access to protection as well as exposure to coercion.

Section 72.14

Slavery, Debt, Captivity, Warfare, and Labor Obligations

Dependency in the preconquest archipelago took several forms, and scholars, including Anthony Reid in his comparative work on Southeast Asia, have emphasized that bondage there operated along a continuum rather than as a single sharp legal category. Spanish accounts describe people becoming dependent through inheritance from parents who were dependents, through capture in war or raids, through failure to pay debts, and through punishment for crimes. A debtor might become the creditor's dependent until the debt was paid, and the debt could be inherited. The Laguna Copperplate Inscription's release of Namwaran and his children from a gold debt in 900 CE shows that debt and its family consequences were legally recognized at least from the tenth century.

Within these categories, conditions varied. In Plasencia's 1589 account of Tagalog society, the alipin namamahay lived in their own houses, farmed, owned some property, married, and paid a share of produce or service to a master, while the alipin sa gigilid lived in the master's house and served directly. Visayan oripun likewise varied. Some dependents could redeem themselves, be freed, or have children whose status improved, and some could hold property and form families. Others, especially captives taken in war and those sold away, faced severe conditions. Some Spanish accounts report that slaves might be killed at the funerals of chiefs in certain Visayan communities, though the frequency of the practice is uncertain. Dependency was not equivalent to wage labor or to voluntary service, and it was not identical to later plantation slavery: it was a system of obligation backed by coercion, in which the degree of constraint depended on category, region, and the master's power.

Warfare and raiding fed the system. Captives were prestigious as spoil and useful as labor, and they were traded between communities and to outside traders. Chiefs gained wealth and followers by capturing and distributing dependents, and captives became integral to political competition. Slaves also formed part of the commerce that linked the archipelago to Borneo and the Malay world, a theme that later became central to Sulu (Chapters 76 and 91). The Spanish, once established, reacted to these institutions in complex ways: they used the categories to assign tribute and labor, and later changed or restricted forms of slavery, a process covered in Chapters 73 to 75.

The relationship between debt and unfreedom requires particular care in using the Laguna inscription. The document establishes that an obligation measured in gold could affect a named person's descendants and could be formally extinguished; it does not establish that the Tagalog categories described by Plasencia nearly seven centuries later already existed in exactly the same form. In the later reports, the possibility of redemption depended on the kind of dependency, household resources, and the creditor's recognition of payment. A family caught between crop failure and an obligation to deliver goods might lose bargaining power without having been captured in war. The resulting conditions could be as coercive as other routes into bondage, even if their legal origins differed.

Section 72.15

Women, Marriage, Inheritance, Property, and Customary Law

Marriage was a central institution of political and economic life. Among the Tagalogs and Visayans, as described by Spanish authors, a marriage involved negotiation between families, in which the groom's family provided payments to the bride's parents and kin, and might include service by the groom in the bride's household. Such arrangements are often described as bridewealth or bride service, though the details varied by region and rank. Marriages among elite families formed alliances. Plasencia describes elaborate payments, including compensation to the bride's parents for rearing her, and property that the bride might bring or receive.

Spanish-period writers describe women in some Philippine communities as possessing meaningful property and inheritance rights, but broad comparisons with contemporary Europe require careful matching of law, region, and social rank. Property belonging to each spouse before marriage was commonly regarded as retained, and property gained together was shared. Inheritance was normally divided among children, and in many accounts daughters as well as sons could inherit. Divorce was possible, and its financial consequences depended on who was held responsible, according to Plasencia. Women worked in farming, weaving, pottery, trading, and food processing, and women held ritual roles as healers and religious specialists (72.17). Some women had considerable standing in their households and communities.

These generalizations require caution. Practices differed by region and rank, and women's status was not equal to men's in all respects. Elite women could be married off for political alliances. Dependent women had far less autonomy. Spanish observers also brought expectations from their own society, which shaped what they noticed and how they described it. Plasencia's text describes Tagalog customs in a late-sixteenth-century setting, and it cannot represent every earlier society in the archipelago.

Plasencia's discussion of inheritance makes household property a historical problem rather than a simple catalogue of rights. A family had to decide what counted as property brought into a marriage, what had been acquired through joint effort, and which heirs could claim a share after a death. Disputes over those matters could draw in relatives and leading households; marriages across social ranks posed further questions about children and succession. The missionary's account is particularly useful because it describes rules and contested cases, but a legal rule recorded for late-sixteenth-century Tagalog communities should not be mistaken for evidence that every household enjoyed the same practical opportunity to assert it.

Marriage negotiations also help explain how social and political networks were reproduced. Bridewealth could involve gold, valuables, work, or promised support, with payments and reciprocal obligations connecting two households beyond the wedding ceremony. For elite families, such ties could secure trading access or peace between leaders. For farming or dependent families, the same negotiations might determine where spouses lived, which relatives offered labor, and who had the power to challenge a separation. A woman might have recognized interests in property or divorce while still facing intense pressure from senior kin or a chief. Explaining those simultaneous possibilities is more accurate than declaring women either wholly autonomous or uniformly subordinated.

Section 72.16

Agriculture, Fisheries, Craft Production, and Household Economies

The economic base of preconquest communities was agriculture and fishing. Rice was grown in wet fields where water allowed and in dry fields elsewhere, and upland communities practiced swidden cultivation, clearing and burning plots and moving them over time. Root crops such as taro and yam, bananas, coconuts, and sugarcane supplemented rice, and in Mindanao and other regions sago palm provided starch. Crops such as maize and sweet potato came from the Americas after Spanish voyages and cannot be projected into earlier periods, although they later became staples. Water buffalo were used in plowing, and pigs, chickens, and dogs were kept. Fishing in rivers, lakes, and seas, with nets, traps, and weirs, was central, especially on coasts and in Sulu, where marine products also entered trade.

Craft production was extensive. Women wove cloth from cotton, abaca (banana fibre), and other fibres, and Visayan textiles were noted by Spanish writers. Potters made vessels for cooking, storage, and burial, and metalworkers worked iron for tools and weapons and gold for ornaments. Boatbuilders constructed vessels for trade, war, and fishing. Houses were commonly raised on posts, with plank or bamboo floors and thatch roofs, near rivers and shores. Daily consumption included rice or other staples with fish, vegetables, and meat on special occasions, along with fermented palm drinks and the chewing of betel nut, leaf, and lime, which was central to hospitality and ritual.

Household labor connected directly to political obligations. Households owed produce, service in building and fishing, or military support to chiefs, and chiefs redistributed resources through feasts that reinforced status. A family's production fed its own members, paid dues, supplied trade goods such as cloth, wax, and gold, and met ritual and marriage obligations. Local ecology shaped specialization: riverine communities concentrated on rice and fishing, upland ones on terraced or swidden agriculture, and sea peoples on marine resources. Scott's and Junker's work shows how these economies supported chiefly competition by generating surplus and labor, but the surplus remained limited and vulnerable to typhoon, drought, and warfare.

Household economies changed with ecology and season. In an irrigated valley, rice cultivation called for organized water management and concentrated labor at planting and harvest. Elsewhere, mixed cultivation, tree crops, gathering, and seasonal movement could spread risk across several food sources. Coastal households might combine fishing with trade and canoe maintenance, while an inland settlement exchanged wax, fibers, or forest goods for salt and metal. Women and men both contributed to production, although the division of particular tasks varied. A chief's military expedition or demand for building labor could interrupt these routines, making political competition consequential for ordinary food security.

The survival of craft goods can also distort the historical picture. Gold ornaments and imported ceramics are durable and visually striking, whereas fishing nets, baskets, bamboo vessels, and everyday textiles often decay. Museum collections consequently reveal elite display more clearly than routine maintenance, childcare, or food preparation. A balanced account uses the spectacular objects as evidence of specialized production and exchange without mistaking them for the entire economy. Boatbuilders and weavers required skilled knowledge transmitted between generations; their technical achievements were sustained by communities as well as patrons. Such work made regional trade possible even where the makers' names have not survived.

Section 72.17

Indigenous Religious Worlds, Ancestors, Spirits, and Ritual Specialists

Religious life in the preconquest archipelago was local and varied, but several widespread patterns appear in the sources. Spanish writers, who were hostile and often described what they saw as idolatry, nonetheless recorded that communities honored ancestors and spirits. The word anito, in various forms, commonly referred to the spirits of the dead and to the carved or made images that represented them, while diwata, a term widely held to derive from Sanskrit devata, referred in Visayan and other usage to spirits or deities of places and natural forces. Spirits were thought to inhabit trees, rocks, rivers, the sea, and houses. Plasencia also reports a Tagalog term, Bathala, associated with a creator or high deity, though how such a figure was understood and whether the idea was shaped by outside traditions is debated.

Rituals marked agriculture, sickness, war, voyages, marriage, and death. Offerings, sacrifices, feasts, and dances addressed spirits and ancestors, and omens guided decisions about travel and warfare. Burial practice was elaborate. Archaeology has found jar burials, as in the Manunggul Jar from Palawan's Tabon caves dating to the late first millennium BCE, wooden coffins, boat-shaped coffins, and graves with ceramics, beads, and gold, which indicate beliefs about the afterlife and the status of the dead.

Ritual specialists, mostly women in many accounts, mediated between the human and spirit worlds. Among the Tagalogs, Plasencia calls such specialists catalonan, who could be men or women; among the Visayans the term babaylan is used, and Spanish writers also describe male specialists who adopted women's dress and roles. These specialists healed the sick, conducted ceremonies, divined the future, and presided at sacrifices. The terms should not be treated as universal. They apply to particular regional communities and reflect Spanish attempts to map local categories, and other communities had different specialists and different relationships between ritual and political authority. In many communities chiefs themselves performed ritual functions, and success in war, trade, or harvest was understood as showing favor from spirits and ancestors, so that political legitimacy and ritual authority were closely linked.

Ritual authority was not simply a separate institution alongside political leadership. A chief might sponsor a ceremony to secure protection for a voyage or confirm an alliance, while a ritual specialist's reputation rested partly on the perceived success of healing, divination, and relations with ancestors. Some specialists could criticize or advise powerful households, but their independence and status differed by place. The accounts of Spanish missionaries classify many practices under their own religious polemics, which makes the local purposes of ceremonies harder to recover. Students should therefore compare specific descriptions of participants, offerings, and occasions rather than assume that anito, diwata, babaylan, and catalonan were interchangeable labels for one archipelago-wide religion.

Section 72.18

Islam, Literacy, Scripts, Artistic Traditions, and Cultural Exchange

Islam spread in the archipelago through trade and marriage networks linking Brunei, Sulu, Mindanao, and the wider Malay world (Chapters 62 to 66). Its first strongholds were in Sulu and southern Mindanao, discussed in 72.9 and 72.10, and by the 1570s Muslim leaders and practices were present in Maynila and other parts of Luzon, particularly along coasts and in elite circles linked to Brunei. The process was uneven: many communities in Luzon and the Visayas had little or no contact with Islam, and Muslim communities differed in organization. Islam should not be equated with a single sultanate, and it neither erased nor simply replaced earlier religious traditions, which persisted in various forms in Muslim and non-Muslim communities alike. Islamic law, titles, and scholarship became important to rulers' legitimacy where they were adopted.

Indic influence is better documented in vocabulary, iconography, and political titles than in a single religion or state. Sanskrit-derived words such as diwata, rajah, and others entered Philippine languages; the LCI used Sanskrit terms and Indic dating; and the Agusan golden image shows a Hindu-Buddhist iconographic tradition. These elements arrived through Malay, Javanese, and other intermediaries and were selectively adopted by local leaders. The evidence does not show that all settlements passed through a Hindu-Buddhist stage, or that Indic states ruled any part of the archipelago, and the extent of Indic religious practice is uncertain.

Writing and literacy varied. The Kawi script of the LCI was used in the tenth century, evidently in connection with Malay-speaking commercial and legal networks. By the sixteenth century, Spanish observers recorded syllabic scripts used by Tagalogs, Visayans, and others, now grouped under the name baybayin, and some writers claimed that many people, including women, could write them, usually on bamboo and leaves for letters and short messages. Related scripts survive in Mindoro and Palawan, among the Hanunoo, Buhid, and Tagbanwa. In Muslim communities, Arabic script was used for religious texts and, adapted, for local languages. It would be wrong to portray preconquest Filipinos as universally literate or illiterate: literacy was regionally specific, varied by medium, and is incompletely captured by surviving documents.

Oral epics, such as the Maranao Darangen and Ifugao hudhud, carried history, law, and values across generations, and songs, riddles, and ritual chants did the same in other communities. Musical traditions included gong ensembles in the south, in which gongs obtained through trade were incorporated into indigenous musical forms. Visual art included tattooing, woodcarving, gold ornament, textile design, and decorated pottery. In each field, communities took ideas and materials from outside and changed them to local use.

Literacy also needs to be considered alongside the materials on which knowledge traveled. A legal charter on metal could survive for a millennium; a short letter scratched on bamboo or written on a leaf usually could not. Surviving evidence thus favors unusual durable objects and foreign observers. Conversely, possession of a script did not guarantee bureaucratic recordkeeping or the existence of a court archive. Oral performance could preserve genealogies, customary expectations, place knowledge, and stories of political legitimacy without reproducing them word for word. In Muslim and non-Muslim settings alike, written and spoken forms of knowledge overlapped. Their relative importance must be reconstructed for particular communities rather than assumed for the archipelago as a whole.

Section 72.19

The Archipelago on the Eve of Sustained Spanish Conquest, 1521–1565

The years between Magellan's death and Legazpi's arrival were not a period of Spanish rule. Survivors of Magellan's expedition, led by Juan Sebastián Elcano, left the archipelago in 1521 and reached Spain in 1522 by way of Maluku. Later Spanish expeditions followed, among them those of Loaísa in 1525 to 1526, Saavedra in 1527 to 1529, and Villalobos in 1542 to 1543. They touched the southern and eastern islands, struggled with food shortages and Portuguese opposition from the Spice Islands (Chapters 68 to 71), and left no lasting colony. Villalobos named Leyte and Samar "Felipinas," the origin of the broader name.

For most inhabitants, daily life continued under local leaders. The independent communities of Cebu, Mactan, Panay, Luzon, Sulu, Maguindanao, and the interior continued to trade and compete. Chinese traders from Fujian continued to visit Luzon, and Brunei, Borneo, and Malay traders kept their connections, while Islam extended its influence in Sulu and Mindanao and along parts of the Luzon coast. Portuguese traders and ships also visited parts of the region from their bases in Melaka and Maluku (Chapters 69 to 71). In these decades Spanish and Portuguese knowledge of the archipelago grew slowly and unevenly.

That knowledge was incomplete. The expeditions depended on interpreters, often Malay speakers, and on guides whose interests were their own. What Pigafetta recorded at Cebu in 1521 was one moment in a political situation that kept changing, and when Legazpi arrived in 1565, he did not find the same arrangement. Local leaders by then had memories of the earlier visitors, some of them hostile, and made their own calculations about whether to resist, cooperate, or ignore the newcomers. This section ends at the threshold of Chapter 73, which treats Legazpi's expedition, the alliances and wars it generated, and the uneven creation of Spanish authority.

The period between 1521 and 1565 is also a warning against making one encounter stand for a whole generation. Some coastal leaders may have heard reports of Iberian visitors through merchant networks, while communities farther from the routes had no reason to organize their affairs around Spanish expeditions. The growth of Muslim trading relationships, local disputes over port access, movement between coastal and river settlements, and the continuing supply of imported goods followed their own trajectories. When a Spanish force finally returned intending to establish a permanent presence, its diplomatic possibilities depended on choices made by local leaders within these longer histories, not on a universal response to outsiders.

Section 72.20

The Philippine Archipelago before Spanish Conquest: Historical Synthesis

Taken together, the evidence shows a set of societies with long histories. By the tenth century, the Pasig and Laguna region used a Southeast Asian legal and written culture; Butuan built plank boats and exchanged gold and goods with the wider maritime world; and by the thirteenth to sixteenth centuries Chinese records and archaeology reveal sustained trade involving many regions. Chiefs organized followers, controlled prestige goods, and competed through alliance, feasting, trade, and war. Households produced rice, fish, cloth, pottery, and metalwork, and obligations of labor and service tied dependents, free followers, and chiefs together.

Political arrangements were diverse. Tondo and Maynila, Cebu, Butuan, Sulu, Maguindanao, and Buayan were distinct centers with different structures and external ties. Muslim polities developed in Sulu and southern Mindanao, linked to Brunei and the Malay world, while other communities maintained other religious and political traditions, and upland peoples organized their own societies with trade links to the lowlands. Religion, law, marriage, and literacy took regional forms, and foreign influences from India, China, Java, Borneo, and the Islamic world were selected and transformed.

For the Spanish, the practical consequence was that they encountered not one polity to conquer but many local powers, with no capital whose capture would yield control. Alliances with some datu and warfare against others, and the use of existing systems of tribute and dependency, shaped the colonization described in Chapters 73 to 76. Neither the arrival of Magellan nor the arrival of Legazpi marked the start of these societies' histories.

Three comparisons clarify the chapter's argument. The Laguna inscription records authority in a legal transaction, while Butuan's excavated vessels reveal technical cooperation and maritime mobility; neither by itself supplies the political map of an entire century. Pigafetta's Cebu narrative records a moment of intense diplomacy, whereas Plasencia later described social institutions in communities already experiencing colonial intervention. Read together, the sources show both continuity and change, and warn against treating the latest detailed testimony as a transparent window onto an earlier period. A defensible preconquest history must distinguish documentary evidence, reasonable reconstruction, and unanswered questions at every stage.

Chapter Summary

This chapter reconstructed the Philippine archipelago before sustained Spanish colonization, treating the islands' societies as historical actors with their own institutions, connections, and histories. It argued that Philippine history did not begin in 1521 or 1565, and that "Philippines" is here a geographic term, not the name of a unified preconquest state.

Evidence is uneven. Archaeology, a very few inscriptions, Chinese records, oral traditions, and European accounts each illuminate part of the picture and each has limits. Later colonial descriptions, such as Plasencia's, are valuable but cannot be projected unchanged onto earlier centuries, and silence in the record does not show absence of institutions.

The Laguna Copperplate Inscription of 900 CE shows legal practice, debt, officials, and connections to Malay and Javanese worlds in the Pasig–Laguna region. Its Kawi-derived script is distinct from later baybayin, and it does not show a unified kingdom. Butuan's boats, gold, and ceramics, with Chinese records, show maritime commerce and craft skill in northeastern Mindanao.

Political authority rested on datu and barangay: kinship, followers, alliances, tribute, and success in trade and war. Tondo and Maynila, Cebu, and Butuan were distinct centers, and datu should not be equated with sovereign kings. Trade in imported goods supported chiefly competition without implying foreign sovereignty.

In Sulu and Maguindanao, Muslim polities developed through trade networks with Brunei and the Malay world. Their genealogical traditions preserve valuable memory but cannot supply secure dates, and Islam spread unevenly. Highland and inland societies, including the Cordillera peoples, had their own institutions and exchange ties, and recent archaeology has revised the dating of Ifugao terracing.

The encounter at Cebu in 1521, known through Pigafetta, shows local leaders negotiating, cooperating, and resisting according to their own interests. Humabon, Zula, and Lapulapu acted within existing political relations, and the encounter should not be mistaken for the start of Philippine history.

Social life was organized by rank and dependency. Regional terms such as timawa, maharlika, alipin, and oripun were not identical, and dependency ranged from relatively autonomous households to severe bondage. Marriage, property, and inheritance gave women significant roles in many regions, though with variation and the limits of colonial testimony.

Religion included ancestor and spirit practice, regionally specific ritual specialists, Islam in the south, and Indic influences, alongside multiple scripts and oral traditions. Selective incorporation of foreign ideas marked each. By 1565 the archipelago was a varied and changing landscape of local powers, and Spanish colonization met that landscape rather than a blank one.

Sakop
Followers or dependents attached to a chief or a barangay, a term used in later colonial descriptions.
Timawa
In Visayan accounts, a free rank between chiefly families and dependents, owing military service but not dependents' labor.
Maharlika
In Plasencia's Tagalog account, a category of freemen; not identical to Visayan timawa.
Alipin
The Tagalog term for dependents, including namamahay (living in their own houses) and sa gigilid (living in the master's house).
Oripun
The Visayan term for dependents, with varied conditions.
Debt bondage
Dependency arising from unpaid debt, which could be inherited and which the Laguna Copperplate Inscription shows in a legal setting.
Mangayaw
Visayan seaborne raiding for captives, plunder, and prestige.
Babaylan
A Visayan ritual specialist; the term should not be assumed to apply across the archipelago.
Catalonan
The term Plasencia records for Tagalog ritual specialists.
Anito
Spirits of the dead and their images, in several Philippine languages.
Diwata
A spirit or deity, widely linked to Sanskrit devata.
Kawi
The Old Javanese script derived from Indian models, used in the Laguna Copperplate Inscription.
Baybayin
The family of syllabic scripts recorded in Spanish-period sources; distinct from Kawi.
Tarsila
A Muslim genealogical record of Sulu and Mindanao, valuable as tradition but recorded later.
Prestige goods
Valued imported or crafted items, such as ceramics and gold, used by chiefs to display rank and reward followers.
Chiefdom
A scholarly term for a political community led by a chief with a following but without a developed bureaucracy.
Lashed-lug construction
Boat construction in which planks with projecting lugs are lashed together and pinned with dowels.
Bridewealth
Payments from a groom's family to a bride's family, one element of marriage negotiation in sixteenth-century accounts.
Swidden
Agriculture in which plots are cleared and burned, cultivated for a time, and then left fallow.
Lantaka
A small bronze cannon of a type made in Brunei and the Malay world and found in Philippine hands.
Tribute
Payments or gifts given to a more powerful party; in Chinese records, tribute missions served diplomacy and trade and do not by themselves show sovereignty.
Austronesian
A language family whose Philippine branches include most of the archipelago's languages; not a fixed ethnic group.

Chapter Reflection

This chapter matters because it changes where the story begins. A history that opens with Spanish ships makes the archipelago a recipient of events; a history that opens with a copper plate recording a debt in 900 CE, with plank boats from Butuan, and with chiefs weighing the arrival of foreign vessels makes its inhabitants the authors of their own past.

The chapter reveals processes shared with the rest of maritime Southeast Asia: trade-based chiefly competition, selective adoption of Indic and Islamic ideas, and networks of kinship and obligation that mattered more than fixed borders. It also reveals features distinctive to the archipelago, notably its political fragmentation, which shaped how Spain would build authority.

Later developments depended on these foundations. Spanish tribute and labor systems, the parish and town structures of Chapter 75, the commercial rise of Manila in Chapter 74, and the centuries of frontier relations in Chapter 76 each worked through, against, or alongside the indigenous institutions described here. Many continuities, in kinship, language, religion, and local political culture, survived colonization.

Finally, the chapter shows the importance of method. Much of what we know about the preconquest world comes through the pens of those who conquered it. Learning to read such sources critically, to value archaeology and oral tradition, and to distinguish what is documented from what is assumed, is the core skill of historians of this region.
