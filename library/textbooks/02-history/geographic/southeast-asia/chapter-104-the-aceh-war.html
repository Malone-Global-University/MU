Chapter Opening

In April 1873 a Dutch expeditionary force landed on the northern tip of Sumatra expecting a short campaign against a weak Muslim sultanate. Its commander was dead within ten days and the expedition withdrew. Thirty years later, with the sultan in Dutch custody, the colonial government still had not secured the countryside.

The Aceh War (1873–1904, with armed resistance continuing to about 1913) was among the longest and most costly conflicts of Dutch colonial expansion. The Netherlands entered it as a power that had spent the previous decades extending the Netherlands Indies beyond Java, a process surveyed in Chapter 103. Aceh was the largest remaining independent state in the Indonesian archipelago, and it sat beside the Strait of Malacca, one of the most valuable waterways in Asia. For Dutch officials the war was meant to close a strategic gap. For the Acehnese it became a struggle over sovereignty, religion, and the right of communities to decide who governed them.

The war cannot be explained by a single event. It began in an international diplomatic setting shaped by the Anglo-Dutch Sumatra Treaty of 1871, by Dutch anxiety about foreign rivals, and by Acehnese attempts to find allies abroad. It continued because of the way Acehnese society was organized. Authority was shared among a sultan, regional aristocrats called uleebalang, Islamic scholars called ulama, and village and district communities. Capturing the royal compound in January 1874 therefore destroyed a symbol and a residence, but not the networks of loyalty, kinship, religion, and obligation that sustained resistance.

The Dutch response passed through distinct phases. Large naval and infantry expeditions gave way to a static defensive line around the Dutch base at Kutaraja (the colonial name for Banda Aceh) from 1884. That strategy preserved a foothold but surrendered the countryside. From the 1890s a more intrusive approach emerged, built on ethnographic and political intelligence, mobile infantry units, agreements with cooperative local rulers, and sustained pressure on civilians who supported fighters. This approach produced the submissions of 1903 and the violent campaign of 1904 in the Gayo and Alas highlands. It also produced a very large human cost.

This chapter treats the Acehnese as political actors with their own institutions, debates, and strategic choices. It avoids two common distortions. One reduces resistance to "fanaticism," a label used by colonial writers who preferred not to analyze Acehnese politics. The other treats the conflict as the early expression of a modern Indonesian nationalism, which projects later categories onto people whose primary commitments were to their sultanate, localities, religion, and kin. The chapter also avoids treating the Dutch as a single unified actor. Governors, generals, scholars, civil officials, and soldiers disagreed about strategy and about what Aceh could become.

The evidence is unevenly distributed, and students should keep that in mind. Dutch military dispatches, colonial government reports, photographs, and the writings of the scholar-official Christiaan Snouck Hurgronje are abundant. Acehnese perspectives survive in manuscripts, notably the Hikayat Prang Sabi, in chronicles, genealogies, oral tradition, and later memory. The chapter reads colonial sources critically, as both indispensable information and products of the occupation they served.

The chapter's chronological scope runs from the diplomatic background of 1858–1873 to the major campaigns ending in 1904 and an epilogue to about 1913. Historians date the war's end differently depending on whether they stress the sultan's submission in 1903, the large campaigns of 1904, or the final suppression of organized armed resistance. This chapter treats 1904 as a major turning point and not as an uncontested end. Later chapters follow Aceh into the twentieth century, including Chapter 213 on Aceh's modern war, tsunami, and peace. Chapter 105 moves west to the British ports of the Straits.

Historical Context

Aceh occupies the northern tip of Sumatra, where the Indian Ocean meets the Strait of Malacca. Its history as a Muslim polity reaches back to the Samudera Pasai period (Chapter 63), and in the early seventeenth century, under Iskandar Muda, Aceh was a major commercial and military power contesting the Strait with Portuguese Melaka and Johor (Chapter 80). By the nineteenth century the sultanate was much weaker at the center. Its rulers retained prestige and ceremonial authority, but real power was spread among regional lords and religious leaders. Aceh nevertheless remained independent while the Dutch, whose colonial state had been remade after the VOC's collapse (Chapter 100), expanded across Java and the outer islands (Chapters 101–103).

The key international background was an Anglo-Dutch understanding. By an 1824 treaty Britain and the Netherlands divided their spheres in the archipelago and, in effect, agreed on respecting Aceh's independence while seeking to keep the northern Strait open to trade and free of piracy. Britain's concern was the security of its commerce and its Straits ports (Chapter 105). During the 1860s and early 1870s changes in shipping, in the Suez route, and in the Netherlands' colonial ambitions weakened that arrangement. The 1871 Sumatra Treaty removed the British objection to Dutch action against Aceh in return for commercial concessions and other adjustments between the two powers.

Within Aceh, nineteenth-century politics were contested. Court succession involved competing claims. Coastal rulers pursued their own interests in the pepper trade. Religious scholars held authority through schools, networks, and moral standing independent of the court. The Dutch invaded a society with no single command center. Section 104.1

The Aceh Sultanate in the Nineteenth Century: Politics, Territory, and Sovereignty

The Aceh Sultanate was an Islamic monarchy with a court at the capital, then commonly called Kutaraja only after the Dutch renamed it. Its sultan was a ruler of real prestige, whose name was recited in religious ceremonies and who was recognized as the legitimate head of the polity. But the sultan's power over the territory was limited by the strength of regional elites. The political system is best understood as a negotiated order. The sultan held ceremonial and arbitrating authority, controlled the royal port and some revenue, and depended on the cooperation of lords who commanded their own followers and income.

The most important of these lords were the uleebalang, hereditary district chiefs whose authority rested on land, ports, trade duties, and armed retinues. Parallel to them were the ulama, scholars who taught in religious schools (dayah), issued legal opinions, and held respect that did not depend on birth. At the community level, the basic unit was the gampong, or village, headed by a village chief and a religious leader. Several villages formed a mukim, a territorial community centered on a mosque, which functioned as a unit of worship, local governance, and defense. In the core district of Aceh Besar, the mukim were grouped into three larger divisions known as sagi, often rendered "corners" or "angles," each with a leading chief.

This structure matters for the war. A European army that seized the sultan's compound expected to seize the state. But the court did not command the countryside. Each uleebalang, mukim, and religious community had its own judgment about whether to accept foreign rule, and allegiance to the sultan was a political and religious tie, not a bureaucratic chain of command. Ironically, the same decentralization that weakened the court's capacity to tax and coordinate made the polity extraordinarily difficult to conquer. There was no single point whose capture could end the war.

Geography reinforced this pattern. The core of Aceh Besar surrounded the capital, with irrigated rice land and densely settled villages. Along the west and east coasts lay pepper-producing districts and small ports often under uleebalang of their own. Behind the coasts rose forested mountains and the interior highlands of Gayo and Alas, inhabited by communities with their own languages and political arrangements, tied to the coast by trade and religion but not simply subordinate to it. Sources for this period include Acehnese chronicles and genealogies, European travelers, and later Dutch ethnography, each with distinct limitations. Dutch writers often described Acehnese politics as chaotic because it did not resemble a European state.

The relationship between the center and the districts also had a history. Aceh's earlier rulers had projected authority through maritime alliances, diplomatic appointments and control of trade, but the nineteenth-century court did not possess a standing bureaucracy able to compel every coastal chief. Pepper wealth could strengthen an uleebalang independently of the palace. Some district leaders claimed legitimacy through inherited titles; others built influence through control of river mouths, connections to merchants or alliances with religious teachers. Recognition of the sultan was important, but it did not oblige identical political conduct throughout the kingdom. The Dutch had to confront this distributed structure repeatedly as the war progressed.

Aceh Besar's three sagi illustrate the point. Each incorporated groups of mukim, whose religious and local-government institutions linked settlement, mosque and agricultural land. These were neither modern provinces nor merely ceremonial divisions. Leaders could coordinate defense, arbitrate matters and negotiate their relationships with the court. The precise balance of authority varied with individual leaders and political circumstances. Dutch documents often tried to convert these flexible arrangements into a hierarchy with one recognizable superior at the top, then found that an agreement reached with that person failed to bind other communities.

In the coastal dependencies, pepper producers, shipowners and port officials likewise had reasons to calculate their interests independently. Revenue from export duties and access to imports could provide wealth and weapons. For the Dutch, bringing the sultan into submission seemed a way to secure all these resources at once. For regional actors, a European declaration of annexation might be only another political claim to be evaluated beside competing Acehnese claims. This difference in expectations is not evidence that Aceh lacked a political order; it shows that the order worked through different institutions from the centralized administration colonial officials wanted to impose.

Historical Vocabulary
Uleebalang

Definition. A hereditary district chief in Aceh, often translated "territorial chief" or "aristocrat," who governed a district (nanggroe) and its port or lands.

Historical context. Uleebalang derived authority from inherited position, control of revenue, and armed followers, and they dealt with the sultan as semi-autonomous partners. In the nineteenth century they were important in the pepper and port economy.

Why it matters. Dutch policy came to depend on dividing uleebalang from the religious leaders and on rewarding those who accepted colonial supervision.

Potential misunderstanding. The label does not mean "collaborator." Some uleebalang resisted, some accommodated, and many changed position as circumstances changed.

Section 104.2

Pepper, Maritime Commerce, Islam, and Aceh's International Relationships

Aceh's nineteenth-century economy rested on agriculture and trade. Pepper was the most important export, grown especially along the coasts and shipped through intermediaries to Penang and Singapore, where British and Asian merchants resold it. The trade made some coastal lords wealthy, connected Aceh to Straits commercial communities, and also provided a fiscal base for rulers who could levy export duties. Merchants, including Arab, Indian, Chinese, and Malay traders, operated in Acehnese ports, and Acehnese traders and shippers were active in the Strait. Rice, salt, areca nut, and other products supported local exchange.

Aceh also belonged to a wider Islamic world. The pilgrimage to Mecca passed through Acehnese ports, so much that the region was popularly called the "Veranda of Mecca" in later tradition. Scholars studied and traveled across the Indian Ocean, and religious ideas moved with merchants and pilgrims. Acehnese rulers sought connections with other Muslim powers. In the early 1870s they sent appeals toward the Ottoman Empire and approached other foreign representatives. These contacts did not amount to a stable alliance. The Ottoman state had limited capacity to intervene in distant Sumatra and its own priorities lay elsewhere. They nevertheless show a diplomatic strategy: Acehnese leaders recognized that Dutch expansion was a danger and tried to place Aceh within an international order of recognized sovereigns.

This diplomacy needs careful description. Religious solidarity was a real resource and a real language of politics, but it did not replace commerce, nor did it determine Acehnese choices. The court also tried to negotiate with Britain, and Acehnese merchants depended on British-controlled ports. Dutch officials, by contrast, read every Acehnese diplomatic move as proof of danger, an attitude that fed the decision for war.

The pepper economy was not a single state monopoly. Gardens depended on household and local labor, while the movement of the harvested crop depended on merchants and coastal authorities who could charge dues, provide credit and control access to anchorage. The rise of Penang as a commercial center furnished alternative buyers and linked Acehnese pepper to wider markets. A district chief who benefited from this commerce might defend autonomy against Dutch customs restrictions, but another could seek an accommodation that promised to preserve local revenues. The maritime economy therefore created both resources for resistance and incentives for negotiation.

For merchants, the strategic geography was practical rather than abstract. Monsoon winds, coastal shallows and the choice of small ports affected the reliability of shipping. A ship that could trade at Penang was not necessarily dependent on the Acehnese royal port. When the Dutch later employed blockades, traders might reroute cargo, break shipments into smaller lots or accept the risks of smuggling. These adaptations help explain the difficulty of turning naval presence into a total commercial isolation of Aceh. They also explain why British traders watched the war carefully even after Britain declined to defend the sultanate diplomatically.

Religious connections involved more than international appeals. Teachers studied Arabic texts and local traditions; pupils circulated among rural religious schools; learned families linked villages through marriage and teaching. Some networks reached the holy cities of the Hijaz. These relationships enabled the circulation of news, religious writing and arguments about a ruler's obligation to defend Muslim society. Yet neither the existence of a religious network nor an appeal to the Ottoman ruler proves a coordinated international military alliance. The historian must separate the symbolic importance of contact from the limited material support that actually reached Aceh.

The diplomatic choices of Acehnese rulers should be read against these constraints. They sought to prevent political isolation by communicating with powers that had their own commercial interests in the Strait. Such correspondence offered possibilities but also gave the Dutch a means to portray the sultanate as a dangerous opening for another empire. Acehnese diplomacy was thus both an effort to preserve sovereignty and, in the calculations of Dutch ministers, a pretext for ending it.

A final economic distinction concerns control of ports versus control of producers. Pepper grown in the interior could pass through intermediaries before reaching the coast, with each relationship creating obligations and opportunities to bargain. Dutch proposals for stricter supervision threatened to reorganize those links, while merchants based in the Straits worried about disruptions to established routes. The invasion therefore occurred in an environment where commercial power was dispersed among growers, harbor officials and foreign buyers. Political sovereignty and control of customs income were connected, but they were not the same thing.

Section 104.3

The Sumatra Treaty of 1871 and the Diplomatic Crisis of 1873

For much of the century the Anglo-Dutch relationship acted as a shield for Aceh. Britain wanted open trade in the northern Strait and distrusted Dutch tariffs and monopolies. The Netherlands accepted constraints, at least formally, because it needed British cooperation elsewhere. Two developments changed this by the 1860s. The opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 increased the importance of the Strait and of secure maritime routes. Meanwhile the Dutch government, having expanded along the Sumatran coasts, saw an independent Aceh as both an obstacle and a risk, since a foreign power might establish itself there.

The Sumatra Treaty of November 1871 fundamentally altered the diplomatic setting. In Article I, Britain withdrew its objections to an extension of Dutch dominion anywhere in Sumatra, including the reservation accompanying the 1824 Anglo-Dutch negotiations. Article II protected commercial equality for British subjects in Siak and in other Sumatran states that might become Dutch dependencies. These provisions were negotiated between two European governments: they did not transfer Acehnese sovereignty to the Netherlands, nor did they preserve a British guarantee of Acehnese independence. The agreement removed a major diplomatic barrier to Dutch expansion without conferring an Acehnese right to rule. Aceh’s leaders recognized the danger and continued seeking supporters abroad.

The immediate crisis came in 1873. Acehnese representatives approached the American consul in Singapore, an effort Dutch officials interpreted, and publicized, as an attempt to secure American protection. Historians stress that this was an immediate trigger rather than the cause of the war. The Dutch government and its colonial officials had already concluded that Aceh had to be brought under control. The foreign contacts provided a justification that could be presented to the Dutch public and to other powers. In late March 1873 the Netherlands declared war.

The relevant treaty history began earlier than 1871. During the restoration of Dutch power after 1816, British officials feared that Dutch domination of the northern entrance to the Strait would restrict maritime commerce. The exchanges accompanying the 1824 Anglo-Dutch settlement reflected that concern. Dutch agreements elsewhere on Sumatra then steadily increased the pressure on Aceh's eastern and southern approaches. By the late 1860s the Hague government had more resources and stronger reasons to challenge a polity that stood outside its claimed sphere. The Suez Canal did not create this ambition, but heightened the perceived importance of shipping connections between Europe and Southeast Asia.

Article I of the Anglo-Dutch Sumatra Treaty of 1871 is decisive. In the treaty's published text, Britain withdrew its objections to the extension of Dutch dominion in any part of Sumatra, including the reservation associated with the 1824 negotiations. Article II addressed commercial equality for British subjects in Siak and in other states that might later become Dutch dependencies. These were agreements between Britain and the Netherlands. They neither transferred Acehnese sovereignty to the Netherlands nor represented an Acehnese acceptance of Dutch rule. They removed an interstate diplomatic obstacle while leaving the political and moral question of invasion unresolved.

Diplomats and officers then sought an incident that could justify action. Acehnese representatives held contacts in Singapore, including with an American consular officer, while Dutch emissaries demanded assurances from the sultan's government. The ensuing communications did not occur in a neutral atmosphere: Dutch decision-makers were already inclined toward coercion and did not treat efforts to secure international support as an ordinary prerogative of an independent ruler. A Dutch war manifesto issued on 26 March 1873 presented a military campaign as a response to dangers and refusals. The order of events matters: diplomatic concern and imperial ambition preceded the asserted immediate offense.

Different audiences interpreted the same exchanges differently. Dutch parliamentarians could hear a claim about keeping the Strait safe; British merchants cared about continued trading rights; Acehnese leaders saw a threatened sovereign position and searched for leverage. A careful account should not infer that every Dutch official secretly desired conquest from the outset, but neither should it take the official justification at face value. Treaty language, official correspondence and Acehnese actions must be read against one another rather than collapsed into a single diplomatic narrative.

Why the Netherlands Invaded Aceh in 1873

Long-term causes

Dutch territorial expansion in Sumatra; the aim of consolidating the Netherlands Indies; concern over the Strait of Malacca and rival powers.

Short-term causes

The 1871 treaty; the Suez Canal's effect on shipping; Dutch anxiety about foreign influence at Aceh.

Immediate trigger

Acehnese contacts with foreign representatives in 1873 and Dutch refusal to accept them.

Consequences

A war lasting decades; a heavy financial and human burden; a model of mobile counterinsurgency.

Historical complexity: Dutch officials also held mistaken assumptions about Acehnese weakness. The invasion was a choice made with poor intelligence, not an inevitable result of structural forces.

Section 104.4

The First Dutch Expedition, April 1873: Invasion and Defeat

The first expedition sailed under Major-General Johan Harmen Rudolf Köhler and landed near the capital in early April 1873. The plan assumed that a rapid advance would capture the palace compound and compel submission. The force was a typical colonial expeditionary army: European and colonial infantry, with naval support, but with little reliable knowledge of the ground, of Acehnese defensive preparations, or even of the exact position of the royal compound relative to the landing area.

Acehnese defenders, including fighters from nearby districts, contested the advance, particularly near the Baiturrahman Mosque, which the Dutch had occupied as a position. Köhler was killed on 14 April while directing operations near the mosque. After the loss of the commander and facing continuing resistance, the Dutch withdrew by late April. The expedition had accomplished none of its main objectives.

The failure was significant for several reasons. Militarily, it showed that naval superiority and modern weapons did not compensate for inadequate intelligence and weak logistics. Politically, it embarrassed the Dutch government, which had publicly justified the war, and made retreat nearly impossible. For Aceh it demonstrated that foreign troops could be defeated. The result was a larger and better-prepared second expedition that would lead to the next phase of the war.

The expedition's operational difficulties began before the battle around the mosque. Dutch commanders had only imperfect maps and inconsistent information about the royal palace, the surrounding waterways and the routes linking the coast to the capital. Reconnaissance on 6 April did not resolve these uncertainties. The main landing on 8 April encountered determined Acehnese opposition; maneuvering artillery and supplies over wet ground proved awkward. The plan relied on quickly finding and occupying symbols of royal authority, but it had not sufficiently accounted for the defenders' ability to concentrate fighters from nearby districts. The result was an operation in which terrain and information repeatedly frustrated the intended timetable.

Fighting near the Baiturrahman Mosque exposed the problem. The mosque had religious and political importance, yet occupying it did not necessarily disclose the route to the palace or destroy Acehnese defensive organization. Dutch advances were interrupted by resistance and by difficult crossings. A contemporary reconstruction records the confusion over a lagoon crossing on 10 April, reconnaissance of the royal compound on 12 April and heavy rain on 13 April. On 14 April the force renewed its assault around the mosque. Köhler was struck and killed, depriving the expedition of its commander during an already uncertain campaign.

His death was a dramatic event, not the only reason for withdrawal. Dutch officers had to judge ammunition, casualties, communications, the condition of troops and the prospect of another attempt with insufficient intelligence. Acehnese forces remained capable of continuing the fight. By late April, a retreat was judged preferable to remaining exposed without a clear path to the strategic objective. A force intended to compel rapid submission instead returned to Java without taking the palace. Contemporary Dutch accounts differed over the decisions and responsibility for failure, a reminder that military archives are not a single voice.

For Acehnese defenders, the resistance was a practical demonstration that fighting at the coast and around the capital could defeat an invading force despite a disparity in ships and artillery. Its significance for Dutch policy was almost the reverse: the public embarrassment created pressure for a larger expedition and made a negotiated retreat politically difficult. The failure thus intensified a conflict that Dutch authorities had initially expected to resolve quickly. A student tracing the campaign should distinguish the date of the declaration of war, the landing, Köhler's death and the final withdrawal; none marks the same event.

Some later accounts describe the first expedition as if it were defeated by a single fatal shot. That story is memorable but insufficient. Planning errors, delays, scattered defenses and uncertain command decisions had already undermined the operation. Köhler's death crystallized a failure whose causes lay in the relationship between operational aims and knowledge of the terrain. Dutch commanders learned they needed greater engineering, provisioning and troop strength. They did not yet learn the more fundamental lesson that holding the palace would not mean controlling Acehnese political society.

Section 104.5

The Second Dutch Expedition, 1873–1874: The Capture of the Capital

The Dutch prepared a much larger force under General Jan van Swieten, which began operations in December 1873. A naval blockade restricted Acehnese access to the sea. Troops landed, built positions, and advanced toward the capital against determined opposition. The Baiturrahman Mosque again became a center of fighting. Dutch forces took the royal compound in late January 1874, and the court withdrew from it.

The expedition's success was costly. Cholera and other diseases struck Dutch troops and the surrounding population, and disease was a major cause of death in this campaign. Equally important, the capture did not deliver the political result the Dutch expected. The sultan and his followers had left, and the districts around the capital did not submit. Capturing a political center is not the same as defeating the networks that support a state. The Dutch had won ground but not authority.

Van Swieten's expedition represented an attempt to correct the deficiencies of April. It brought a far larger body of troops, artillery, naval support and transport arrangements, together with greater attention to intelligence about approaches to the capital. Preparations included efforts to secure a beachhead and lines of supply rather than relying on an immediate rush toward the palace. The colonial force was nevertheless multinational in composition: Europeans served alongside Indonesian and other Asian soldiers. Describing its losses simply as Dutch deaths can conceal that many of those wearing the colonial uniform were not Dutch by origin.

The fighting lasted through December and into January. Once again the area around the Baiturrahman Mosque became significant, but the military objective was the royal compound and the surrounding capital. Acehnese defenders could not hold every built position against concentrated artillery and infantry. The palace was occupied on 24 January 1874, after the court and many supporters had withdrawn. Dutch reports celebrated this as a decisive military achievement. From the viewpoint of governing an entire country, however, a vacated palace offered only a headquarters, a symbolic victory and a confined base.

Disease was a major element of the campaign. Troops operating in an unfamiliar environment faced cholera and other illnesses as well as battle casualties; transport, sanitation and medical capacity were all under strain. The human burden extended beyond soldiers to porters, camp followers and civilians whose normal livelihoods were interrupted by the expedition. A numerical tally of combat casualties would therefore understate the full military cost. At the same time, the death of the sultan during this crisis would profoundly affect the political language of both sides.

The Dutch could occupy major buildings and declare their intentions, but it could not compel regional chiefs to deliver revenue or provide safe passage merely by placing a flag over the capital. Acehnese fighters could continue from inland positions and communities along the coasts. The episode illustrates a distinction that will recur throughout this chapter: capture means possession of a specific place, annexation is a political-legal claim, and control requires enforceable relationships with people across territory. In January 1874, these conditions were very far from identical.

The Baiturrahman Mosque was itself more than a tactical structure. As a prominent institution, damage and fighting around it could communicate messages about the invaders' relationship to Acehnese religious life. Colonial engineers assessed approaches and defensive positions; Acehnese observers could interpret the same battlefield in terms of legitimacy and sacrilege. This dual significance helps explain why a purely operational narrative of captured buildings leaves out part of the experience of conquest. The army's emphasis on decisive symbols assumed those symbols could stand in for the distributed authority of districts and schools.

Section 104.6

The Sultanate after 1874: Political Continuity and Colonial Annexation

Sultan Mahmud Syah died of cholera in January 1874, soon after the fall of the compound. The Dutch government then declared the sultanate abolished and Aceh annexed. That proclamation had legal and political force in the Netherlands and in the eyes of European diplomacy, but it did not change loyalties in Aceh. A successor, Muhammad Daud Syah, then a child, was recognized by many Acehnese, and royal authority continued to exist in the interior and in the districts that rejected Dutch claims. For many regional leaders the sultanate had not ended.

The gap between proclamation and reality defined the period. Dutch officials governed the narrow area their troops could defend. Beyond it, uleebalang, mukim, and ulama exercised authority, collected resources, and made their own decisions. The Dutch spoke as sovereigns, but the realities of control belonged to others. The distinction between annexation on paper and government on the ground is a central theme of this chapter.

The fall of the royal compound and Sultan Mahmud Syah's death made it tempting for Dutch officials to write an ending to the campaign. But an Acehnese understanding of sovereignty was not extinguished when the incumbent sultan died. Royal descent, religious recognition and the loyalty of major regional authorities could sustain a successor outside the seized capital. Muhammad Daud Syah, still a child, became a focal point for continued legitimacy. A court removed from its palace could not wield the same public authority, yet it could provide a political standard around which chiefs and religious figures organized resistance.

The chronology of January 1874 is important. The compound fell on 24 January. Sources place the sultan's death from cholera in the closing days of January, with a discrepancy of a day in some reconstructions and calendar conventions. On 31 January Van Swieten proclaimed Dutch sovereignty over the whole of Aceh. That declaration had an unmistakable political purpose: it represented battlefield success as a completed transfer of authority. It did not follow from an Acehnese treaty of submission and was not accepted by the country as a whole. Describing it as 'annexation' is accurate only if the word is understood as a Dutch colonial assertion.

Several features of Acehnese politics prevented a quick consolidation. Chiefs could raise forces and income independently. Religious teachers could mobilize a public that did not always look to the royal court for instructions. Village and mukim communities could provide intelligence, supplies, refuge or obstruction. The withdrawal of the ruler from the capital shifted the theater of war without necessarily removing its political justification. Colonial documents that spoke of scattered bands or disturbances were also recording the continuing mobilization of a society whose authority was not concentrated in one place.

The Dutch faced a hard choice. They might bargain with leaders who controlled only part of the territory, or attempt to occupy more land at the cost of manpower and money. An agreement with one coastal chief could open a port but expose that chief to accusations of betraying the sultan. A military sweep could clear a district temporarily without preventing fighters from returning. By proclaiming an end to the sultanate while lacking the means to enforce the claim, the colonial state created a lasting divergence between official declarations and actual governance. The next stages of war emerged from that divergence.

The sultan's death also created a succession challenge within the resistance. Recognition of a child successor required guardians, senior chiefs and religious figures to coordinate public authority without the advantages of a secure court. That arrangement could sustain legitimacy but limited centralized military command. It is therefore misleading to imply that the young ruler personally directed all fighting from the interior. Contemporary Dutch officers sometimes took the inability to negotiate with a single representative as evidence of disorder; for many Acehnese, it was instead a consequence of the invasion and of the political autonomy that local communities already possessed.

Section 104.7

The Resistance Coalition: Ulama, Uleebalang, and Regional Leadership

Resistance after 1874 was not directed by a single organization. It combined several elements. Members of aristocratic families, including relatives of the court, led forces drawn from their districts. Ulama preached, organized, and in some cases commanded. Village leaders and mukim councils provided food, shelter, and recruits. Merchants supplied weapons and goods where they could. Women maintained households and networks under occupation, and some took more direct roles (Section 104.18).

The most celebrated religious leader was Teungku Chik di Tiro, a scholar whose network of followers made him the principal organizer of resistance in the 1880s until his death in 1891. His career shows how religious authority could be converted into military and political leadership. But the coalition was never uniform. Some ulama sought accommodation, and some uleebalang fought hard against the Dutch. Others, protecting their districts or hoping to preserve their rank, negotiated with Dutch officers. The common claim that ulama resisted while aristocrats collaborated is a simplification, though over time tensions between the two groups did become politically significant, and Dutch policy sought to exploit them.

What sustained the resistance was a convergence of motives. Religious obligation to defend the community, loyalty to the sultanate, defense of local autonomy, kinship ties, grievances against Dutch actions, and the personal prestige of leadership all operated together. Historians reconstruct these motives from Acehnese literature, Dutch reports, and later oral accounts, and each source type presents difficulties.

The resistance coalition cannot be understood as a permanent alliance with a single command. The sultan's supporters defended dynastic legitimacy, but district chiefs also sought to protect customary rights, toll income and relationships with merchants. A religious teacher might regard warfare as an obligation to defend a Muslim political community, yet could disagree with a secular aristocrat over who should lead or how resources should be allocated. These tensions were not merely weaknesses: they were also the reason for the movement's endurance, because the defeat or accommodation of one leader did not necessarily decide the actions of others.

The ulama became increasingly prominent as the war continued. Teachers trained students in networks of dayah schools and could carry appeals across districts where the court's direct influence was limited. Teungku Chik di Tiro, who emerged as a central religious and military figure in the 1880s, drew on these social resources. Preaching and poetry could link a local fight to the wider moral language of defending Islam. Nevertheless, the existence of religious leadership does not imply that every participant interpreted the conflict through the same doctrine. The social foundations of an army also included kinship ties, landholding, patronage and experiences of Dutch violence.

The uleebalang responded differently in different places and at different times. Some maintained armed opposition and helped mobilize supplies; some accepted Dutch protection in the hope of retaining authority; others moved between courses as conditions changed. Colonial officers often tried to convert accommodation by a chief into submission by his followers. A local community might reject that equation. Nor is it accurate to assume that all religious scholars refused negotiation. Historians need to ask who benefited from a particular arrangement and whether a chief could actually enforce it.

Ordinary people were indispensable to all sides. Fighters needed food, shelter, guides, boats and news of enemy movements. They also needed labor to move supplies and maintain defensive positions. Villages could sustain resistance, but these obligations became costly when fields were neglected and reprisals threatened noncombatants. The coalition's ability to continue therefore depended on social trust and local resources as much as on battlefield success. To portray resistance solely through a few celebrated commanders is to overlook the dispersed institutions and households that made years of fighting possible.

Recruitment and support also changed across decades. A chief might once have supplied armed retainers through established obligations, while a scholar could draw pupils and sympathizers through persuasion and religious commitments. As the war grew more destructive, personal losses could reinforce enlistment, while the cost of prolonged fighting pushed other people toward neutrality or conditional cooperation. This shifting balance helps explain both the endurance of opposition and the difficulty of describing a single 'Acehnese position' on the conflict. Dutch attempts to sort leaders into reliable and unreliable categories often misread this changing local context.

Section 104.8

Geography, Logistics, and the Organization of Acehnese Warfare

Acehnese fighters used a landscape that favored defenders. River valleys, forests, hills, fortified villages, and local paths were familiar to those who lived there and unfamiliar to Dutch troops. Fighting often took the form of ambushes, raids on supply lines, and sudden attacks on isolated posts, with withdrawal into territory the Dutch could not hold. Acehnese forces used firearms acquired through trade, including through Straits ports, alongside traditional weapons such as the rencong dagger and swords, and they built earthwork forts (kuta) of timber, earth, and thorny barriers.

Local knowledge was a military resource. Villagers knew water, food, trails, and hiding places. Communication occurred through kin networks, religious ties, and trade. By contrast, Dutch columns moved with heavy equipment, depended on supply from the coast, and suffered from disease and exhaustion. A small garrison at a distant post could be cut off. These conditions explain why the Dutch could win battles and occupy places yet find the countryside slipping from control.

Aceh's geography offered military opportunities but did not automatically favor resistance. The coastal lowlands around the capital were accessible to naval fire and troop landings, while river mouths allowed access to trading settlements farther from the Dutch base. Movement inland required knowledge of floodplains, paths, ridges and mountain passes. Heavy rain could make a road unusable and complicate the transport of artillery, while local fighters accustomed to the terrain could move by paths poorly represented on Dutch maps. Geography became a military resource only when it was combined with local knowledge and the cooperation of people living along those routes.

Acehnese armed groups varied widely in size. Some assembled substantial forces around defended settlements; others operated as smaller mobile parties that attacked patrols, cut communications and withdrew before a larger column could engage them. Firearms arrived through trade, inheritance, capture or purchase, while local weapons retained their importance. Combat often involved a mixture of firearms, edged weapons, trenches and earthworks. It would be misleading to treat all Acehnese fighters as technologically uniform or to label every encounter 'guerrilla warfare': a fortified village might be defended conventionally, while the same men later conducted an ambush.

Supply was a decisive constraint. Pepper sales and commercial ties could support the purchase of weapons, but a naval blockade made open import more dangerous. At the local level rice, salt, cloth and transport animals mattered as much as guns. Leaders who could secure food and recruit porters could keep a force in the field longer. Dutch troops were also constrained by supplies and were often dependent on long routes from their ports and fortified posts. Each side tried to disrupt the other's capacity to move resources, although the colonial state commanded greater money and access to ships.

The same network that supported fighters could become a target. An army unable to catch mobile opponents might punish villages suspected of providing shelter or food. Families then faced competing demands: helping relatives in resistance could bring Dutch retaliation, while refusing aid could provoke accusations from fighters or neighbors. This is why geography and logistics must be studied alongside civilian experience. The war was sustained through populated landscapes, not fought in an empty wilderness. The changing capacity of those landscapes to feed, hide and inform armed groups was central to its military outcome.

Intelligence was inseparable from social relationships. A fisher who knew a landing place, a trader familiar with routes, or a villager aware of movements between districts possessed information valuable to either side. But sharing it could bring danger. Dutch officers could misinterpret silence as sympathy with resistance, while fighters might suspect informants of betrayal. Such uncertainty contributed to reprisals and to the difficulty of distinguishing civilians from combatants. It also made the search for trusted local intermediaries a central feature of late nineteenth-century colonial military planning.

Division III — Protracted War, Religious Mobilization, and Military Stalemate, 1874–1896

Central question: How did Acehnese resistance continue for decades, and why did Dutch military policy fail to secure the countryside?

Section 104.9

Dutch Garrisons, Blockades, and the Limits of Military Occupation

After 1874 the Dutch held the capital area through fortified posts, supplied by sea and protected by naval patrols. A blockade of Acehnese coasts aimed to cut weapons imports and pepper exports. Expeditions moved out from the posts into surrounding districts, destroyed fortifications, and withdrew. Military administration, not civil government, ruled the occupied zone.

The strategy was expensive and ineffective. Garrisons suffered from disease, and troops faced attacks on roads and outposts. Intelligence was poor, partly because the Dutch relied on a few informants who did not always have reliable information. The blockade was porous: Acehnese traders and smugglers used small boats and remote coves, and the long coastline resisted patrolling. The colonial budget bore the cost of a war that had been expected to be brief, and criticism grew in the Netherlands over its expense and its aims. The result was a fortified enclave surrounded by a society that largely refused to accept Dutch authority.

The Dutch initially tried to extend their control through a sequence of expeditions and posts. Each new garrison promised secure ground from which troops could patrol and monitor routes. Yet roads between posts were vulnerable, reconnaissance required guides whose reliability varied, and a detachment that advanced far from its supplies risked isolation. Even a successful sweep might result only in temporary occupation; resistance could resume after the soldiers withdrew. This pattern created a persistent distinction between territory traversed by a column and territory governed through daily institutions. The official map steadily acquired more claimed lines than the colonial administration could enforce.

The maritime strategy was similarly limited. Naval forces could stop vessels near major harbors and impose costs on commercial life, but Aceh's long coast contained minor landing sites and local shipping networks that did not disappear. Smuggling, coastal kinship and trade with the British Straits complicated attempts at a comprehensive blockade. Commercial punishment was also politically dangerous. Restricting ordinary exchange could weaken civilians who were not participating in armed opposition while encouraging others to regard Dutch authority as an enemy of their livelihoods. The blockade therefore needs to be evaluated as an economic weapon, not only as a naval maneuver.

Garrison life was burdensome. Disease, supply shortages and fatigue could impose casualties even in months without major battles. Maintaining hospitals, transport and fortifications required funding, labor and continuous replacement of men. Colonial troops were not exclusively European: soldiers recruited from different parts of the archipelago served alongside Dutch personnel. They experienced the war through varying pay, career prospects and loyalties, all within an institution that disciplined its ranks and pursued the objectives of the colonial government. Calling every uniformed soldier 'Dutch' erases this internal diversity.

Political disagreements followed. Some administrators advocated limited control of coastal points to contain costs, while military advocates of more active campaigns believed defensive arrangements conceded initiative to the resistance. Officials in Batavia and ministers in The Hague faced mounting expenditure and criticism but also feared that withdrawal would discredit imperial prestige. In this environment the war became a test of colonial capacity: a state with substantial financial and military resources still struggled to translate victory at one location into legitimate, enduring authority in another. This unresolved problem led to the policy of concentration in the 1880s.

Costs accumulated in forms that official campaign budgets might not show. Transport crews were diverted from ordinary trade, supply animals were requisitioned, and imported equipment deteriorated in a demanding climate. In Europe the financial burden of a war without a clear endpoint provoked argument over whether prestige justified further spending. Yet retrenchment was politically difficult because ministers feared that withdrawal would invite diplomatic rivals or undermine authority elsewhere in the Netherlands Indies. Thus fiscal exhaustion and imperial ambition pulled policy in opposite directions for much of the middle phase of the conflict.

Section 104.10

The Concentration Line of 1884 and the Failure of Defensive Control

In 1884 the Dutch changed course. They abandoned exposed positions and concentrated forces along a fortified perimeter around Kutaraja and its port, known as the Geconcentreerde Linie, the concentrated line. It consisted of forts, blockhouses, roads, and connecting lines meant to secure the core against attack while the government waited for Acehnese resistance to weaken or for Acehnese leaders to come to terms.

The policy protected the Dutch position and reduced casualties, but it conceded the countryside. Acehnese forces operated freely beyond the line, raiding and sometimes attacking it. Dutch officials expected economic pressure and the passage of time to erode resistance. Instead, the stalemate allowed Acehnese leaders to organize. It also provoked criticism from officers and administrators who argued that a passive strategy accepted defeat. The concentrated line is a documented turning point because it forced the colonial government to reconsider its assumptions about the nature of Acehnese resistance and to seek better knowledge of Acehnese society.

The decision to concentrate forces was a response to overstretch. Dutch troops and their auxiliaries had been deployed across too many posts to defend them efficiently or to carry out continuous offensive operations. Rather than maintain a broad and vulnerable footprint, the government drew back toward Kutaraja and its port, Ulee Lheue, protecting a smaller zone with a linked network of fortified positions. Roads, patrols and communications were intended to make the perimeter defensible. The arrangement was not simply a line drawn on a map: its effectiveness depended on supplies reaching each post, soldiers monitoring movement, and intelligence about possible breaches.

The military trade-off was severe. Concentration reduced the number of isolated garrisons but effectively abandoned areas that earlier proclamations had placed under Dutch sovereignty. Religious and regional resistance groups could mobilize beyond the line and attack its approaches. A well-defended enclave might be safe from capture without generating tax revenue or stable political cooperation beyond its borders. This made the strategy defensible in budgetary terms but humiliating to advocates of a total conquest. The very existence of the perimeter became evidence that claims of colonial sovereignty and effective territorial control had diverged.

Troops nevertheless could not remain entirely behind fortifications. Reconnaissance patrols moved outside the defended zone, and mobile detachments sought to disrupt approaching fighters. In October 1887 a large infiltration raid associated with Teungku Chik di Tiro demonstrated the vulnerability of Dutch arrangements even within the protected area. By 1888 authorities authorized more limited patrols beyond the line. The later creation of specialized mobile units grew out of this practical experience, rather than appearing abruptly as a complete invention of Van Heutsz in 1898.

The concentrated line also reshaped civilian space. Farmers traveling to fields, traders bringing goods to market and families with relatives on either side of the perimeter could face inspection and suspicion. Where a military boundary divided ordinary movement, daily life became politicized. Such details help explain why the line stabilized a colonial foothold without winning allegiance. The army could make movement costly; it could not by those means alone create the relationships required to administer Acehnese villages. The transition from concentration to more active counterinsurgency was a response to that failure and to political criticism of the continuing stalemate.

The protected perimeter had a politics of its own. Residents of nearby villages could benefit from protected markets and regular supply deliveries, while others experienced disruption or surveillance at crossings. Some people moved between the defended zone and districts under resistance influence, making rigid labels of 'inside' and 'outside' inadequate. A commander might measure the line's success by the safety of a post; a farmer might measure it by whether water and labor reached the fields. The same arrangement could be a military success and an administrative failure depending on the evidence being examined.

Section 104.11

The Ulama, Prang Sabil, and the Religious Literature of Resistance

Religious language gave the resistance a framework for meaning and sacrifice. The concept of prang sabil, war in the path of God, held that defending the Muslim community against non-Muslim attack was a religious obligation. Ulama taught and applied this concept through sermons, schools, and texts. The best-known literary expression is the Hikayat Prang Sabi, a poem composed in Acehnese in the later nineteenth century and attributed to the scholar Teungku Chik Pante Kulu. It was performed aloud and copied in manuscript, and it circulated among fighters and villagers, describing the rewards of martyrdom and the duty of defense.

Dutch officials recognized its importance and confiscated copies, since they saw it as politically dangerous. Multiple manuscript versions survive, which means that the text was not fixed but adapted in transmission. The work shows how religious narrative could support political organization, personal sacrifice, and a sense of shared purpose. It does not, however, prove that every participant acted from the same belief. Religious duty overlapped with loyalty to leaders, family obligation, anger at Dutch actions, and local interests.

The expression prang sabil referred to warfare understood as religiously meritorious, and its power depended on the institutions through which that language was taught. Religious schools linked teachers and students across districts. Sermons, conversation and the performance of Acehnese verse carried arguments about duty and sacrifice to people who might never read a formal manuscript. These forms of circulation could connect village-level concerns with a larger understanding of defending Islam against foreign rule. They also offered an explanation for continued resistance after the sultan's palace had been taken and some chiefs had negotiated with the Dutch.

The Hikayat Prang Sabi is not a single fixed document. Manuscripts copied at different times contain variants, and their stories and exhortations belong to a literary tradition. Some texts depict the rewards awaiting those who die in defense of the faith; others use narratives of suffering, devotion and courage. The purpose was not to produce a neutral military chronicle. The historian can learn about moral ideals, intended audiences and the mobilizing power of poetry, but cannot automatically treat every episode as an eyewitness account of a named battle. Dating and manuscript provenance are therefore essential to the chapter's source window.

Dutch authorities paid attention to such texts because they considered their circulation dangerous. Confiscation and surveillance are evidence of colonial fear, although the act of suppressing a poem does not tell us precisely how many readers it influenced. The surviving manuscript known as Leiden Cod. Or. 8689 has a history associated with confiscation in 1902. The British Library's Endangered Archives Programme catalogues another manuscript, EAP329/9/3, classified as twentieth century. They are distinct witnesses, and an argument about the wording of one should not be applied uncritically to the other.

Religious mobilization was not separate from political organization. A teacher's authority could encourage people to refuse a Dutch appointment, raise supplies or sustain a defense. Yet communities also responded to coercion, kinship obligations, concern over land and taxation, and loyalty to regional leaders. The colonial description of resistance as 'fanaticism' minimized these considerations and treated religious commitment as evidence that rational negotiation was impossible. Equally misleading is the assumption that every religiously framed act was part of a unified national liberation program. The relevant political community was Aceh and its local and transregional relationships, not a modern nation-state already formed in the nineteenth century.

The literary form of the hikayat matters. Rhymed or rhythmically patterned narrative can be memorized, recited and circulated through performance. A manuscript preserved today may represent only one stage in a wider oral and scribal history, so dating a surviving copy does not necessarily date the initial composition. Nor should all copies be assumed identical merely because libraries give them the same title. Careful textual analysis may reveal changes in emphasis as campaigns intensified, leaders died or audiences changed. The source window should use the manuscript identifiers as locators, not as substitutes for examining an original text.

Teuku Umar, Political Negotiation, and the Changing Alliances of War

Teuku Umar, from the western coast, became a leading commander in the 1880s, with a following drawn from his district and kin networks and connections to other aristocratic families, including through his marriage to Cut Nyak Dhien. In 1893 he accepted an accommodation with Dutch authorities, receiving recognition, a title, and military supplies and followers. The Dutch regarded this as a major success that detached a prominent fighter from the resistance. In 1896 Umar left the Dutch side, taking with him weapons and men, and resumed war.

Interpretations differ. Dutch officials called it betrayal. Some Acehnese and later Indonesian accounts portray it as a deliberate strategy to obtain weapons, but the evidence for a long-planned scheme is limited, and historians caution against retrospectively treating every change of allegiance as part of a single design. A more cautious reading sees a leader pursuing his own and his community's interests in a fluid political situation, with options open to him until the evidence of events narrowed them. His case also shows the limits of Dutch co-option: arrangements with local leaders could be reversed.

Teuku Umar's significance lay partly in his ability to assemble fighters and supplies in the western coastal districts. His position depended on relationships with chiefs, merchants and households, and it was never simply that of an officer in a centralized army. Colonial attempts to understand his conduct often assumed that a political leader who accepted a Dutch arrangement had become permanently loyal. But local power could be negotiated, revised or revoked as military conditions and personal interests changed. The capacity to move between armed opposition and conditional accommodation was a feature of the fractured political environment.

His 1893 agreement with Dutch authorities offered a striking example. The colonial state provided recognition and access to military resources in expectation that a celebrated leader would help restore order. For Umar, the arrangement brought opportunities but also obligations and pressures from those who supported resistance. The meaning of this accommodation cannot be reconstructed solely from later nationalist stories or from Dutch accusations of deceit. Historians must compare the sequence of negotiations, official promises, the movement of men and arms, and the reactions of contemporaries before deciding what he intended at each stage.

In 1896 Umar returned to armed opposition, taking Dutch-provided equipment and followers with him. The loss embarrassed colonial authorities and suggested that the policy of purchasing allegiance had serious limits. Fighters who benefited from equipment acquired during accommodation could strengthen local resistance, but the withdrawal also exposed how much a colonial agreement depended on one leader's personal influence. The Dutch thereafter placed greater emphasis on intelligence, pursuit and military pressure. Umar's career consequently links the era of concentration and bargaining to the more intrusive campaigns of the late 1890s.

Cut Nyak Dhien should be included in this political story rather than introduced only after Umar's death. Their relationship connected military leadership to the households and social networks that enabled west-coast resistance. Later accounts sometimes treat Umar's changes of allegiance as the expression of a single brilliant plan and Dhien as an unchanging moral counterpoint. Both depictions risk making hindsight substitute for evidence. Their choices occurred under immediate danger and uncertainty. The most defensible conclusion is that negotiation and warfare were available political instruments, and that the Dutch could not assume a treaty with a commander would settle the decisions of his followers.

His agreement with colonial authorities also revealed a fundamental problem of governance through co-option. A ruler or commander could accept money, titles and arms, but what did those benefits purchase: permanent loyalty, temporary cooperation, or merely a cessation of attacks in one district? Colonial administrators tended to assume a more durable transaction than local politics guaranteed. Umar's return to fighting showed that personal bargains could unravel, especially if they failed to protect the interests of followers. The episode therefore belongs in the history of institutional failure as well as in the biography of a celebrated leader.

Christiaan Snouck Hurgronje: Scholarship, Intelligence, and Colonial Policy

Christiaan Snouck Hurgronje was a Dutch scholar of Islam who had studied in Mecca and entered colonial service. He conducted research in Aceh in the early 1890s and published the Dutch-language De Atjèhers (1893–94), later translated as The Achehnese. His work described Acehnese society, law, religion, and politics in detail. It is an invaluable source and also a colonial document.

His analysis shaped policy. He argued that Acehnese resistance was driven largely by the ulama and by local political structures, not by a united "fanatical" people, and that the colonial state should distinguish religious practice, which it could tolerate, from political leadership, which it should break. He recommended working with uleebalang while isolating ulama who led resistance, and he urged aggressive military action against those who continued fighting. Descriptions of institutions and recommendations for their subordination should be separated analytically, but in practice they were joined. Scholarship in the service of occupation produced knowledge that made the occupation more effective.

Snouck Hurgronje came to Aceh as a scholar whose training in Islamic studies and languages offered colonial officials a kind of information they had lacked. His investigations in and around Kutaraja in 1891–1892 drew on conversations with informants, textual materials and his knowledge of Muslim institutions. The resulting work, published in Dutch as De Atjèhers in 1893–1894 and translated as The Achehnese in 1906, gave detailed accounts of social organization, law, religious learning and literature. Its rich descriptive chapters are valuable historical sources, but they must be read with awareness of the relationships through which information was obtained and the colonial setting of its production.

The practical significance of his research lay in how administrators used categories of authority. Snouck distinguished the political influence of regional chiefs from the religious influence of scholars and urged the colonial state to exploit those differences. He recommended accommodation with elites who would acknowledge Dutch supremacy, alongside determined action against armed opposition. This did not mean that he simply equated all religious practice with rebellion. He drew distinctions between religious worship, scholarly life and mobilization against colonial rule. But those distinctions were deployed toward a political purpose: the consolidation of foreign control.

The scholarship and policy were not perfectly aligned with military practice. Snouck believed knowledge of Acehnese institutions could make conquest more effective and, in his view, less indiscriminately destructive. Military commanders emphasized operational speed and punishment. Their relationship involved cooperation but also disagreement, especially over the handling of populations, the meaning of submission and the use of extreme force. Neither side was advocating Acehnese self-determination. The disagreements were arguments within a colonial project over how best to govern a society resisting it.

A historian must distinguish three kinds of evidence in this material. Descriptions of kinship and mosque administration may preserve observations otherwise difficult to recover; generalizations about a collective 'Acehnese character' can reproduce colonial stereotypes; and policy recommendations are arguments for action, not neutral reports. The book's detailed coverage of religion and literature is indispensable for understanding why those institutions mattered, while its authorial position illustrates the ethical problem of research that could become an instrument of surveillance and conquest. Students should evaluate a specific passage rather than merely labeling the entire work either objective or biased.

Snouck's claims to knowledge should also be situated within the practical constraints of his research. Access to interlocutors in a besieged colonial town was shaped by protection, surveillance, translation and networks of informants. Knowledge about institutions beyond the concentrated line could depend on intermediaries rather than direct observation. Such mediation does not render his detailed descriptions useless, but it complicates their authority. A methodologically sound reading asks when a statement is a reported local practice, when it is a broad ethnographic generalization, and when it is the author's recommendation to officials planning conquest.

The Korps Marechaussee and the Development of Mobile Counterinsurgency

The Korps Marechaussee, formed in 1890, was a corps of mobile units composed of European officers and non-commissioned officers and Indonesian soldiers, many recruited from Ambonese and Javanese communities in the colonial army. The units were small, lightly equipped, trained for rapid movement, and organized to pursue guerrillas into difficult terrain. They differed from large expeditionary columns and from static garrisons.

The units depended on intelligence from informants and prisoners and on cooperation with local leaders. They sought to find and attack fighters and their supporters rather than to hold ground. This approach improved Dutch effectiveness and was also more directly violent toward communities that supported resistance. J. B. van Heutsz, appointed civil and military governor in 1898, made it the center of a more aggressive territorial campaign. Research on the Dutch campaign has identified this shift as fundamental to its eventual success, and also stresses the severe violence it involved.

The creation of the Korps Marechaussee in 1890 emerged from the need for troops who could move rapidly outside the concentrated line. A conventional infantry detachment burdened by transport and artillery might arrive after its opponents had withdrawn. Specialized small units could patrol difficult routes, search for mobile groups, respond to intelligence and operate with less logistical weight. Their development was gradual, shaped by field experience and by the increasing willingness of Dutch authorities to undertake offensive operations beyond the protected perimeter. They were not identical to the European military police organizations with which the name might be confused.

The corps drew heavily on Indonesian soldiers as well as European officers and noncommissioned personnel. Men recruited from Java, Minahasa, Ambon and elsewhere served in the colonial army, making its campaigns a case of imperial warfare conducted through soldiers from several colonized societies. Training emphasized mobility, close combat and familiarity with terrain. Firearms and edged weapons both had roles in operations that might bring patrols into contact with fighters in dense vegetation or confined settlements. The force was better suited to pursuit than the large columns of the earlier expeditionary phase.

These operational improvements carried serious risks for civilians. A patrol designed to strike before a resistance group dispersed might also treat a village suspected of providing intelligence as a legitimate target. Quick movement could facilitate surprise, raids and collective punishment. In official language, aggressive operations were meant to restore 'order'; from the viewpoint of households caught between opposing forces, they could make ordinary farming, travel and market activity dangerous. No account of military modernization should treat organizational efficiency as an adequate measure of its effects. The means of obtaining intelligence and compelling cooperation must be examined as closely as tactical success.

Van Heutsz's rise in 1898 gave the new approach stronger political support. He could combine mobile operations with arrangements through local chiefs, supply restrictions and administrative follow-up. Yet it would be mistaken to attribute the entire shift to one individual. The concentrated line had already generated patrol experiments, the Marechaussee had been formed years earlier, and scholars and officers had been gathering information about local politics. Van Heutsz accelerated and coordinated tendencies already developing within the colonial military system. Explaining the transition in those terms makes clear how policy, logistics and local actors together shaped the later campaigns.

A further distinction concerns the chain of command. Specialized units did not operate independently of colonial strategic priorities, and their apparent flexibility depended on supplies, intelligence and protection from larger forces. Small patrols could reach places inaccessible to heavy columns, but holding territory afterwards required posts, agreements and local administrators. An account focused only on weapons or training risks giving tactical changes more explanatory weight than they deserve. The success of later operations came from combining mobility with the disruption of support networks and the reorganization of local authority.

Section 104.15

Van Heutsz's Campaigns, Teuku Umar's Death, and the Erosion of Resistance

After Teuku Umar's return to resistance in 1896, Dutch forces pursued him and his followers, particularly along the western coast. In February 1899 he was killed in a Dutch ambush near Meulaboh. His death was a serious blow to resistance in that region, though his widow Cut Nyak Dhien continued the fight.

Under Van Heutsz, mobile columns occupied districts previously outside Dutch control, constructed posts, pressured regional rulers, and pursued leaders. Pressure on supply networks, food, and households made continued resistance harder. Agreements with uleebalang, formalized through a short written declaration (Section 104.19), allowed the Dutch to hold territory with fewer troops. Military gains thus rested on both force and political bargaining.

Van Heutsz was appointed civil and military governor of Aceh in 1898, when earlier attempts at containment had failed to produce accepted authority beyond the Dutch foothold. His program combined mobile operations with pressure on regional leaders to sign agreements accepting colonial supervision. Rather than seek one climactic engagement, troops moved repeatedly through districts whose villages, paths and food supplies supported resistance. The intent was to deprive armed groups of freedom of movement, information and resources, while offering more favorable terms to chiefs willing to cooperate. A system of posts and roads followed advancing columns, but the pace and reach of administration varied across localities.

The campaign in the Pidie region in 1898 illustrates the changed method. Troops advanced with military objectives tied to breaking support networks and gaining intelligence, not merely occupying a symbolic palace. That approach could achieve local results where earlier sweeps had not. Yet the army still faced the challenge of distinguishing willing fighters from noncombatants, a distinction its commanders often subordinated to demands for information and compliance. Forced movement, seizure of food, destruction of property and punishment of suspected supporters became part of the war's social landscape.

Teuku Umar remained a prominent opponent after returning to resistance in 1896. His connections along the western coast allowed him to draw on local knowledge and supporters, but increasingly mobile Dutch columns limited those advantages. He was killed on 11 February 1899 near Meulaboh. His death was a major setback for resistance organization in the region and a propaganda victory for the Dutch. It did not make other leaders' political decisions automatic. Fighters could continue under different commanders, including women such as Cut Nyak Dhien, while communities still weighed the costs of aiding them.

A broader shift followed as chiefs and district leaders negotiated agreements under military pressure. The Dutch needed local officeholders to collect information, police communities and maintain access to roads. Chiefs who signed terms might preserve some formal status but lose freedom over relations with other powers and armed groups. Cooperation could therefore be both a survival strategy and a means by which colonial authority extended itself. The eventual collapse of major resistance networks cannot be credited to technology alone. It reflected coordinated pursuit, intelligence, changes in local alliances, repression of support systems and exhaustion after decades of conflict. The political costs of these methods must remain visible beside their military effectiveness.

The pursuit of Teuku Umar also underscores why a commander's death rarely settles a decentralized war. Followers could disperse, join other leaders, return to farming or continue smaller attacks. Their choices were shaped by whether colonial forces remained in the area and by the protection that families expected from either side. Dutch announcements of victory thus often marked the end of one organized force, not the disappearance of opposition. The historian should read military communiqués against later evidence of patrols, arrests and renewed violence before assigning an endpoint to a campaign.

The Sultan's Submission, the 1903 Settlement, and the Gayo–Alas Campaign of 1904

By the early 1900s the sultan, Muhammad Daud Syah, was under severe military pressure. Dutch forces held members of his family, and in January 1903 he submitted. The circumstances were coercive, since his decision followed the detention of relatives and the threat of continued pursuit. Panglima Polem, a major commander, submitted in September 1903. These submissions removed key symbols of resistance and ended the sultanate's claim to independence.

In 1904 an expedition under G. C. E. van Daalen entered the Gayo and Alas highlands, which had not been subdued. The column attacked fortified settlements, including Kuta Rih, assaulted in June 1904. Dutch records and photographs indicate that large numbers of people, including women and children, were killed. Dutch accounts of the campaign recorded thousands of deaths across the highlands. The numbers are approximate and drawn from the perpetrators' own reports, so they must be treated critically. Official reports described the campaign as pacification, while the visual and documentary record shows massacre.

The submissions of 1903 resulted from sustained military pressure but cannot be treated as simple acceptance of Dutch legitimacy. Sultan Muhammad Daud Syah was increasingly isolated as campaigns disrupted routes between his supporters and as colonial forces pressed into areas that had once offered refuge. Dutch authorities also used his family as leverage. When he came into Dutch hands in January 1903, the event gave the government a powerful symbol: the claimant to royal authority was no longer able to sustain an independent court. Yet an agreement by the sultan did not compel every scholar, district chief or armed group to surrender. His political position had been weakened before he submitted, and resistance networks still existed outside his control.

Panglima Polem's submission later in 1903 signaled another important erosion of aristocratic leadership. The chronology should distinguish such individual events from the end of the war as a whole. Colonial officials could claim that major leaders had been neutralized, but military patrols still faced resistance in the interior and highlands. The highland societies of Gayo and Alas were not simply remote extensions of the sultan's court. They had their own communities, local authorities and relationships with the coastal lowlands. Their incorporation required operations that differed from those undertaken around Kutaraja and Meulaboh.

The 1904 expedition under Lieutenant-Colonel G. C. E. van Daalen traversed the Gayo and Alas highlands during a campaign lasting months. Dutch forces attacked defended settlements in which fighters and civilian families were present. On 14 June 1904 the assault on Kuta Rih in the Alas region resulted in catastrophic loss of life. The colonial military count recorded **313 men, 189 women and 59 children killed**, a total of 561 at that locality. These are perpetrator-generated figures and do not include every person who may have died on the approaches or subsequently. The presence of so many women and children among the recorded dead makes an account of ordinary battlefield combat inadequate.

Medical officer H. M. Neeb's photographs and the contemporary military documentation make this episode particularly well evidenced. The images record the aftermath and were produced within the invading force; they must not be mistaken for the victims' own testimony. The Dutch campaign's reported achievements in restoring control must be assessed against the methods employed and the lives destroyed. Nor did the campaign establish the final endpoint of resistance. To call 1904 a turning point is defensible because Dutch territorial dominance expanded sharply, but describing it as the uncomplicated end of the Aceh War would erase both continuing opposition and the violence required to enforce colonial claims.

The reported Kuta Rih deaths should never be merged indiscriminately with figures for the entire Gayo–Alas expedition. Other fortified settlements were attacked and other civilians were killed, but each report has its own place, date and counting conventions. When later accounts supply an expedition-wide total, it must be labeled separately from the 561 deaths listed at Kuta Rih. This method also preserves the identities of Gayo and Alas communities rather than absorbing them into an undifferentiated picture of Acehnese resistance. The highland campaign was part of the war, but its victims belonged to communities with distinct local histories.

Section 104.17

The Human Cost: Civilian Deaths, Displacement, and Colonial Reprisals

The war's burden fell heavily on civilians. Villages were burned, crops destroyed, livestock requisitioned, and households displaced. Trade was disrupted by blockade and fighting, and disease spread in crowded and disrupted settings. The Dutch used collective punishment, taking hostages, destroying villages that sheltered fighters, and mounting punitive expeditions. Acehnese forces also pressed communities for food and loyalty and punished those suspected of cooperating with the Dutch, so civilians were caught between two armed sides.

Casualty figures must be handled with care. Dutch military deaths, including colonial Indonesian soldiers, number in the thousands, with disease responsible for many. Estimates of Acehnese deaths vary widely, from tens of thousands to figures above one hundred thousand, depending on whether they count combatants, civilians killed directly, or deaths from famine and disease. These categories should not be merged into a single falsely precise total. Specific incidents, such as the 1904 killings in the Gayo and Alas highlands, rest on perpetrators' own reports and photographs. Photographs are crucial evidence, but they raise ethical questions: they were made by those who carried out the violence, and displaying images of the dead requires respect for the victims and awareness of the original purpose of the images.

The war's human costs cannot be recovered from battlefield tallies alone. Villages lost access to fields when families fled, troops occupied roads or armed groups operated nearby. Irrigation systems and gardens could deteriorate when labor was diverted to defense, carrying supplies or avoiding patrols. Trading routes that had supported pepper production and household exchange became dangerous or more costly. The loss of a harvest was not just an immediate food shortage: it could also weaken a family the following season by consuming stored seed, livestock or savings. These gradual burdens rarely appear in a commander's concise account of a successful operation.

The violence took different forms over time. Early expeditions inflicted destruction during advances toward the capital. Protracted occupation brought blockades, patrols and attacks on suspected resistance strongholds. Later counterinsurgency emphasized intelligence, sudden movement and restrictions on support networks. These measures often blurred distinctions between combatants and the communities on which they depended. Collective punishment could include taking property, detaining relatives, destroying buildings or compelling movement. Such acts were not incidental to local memories of conquest; for people living outside the capital, they were among the principal ways colonial power was experienced.

Casualty estimates vary with the period and the method used. One historical synthesis places approximately 70,000 Acehnese dead and 17,500 deaths on the colonial side over the long war, while other estimates differ. These totals are not equivalent to audited lists of individuals and should not be presented as precise demographic findings. Combat losses, disease, hunger and indirect mortality are counted differently across sources. Nor did the colonial side consist only of European Dutch soldiers. The responsible practice is to give an estimate's author, period and counting convention before treating it as evidence of relative suffering or military success.

The 1904 Kuta Rih records demonstrate both the value and the limits of unusually detailed evidence. When a military report lists the deaths of hundreds of women and children, it supplies a fact that cannot be dissolved into the vague phrase 'heavy casualties.' Yet the report was produced by the force that killed them, and the accompanying photographs were framed by its personnel. Historians can establish the place, the date, the reported breakdown and the relationship between the images and the campaign. They cannot automatically reconstruct every victim's actions or the exact sequence of events inside the settlement. Ethical use of these materials requires precision, care with captions and recognition of whose stories remain absent.

Memory of war can survive in material surroundings as well as official documents. Abandoned garden land, destroyed buildings, cemeteries and disrupted settlement patterns may testify to episodes poorly covered in surviving correspondence. Oral histories can identify places remembered for flight or killings, although such testimonies require dating and comparison with other evidence. A comprehensive history should not privilege a colonial tally solely because it is numerical or dismiss a local tradition solely because it was recorded later. Each kind of source is useful for some questions and limited for others.

Section 104.18

Women in the Aceh War: Cut Nyak Dhien, Cut Meutia, and Religious Leadership

Acehnese women held notable positions in society, including in landholding, trade, and religious education, and in the war they appear as leaders, fighters, organizers, and sustainers of households. Cut Nyak Dhien, from an aristocratic family and the wife of Teuku Umar, continued leading a guerrilla force after his death in 1899. Aging and ill, she was captured in 1905 and exiled to Sumedang in West Java, where she died in 1908. Cut Meutia fought in northern Aceh alongside her husbands, continued resistance after their deaths, and was killed in 1910. Teungku Fakinah, a religious teacher, is remembered for organizing education and wartime support.

Focusing only on these commanders risks leaving out most women. Many were displaced, widowed, or left to support families alone. Women worked in fields and markets, carried messages and supplies, nursed the wounded, and maintained religious and kin networks that sustained communities under occupation. Evidence for these ordinary roles is thinner, found in Dutch reports, oral memory, and later biographies, and it should be used carefully, since later commemoration has sometimes made individual women into national symbols.

Women's participation cannot be understood solely through the category of the woman warrior. Many Acehnese women possessed influence within households, trade and kinship networks that mattered to the ability of fighters to travel, find shelter and obtain food. Others managed land and family property in the absence of men, protected children during military operations or organized the movement of relatives to safer locations. Their work could sustain resistance without appearing in the military sources that catalogued commanders and engagements. The relative invisibility of these activities is an archival problem, not proof that the activities were unimportant.

Cut Nyak Dhien's political and military significance preceded Teuku Umar's death. Her relationship with leading west-coast families connected personal experience, regional leadership and the strain of continued conflict. After Umar was killed in 1899 she remained associated with resistance despite worsening conditions, age and illness. Captured in 1905, she was eventually removed from Aceh and exiled to Sumedang in West Java, where she died in 1908. Later Indonesian commemoration has rightly emphasized her public role, but celebratory biography can compress the uncertainty, losses and practical pressures through which she lived.

Cut Meutia's resistance was rooted in northern Aceh and continued into the period after the main Dutch proclamations of victory. Accounts of her campaigns describe partnerships with male commanders and her own continuing leadership under increasingly dangerous circumstances. She was killed in 1910, a direct reminder that 1904 did not mean the cessation of organized armed opposition. Teungku Fakinah represents another pattern: religious teaching, organization and support for wartime networks were forms of leadership that conventional military histories often miss. The lives of these women differed; they should not be turned into interchangeable examples of heroism.

The evidence also requires caution. Later nationalist portraits, regional oral histories, biographical retellings and Dutch security reports were written for different audiences and purposes. A dramatic speech attributed to a woman decades after her death is not automatically a reliable verbatim quotation. A colonial officer's description of a woman as merely accompanying a commander may understate her authority. By comparing source types, students can reconstruct participation without pretending that every detail is documented. A strong women's history asks who performed the work of survival as well as who carried weapons, and recognizes that colonial violence disproportionately burdened those responsible for keeping households together.

Exile also has a distinct significance in these biographies. Removing a resistance leader from Aceh prevented communication with local supporters and transformed that person's political role into one shaped by displacement, surveillance and remembered reputation. Accounts of Cut Nyak Dhien's life in Sumedang consequently belong to the aftermath of conquest as well as to the history of wartime leadership. Her burial place and later commemoration generated new relationships between a local Acehnese history and a wider Indonesian public. Those later acts of remembrance should be dated separately from her decisions during the war.

Section 104.19

After 1904: Continuing Resistance and the Construction of Colonial Government

The submissions of 1903 and the campaigns of 1904 did not end all resistance. Armed bands continued to operate, led by figures such as Cut Meutia and other local commanders, until about 1912–1913, when organized resistance was largely suppressed. The scale of fighting diminished, but the ability of Acehnese communities to oppose the Dutch persisted in other forms, including religious teaching and local networks.

The Dutch built a colonial administration on the basis of agreements with local rulers. The Korte Verklaring, or Short Declaration, was a brief written statement through which an uleebalang acknowledged Dutch sovereignty and accepted specified obligations, while retaining a measure of local authority. Together with policing, taxation, and administrative supervision, it created a system of indirect rule that relied on cooperative aristocrats. This arrangement shifted power within Acehnese society and later became a source of tension. The difference between defeating leaders and establishing broadly accepted authority remained. The later twentieth-century history, including the role of Aceh in the independence struggle and later conflicts, is reserved for other chapters (see Chapter 213).

After the great expeditions, Dutch officials tried to convert military superiority into routine institutions. A district had to be mapped, its leaders identified, disputes referred to recognized authorities, and taxes or labor demands collected. These operations required local cooperation and generated new reasons for resistance. Military posts remained necessary in some places, while residents and subordinate officials sought a more predictable administrative order. A government that could compel a chief to sign a document still had to discover whether communities would accept his authority and the new duties imposed upon them.

The Korte Verklaring, or Short Declaration, became a familiar instrument of colonial relations with indigenous rulers. Its short formula demanded acknowledgment of Dutch supremacy, restrictions on independent foreign contacts and obedience to colonial regulations. The document could simplify Dutch legal claims, but it did not by itself define the practical reach of a ruler's authority. Some chiefs used the relationship to survive or strengthen their position relative to rivals; others faced suspicion from local opponents. The resulting arrangements altered the balance among hereditary leaders, religious institutions and villages, sometimes widening divisions that had emerged during the war.

Resistance did not vanish in 1904. Figures including Cut Meutia continued fighting, and smaller armed groups and individual attacks occurred beyond the years of the major campaigns. Some scholarship chooses approximately 1912–1913 as the end of the principal military phase, while studies of colonial violence track conflict and opposition into later decades. The disagreement is partly definitional. Was the war over when the sultan submitted, when the colonial army could patrol most districts, when large coordinated forces ceased to operate, or when acts of violent opposition became uncommon? Each test yields a different chronology.

Colonial occupation also changed everyday legal and economic life. A household might encounter new registration requirements, policing, revised courts or levies that were administered through local chiefs. Roads and posts that enabled trade could also enable surveillance. Religious teachers retained influence, but officials monitored religious gatherings that they associated with political mobilization. The experience of 'pacification' was therefore not a single peace settlement. It was an extended transformation in the means by which authorities extracted information, settled disputes and compelled obedience. The chapter should end this story at the point where the colonial state's basic dominance was established, without implying that Acehnese political aspirations ceased to exist.

The continuing struggle also shaped colonial institutions that outlived the period of principal military campaigns. Registration of households, police intelligence, supervision of chiefs and regulation of travel created durable channels of state information. Even where a region experienced declining open warfare, people could remain vulnerable to surveillance or punitive action. This is why the term 'pacification' requires quotation marks or an explicit explanation: it was the vocabulary of the conquering administration, not a guarantee that violence had ceased or that local populations freely accepted the new political order.

Everyday Life and Social History

A Village in a Contested District

Consider an Acehnese village in the years after 1896, an illustrative composite based on patterns historians document rather than a single named community. Rice land and gardens support households. The village chief and religious leader mediate disputes. A mukim mosque anchors worship. When fighters pass through, they ask for food and shelter. When Dutch columns arrive, they demand information and punish those suspected of aiding resistance. Fields may be abandoned or burned, and men absent for fighting or hiding.

Women farm, trade, and care for children and the elderly. Some families send members to fight and others seek accommodation. Religious teaching continues in homes and dayah, and recitation of poems like the Hikayat Prang Sabi may take place, though possession of texts can bring suspicion. For many, the war meant uncertainty, hunger, and displacement more than battle.

Section 104.20

Historical Synthesis: The Aceh War and the Limits of Colonial Power

The Aceh War combined several dimensions. Diplomatically, it grew from the Anglo-Dutch settlement of 1871 and Dutch fear of foreign influence. Politically, it pitted a colonial state against a decentralized sultanate. Religiously, it mobilized ideas of defensive war and the authority of ulama. Militarily, it passed from expeditions to a defensive line and mobile counterinsurgency. Socially, it brought displacement, killing, and the reshaping of local elites.

The Dutch needed decades because Acehnese society had no single point of surrender, because local communities and networks sustained resistance, and because colonial knowledge and strategy developed slowly. The Acehnese strategy changed too, from defense of the capital to dispersed guerrilla warfare to localized resistance under mounting pressure. The war's meaning has also been contested. Dutch accounts celebrated pacification, and later Indonesian national memory honored figures like Cut Nyak Dhien as heroes. Both narratives are historical constructions. Nineteenth-century Acehnese defenders fought for their sultanate, religion, and communities, not for an Indonesian nation that did not yet exist as a political project (see Chapter 126).

The war's beginning and end are best understood through the mismatch between political claims and social control. The Netherlands could issue a declaration of war in March 1873, and later a proclamation of sovereignty in January 1874, but neither document could dictate how regional Acehnese leaders or communities would respond. The defeat of the first expedition demonstrated the danger of relying on poor intelligence and assumptions about the vulnerability of a royal capital. The second expedition occupied that capital without destroying the political networks sustaining resistance. What appeared at first to be a straightforward invasion became a conflict over the organization of society itself.

Acehnese resistance changed without losing its local roots. Royal authority furnished a symbol of sovereignty, while uleebalang and ulama could mobilize resources through their own networks. Religious writings gave moral language to struggles that also concerned land, ports, families and regional autonomy. Negotiations and changing allegiances were part of this history, not anomalies to be explained away. Teuku Umar's decisions and the distinct careers of female leaders show how political action remained contingent. Individuals confronted choices made under pressure, with uncertain information about what the next campaign would bring.

Dutch military strategy developed through expensive failure. Coastal attack and occupation gave way to scattered posts, then defensive concentration, then a greater reliance on intelligence, mobile troops and arrangements with chiefs. The last phase made the colonial army more effective at imposing its will, but the resulting atrocities cannot be separated from the history of its success. The 1904 Gayo–Alas campaign provides unusually specific evidence of women and children killed during operations officially described as pacification. Such evidence is not a marginal moral footnote: it is essential to explaining what military control demanded of the people living in the districts conquered.

Finally, memory has its own chronology. Colonial authors could celebrate submission as order restored; Indonesian commemorations later honored Acehnese figures within a national narrative of resistance. Both traditions illuminate how later generations used the past, but neither can substitute for reconstruction of nineteenth-century aims. The appropriate conclusion is not that Aceh had already become a modern Indonesian nation, nor that conquest was destined to prevail. Rather, its political institutions and social networks prolonged resistance for decades, while colonial coercion ultimately reorganized the region on terms its inhabitants had not freely chosen. The distinction between a declared victory and lived political authority remains the chapter's governing insight.

A comparative reading with Chapters 101 and 103 clarifies what was distinctive. The Java War ended with the capture of a central leader and a comparatively rapid consolidation of state authority in core districts; the Aceh War persisted amid multiple overlapping centers of allegiance. Elsewhere beyond Java, Dutch treaties sometimes preceded military occupation by decades. Aceh combined a vulnerable international diplomatic position with exceptionally durable regional and religious resistance. This comparison avoids treating either Aceh or Dutch power as timeless or exceptional by nature, and shows how institutions and historical circumstances shaped particular outcomes.

Chapter Summary

The Aceh War began in 1873 when the Netherlands, freed from a British diplomatic constraint by the Sumatra Treaty of 1871, declared war on the Aceh Sultanate. Dutch officials justified it by pointing to Acehnese contacts with foreign representatives, but the underlying cause was a long-standing ambition to consolidate Dutch control of Sumatra and the northern Strait of Malacca.

The first expedition of April 1873 failed. General Köhler was killed, and the force withdrew, exposing weak intelligence and logistics. A larger second expedition took the royal compound in January 1874, but the sultan's death from cholera and the Dutch proclamation of annexation did not end Acehnese political authority.

Acehnese society was decentralized, with power shared among the sultan, uleebalang, ulama, mukim, and villages. This made it hard to conquer but also hard to coordinate. Resistance drew on royal legitimacy, religious obligation, local autonomy, and kinship, and it cannot be reduced to fanaticism or to anticipatory nationalism.

From the 1870s to the 1890s, resistance under leaders such as Teungku Chik di Tiro and Teuku Umar forced the Dutch to a defensive posture. The concentrated line of 1884 secured the area around Kutaraja but left the countryside to Acehnese forces. The Hikayat Prang Sabi and the idea of prang sabil show how religious literature supported mobilization.

In the 1890s Dutch policy changed. Snouck Hurgronje's research and advice provided knowledge of Acehnese divisions, and the Korps Marechaussee provided mobile forces. Under Van Heutsz from 1898, mobile columns, agreements with uleebalang, and pressure on civilians eroded resistance. Teuku Umar was killed in 1899.

The sultan submitted in January 1903 under pressure, and Panglima Polem followed. In 1904 Van Daalen's expedition into the Gayo and Alas highlands, including the assault on Kuta Rih, killed large numbers of civilians according to Dutch records and photographs.

Civilians bore a heavy burden: displacement, destruction, disease, collective punishment, and killing. Casualty estimates vary widely and must be distinguished by category. Women served as commanders, teachers, and sustainers of communities, and figures such as Cut Nyak Dhien and Cut Meutia continued resistance after 1904.

Armed resistance persisted until about 1912–1913. The Dutch built colonial government through the Korte Verklaring and cooperation with uleebalang. Military victory, political submission, and acceptance of Dutch rule happened at different times, which is why the war's end is contested.

The war revealed the power and limits of European imperialism. Dutch resources eventually prevailed, but only through adaptation, intelligence, political bargaining, and overwhelming coercion, producing a destructive transformation of Acehnese society whose consequences outlasted the colonial proclamation of victory.

Chapter Reflection

The Aceh War matters because it complicates the picture of nineteenth-century colonial conquest as a smooth advance of superior technology. Dutch resources were greater, but they were insufficient until the Dutch learned to combine military mobility with political knowledge and sustained coercion.

It also shows how a decentralized society could generate resilience. Authority resting on sultan, aristocrats, scholars, mukim, and villages meant that no single loss could end resistance. The same fragmentation, however, allowed the Dutch to exploit divisions through bargaining and co-option.

The war shaped later history. The colonial system built on the Korte Verklaring altered the position of local elites, religious networks persisted, and memory of the war became part of Acehnese identity and, later, of Indonesian national commemoration. Students should keep nineteenth-century motives distinct from later constructions.

Chapter 105 turns to the British ports of Penang, Singapore, and Melaka, whose commercial networks formed part of the setting in which Aceh's war unfolded.
